## What this book is

A devotional, popular retelling of twenty janamsakhi stories of Guru Nanak, each novelised with invented dialogue and usually closed by the author's moral reflection; Dhillon says he stripped the stories "of all supernatural elements, including miracles" (p. 13, folio 13) and "used my imagination to build up details of backgrounds, settings, incidents and even characters" (p. 13, folio 13). Everything below from the stories is **Dhillon's retelling of janamsakhi tradition, not history**; the Introduction (pp. 9–16) and the Notes (pp. 261–267, many glosses citing "Wikipedia") are his own expository prose, and the back cover (p. 271) is the publisher's blurb. It says almost nothing about the present state of any shrine; most archive attachments are person- or place-level. The text layer is readable but has systematic, non-reversible letter confusions (`t`→`c`/`r`), flagged `[text-layer?]` on every carried name, place and numeral.

### gurdwara-darbar-sahib-kartarpur — Gurdwara Darbar Sahib Kartarpur

The book's "Kartarpur (the abode of God)", garbled also as "Karrarpur", "Kanarpur", "Karcarpur" [text-layer?] [Kartarpur]. Mapped because the book places it on the Ravi and "now in Pakistan" (pp. 175–176, 257). Kartarpur passages that do not themselves place the town (p. 134 "details given later"; pp. 224–227) are attached here by that identification.
- **Saint**
  - "Kartarpur, which Guru Nanak founded and where he breathed his last on 22 September 1539, is at a distance of 80 km from Lahore and 180 km from Nanakana Sahib (earlier called Talwandi) via Lahore and only about 2 km from the Indo-Pak border on the right bank of river Ravi" (p. 175, folio "l92" [text-layer?] [192]). The printed year agrees with the row's `figure_died` 1539.
  - Founding, in Dhillon's retelling: Nanak goes to "the region between the rivers Ravi and Beas"; "On the banks of the Ravi" he chooses land for "his permanent home"; "Ajic [text-layer?] [Ajit] Randhawa of Pakhoke village, along with a group of other farmers, pledged this piece of land", and "the foundations of a new settlement, Kartarpur (the abode of God) were laid" (p. 175, folio "l92" [text-layer?] [192]).
  - After "a few months in Kartarpur, Nanak resumed his third journey" (p. 184, folio 203). "Finally Nanak reached Karcarpur [text-layer?] [Kartarpur], which marked the end of his fourth and last udasi" (p. 225, folio 247); there he "laid aside the pilgrim's clothes… and assumed the garb of an ordinary householder" (p. 227, folio 251).
  - Death: Nanak lay dying "under a uee [text-layer?] [tree] on the bank of the river Ravi, just outside Kartarpur" (p. 257, folio 285); "(The date: 22 September 1539.)" (p. 259, folio 287). Muslim followers claimed the body for burial ("He is ours, our peer"), Hindus for cremation (pp. 257–260, folios 285–288; p. 258, folio 286).
- **Shrine / Darbar**
  - "Nanak's shrine stands our [text-layer?] [out] as there is no habitation around"; a nearby railway station "is named Darbar Sahib Karrarpur [text-layer?] [Kartarpur] on the Lahore-Chak Amru line, in Shakkar Garh, district Narowal ofWest Punjab (now in Pakistan)" (p. 175, folio "l92" [text-layer?] [192]; p. 176, folio 193). This is the book's only description of any present-day site.
  - Distinguished from "the Kartarpur town that today flourishes about 15 km from Jalandhar", founded "in 1594" by Guru Arjan Dev (p. 175, folio "l92" [text-layer?] [192]).
- **Practices & Events** (Dhillon's retelling / framing)
  - People "of every faith and religion" came; Kartarpur, "like Talwandi and Sulcanpur [text-layer?] [Sultanpur], soon took on the semblance of a pilgrim town"; before the third udasi "both his and Mardana's parents came and cook [text-layer?] [took] up residence in Kartarpur" (p. 176, folio 193). It "had by now grown into a full-fledged township" of people "from different social backgrounds… castes and creeds" (p. 227, folio 251).
  - The dera: the Brahmin comes "in the vicinity of the dera chat [that] was being built at Karcarpur [text-layer?] [Kartarpur]" (p. 178, folio 195); Duni Chand of Lahore was "one of the greatest contributors" to "the dera (can1p [text-layer?] [camp] or sertlemenc [text-layer?] [settlement]) and the township at Kartarpu.r [text-layer?] [Kartarpur]" (p. 134, folio 148); disciples "flocked to Karrarpur [text-layer?] [Kartarpur] and made their homes in che [text-layer?] [the] dera" (p. 240, folio 265).
  - "The three great traditions chat [text-layer?] [that] were established in Karrarpur [text-layer?] [Kartarpur] were the traditions of seva or sel.Bess [text-layer?] [selfless] service, kirtan or religious singing and langar or free kitchen" (p. 228, folio 252); "In Kanarpur [text-layer?] [Kartarpur], these traditions became firmly entrenched" (p. 229, folio 253).
  - Kirtan: the Guru's compositions "were sung at the appropriate times ofthe day to the accompaniment ofMardana's rabaab" (p. 229, folio 253).
  - Langar: everyone "whoever could do so" drew water, ground wheat, collected wood, cooked, served and scrubbed; it "came co [text-layer?] [to] foster the spirit of equality, humility and brotherhood" (p. 229, folio 253); people brought "whatever they could for the evening langar" (p. 184, folio 203); langar in the Brahmin story (pp. 179, 182, folios 196, 199).
  - Routine: its people were "by no means members ofa [of a] religious or monastic order"; each went about "his or her ordinary, everyday occupation" (p. 227, folio 251). Fields worked by the Guru and disciples, "sevadars" with "bullock-carts", and a "dharamshala" with a "newly built wing" that nearly collapsed in the monsoon until Lehna led an overnight repair (pp. 252–254, folios not stated / 281).
  - Settlers: "the original inhabitants of Kartarpur, who had come and settled here, when the Guru had first earmarked the site", and "new settlers" from follower communities "all along the routes that he had followed on his udasis" (p. 230, folio 254).
- **Legends & Miracles** (Dhillon's retelling)
  - The Brahmin and his "kitchen": digging a cubit of consecrated ground turns up bones; the Brahmin relents: "Can we please go and partake of the kmgar [text-layer?] [langar]?" (pp. 178–182, folios 195–199; bones p. 180, folio 197).
  - Moola Keer, "One of the earliest settlers in Kartarpur", covers for the guest who stole his wife's jewellery; the thief confesses after kirtan (pp. 230–237, folios 254–261; p. 236 folio not stated).
  - Lehna, on pilgrimage to Jwalamukhi, "reached Kartarpur" and stayed, working in fields, at "a construction site" and in the "l.angar" [text-layer?] [langar] (pp. 248–249, folio not stated / 276); ordained "the second Guru of the Sikhs, Guru Angad Dev" (p. 255, folio 282).
  - The flowers laid by Muslims and Hindus beside the dying Nanak were both found unwithered next day (pp. 258–260, folios 286–288).
- **Significance**
  - Author: "Even in death, Nanak had underscored the lesson that he had taught all his life: All human beings are equal" (p. 260, folio 288).

### gurdwara-rori-sahib — Gurdwara Rori Sahib

Person rule: Bhai Mardana (the book's "Mardana"). The book never names Eminabad or Rori Sahib; person-level throughout.
- **Saint**
  - "Accompanied by his friend Mardana, the rabaab player (note 13), Nanak set out on his travels" (p. 53, folio 59; p. 54, folio 60). He travels with Nanak eastwards "all the way to Assam" (p. 109, folio not stated), to Haridwar (p. 78, folio 86), Prayag (p. 102, folio 113), Lahore (p. 126, folio 140), Sialkot (pp. 137–158), Multan (pp. 169–170, folios 185–186), Mecca, Medina and Baghdad (pp. 194, 196, 204, folios 214, 216, not stated), "Tilla Balnath and Katasraj" (p. 224, folio 246).
  - Character, in Dhillon's retelling: "perpetual hunger" (p. 56, folio 62); "perpetually suffering from foot sores" (p. 84, folio 93); homesick — "Often he would sob into his pillow" (p. 110, folio 122).
  - His parents took up residence in Kartarpur (p. 176, folio 193); he and Nanak "had spent a lifetime on the road" (p. 184, folio 203). He "whose multifaceted relationship with the Guru defied a label or a name" had died before Nanak (p. 257, folio 285).
- **Practices & Events**
  - The rabaab: he would "take up his rabaab and provide an accompaniment for the divinely inspired verses that Nanak sang" (pp. 228–229, folios 252–253); "'Mardana, Bani aaye hai - I have inspiration for a Bani'" (pp. 228–229, folios 252–253); "Mardana rook [text-layer?] [took] up his rabaab" (p. 115, folio 127); "Mardana coo [text-layer?] [too] played his rabaab" at Prayag (p. 102, folio 113); "Mardana tuned his rabaab and struck up the accompaniment co [text-layer?] [to] his master's singing" at the Baghdad tomb (p. 206, folio 227).
- **Legends & Miracles** (Dhillon's retelling)
  - At Saidpur he refuses Lalo's millet roti until Nanak calls it prasad (p. 60, folio 66; p. 61, folio 67); fetches Lalo's roti to Bhago's house (p. 70, folio 76; p. 71, folio 77).
  - Turned away house to house in the inhospitable village (pp. 113–114, folios 125–126); asks why the two villages received opposite blessings (pp. 120–121, folios 132–133).
  - At Sialkot he is sent to the faqir, given the two slips, and helped by Moola (pp. 151–158, folios 166–173).
  - At the Multan gate he asks why the milk is "not sufficient co [to] provide susrenance [sustenance] ro [to] even one of us" (p. 169, folio 185; p. 170, folio 186).
  - At Saidpur "legend has it that he and Mardana coo [text-layer?] [too] were taken prisoner" (p. 224, folio 246).

### gurdwara-khoohi-bhai-lalo-bhai-lalo-di-khooi — Gurdwara Khoohi Bhai Lalo (Bhai Lalo di Khooi)

Person rule: the book's "Lalo", "Bhai Lalo", "La.lo [text-layer?] [Lalo]". The story is set at "Saidpur", which the book does not identify with Eminabad (see Doubts); person-level only.
- **Saint**
  - A low-caste carpenter in a "single room, thatched" shanty on the outskirts of Saidpur: "'People call me La.lo. I am a carpenter'" (p. 57, folio 63; p. 58, folio 64). He works "on a daily wage" for "Ramdev, the master carpenter" and "could nor [not] afford to miss a single day of work" (p. 63, folio 69).
  - "they came to Saidpur, where one of Nanak's fuse [text-layer?] [first] disciples Lalo resided" (p. 224, folio 246).
- **Practices & Events** (Dhillon's retelling)
  - His poverty: one oil lamp, a loosely strung charpoy, "a single, thick bajre ki roti (millet roci [roti]) and a single, small onion" (p. 60, folio 66).
  - He warns Nanak that staying with him risks excommunication "from your biradari"; Nanak: "in God's eyes a man's worth is never measured in terms of his caste or social status or his wealth" (p. 59, folio 65).
  - He sings "a Kabir (note 14) bhajan" while sweeping (p. 62, folio 68); Nanak asks him to lead "one of Bhagat Kabir's hymns" (p. 64, folio 70; p. 65, folio 71); at his hut Nanak sings "Ik Onkar Satnam ... (there is only one God Satna.m [text-layer?] [Satnam] ...)" and villagers gather (p. 64, folio 70).
- **Legends & Miracles** (Dhillon's retelling)
  - Lalo marvels "at the prescience displayed by Nanak" (p. 63, folio 69).
  - Malik Bhago calls his bread "the coarse bajre ki roti from a wretched carpenter's hovel" (p. 71, folio 77); Nanak says Lalo eats only what "has been earned through hard labour" and its ingredients "arc [text-layer?] [are] the milk of human kindness and generosity" (p. 72, folio 78), while Bhago's twenty-one dishes are "the sweat and blood and tears of the suffering poor" (p. 71, folio 77). Bhago repents; "Nanak moved on from Saidpur" (p. 72–73, folios 78–79).

### gurdwara-babay-nanki — Gurdwara Babay Nanki

Person rule: Bebe Nanaki, the book's "Nanki", "Na.nki", "Nank.i" [text-layer?] [Nanki]. The book places her at Sultanpur; the row is in Lahore (see Doubts).
- **Saint**
  - "Na.nki, Nanak's siscer [sister]" and "her husband Jai Ram", "a revenue official in the employment of Nawab Daulat Khan Lodhi, the governor of Sultanpur", arrange for Nanak to come to Sultanpur (p. 43, folio 48). Earlier she restrains her father when he berates Nanak (p. 38, folio 42).
  - When Nanak disappears in the river she is the "one other person" besides Daulat Khan who believes he lives: "'My brother will return safe'" (p. 46, folio 51). "the most ardent of these disciples were his sister Nanki and her husband Jai Ram" (p. 53, folio 59).
  - Joy at his returns (p. 124, folio 138; p. 172, folio 188; p. 193, folio "2 13" [text-layer?] [213], "Nanki and ]AI RA.M's [text-layer?] [Jai Ram's] hearts"); "Nanki, though saddened by her brother's decision, did not attempt ro [to] sway him" (p. 174, folio 191); at her plea he deferred departure (p. 194, folio 214).
  - Death: "Nanki came down with a mysterious ailment and breathed her last in 1518 at the age of 6:fcy-four [text-layer?] [sixty-four]"; Jai Ram, "not able to sustain the loss, also passed away" three days later (p. 194, folio 214). "1518" reads clean and agrees with the row's `figure_died`. Named again among those who died before Nanak (p. 257, folio 285).

### gurdwara-sacha-sauda — Gurdwara Sacha Sauda

**Judgement mapping.** The book ties the story to the woods between Talwandi and "the nearby market town of Chuharkhana"; it names no gurdwara and no modern town. The row's location "Farooqabad (formerly Chuharkana)" comes from the index, not the book.
- **Legends & Miracles** (Dhillon's retelling of ch. 2, "Sacha Sauda", glossed "*The true bargain.")
  - Mehta Kalu gives Nanak "twenty silver coins" and a list of goods to buy "from the nearby market town of Chuharkhana at wholesale prices" to sell in Talwandi (p. 25, folio 29).
  - Nanak and Bala take "the path through the woods" instead of "the thoroughfare to Chuharkhana" and meet "dozens of sadhus in the forest", the Nagas, unfed "in a week" (pp. 26–32, folios 30–32; pp. 31–32 folio not stated).
  - In Chuharkhana he buys from "the biggest wholesale grocer" "enough food to feed fifty starving sadhus", with vegetables, fruit, firewood, two cooks and two porters, and feeds them at "the grove of trees" (pp. 33–35, folios 37–39). "Any dilution of my bargain will be a dilution of the truth" (p. 34, folio 38); feeding them is "a truly selfless service" (p. 35, folio 39).
  - Mehta Kalu confronts him (pp. 36–37, folios 40–41) and calls him a failure; Rai Bular defends him — he "used the money to feed a whole akhara of famished sadhus", a bargain "true in the eyes of God" (p. 38, folio 42; p. 39, folio 43). Pandit Hardyal gives his age: "any ocher [text-layer?] [other] sixteen-year-old" (p. 40, folio 44).

### Guru Nanak (person-level: 18 rows)
**gurudwara-janam-asthan-nankana-sahib**, **gurdwara-babay-de-ber**, **gurdwara-balila-sahib-bal-lila-sahib**, **gurdwara-chakki-sahib**, **gurdwara-choa-sahib**, **gurdwara-darbar-sahib-kartarpur**, **gurdwara-khoohi-bhai-lalo-bhai-lalo-di-khooi**, **gurdwara-malji-sahib**, **gurdwara-panja-sahib**, **gurdwara-patti-sahib**, **gurdwara-pehli-patshahi-jind-pir-sukkur**, **gurdwara-rori-sahib**, **gurdwara-sach-khand-sahib**, **gurdwara-sacha-sauda**, **gurdwara-sahib-saidpur-guru-nanak-dev-ji**, **gurdwara-sri-kiara-sahib**, **gurdwara-sri-tilganji-sahib**, **gurdwara-tambo-sahib**

The book's "Guru Nanak", "Nanak", "Nanak Dev", "the Guru". Person rule; no story is tied by the book to these sites except where given in the single rows above.
- **Saint** (author's Introduction and Notes)
  - The Janamsakhis are "cycles of tales purportedly drawn from the life of the first Guru of the Sikhs, Guru Nanak (f469-1539) [text-layer?] [1469-1539]" (p. 9, folio 9); Nanak's Bani "was compiled as part of the Adi Granth by Guru Arjan Dev, the fifth Guru, in 1604" (p. 9, folio 9).
  - Nanak was "no unworldly mystic" but "a householder in every sense of the word" who "brought a message of peace and harmony to believers of the mutually hostile religions of Islam and Hinduism" (p. 12, folio not stated); he taught that "all men and women were equal, irrespective of caste, creed, wealth, social standing and gender", and that a good life rested on honest labour, compassion, selfless service and prayer (pp. 12–13, folio not stated / 13).
  - "Guru Nanak's clear and emphatic disavowal of the miraculous" (printed "dear" [text-layer?] [clear]) (p. 11, folio 11).
  - The stories are placed "against the background of Guru Nanak's udasis (travels)" (p. 13, folio 13).
  - Two udasi schemes: "According to the Puratan Janamsakhi… he undertook five journeys (also known as udasis)": first "(1499-1506)", second "(1506-1513)", "Third ,ul.asi [text-layer?] [udasi] (1514-1518)", "Fourth tulasi [text-layer?] [udasi] (1519-1521)", "Fifth udasi (1523-1524)" (pp. 54–55, folios 60–61). "More modern accounts" give four, "which spanned rwency-three [text-layer?] [twenty-three] years": "(1499-1505)" central and eastern India; "(1506-1509)" southern India and Sri Lanka; "(1514-1516)" Gangetic plains to Tashkent and back via Kashmir; "(1518-1521)" Arab countries (p. 55, folio 61). The author: "Personally, I believe chat [that] he undertook four udasis and not five" (p. 56, folio 62).
  - Publisher's back-cover blurb: "The earliest known janamsakhi dates bade [text-layer?] [dates back] to 1658 nearly 120 years after the death of Guru Nanak in 1539"; "~one [text-layer?] [None] of the Janarnsakhis [text-layer?] [Janamsakhis] can claim to be the most authentic or authontatne [text-layer?] [authoritative] version" (p. 271, folio not stated).
- **Life, in Dhillon's retelling** (Talwandi material is also carried in the Nankana Sahib group below)
  - Birth "On 14 April 1469" to Kalyan Chand Das Bedi, "or Mehta Kalu as he was affectionately called", accountant of the Bhatti family under Rai Bular (pp. 17–18, folios 19–20); "Pandit Ha.rdyal [text-layer?] [Hardyal], the village astrologer" foretold greatness (p. 18, folio 20).
  - At thirteen he refused the janeu in "Mehta Kahis [text-layer?] [Mehta Kalu's]" courtyard: "it is our actions and our actions alone" that give position among men; "the beginning of a lifelong crusade against the performing of empty rites and rituals" (pp. 19–21, folios 21–23).
  - At sixteen he herded cattle (p. 23, folio 27); "became well acquainted with the dervish Sayyad Hussain and gained an intimate knowledge of the Sufi line of thought" (p. 24, folio 28).
  - "Nanak moved to Sultanpur (around 60 km from Talwandi)" as keeper of Nawab Daulat Khan's "modikhana (score [text-layer?] [store])"; bathing in "the Bein rivulet"; evening gatherings to hear his hymns (p. 44, folio 49).
  - "He got married on 24 September 1487 to the gencle [gentle], kind-hearted Sulakhini, daughter of Mul Chand and Chando Rani of the town of Batala" (p. 44, folio 49); sons "Sri Chand (8 September 1494) and Lakhmi Das (12 February 1497)" (p. 44, folio 49).
  - Vanishes in the stream a week; found "on che [the] left bank of the river": "There is only one God. There is no Hindu, no Mussalman." (pp. 45–46, folios 50–51). The qazi of "Sulranpur [text-layer?] [Sultanpur]" and the Friday namaz: "My prayers were not true prayers" (pp. 46–50, folios 51–55).
  - Sets out "dressed… in a strange garb - an approximate combination of what the holy men of Hinduism and of Islam would wear" (p. 53, folio 59).

## Cross-cutting material

- **The author on his sources.** The Janamsakhis "are not designed as biographies" but serve "to preach a moral or illustrate an essential part of the Guru's life and his teachings" (p. 9, folio 9). Four extant sets: "the Bala Janamsakhis, the Puratan Janamsakhis, the Meheraban Janamsakhis" and those "written supposedly by Bhai Mani Singh", drawn from Nanak's Bani and the first var of Bhai Gurdas (p. 9, folio 9). Embellishment "led to contradictions and anachronisms"; the Sakhis "do not form any part of formal Sikh religious texts, largely because many of them are apocryphal" (p. 10, folio not stated). "the veracity of the entire cycle of stories in the very popular Bala Janamsakhis became suspect" (p. 11, folio 11), yet "there can be no doubt of the essential veracity of the picture of Guru Nanak that emerges" (p. 13, folio 13). "The Joy of Giving and The Widow and Her Lost Son" have no written reference he could find; he heard them in childhood sermons (p. 13, folio 13).
- **Notes and bibliography.** Cited: Kirpal Singh, *Janamsakhi Tradition – An Analytical Study* (Amritsar, 2004); Harbans Singh, *Perspectives on Guru Nanak* (1975) and *Guru Nanak and Origins of the Sikh Faith* (1969); K. S. Duggal; Khushwant Singh, *A History of the Sikhs*, Vol. I "1469-1839" (1963) and *Japjee* (1999); Gurbachan Singh Talib; W. H. McLeod, *Exploring Sikhism* (2003); many glosses cite "Wikipedia" (pp. 261–267). Further Reading adds Dhillon's own earlier books, Navtej Sarna, and Kirpal Singh & Kharak Singh (SGPC, 1994) (p. 268, folio 297). Sources acknowledged: Bhai Sikander Singh for "the original 'azaan'", Bhim Inder for Bani quotations (p. 269, folio 299).
- **Sufis in the book's frame.** "a Muslim sect called the Sufis introduced music as an integral part of the practice of Islam" (p. 16, folio 18); "The Sufis were revered by both Hindus and Muslims in India, and to this day the Sufi dargahs (shrines) stand testament to the fact" (p. 24, folio 28). Multan's conversions "were by and large voluntary, spurred on by the renunciation char [that] the Sufi saints practised" (p. 167, folio 183). Compassion to the fallen is what "followers of all Sufi traditions practise" (p. 159, folio 174). Dervishes "first appeared in the cwelfth [text-layer?] [twelfth] century" (note 5, p. 261, folio 289). The Bhaktas preached one formless God and human equality (p. 16, folio 18).
- **Holy men and their power.** "deep in the subconscious of almost all Indians, there lurks the certainty that a holy man may not always have the power to help, but he certainly has the power to cause harm" (p. 151, folio 166); the moral of the Sialkot story: "compassion and piety were more becoming to men of God than the exercising of occult powers" (p. 161, folio 177). Vows of the firstborn to a faqir's "dertt.. [text-layer?] [dera]", with the author's claim "Historically, over 90 per cent of the parents who gave up their firstborn to God soon went on to have a second child" (p. 142, folio 157); "the chaliha, the forty-day period of fasting and self-mortification" (p. 151, folio 166); forty days' "quarantine" of mother and child, then a feast for "all the holy men of the city" (p. 143, folio 158).
- **Langar, seva, dharamshala.** Seva defined: "voluntary service, physical or otherwise, undertaken for the welfare ofthe [text-layer?] [of the] community with no regard for reward"; "the more menial the task, the more it was prized" (p. 246, folio 273). Langar set against "the distinctions of caste, religion and social standing" (pp. 228–229, folios 252–253). Karori's community kitchen, "two simple meals a day", dispensary and school (pp. 188–189, folios 207–208). Shivnabh's dharamshala for satsang (p. 164, folio 180). Village dharamshalas as free hospitality (p. 58, folio 64; p. 113, folio 125).
- **Village economy at Saidpur (retelling).** Houses ringed "in concentric circles" round the haveli by caste, untouchables on "the very outskirts" (p. 57, folio 63); Malik Bhago's "lagaan (land cax [tax])" with his own commission, his munshi "a proxy moneylender" at "usurious rates" (p. 65, folio 71; p. 66, folio not stated); a haveli temple with priests praying "in a relay", and a Navratras feast with Brahmin cooks only and lower castes barred (pp. 66–67, folios not stated / 73).
- **Rituals described.** Janeu ceremony (p. 19, folio 21); Naga practices (pp. 29–31, folios 33–34); Haridwar water offerings to "Suryalok" (p. 78, folio 86); Kumbh Mela akharas as "a tented city", the Mauni Amavasya bath at "Brahma muhurta" (p. 101, folio 112; p. 104, folio 115); the Baghdad tomb with "Little oil lamps and a fresh chaddar covering the grave" after "the Thursday evening prayers" (p. 206, folio 227); the yogi's prescription of the "Gayacri Manua" [text-layer?] [Gayatri Mantra] "ten thousand times" and "agodaan" [text-layer?] [a godaan] (p. 242, folio 267).
- **Music and worship.** "Singing is an essential part of our worship" versus "Music does not form a part of our worship" (p. 207, folio 228); "The azaan is considered by many, including non-Muslims, to be the most beautiful music in the world" (p. 209, folio 230); "The Prophet Muhammad had banned the singing of songs, which could cause evil passions" (p. 210, folio 231). ## Doubts and review points

**Numerals and dates** (all carried as printed)

- Udasi arithmetic: the "more modern" four udasis are printed 1499–1521 yet said to span "twenty-three years"; the Puratan's "five journeys" sit beside "all the four corners of the country" (pp. 54–55). Recorded as printed.
- Baba Budha "125 (6 October 1506 to 8 September 1631)" (p. 240) as printed. Guruship spans (Angad 1539–1552, Amar Das 1552–1574, Ram Das 1574–1581, Arjan 1581–1606, Hargobind 1606–1644; p. 240) are Dhillon's.

**Mapping doubts**
- **Guru Nanak (18 rows)**: person-level throughout, not site-specific; the chunk 009 material rests mainly on the publisher's blurb.
- **Nankana Sahib (7 rows)**: place-level. Pass-1 chunks 001, 002 and 004 declined to map Talwandi (the original brief said the book never names Nankana); the correction (p. 175, "Nanakana Sahib (earlier called Talwandi)") is applied here to all Talwandi material from every chunk. The spelling "Nanakana" is the book's. The group includes `gurdwara-panjvi-chati-patshahi` (principal figures Arjan Dev and Hargobind), which is in Nankana Sahib.
- **Saidpur**: unmapped. The book says only that it is "in Pakistan" (p. 54, folio 60). Another book in this corpus (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115) identifies Saidpur as Eminabad; this book does not. So not mapped to the Islamabad Saidpur-village rows nor the Eminabad rows; Lalo and Mardana take their rows by the person rule only.
- **Judgement mappings**: `gurdwara-sacha-sauda` rests on the Chuharkhana story (the book's spelling "Chuharkhana" vs. the index's "Chuharkana"; no gurdwara named); `gurdwara-babay-de-ber` on Sialkot plus "an old beri (jujube) tree"; `shaktipeeth-shri-hinglaj-mata-mandir` on the name plus "now in Balochistan" (no "hinglaj" key in toponym_map; no temple named); Tulamba→Multan rests on "(modern Makhdumpur, in Multan, Pakistan)" and may be judged too indirect.
- **Kartarpur**: mapped because the book places it "on the right bank of river Ravi" in "district Narowal ofWest Punjab (now in Pakistan)" (pp. 175–176) and on the Ravi at p. 257. "West Punjab" is the book's wording. Pass-1 chunks 005 and 007 declined to place their Kartarpur mentions (pp. 134, 224–227); they are carried in the Kartarpur row here on the book's own identification — confirm.
- **Lahore at pp. 175–176** ("80 km from Lahore", "the Lahore-Chak Amru line") was judged by the Pass-1 worker not to be material about Lahore; not added to the Lahore group. The Lahore group itself is place-level and thin outside the Duni Chand story.
- **Bebe Nanaki**: the book places her at Sultanpur throughout; the row is in Lahore. Person-level only.
- **Baba Farid**: the book calls Pak Pattan "a town near Sirhind" (p. 171, folio 187); the row locates the shrine at Pakpattan, Punjab. Needs a human check. "he personally included 134 hymns" — "he" read as Nanak from context. Faridkot "in Punjab" is not placed on either side of the border; "Tilla Baba Farid" is not `tilla-jogian`.
- **Shiva (8), Durga (1), Gorakhnath (2), Arjan Dev (3), Hargobind (6), Ram Das (1), Gobind Singh (1)**: person-level, bare explanatory or bare-name mentions; none tied to an archive site. - **Place-level bare itinerary names**: Multan (8; the book names only the Suraj temple, not Prahladpuri), Sialkot (5), Sukkur (5), Peshawar (10), Hasan Abdal (`gurdwara-panja-sahib`), Katasraj (`katas-raj-temples`, name match).
