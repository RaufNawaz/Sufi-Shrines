## What this book is

An ethnomusicological monograph on Qawwali — "conventionally understood as the music of Sufism in
South Asia" (p. 290, folio not stated) — built on fieldwork at Sufi assemblies, above all at the
shrine of Nizamuddin Auliya in Delhi, and on the repertoire and working practice of the hereditary
Qawwal Bachche who serve it. The jacket classifies it "MUSIC/ANTHROPOLOGY" and the imprint is "THE
UNIVERSITY OF CHICAGO PRESS" (p. 290, folio not stated); the author is given there as "REGULA
BURCKHARDT QURESHI is professor of music at the University of Alberta" (p. 290, folio not stated).
Its method is to read the sound against the occasion: "What this key unlocks is meaning, non-musical
meaning" (p. 228, folio 208), and the book's central claim is that "Qawwali music articulates the
ideology of Sufism and conveys the meaning of both its structure and its dynamic" (p. 248, folio 228).

**For this archive it is a thin book and an unusually valuable one, in different registers.** It is
thin hagiographically: it names almost no Pakistani shrine and almost no Pakistani saint, because
its field is Delhi, Ajmer, Kaliar Sharif, Gulbarga, Kakori, Lucknow and Agra — all Indian, none of
them archive rows. Only **two** of the 169 archive rows are touched at all, and both are touched by
name rather than by site: `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` (as "Baba Farid", Nizamuddin Auliya's
spiritual guide, and once in a silsila table with `d. 1266, Pakpattan`) and `data-darbar` (as the
cited author "Hujwiri"). It is valuable institutionally and economically: it is the corpus's
fullest account of how a shrine assembly is financed — the offering transaction, the sheikh's
position as redistributive agent, hereditary performing rights (*maurusi*, *sar chauki*), the
absence of property endowment at a major shrine, the annual assembly load, the exclusion of women
from the assembly, and the sequence of performing groups attached to a shrine. That material is
cross-cutting and is recorded under **Cross-cutting material**, not under a shrine id.

### data-darbar — Data Darbar, Lahore (Ali ibn Usman al-Hujwiri)

## Cross-cutting material

*This is where the book's weight for the archive lies. None of it is attached to a Pakistani site
by the book, and almost all of it is observed at Nizamuddin Auliya in Delhi; it is recorded here
because it is a model of shrine practice and shrine economics that a Pakistani entry can cite as
comparative, not as description.*

### What the book says about Pakistan specifically

- **The only sustained passage.** "In Pakistan… a newly evolving elite structure with a strong
  immigrant component (including sheikhs and Qawwals who had themselves migrated from Sufi centres in
  India), has provided support for Sufism and its practice in the major cities. In addition,
  Pakistan's linguistic regions have their own traditions of Sufism and sama'; these are rooted in
  local shrines and often linked to the local feudal establishment. Pakistan's search for an Islamic
  cultural identity has provided all kinds of sama music, but particularly Qawwali, with new
  opportunities for institutional patronage of a secular nature" (pp. 113–114, folios 93–94). **No
  Pakistani shrine, region or city is named in it — all (not stated).**
- **Language.** "Because Urdu predominates in Pakistan as the national language, Qawwali there abounds
  in both types of Urdu poetry", and "there is, furthermore, a recent trend in Pakistan toward
  incorporating more Arabic phraseology into Qawwali poetry" (p. 105, folio 85). Urdu ranks lowest of
  the three Qawwali languages in spiritual prestige — "Because Urdu Poetry is no more than two
  centuries old and has little direct association with Sufi saints, it has less spiritual prestige
  than either Farsi or Hindi" (p. 105, folio 85) — and "Urdu, at least theoretically, has no place in
  a satna' `[text-layer?: samāʿ]` gathering of high spiritual status" (p. 208, folio 188). The stated
  consequence is enforced: "in the early seventies a performing group was evicted from the principal
  'urs assembly at the shrine of Ajmer for singing in Urdu" (p. 208, folio 188).
- **Ritual difference between the two countries, stated flatly.** "At Nizamuddin Auliya no Qawwali
  event can start any other way, while elsewhere in India and Pakistan the Qaul serves as a
  conclusion" (p. 41, folio 21).
- **The 1995 preface on the state.** "Qawwali has also gained in public stature, particularly in
  Pakistan, as an expression of national culture, through a blending of governmental and religious
  control over performances in shrines and concert halls and on television. In India, on the other
  hand, Qawwali is being coopted into the cosmopolitan, multireligious or 'secular' realm of public
  culture" (p. 14, folio xii). And: "Their music, however, continues to be heard at Delhi's
  Nizamuddin and at shrines all over South Asia, if under diminishing patronage" (p. 14, folio xii);
  "The real question is whether Qawwali's hereditary performers will have the economic support to
  teach their children" (p. 14, folio xii).

### The dargah as an economic institution

- **Endowment begins with imperial patronage**: "the imperial rulers were generous in granting
  property endowments to such Sufi establishments, particularly the shrines built around the graves of
  their founders" (p. 111, folio 91). The framework: Sufism "took root during the thirteenth century,
  within the socio-cultural framework instituted by Muslim rule, and under its patronage and
  protection"; "Through a series of dynasties ruling from the eleventh to the eighteenth century
  Muslim rule imposed a centralized agrarian bureaucracy over an existing feudal economy" (p. 110,
  folio 90).
- **The decisive structural change is the shift from spiritual to hereditary succession**: there came
  "an expansion in the reckoning of Sufi lineages from the principle of purely spiritual descent to
  the inclusion of familial descent from a founder saint", giving "the physical descendants of saints
  the hereditary right to control and manage the endowments, both spiritual and material, of their
  ancestor". Hence "a dargah, or Sufi shrine, whether endowed with property or not, came to be
  controlled by a legitimate representative of the saint buried there, whose right is based on
  familial rather than spiritual descent" (p. 112, folio 92).
- **Succession is contested by design**: "Theoretically, there is one single successor to the
  leadership of the saint and his tomb, the sajjdddnashin or gaddinashin `[text-layer?: sajjādanashīn
  / gaddīnashīn]`…, but at most large shrines at least two if not more descendants often claim this
  right"; at major Chishti shrines "entire communities of such representatives have a hereditary share
  in managing the shrine's spiritual and material benefits" (p. 112, folio 92).
- **What the descendants hold**: they "receive the income or revenue from shrine endowments"; they
  distribute amulets (`tdwiz` `[text-layer?: taʿwīz]`); each acts as "the agent {wakil} `[text-layer?:
  wakīl]` of the Sufi devotees"; and "Most important of all, he is entitled to accept on behalf of the
  saint the propitiatory offerings of the devotees" (p. 112, folio 92). The Glossary supplies the
  mechanism: "ban 'turn' to collect shrine offerings which are shared by the descendants of a saint"
  and "bdriddr 'holder of a turn' to collect shrine offerings" (p. 262, folio 242).
- **The author's summary, twice over**: "At the level of a self-contained institution the Sufi dargah
  is a quasi-feudal establishment in which a hereditary appropriating class of saindy `[text-layer?:
  saintly]` representatives controls the resources, whether they be property revenue or offerings
  received from devotees" (p. 113, folio 93); "Viewed in relation to its larger socio-economic base,
  however, the dargah is ultimately an institudon `[text-layer?: institution]` of patronage" (p. 113,
  folio 93). The exchange: "In return for this worldly support the Sufi dargahs have historically
  provided religious legitimization for the Muslim ruling elites" (p. 113, folio 93).
- **Twentieth-century decline of the support base.** Under British rule "Sufi establishments continued
  to operate with relative stability (apart from some interruptions such as the 1856 Mutiny)"; after
  independence "their traditional socio-economic support base has weakened considerably, due to the
  lack of Muslim political dominance, and, more generally, the recent decimation of the feudal-based
  Muslim establishment which was caused by land reforms on one side and migration to Pakistan on the
  other" (p. 113, folio 93). The compensation: "individual patronage from among the larger public has
  assumed more importance for Sufi institutions, including patronage from the non-Muslim elite"
  (p. 113, folio 93). The forecast: "the generational and economic ties that united Sufis, Qawwals
  and devotees around a major shrine are weakening" (p. 114, folio 94).
- **Service professionals are hereditary and dependent**: "professionals providing menial services
  such as sweeping the shrine or cooking food which devotees wish to give away to the needy in the
  name of the saint", and "the Qawwali performers who are indispensable to the performance of sama'
  assemblies as well as shrine rituals"; "Notwithstanding their superior professional skill and
  service, the Qawwals, along with all other service professionals, belong to the servants of the
  dargah and stand in a servile or 'client' relationship of dependence to the shrine descendants"
  (p. 112, folio 92).
- **Shrines are a network, not isolated sites.** "Both spiritual ties and actual encounters through
  pilgrimage and travel link shrines and urban centres across the enure `[text-layer?: entire]`
  subcontinent into one extended Sufi community" (p. 33, folio 13). "A major Sufi shrine… is the locus
  of Qawwali performances of every kind, of a stable group of performers with a standard core
  repertoire and a predictable audience complex - in short, a local universe of Qawwali" (p. 33, folio
  13). Repertoire follows the network: "networks of related shrines share repertoires oriented to
  particular saintly lineages… Within shrine networks, each shrine of some import has its own special
  songs" (p. 34, folio 14), and "Not movable are musical settings of ritual songs or of songs with a
  special shrine association" (p. 39, folio 19).
- **Orders in India.** Four entered "during the thirteenth and up to the sixteenth century": "the
  Chishtiya and the Suhrawardiya, followed by the Naqshbandiya and the Qadriya" (p. 111, folio 91);
  the Chishtiya "is the earliest and the one spread most widely throughout India; in fact, it has in
  the main remained an Indian and Pakistani order with little presence outside the subcontinent"
  (p. 111, folio 91). **Samaʿ is not universal among them:** "Orders with a more orthodox orientation,
  like the Naqshbandiya or the Suhrawardiya prohibit its use altogether or compromise by permitting
  mystical songs unaccompanied by instruments" (p. 102, folio 82); at the shrines of Nizamuddin Auliya
  and Gesudaraz "a special samd assembly called band samd ('closed' samd) continues to be held, where
  the usual instruments are silent (Hussaini 1970)" (p. 102, folio 82).
- **The turn to the tomb.** "Later Sufism has also come to emphasize nearness to these saints and
  their power in spatial terms, at the abode of their final union with God, that is, their tomb.
  Accordingly, Sufism in India (Eaton 1978) as elsewhere… has in recent centuries shifted its focus of
  orientation to saintly shrines, their founder-saints and spiritual power" (p. 101, folio 81); "the
  devotional veneration of saints as spiritual intermediaries and mediators of divine benefice
  (barkat) becomes an integral and central part of Sufi practice" (p. 101, folio 81).

### A shrine with no endowment: the Nizamuddin case, worked in full

- **The whole economy is offerings.** "A special aspect of the Nizamuddin shrine is its total lack of
  any property endowments. The only income, therefore, even for thepirzdde `[text-layer?: the
  pīrzāde]`, comes from offerings and gifts" (p. 116, folio 96). **This is the corpus's clearest
  worked example of a major shrine running on donation alone**, and its substitute for endowment is
  political: "there is a great political potential to be utilized in this capital city of a nation
  state, professing a secularism which is, in part, expressed through the official patronage of
  minority religions. Officials and functionaries therefore constitute important sources of direct
  and indirect support, as do foreign Muslim visitors" (p. 116, folio 96).
- **Custody is collective and worked by rota.** The shrine "is managed and controlled collectively by
  a group of descendants or pirzdde (sing, pirzddd…)…; they live in the walled 'village' (bastl)
  `[text-layer?: bastī]` outside the shrine" (p. 114, folio 94). "Tracing their descent from the
  saint's nephew or sister (he himself never married), the pirzade are organized into three operative
  family groups which share the income of shrine offerings… according to a complicated system of
  week-long 'turns' (bdri; the holder of a turn is called bdrlddr…)" `[text-layer?: bārī / bārīdār]`
  (p. 115, folio 95). Plate 4: "Pirzdde, descendants of the saint Nizamuddin Auliya, take their turn
  (ban) `[text-layer?: bārī]` watching the tomb and accepting offerings from devotees" (p. 114, folio
  94). A second income stream is regional clientage: "Individually, they act as agents or advocates
  {waklt} `[text-layer?: wakīl]` to families of devotees on the basis of their regional affiliation,
  and receive their personal offerings at the shrine" (p. 115, folio 95).
- **Three rival claimants.** "Most prominent among these are three leading figures, one of each family
  group, each of whom claims to be the sole legitimate successor of the saint (sajjdddnashin)
  `[text-layer?: sajjādanashīn]`. Each has a personal following of disciples or devotees and uses one
  or more shrine cells to minister to them" (p. 115, folio 95). They are named in the book: **Pīr
  Zamin Nizami Syed Bukhari**, **Khwaja Hasan Sani Nizami** and **Qazi Safdar Ali** (pp. 115, 125,
  126; folios 95, 105, 106).
- **Purpose-built Qawwali halls, privately owned.** "The leaders of two families have been the
  principal patrons of Qawwali occasions; they each own a hall situated just outside the shrine and
  built expressly for large public Qawwali performances during 'urs celebrations. At the time of
  writing it was the well-known Pir Zamin Nizami who controlled the Urs Mahal {'urs palace), while
  Khwaja Hasan Sani Nizami was managing Qawwali Hall alongside the tomb of his own father, Khwaja
  Hasan Nizami" (p. 115, folio 95). **No building date and no builder is given for any structure in
  the book: (not stated).**
- **The dependency chain.** Service professionals "stand in a dependent client relationship to the
  pirzade, insofar as the pirzade control the shrine and all its activities and manage the devotees,
  who are the only source of income for all" (p. 115, folio 95); and "visiting devotees can also
  benefit Qawwali performers who may be singing in front of the tomb at the right time to receive
  their offerings, but it is the pirzdde `[text-layer?: pīrzāde]` who have direct access to their
  donations" (p. 116, folio 96).
- **The three leaders' assemblies are socially differentiated.** Khwaja Hasan Sani's Qawwali Hall
  gatherings "are always dominated by a spiritual elite, regardless of the number of listeners";
  "The third leader, Qazi Safdar Ali, holds his assembly in the open; it attracts a more
  entertainment-oriented crowd such as gathers at the fair surrounding the shrine during the 'urs,
  for Qazi Safdar Ali is a highly popular spiritual guide" (p. 125, folio 105).
- **No one controls the qul, because the right is shared.** "the qui attracts large numbers of
  devotees - including women, who are permitted in the shrine compound. There is a set time for the
  qui, in the late morning, and its duration is limited, although no one person is in control of this
  event, due to the peculiar situation of collective representational rights shared between pirzade"
  (p. 125, folio 105).

### ʿUrs, calendar and the types of assembly

- **The ʿurs defined, in the book's own two formulations**: "the 'urs of such a saint, the
  commemoration of his own final union with God on the anniversary of his death" (p. 21, folio 1); and
  in the Glossary, "'urs  'wedding', the celebration of a saint's final union with God on his death
  anniversary; a saint's death day" (p. 266, folio 246). "There is no Qawwali experience more vivid
  and profound than the 'urs of such a saint" (p. 21, folio 1).
- **The calendar of occasions**: the ʿurs is "the prime raison d'etre for holding Qawwali assemblies";
  also "the saint's monthly or weekly death days"; and "Qawwali is performed weekly on Thursday, the
  day for the remembrance of the dead in Islam, or on Friday, the day of congregational prayer"
  (p. 123, folio 103). Ordinary days have a name: "sddhe din  'plain, ordinary days' at a shrine, that
  is, without ritual requirement for Qawwali singing" (p. 265, folio 245).
- **A rare workload figure**: "An important shrine may require its performers to sing at about 50
  assemblies in a year, around half of them major ones" (p. 123, folio 103). Scale varies "from a
  single Qawwali occasion led by the representative of a small shrine… to a week-long succession of
  many assemblies held by different spiritual personages for different audiences among large numbers
  of pilgrims at a major shrine" (p. 123, folio 103).
- **Dates at Nizamuddin Auliya** (the only anniversary dates the book prints): "the two anniversaries
  of Nizamuddin Auliya and Amir Khusrau… fall exacdy `[text-layer?: exactly]` six Islamic months apart
  on the seventeenth of the Islamic months of Rabi-us-Sani and Rajab respectively. Each is celebrated
  for over five days" (p. 115, folio 95). **No Common-Era equivalent and no era marker is given: (not
  stated).** Baba Farid's anniversary, Hazrat Ali's, Imam Husain's martyrdom, and "Basant, actually the
  Hindu spring festival, when an event from the saint's life is celebrated with special songs by
  Khusrau" complete the shrine's annual round (p. 116, folio 96).
- **The melā, the crowd and the shrine poor**: "The 'urs celebrations, along with the adjacent fair
  (meld) `[text-layer?: melā]`, attract hundreds from Delhi and outside, in addition to the steady
  number of poor people who live in and around the shrine, benefiting from food, offerings and
  charity" (p. 116, folio 96). The author cites "Census of India [1961], 1966" for visitor numbers but
  **prints no figure: (not stated)**. The poor are named again and dismissed: "there is always the
  destitute component of any common audience, especially in assemblies held at shrines where beggars
  and mendicants abound. These, however, are hardly given any consideration, either by leaders or by
  performers" (p. 210, folio 190).
- **Food offerings**: the ʿurs involves "ritual offerings to the saint in the form of special food
  blessed and dedicated and distributed publicly (tabarruk)" (p. 116, folio 96). **The book never uses
  the word langar and describes no free kitchen: (not stated).**
- **The four categories of occasion at a major shrine** (pp. 124–125, folios 104–105): (1) the intimate
  `mahfil-e-khds` `[text-layer?: mahfil-e-khāṣ]`, "really at the heart of Sufi Qawwali at Nizamuddin
  Auliya", "relatively short and held during daytime", where "Only choice Qawwals are admitted"; (2)
  "The major, celebrational assembly held in a large public place", "the mainstay of every 'urs
  celebration", "held at night and last for hours, their sound spread wide by loudspeakers"; (3) "the
  Major Shrine Ritual, a large assembly held in the shrine compound facing the sanctuary… the official
  death ritual of the 'urs called qui or khatam"; (4) "the minor ritual and non-ritual Qawwali
  occasion". A fifth kind is not shrine-based: "the gathering of disciples around their sheikh or plr
  `[text-layer?: pīr]`" (pp. 124–125, folios 104–105).
- **Order of service** (Table 23, p. 136, folio 116): "I Koranic recitation (duration: 5-15 minutes) —
  Leader; a) prayer for the dead (fdtehd `[text-layer?: fātehā]`); b) other passages (qira'at); c)
  Sufi genealogy (shijrd `[text-layer?: shijra]`) — leader or expert; d) intercessary prayer (du'd).
  II Qawwali singing (duration: 'A-5 hours `[text-layer?: ½–5 hours]`) — Performers. III Koranic
  recitation". "The religious cast of the Sufi assembly is formally expressed in the fact that it
  'begins and ends with the Koran'" (p. 135, folio 115). Plate 12: "The saint's spiritual genealogy
  (shijra) is recited during the Qui `[text-layer?: Qul]`, major anniversary ritual of the 'urs of
  Nizamuddin Auliya held in front of his tomb. Leading sheikhs from this and other shrines are
  present" (p. 137, folio 117).
- **Obligatory repertoire is lineage-specific, and this is where repertoire becomes shrine
  property**: "All Chishti and most Qadri descendants and followers include in their tradition at
  least one obligatory hymn, the Qaul…, which marks either the beginning (for example Nizamuddin
  Auliya, Chiragh-e-Delhi) or the end (for example Muinuddin Chishti, Syed Muhammad Gesudaraz) of
  their assemblies"; "To this, prominent saintly lineages add one or more obligatory hymns which refer
  to their founding saint" (pp. 136–137, folios 116–117).
- **Time and duration.** "Four to five hours are quite standard for the duration of a Qawwali occasion
  not limited by ritual requirements" (p. 133, folio 113); ritual-bound events "generally last from
  one to two hours" (p. 132, folio 112). Assemblies are pegged to the prayer times fajr, zohr, asr,
  maghrib and isha (Table 21, p. 130, folio 110).
- **Place** (Table 21b–c, p. 130, folio 110): "saint's tomb, shrine {dargah} / saint's dwelling place
  (hujrd, chilld) / spiritual guide's abode"; and by locale, "public place: shrine compound / hall /
  open space/field/tent / private place: cell (hujrd) / room". In prose: "the primary place for
  holding a Qawwali assembly is one linked to mystical precedent, most of all the locus of a saint's
  final union, that is the shrine" (p. 132, folio 112), "but also any other locality having an
  associational link with a saint" (p. 132, folio 112).

### The hereditary performers: brādarī, right, record book and earnings

- **The Qawwal Bachche.** "At Nizamuddin Auliya, the hereditary Qawwal community traces its descent to
  the original Qawwali singers who are said to have been trained by Amir Khusrau himself, and who are
  known as Qawwal Bachche, 'the (original) Qawwal Offspring'" (p. 119, folio 99). "Numbering 23 adult
  male members at the time of writing, the Qawwal Bachche come from several family groups (khdnddn)
  `[text-layer?: khāndān]`, identified by their four towns of origin near Delhi (Dasna, Sikandarabad,
  Khurja, Hapur), and collectively belong to the same brddri `[text-layer?: birādarī]`" (p. 119, folio
  99).
- **The record book of token payments — the sharpest institutional detail in the book.** "Their
  affiliation with Nizamuddin Auliya is enshrined in an historical entitlement (mohtdd) from the
  shrine's income, which they value most highly and record carefully, even though it amounts to no
  more than a few paisas" (p. 119, folio 99). Plate 7: "The Qawwal Bachche's record book of mohtdd,
  token payments confirming their hereditary right to perform at the Nizamuddin Auliya shrine"
  (p. 119, folio 99). **The term `mohtdd` is damaged and is not resolvable — see Doubts; the meaning
  is secure, the spelling is not.**
- **Hereditary right is conditional, not absolute**: "At the shrine of their affiliation they have a
  hereditary right to the performance opportunities for Qawwali generated and controlled by their
  patron, but they are in turn obligated to provide their performing services whenever needed,
  otherwise their patrons can admit outside performers into the hereditary performer group" (p. 117,
  folio 97). The Glossary names the right: "maurusi  'hereditary'; among Sufis the term refers mainly
  to rights and privileges associated with shrine affiliation" (p. 264, folio 244); and the office,
  "sar chauki  'head performing group' of Qawwals at a shrine" (p. 266, folio 246).
- **The sequence of performing groups at a shrine is inherited**: "While the leader has ultimate
  control over performance turns, there is, at most shrines at least, a customary sequence of those
  performing groups which have a special attachment to the shrine; and this sequence is maintained
  from generation to generation, always under the name of the original head of the group. Such turns
  always occur at the beginning of the occasion" (p. 211, folio 191). "Because of its location at the
  shrine, the Qawwal Bachche have a first right to perform at this assembly" (p. 195, folio 175).
  An outsider "obtain[s] their 'turn' at an assembly by first paying their respects to the leader, so
  as to get 'on his list'", with "the usual norm of two songs per turn" (p. 211, folio 191).
- **Two earnings regimes, and the share rules.** "there are two kinds of singing engagements,
  mushtarkd gdnd or panchayati gdnd ('mixed' or 'communal' singing) and bdrikdgdnd or parti kd gdnd
  ('singing by turn' or 'party singing')" `[text-layer?: mushtarka gānā / panchāyatī gānā; bārī kā
  gānā / pārtī kā gānā]` (p. 120, folio 100). Communal singing "covers all the singing which is done
  directly in front of the shrine sanctuary, that is, all ritual days, as well as Thursday and Friday
  singing" (p. 120, folio 100). **The rule, exactly:** "The earnings from all communal singing are
  distributed equally, but strictly to those present at the time of receipt, so that Meraj becomes
  eligible for a share from the moment he sits down to sing, even if just before that a big offering
  has come in" (p. 120, folio 100). At the big anniversaries the rule relaxes: "all those who are
  present on the first evening are entitled to a share"; "Old men with failing voices are subject to
  this right too, and at the time of writing even the deaf-mute son of a senior performer!" (p. 120,
  folio 100). In party singing "he controls the singing and also the earnings"; in community singing
  "the control over both singing and earnings is shared" (p. 210, folio 190). The party leader "takes
  a double share of the earnings, while giving equal shares to the others, in accordance with the
  brddri rules" (p. 120, folio 100) — **and the book flags this as new: "The double share for the
  leader is a relatively recent practice running counter to the traditional brddri" `[text-layer?:
  birādarī]` (p. 262, folio 242 and note).**
- **Mistrust of the collector is proverbial**: shares are calculated instantly "for self-protection,
  lest the money 'get lost in the pick-up man's fingers' {ungliyoh ke bhul bhulayye ban j die haih,
  literally: his fingers turn into a maze)" `[text-layer?: ungliyoṅ ke bhūl-bhulaiyye ban jāte haiṅ]`
  (p. 120, folio 100).
- **A performer's counter-strategy against his own brādarī**: a party leader "will plan to keep the
  communally sung ritual songs short, in order to save for himself and his group both performance time
  and his listeners' offering money, rather than to have more income distributed among the greater
  number of community members", and "may even go so far as to avoid taking a lead position there…
  instead of wasting it when the reward for it will be dissipated to his entire brddri - a strategy
  Meraj follows regularly at Nizamuddin Auliya" (p. 211, folio 191).
- **Women are excluded from the profession outright**: "Boys are instructed by their male elders, for
  women have no part in Qawwali singing at any stage" (p. 118, folio 98). The brādarī is "endogamous
  patrilineal… subject to a governing body of elders (panch)"; within it "women normally do not
  seclude themselves from male bradri members, though otherwise female seclusion is standard practice
  among Qawwals", with "a preference for marriage within the kin group" (p. 117, folio 97).
- **Housing and patronage**: "Meraj, along with a few of his close relatives, lives by the shrine where
  he and his wife and four young children share a small one-roomed house built on a plot owned by
  Khwaja Hasan Sani, his patron" (p. 119, folio 99); Plate 8, "Qawwal's house (extreme left) near
  Nizamuddin's tomb, surrounded by graves and hutments" (p. 121, folio 101). He must "cultivate his
  patron who will favour him over his colleagues and pass any private performing work to him (kdm
  dildnd) `[text-layer?: kām dilānā]`. As a special service, Meraj offers a weekly performance at the
  tomb of his patron's father" (p. 122, folio 102).
- **Status of the class**: "Qawwali music the province of very low-status hereditary performers"
  (p. 17, folio xv); "identified with a low social class without assets, 'a despised lot'" (p. 157,
  folio 137); claims by Qawwals to inherited spiritual status are "quite categorically denied by
  Sufis" (p. 157, folio 137); and "a performer, almost by definition, cannot also be a Sufi" (p. 148,
  folio 128).

### The offering: the transaction that pays for the music

**This is the book's central economic finding, and it is worked out step by step.**

- **What the offering is.** "the external format of encountering the saint or 'meeting the Sheikh'
  (sheikh se milna) is constant. In essence, it consists of making a formal offering {nazrdnd
  `[text-layer?: nazrāna]`}" (p. 143, folio 123). Its root is the court: hierarchical relationships
  "were governed by formalized codes of behaviour (adab), and elaborate court ritual. At the core of
  both lay the articulation of submission and allegiance to a superior by means of a gift or offering
  (nazr), which, once accepted, entailed an obligation to confer benefice on the donor" (p. 110, folio
  90). The author's own bridge: "Bribery, though universally condemned in recent times, can be seen as
  a natural concomitant of this pattern: it represents the material link of an offering between client
  and patron" (p. 111, folio 91).
- **The gesture.** "the devotee rises to approach the spiritual superior, bows down (with unbent legs)
  or kneels before him, and extends on his open palms - right hand over left - an offering of money,
  usually a single note. This note is lightly picked up or touched by the recipient as a gesture of
  acceptance and placed on the floor before him" (p. 143, folio 123). The roles it fixes: "the offerer
  puts himself in the position of supplicant, a 'taker', whereas the spiritual superior, while the
  recipient of a gift, is nevertheless himself the benefactor or 'giver'" (p. 145, folio 125).
- **The upward chain.** "unless he occupies the highest spiritual status in the assembly, he too is
  required to present the offering to a spiritual superior, most likely the leader; thus he
  articulates his own place in the spiritual hierarchy and thereby validates the structural universe
  of the royal court of saints" (p. 145, folio 125). Rarely the chain loops: a leader's own offering
  "to any other saint's representative… will then be obliged to present it back to him as the
  spiritually highest person present" (p. 145, folio 125).
- **The conversion.** "Once accepted by him, however, their purpose of articulating a spiritual
  relationship is completed and they now become money to be given away, in keeping with the Islamic
  tradition of turning religious tributes into charity. In this particular situation the money serves
  to remunerate the performers for their service" (p. 146, folio 126). The justification cited: "the
  recipient of a precious gift brought to him by the donor's servant must reward the servant for
  bringing it, even though this reward has no relationship to the gift itself (Idris Khan 1973:5)"
  (p. 146, folio 126).
- **The hand-back, and the gesture that keeps the ranks straight.** "The standard gesture of turning
  over the money to the performer is the reverse of the offering: the leader drops the money into the
  extended palm of the performer, thus always maintaining the formal distinction between the giver and
  the taker" (p. 147, folio 127); "The taker raises the money to his eyes… and returns, ideally by
  walking backwards, to place the money on the harmonium or next to the lead singer" (p. 147, folio
  127). **The whole point, stated:** "The leader, then, plays an essential part in managing the
  transformation of the offering from a token of spiritual deference into a payment for service"
  (p. 147, folio 127). Plate 19 photographs it: "A rupee note about to be transformed from offering to
  pay, as it passes from the devotee to the performer via the sheikh" (p. 158, folio 138). The circuit
  is diagrammed in "Table 27 Performer's Earning Process" (p. 158, folio 138).
- **Who controls it.** "the leader exercises de facto control over the offering transaction; after all,
  the performer gets his reward only by the leader's leave" (pp. 150–151, folios 130–131). Historical
  change: "In past times, assembly leaders exercised total control over the economic dimension by
  retaining the offering and rewarding the performers later at their discretion — an expression of a
  more solid feudal tie between them. But even today, the leader retains his economic position of
  power as a redistributive agent" (p. 150, folio 130). **No period, reign or date is given for "in
  past times": (not stated).**
- **The pick-up is itself an institution, and it varies by shrine.** "In the few shrines where the
  leader provides one of his men for the task the performer has nothing to do with the matter, but in
  most assemblies it is he who designates one of his singers to look after the transfer of offerings
  from the leader to the performer" (p. 223, folio 203). A rule of decorum: "the performer will see to
  it that money is never left lying in front of the leader" (p. 223, folio 203).
- **Two contradictions the book prints side by side and does not reconcile.** (1) Access is
  means-tested and simultaneously free: an offering "is available only to those who have money. While
  Sufis do not consider this to be an actual rule, it is nevertheless a general fact" (p. 145, folio
  125) — yet "the spiritual meaning of the offering makes it equally legitimate for him to offer much
  or nothing" (p. 150, folio 130). The book reconciles them only by register (means of linking up vs.
  legitimacy of the amount). (2) The performer is "accorded the least possible significance" and
  "controlled by the audience", yet holds "his position as an originator of strategy" (p. 156, folio
  136); the two statements are consecutive and the control is called "variable".
- **Social obligation to give, and the sanction for not giving.** "it is the accepted social norm that
  listeners of high worldly status should be seen offering generously and in accord with their status,
  while failing to offer is considered to result in a loss of face" (p. 149, folio 129); giving is
  "the social gesture par excellence for expressing high status and a position of patronage" (p. 149,
  folio 129). It has a cost: "For devotees with social status, limited financial ability can actually
  operate to limit their attendance at Qawwali assemblies" (p. 149, folio 129).
- **Offerings circulate as display among a saint's descendants — and the performers' verdict on it.**
  "by passing offerings, once received, to each other rather than `direcdy` `[text-layer?: directly]`
  to the leader, these descendants demonstrate mutual respect and validate each other's spiritual
  status before the larger audience" (p. 219, folio 199); but "often what looks like much offering
  money is just 'one rupee making the rounds' (`ek rupyd chdroh tarafghumtd hai`) … but when it
  reaches the performer 'it's still only one' (`to ek hi hai`)" (p. 219, folio 199).
- **The only two sums of money in the entire book**, both at one assembly: during the Rang, "An even
  greater number (20 persons) offers a total of 51 rupees, so that it all takes more time" (p. 172,
  folio 152); and during the Masnavi, "only one rupee is offered which the leader in turn offers
  formally to his elder brother, Pasha" (p. 174, folio 154). **No fee, rate, share, endowment income,
  total or per-occasion figure is given anywhere else: (not stated) throughout.**
- **What makes offerings come.** Offerings are "certain to be made… during the obligatory hymns,
  especially where they introduce the performance" (p. 145, folio 125); the performer holds a line in
  repetition "at least until all offerings have reached their destination" (p. 219, folio 199), and
  "once offerings are coming, no matter what level of arousal stimulates them, the performer will keep
  up multiple repetition of the text unit that generated them, hoping for more" (p. 239, folio 219).
  `nisbati` verses — "'connectional' or 'linking' verses" — "have a more or less guaranteed appeal
  which requires an offering response from devotees so as to activate the link element. Such verses
  are of course never omitted" (p. 225, folio 205). **A doctrinal limit:** a purely musical insertion
  "will not bring offerings directly - for it would not be in keeping with Sufi tenets to reward a
  tune without words" (p. 224, folio 204). **And a limit the leader enforces:** "the leader may show
  displeasure at too many repeated appeals to the saint's name, for he considers this too obvious a
  ploy for compelling the devotees to confirm their allegiance by an offering" (p. 221, folio 201).
- **The performer's position.** "a performer sings for money and will operate not only on the
  spiritual but also on the material plane of making a living" (p. 150, folio 130); "his remuneration
  does not come to him as a direct reward for a good performance. Rather, it is the indirect material
  result of what is essentially a non-material transaction… a transaction not involving the performer
  at all" (p. 158, folio 138). The windfall is attributed not to generosity but to blessing: "an
  economic windfall for the performers — or its absence — is seen in terms not of human generosity but
  of `karam` or divine blessing" (p. 150, folio 130). His own formula: "'I am a complete Qawwal …
  looking equally to spiritual benefice and to money.'" (p. 159, folio 139). The Sufi view of him,
  quoted: "'that's the Qawwal's nature, pulling money (`paise luraknd`) out of people'" (p. 150, folio
  130).

### Admission, exclusion and rank in the assembly

- **Who convenes it.** "the assembly must be in the charge of a spiritual authority, whether in the
  person of a spiritual guide (sheikh) or a saint personified by his representative" (p. 129, folio
  109); the office is the "'chief of the assembly' (mir-e-mahfif `[text-layer?: mīr-e-maḥfil]`)"
  — **printed `mir-e-mahfil` in Table 20 on the same page; both forms are recorded and neither
  chosen** (p. 129, folio 109). "the leader gives the gathering its character, hence assemblies are
  often identified by their leader" (p. 129, folio 109).
- **Sponsorship buys no authority.** "Sponsorship, in this case, implies material backing only - for
  instance, making a location available - while the authority over the assembly is placed entirely in
  the hands of the spiritual leader" (p. 130, folio 110). Precedence can be displaced by a visiting
  representative of a higher saint, who "will be recognized as the ceremonial head of the assembly"
  (p. 130, folio 110).
- **Women and boys are excluded — and the book records the counter-norm in the same paragraph.**
  "Women, as well as young boys, are specifically excluded because of the temptation which their
  presence constitutes. This rule is generally enforced; for women in particular, a separate enclosure
  may be provided, in keeping with Indo-Muslim social custom" (p. 131, folio 111). Against it: "there
  is an ideological commitment in Indian Sufism to accept all comers, which in effect means that no-one
  is to be prevented from attending a Qawwali assembly - exceptionally not even a woman (as is
  evidenced by this study)" (p. 131, folio 111). **Both are printed; both are kept.** Women are
  admitted to the shrine compound for the qul (p. 125, folio 105).
- **Ritual purity is required**: the assembly is open to those "who are in a spiritual frame of mind
  and ritually pure (bd-wuzu `[text-layer?: bā-wuzū]`…)" (pp. 130–131, folios 110–111).
- **Rank is seated, and the rank order is printed.** "The highest place is assigned to the saint
  presiding over the assembly, either represented by his tomb… or else by his gaddi (throne)…
  Directly opposite is the space set apart for the performers, who thus principally face and address
  the 'throne'" (p. 134, folio 114). "If the assembly is held at a saint's tomb, the row facing in the
  direction of the Holy Kaaba is reserved for the leading saintly representatives" (p. 134, folio 114).
  Audience categories run "titular or outstanding shrine representative", "shrine representative",
  "descendant", "guide", "senior devotee", "patron", "rich man", "devotee", "young man" (Table 31,
  p. 229, folio 209). **The audience is seated by rank with a 'lower' end** (p. 226, folio 206).
- **A status marker worth carrying into an entry:** "Conspicuous Sufi dress, on the other hand, is
  generally worn by representatives of minor shrines" (p. 134, folio 114) — high-ranking Sufis prefer
  plain colouring or "the traditional formal apparel of the Muslim secular elite, adorned by a scarf
  that marks their particular saint's identity" (p. 134, folio 114).
- **Restraint marks status; ecstasy is for those who have none.** "restraint and controlled behaviour
  are characteristic of high status, so that even among Sufis or spiritual personages the frequent and
  expansive expression of strong arousal, especially ecstasy, is associated with lower status… Leading
  Sufis, then, validate their status with self-control" (p. 149, folio 129); "The socially prominent
  devotee, therefore, tends to avoid reaching a state in which he may indulge in any eccentric
  behaviour… For the devotee who lacks social standing, on the other hand, reaching such a state gives
  him momentary prominence" (pp. 148–149, folios 128–129). In practice "it is elderly devotees of lower
  status who are most likely to be seen dancing ecstatically" (p. 149, folio 129), and a long ecstasy
  by a low-status listener is cut short because "he is keeping higher-status listeners standing"
  (p. 149, folio 129).
- **The leader validates ecstasy bodily**: "it is the leader who gives recognition to the ecstatic
  state by rising… whereupon everyone in the audience does likewise… when he perceives the state
  subsiding, he chooses the right moment to sit down" (p. 146, folio 126).
- **Censorship of repertoire is the leader's job**: "explicitly inappropriate is love poetry that lacks
  a link (nisbat) with mysticism, whether through its content, its author or its historical
  association"; "it is part of the general expectation that the leader will exercise a veto over the
  performer" (p. 138, folio 118). Musical limits: no "artful vocal display", no "current popular song
  style", no "theatrical performance style" (p. 138, folio 118).
- **The two Sufi names for the occasion, and the book's use of them**: "one is the mahfil-e-sama or
  'gathering for listening', the other the darbdr-e-auliyd `[text-layer?: darbār-e-auliyā]` or 'royal
  court of saints'" (p. 126, folio 106) — the second is the book's key to the whole hierarchy.

### The author's arguments

- **The book's central structural claim**: "the central institution of Sufism in India, the dargah,
  clearly reflects the larger socio-economic structure of traditional Indo-Muslim society"
  (pp. 112–113, folios 92–93). The underlying axis: "What links the entire Muslim social structure
  together and distinguishes it from Hindu caste society is that power, both political and economic,
  constitutes the primary criterion of status differentiation. Religion can at most play a legitimizing
  role" (p. 111, folio 91). And: "Indeed, it came to represent and legitimize that rule, and in time
  Sufi practices and institutions came close to mirroring the social structural pattern of that rule"
  (p. 111, folio 91).
- **Why the performer is nobody, and her correction of the received account**: "the performer of Sufi
  music occupies a totally insignificant position; this is so despite the obvious fact that he has a
  key function in the central ritual of Sufism. The ideological explanation of this apparent
  incongruity is that Sufism admits music into the samd assembly only as a medium for spiritual
  advancement… But this ideological distinction between listening to music and performing it needs
  also to be seen in its socio-economic manifestation" (p. 116, folio 96) — namely "the more
  fundamental opposition between two general classes of society: those who control resources and those
  who produce either resources or services" (p. 116, folio 96). Compactly: "a totally insignificant
  and powerless musician is put in total charge of a profoundly significant and highly potent musical
  communication" (p. 251, folio 231), and "Social dependence thus means musical dependence" (p. 250,
  folio 230).
- **Why the assembly is the right object of study**: "the Qawwali assembly provides a prototype context
  for a formal 'acting out' of the structural and process-related features of Indian Sufism, since it
  is the one formal setting in which the entire Sufi community is represented" (p. 113, folio 93).
- **The economic dimension is real but unspoken**: "The social and economic dimensions of the Qawwali
  ritual are of course not conceptualized formally, nor are they often made explicit even on
  inquiring, but Qawwali participants do acknowledge them, performers and devotees more than Sufi
  sheikhs" (p. 126, folio 106); and "participants' awareness of the purely socio-economic and political
  implications rarely becomes manifest" (p. 110, folio 90). Her method answers it: "The analyis
  `[text-layer?: analysis]` will deal first with the Qawwali occasion at the level of its manifest
  religious function… To complement this detailed outline, the entire structure will then be considered
  at the level of the latent social and economic function being served by it at the same time" (p. 125,
  folio 105).
- **Context, not music, supplies the motive force** — the closing claim of Part II: "the contextual
  dimension of the Qawwali assembly also contains the motivational dynamic for making a Qawwali event
  happen, including the music; in other words, this motivational dynamic is ideological, social or
  economic; it is not intrinsically musical" (p. 151, folio 131). The hinge that gets her there: the
  musical grammar "can list the choices by which Qawwali musical structure is rendered flexible and its
  units manipulable, but it cannot provide a programme for using those choices" (p. 93, folio 73).
- **Why samaʿ matters more in India, with the author's own caveat**: "there is, in Indo-Pakistani
  Sufism, yet another contributing factor to the importance accorded to the sama' concept: the
  ubiquitous presence of religious music in Hinduism… early Indian Sufis recognized that to spread
  Islamic mysticism in a Hindu environment justified giving special emphasis to sama' (Begg i960
  `[text-layer?: Begg 1960]`)"; she immediately qualifies: "While there may be some element of apology
  in this justification, the general Sufi interpretation… is to accord special significance to music as
  a means for giving a more universal reach to Islam" (p. 103, folio 83).
- **A stated limit on the book's own reach**: "the somewhat marginal place which Sufism and Qawwali
  occupy in today's Muslim society — both in India and Pakistan — can in no way account for the power
  the Qawwali assembly still has as a socio-religious metaphor" (p. 110, folio 90).
- **Methodological honesty worth recording**: "my position as a patron resulted at times in my 'client'
  not wishing to answer my question in the negative" (p. 32, folio 12); and "the anomaly of being a
  foreign female of obvious prosperity… made it quite acceptable to convert the requirement for service
  into generous financial reward" (p. 32, folio 12). Fieldwork: "My field study of Qawwali started in
  1968/69 as part of a survey of Islamic music in India and Pakistan… intensive field research during
  1975/76" (p. 17, folio xv), all interviews "(all in Urdu)"; the corpus is "433 performances of 261
  songs with 179 different tunes" and "83 performing groups at fifteen centres" (p. 34, folio 14).

### 3. `[text-layer?]` damage inventory, by class — **inventoried once here, and flagged inline on every word carried above**

### 5. The locator rule — **no index, endnote or cross-reference number in this book is a PDF page**

**This book's in-text cross-references, plate locators, index locators and note references are all
PRINTED FOLIOS.** Folio = PDF − 20 through the body, confirmed independently four separate times
(the chunk-008 check on "Chapter 4E, pp. 128 ff." landing exactly on section E is the cleanest).
**Nothing above has been cited as `(p. N)` from a locator.** Where a conversion was needed it is
labelled as a conversion: Plate 23's locator folio 146 = PDF p. 166; Table 18's folio 81 = PDF p. 101;

bibliography entries (e.g. "pp. 142-53", p. 267, folio 247) are pages of *other publications* and are

### 6. Contradictions the book prints and that are kept side by side above, not tidied

1. **The same ʿurs date is printed two ways on the same page**: "Urs Amir Khusrau, 23 Oct. 1975 (17th
   shawal)" **and** "'Urs Amir Khusrau, 23 Oct. 1975 (18th shawal)" (p. 13, folio xi). **Both readings
   are clean in the text layer — the digits are unambiguous — so this appears to be a PRINTED
   inconsistency, not damage.** A human must check it against the rendered image of PDF p. 13 before
   either Islamic date is used.
2. **The Chilla of Baba Farid has two incompatible locations** — see §1.
3. **The death ritual is spelled two ways**: "the appropriate ritual for the dead (qui, khalam)"
   (p. 123, folio 103) and "the official death ritual of the 'urs called qui or khatam", with "khatam"
   repeated (p. 125, folio 105). **`khalam` and `khatam` cannot both be right**; `khatam` is the form
   the book uses when it repeats the word, **so an entry should use `khatam` and should not quote
   `khalam` as a variant term.** `qui` is the measured `l`→`i` damage for **qul**.
4. **Qaul (the hymn) versus Qul (the ritual) is a real ambiguity the text layer creates and it is not
   resolved.** The damaged form "qui" is what the text layer supplies for the *ritual* at p. 132
   (folio 112) and p. 137 (folio 117), and it is one letter from "Qaul", the *hymn* at p. 136 (folio
   116). Above, the p. 132 and p. 137 instances are read as the ritual **Qul** and the p. 136 instance
   as the hymn **Qaul**, on the sense of the sentences. **A consolidator carrying either term into an
   entry must verify it against a rendered page image.** Table 23 compounds the two in the item
   "rang-qaul" (p. 136, folio 116).
5. **Access to the offering is both means-tested and free**, and **the performer is both powerless and
   an originator of strategy** — see the two contradictions quoted in full under Cross-cutting
   material (pp. 145, 150, 156; folios 125, 130, 136). The book states both halves of each in
   consecutive sentences.
6. **Women are both excluded from and admitted to the assembly**, stated in the same paragraph
   (p. 131, folio 111), and admitted to the shrine compound for the qul (p. 125, folio 105).
7. **Amir Khusrau and Nizamuddin Auliya are both given `d. 1325, Delhi` in Table 18** (p. 101, folio
   81). Recorded as the table prints it.
8. **Plate numbering: 27–32 are listed, 27–31 are credited.** The list of plates ends "27-32 Ecstatic
   arousal during the Hujra Mahfil 178-9" while the credit line reads "…and 27-31 (Sabina Qureshi)"
   (p. 9, folio vii). Possibly printed, possibly a lost digit; not resolvable from the text layer.
