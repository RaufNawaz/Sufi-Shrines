## What this book is

Haroon Khalid's *In Search of Shiva* is travel writing and ethnography of folk shrines in Pakistani Punjab: phallic and fertility shrines, sacred trees, animal shrines and malang lodges, often visited with the historian Iqbal Qaiser. The author argues that the practices he met, "Even though Islamic in its garb", have origins that "predate the birth of Islam", and that many go back to "the different spiritual traditions that developed around the cult of Lord Shiva" (p. 6, Introduction, folio not stated). He sets this continuity against orthodoxy, the two-nation theory and identity politics. Reliability: the text is a clean born-digital EPUB with no text damage. The notes keep four registers apart: the author's own observation (reportage), what a named informant told him, the book's historical prose, and the author's argument. Informant claims are attributed throughout.

### shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar — Shrine of Fariduddin Ganjshakar

The book's "Baba Farid", "his title Ganj Shakar (Treasure of Sugar)", "Baba Farid Shakarganj of Pakpattan", "Hazrat Baba Farid Shakarganj", "the shrine of Baba Farid", plus the toponyms "Pakpattan" and "Ajodhan" (Pakpattan's only archive row, so the place material sits here too). Five chunks. The ch. 7 visit is reportage; the ch. 2 Pakpattan lines and the ch. 10–11 lines about Chistian and Kothewal are place-level or informant material, so weigh them accordingly.

- **Saint**
  - "The city of Pakpattan was a small, insignificant one known as Ajodhan on the bank of river Sutlej when Baba Farid had decided to settle here in the thirteenth century". He was "at that point the head of Chisti order with its centre in Delhi"; believing the order "should distance itself from the imperial authority", "he had shifted the centre from Delhi to Ajodhan" (book's historical prose) (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - The period, side by side: settled at Ajodhan "in the thirteenth century" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated) vs "the famous Muslim mystic from the twelfth century" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
  - On his title: "several myths as to how the saint earned this title—one of them being that he was extremely soft-spoken" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - He "had lived a life of poverty and away from the politics of his time" (author's argument) (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - Birthplace, Tibba Haji Deen / "Kothewal" (informants). A shrine official said privately: "'Baba Farid did not hang inside the well, as it is believed ... This well belonged to his family. Baba Farid was born here and this well was part of his house'". Iqbal Qaiser asked "'Wasn't he born in Kothewal?'" and the official answered "'This is Kothewal. Kothewal was its old name'" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated). In "the house where Baba Farid was born", "In an open courtyard were seven graves"; an old caretaker said "the graves belonged to Baba Farid's father, grandfather, uncles, and siblings", and that a further "small grave" was one of his own ancestors, "who, like him, also spent her life looking after this sacred place" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated). The author wondered whether the caretaker's family "once served the family of the Muslim saint before they moved to Pakpattan" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
  - Family and Chistian. A guide cleaning a grave at Chistian said: "This is the grave of the nephew of Hazrat Tajuddin Chisti, the grandson of Baba Farid Shakarganj of Pakpattan. That is the shrine of Hazrat Tajuddin Chisti" (p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated). "According to Tajul Auliya, a book written by a resident of this city, Chistian was founded by the grandson of Hazrat Baba Farid Shakarganj": there was an old fort with a jungle outside it, "Within that jungle there was a spot where Baba Farid had spent some time in meditation. Later when the saint settled at Pakpattan, he asked his grandson, Tajuddin Chisti to populate the place of his meditation" (p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated). "The name of the city is derived from the syncretistic school of thought that Baba Farid and his progeny espoused to" (endnote 93, as Pass 1 recorded it) (p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).
- **Shrine / Darbar** (reportage, ch. 7 visit; visit date not stated)
  - The author, with Maryam and Anam, "had driven here from Lahore, a journey of about four hours, to do a piece on the shrine of Baba Farid" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - "The shrine of Baba Farid is located on the top of a mound that overlooks the entire city"; "The madrassah of Baba Farid was located on the mound where he is buried" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - "It seems as if the entire city of Pakpattan has developed around this shrine. All roads lead here. The name of every other shop or business entity either includes Baba Farid or his title Ganj Shakar" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - Layout: stairs lead up to "an open courtyard with a new mosque on one side and the shrine of Baba Farid in front", with "a huge line of men" waiting. "Women are not allowed inside the chamber where the grave is located"; instead there is "a verandah adjacent to the shrine, from where they can catch a glimpse of the grave". "This was a modest building" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - "Behind the shrine is a splendid structure rising several metres higher. This is where the progeny of Baba Farid is buried. The graves of his female family members are covered with purdah. Here too women are not allowed" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - "Facing the shrine is an ancient Karir tree". Nizamuddin Auliya, "a disciple of Baba Farid", wrote that "the day dela, an unripe fruit of the wild Karir tree, was cooked it was regarded as a feast by the students" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - "Behind the qawwals was the langar hall, offering free food to all" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - Security: "A couple of years prior to our visit there was a blast at the entrance of the shrine killing six people" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). Women were searched in "the small makeshift room covered by curtains" by "female police officials", and the author showed his press card to "the head of security", who then allowed cameras inside (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). Said of "all such shrines" in this passage: "Photography was strictly prohibited"; there were "elaborate security measures at the entrance"; "Earlier these shrines would remain open throughout the night providing shelter to all, but now they are shut at midnight on the order of the government" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - Custodianship: "The current guardians of the shrine are from the family of Baba Farid and they are not only one of the richest but also one of the most powerful families of the region". His progeny "received gifts and awards from different rulers" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
- **Practices & Events** (reportage unless marked)
  - "always a rush outside the shrine, where there are several shops selling religious paraphernalia"; devotees buy "chaddar, flowers and incense sticks to be presented to the caretakers of the shrine"; "It is considered inauspicious to visit the grave of a saint without a gift" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - "At the shrine of Baba Farid, the most important Chisti Sufi in Pakistan, there are qawwals performing at all times of the day". A group sat "under the shade of a building across from the Karir tree", "one man on the harmonium and one on tabla while the rest of them clapped"; "Aptly the musicians were singing verses of Baba Farid" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - "a young man, wearing an off-white shalwar kameez, started whirling on his knees in a state of trance"; another devotee "threw notes of ten rupees in the air", which an assistant collected; "Here women and men sat together"; after "about half-an-hour" the qawwals "were replaced by another group"; devotees came from "different socioeconomic backgrounds", and "several more devotees around me slip into a state of trance" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - "For years now Sikhs have petitioned the Pakistani government to grant them visas to attend the urs of Baba Farid but in vain" (book's historical prose) (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - "The well of Baba Farid" at Tibba Haji Deen: "Signs on the walls pointed towards the well"; pilgrims walked "barefoot on the dusty roads of the village, out of respect for the holy village"; "The well was covered by an iron cage, on which devotees had tied saffron-coloured threads"; a boy drew water "for the pilgrims to consume"; "There were coins and notes at the base of the well, thrown in by pilgrims to seek the blessings of the saint" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated). A shrine official said: "'Drinking water from this well will cure all your problems!'" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
  - Caretaker at the birth-house (informant): "'Our family has been serving this shrine for the past seven generations'"; "'I am soon going to put up Baba Farid's family tree here. I have given it for composing'". The author wondered whether "seven generations was rhetorical"; the caretaker "was not educated but he knew the saint's poetry and his family history ... learned it from his elders" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
- **Legends & Miracles**
  - The well, two versions side by side. "According to tradition, he hung himself inside a well not far from the shrine, while performing religious meditation for a number of days" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated). Against this, the shrine official: "'Baba Farid did not hang inside the well, as it is believed'" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
- **Significance**
  - "Perhaps the greatest contribution of Baba Farid is not in religion but in literature. He is believed to be the first poet of Punjabi" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - Guru Nanak: Baba Farid "became an inspiration for Guru Nanak when he visited the shrine during his travels. Here through the descendants of Farid he learned about the poetry of Baba Farid", and so "the poetry of Baba Farid found its way into the Guru Granth Sahib, making Baba Farid a saint in Sikh tradition as well" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). At Kothewal the author writes that Nanak "was fascinated by Baba Farid's poetry which was later also recorded in the Guru Granth Sahib. By visiting the city of Farid's birth, Nanak probably wanted to pay homage to the Muslim saint" (author's argument) (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated). "There is a picture of Baba Farid accompanying the picture of Guru Nanak at the shrine of Guru Nanak at Nankana Sahib" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated; see the Nankana Sahib group).
  - The author reads the modest tomb and the grander progeny building as showing "the transition that occurred after the death of Baba Farid" (author's argument) (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - Orthodox critics (as the book reports them) argue that Sufis "like Baba Farid" condoned qawwali "because they wanted to win over Hindus into Islam"; "However today there is no need for qawwali" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - The author wonders "why our heroes cannot be people like Baba Farid, Waris Shah and Bulleh Shah, who spread the message of peace and brought several religious communities together instead of shedding blood" (p. 14, ch. 8, folio not stated).
- **Pakpattan place-level and bare mentions** (ch. 2; weigh accordingly)
  - Bare mention: after finding no phalli at 50 Chak PS, the author says, "At least we will get to see Baba Farid's shrine at Pakpattan". Nothing more is said about the shrine in this chapter (p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated).
  - The Aban Shah shrine of 50 Chak PS (not an archive row), side by side. Iqbal Qaiser: "Let's make a trip to Pakpattan. There is an interesting shrine there" (p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated). The author calls it "another shrine of phallic offerings also referred to as Aban Shah near Pakpattan" (p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated). Hanifa (per her brother) places the saint's real grave "in a village called Lakhewali Haveli near Pakpattan next to the river [Sutlej]" (p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated).

### madho-lal-hussain — Shrine of Hazrat Madho Lal Hussain (Shah Hussain Darbar)

- **Saint**
  - "Around the same time that he [Damodar] lived, there was a wandering malamati Sufi in Lahore known by the name of Shah Hussain, a spectacular Punjabi poet" (book's historical prose) (p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).

- **Significance** (book's historical prose)
  - "He for the first time, transformed the story of Heer Ranjha from a secular epic to a spiritual legend. He compared the love of Heer for Ranjha to that of a believer for his God, a theme that was subsequently picked up by Bulleh Shah and Waris Shah" (p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).

### mazar-of-bulleh-shah — Mazar of Bulleh Shah

**Person-level only (person rule):** Bulleh Shah is a Sufi exemplar, poet, a figure in an informant's legend and a bare name in lists; no tomb is described. The book's "Bulleh Shah", four chunks.

- **Saint**
  - Described as "the Punjabi mystic and poet of the early eighteenth century, Bulleh Shah" (p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated); "one of the most celebrated Sufis from Punjab", who "worked as the servant of a dancing girl for twelve years". The author reads this as "an act clearly meant to criticize the divisions of caste in society": "Bulleh Shah was a Syed, and the dancing girls, like the eunuchs, occupy the lowest rungs of society" (p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated).
  - Iqbal Qaiser (informant): "Bulleh Shah was a Qadri. The Qadri sect has a history of peaceful coexistence with other religions but that changed when Mumtaz Qadri murdered Salman Taseer." (p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated).
- **Legends & Miracles** (informant: Iqbal Qaiser, pointing to a gurdwara in "the village of Daftu")
  - "A few kilometres from here is the village of Pandoke where Bulleh Shah's father worked as an Imam in the local mosque". After his father's death, "the chaudris of the village asked Bulleh Shah to take his place but he refused"; threatened with a beating, "he fled from the village and headed towards Kasur". He took refuge in this gurdwara, saying "I am in the house of God. Let's see who can take me from here." The Sikhs refused to throw him out, and "Eventually after protracted discussions, Bulleh Shah was allowed to proceed to Kasur". Iqbal Qaiser calls the Daftu gurdwara "of immense importance" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- **Significance**
  - The motif of the mystic as a despicable dog before God appears in his poetry: "'Dogs are awake at night, dogs are superior to you [believers].'" (p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated).
  - Bare mentions: named with Baba Farid and Waris Shah as heroes "who spread the message of peace and brought several religious communities together instead of shedding blood" (p. 14, ch. 8, folio not stated); Shah Hussain's spiritual reading of Heer was "a theme that was subsequently picked up by Bulleh Shah and Waris Shah" (p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).

### Arguments and interpretations

**Method and frame (author's own account)**
- The book is "a product of several journeys, spread over a few years", for articles, research, social visits and recreation; travels are "not narrated in a chronological order" but combined "to suit a particular theme"; research limited "around my hometown of Lahore", with "several areas and shrines that are unexplored" (p. 6, Introduction, folio not stated).
- Thesis: the practices "all represented an alternative version of practised Islam"; their common thread "was their connection to antiquity"; they "defied the dogma of the exclusivity of the two religions, Islam and Hinduism" and show "much borrowing and lending between different religious traditions"; "Even though Islamic in its garb, their origins predate the birth of Islam—some of these links go back to the ancient Indus Valley civilization"; "the religious tradition found its roots in the different spiritual traditions that developed around the cult of Lord Shiva" (p. 6, Introduction, folio not stated).
- Frame: the traditions are read "in the backdrop of the rising tide of religious puritanism and extremism in Pakistan; the creation of Pakistan, the two-nation theory and its economic development. All these factors affect how these shrines are perceived and survive" (p. 6, Introduction, folio not stated).
- Front matter: the author "has an academic background in Anthropology from the Lahore University of Management and Sciences (LUMS)", travel writer and freelance journalist "since 2008", author of *A White Trail*; "This is his second book" (p. 1, front matter, folio not stated). Publisher "Rupa Publications India Pvt. Ltd 2015", "First impression 2015", eISBN "978-81-291-3902-3" (p. 3, front matter, folio not stated). Dedication to his teachers (p. 4, front matter, folio not stated). Acknowledgments name Iqbal Qaiser "my guru and mentor", "Anam Zakaria" (the author's wife), "Maryam Altaf", "Bilal Ejaz and Rida Arif", agent "Kanishka Gupta", editor "Jyotsna Mehta" (p. 18, Acknowledgments, folio not stated).

**Continuity from antiquity (author's argument)**
- The phallic shrine is "a continuation of a tradition that evolved long before the birth of Islam"; Indus figurines and seals were votive fertility offerings ("consensus amongst archaeologists") (p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated).
- Offering toys and venerating trees continue Indus practice; "it is the same people with similar beliefs who live here today", the changes "only … on the surface" (paganism, Buddhism, Jainism, Hinduism, Islam); the banyan sacred in Hindu, Buddhist "and even Muslim traditions"; "waan trees … considered sacred in the Muslim saints' hagiography" (p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated).
- The author doubts "Aban Shah was his real name" and that any saint started the tradition "350 years ago. This is an ancient tradition"; "sometimes Muslim saints and such legendary stories were invented to justify devotion to a 'non-Muslim' tradition"; Aban Shah "a figure that was resurrected to fit within the framework of a newly created Islamic country" (p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated).
- Baba Lal Hassan "must have been an ancient shrine, dedicated to the acacia tree which later became associated with the saint"; "The story must have been created by his followers"; "I believe that like the shrines of Aban Shah, this too must have been an ancient shrine, dedicated to the acacia tree". Evidence offered: Indus seals depicting sacred trees, peepal and acacia "most frequently depicted", swastika associated with acacia; James's *Tree of Life*; Sleeman on Dashara tree worship; Khadiravani Tara, "the Tara of the acacia forest"; Mirza-Sahiban; pre-Islamic Arabian acacia worship and Palgrave's 1862-63 observation of Muslims dancing round an acacia for rain (p. 9, ch. 3, folio not stated). [Side by side: Iqbal Qaiser — "that tree wasn't an acacia tree ... It was shisham" (p. 9, ch. 3, folio not stated).]
- Djinns "are yakshas, ancient Hindu tree spirits"; banyans are "'bodh' in Punjabi, a name that they borrow from Buddhism" (p. 9, ch. 3, folio not stated).
- Apal Muri: "Like other shrines this one also would have converted after Partition. The grave could have been a samadhi or an idol"; "The tree became a Muslim banyan tree" (p. 9, ch. 3, folio not stated). Salman Rashid (informant): "There was no Islam at that time so how could there be a Muslim saint?" (p. 9, ch. 3, folio not stated).
- Malang and eunuch practice (androgyny, hashish, ash, matted hair, dogs) linked to "Hindu Shaivism", largely via "Jürgen Wasim" (Frembgen) (p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated); of barefoot ascetics near Shergarh: "Did these malangs know that in their asceticism they were Shiva, the ascetic?" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated). At Shergarh, Iqbal Qaiser on the saint's footprints: "If you go to Jain temples there are footmarks of their prominent priests which become objects of veneration. This is exactly like that"; the author: the malang "looks like a Digamabar Jain monk" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
- Circumambulation of Baba Jan's grave compared with the Buddhist circling of the Dharmarajika stupa (p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated).
- The Kallar Kahar peacock tradition "is likely to have drawn inspiration from ancient religions irrespective of what the plaque says" (Krishna, Sarasvati, Buddha "a golden peacock") (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Ghore Shah: written records "belong to the nineteenth century, written almost 300 years later by British colonialists"; the toy horses resemble those "unearthed from the Indus Valley Civilization"; the cult "originated in the depths of antiquity"; deendars ("Muslim converts who were originally Hindu untouchables") still make the offerings; "Mary Kenoyer" cited (as printed) as denying the Aryan invasion theory (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated); endnote 8: "Senior archaeologists like Mark Kenoyer have rubbished the Aryan invasion theory" (p. 19, Notes, note 8, folio not stated).
- Kawan Wali Sarkar's crow-feeding is "a tradition that he clearly picked up from Hindu mythology" (Hindu belief that "crows are a bridge between the living and the dead") (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Gur Baksh Masih's shrine "must have originally been the samadhi of Gur Baksh Singh, converted posthumously"; Maraka: "There was no need for a samadhi in post-Partition Pakistan, so two graves were constructed instead, I thought" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- Sahari Mal's Muslim devotees, "irrespective of the fact that the patron saint here was not a Muslim but a Hindu": "For a country formed from the Hindu and Muslim division, this is an extraordinary feat, throwing the two-nation theory topsy-turvy"; the grave conversion "an attempt to woo conventional devotees" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- Sacred cows at a Muslim shrine contradict the Partition-era line "They worship cows and we slaughter them" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- Naulakh Hazari's rock: "how is this sacred rock at a Muslim shrine any different?"; the lion-and-lamb image "clearly adopted from the Biblical references"; the lotus pillar shows "cultural symbols which have been sacred for thousands of years to the people of this region are still sacred" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- "Pakistani civilization, in one way or the other, is a continuation of that [Indus Valley] civilization" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- "True to its pagan roots, folk religion in Pakistan has specialized shrines for particular needs" — "Aban Shah for fertility, the shrine of crows for people with speech impediments and Heer Ranjha for love" (p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).

**Saints, outcasts and the malamati (author's argument)**
- Saints' love for outcasts (Baba Mast, Peer Jahania, Bulleh Shah, Musa Sahi Suhag) read as "rebellion against the normative society" and self-debasement (p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated).
- Peer Abbas's dog-company as Malamti self-humiliation; a lineage traced "From the cynics … to the holy fools of Christianity, and then to holy men like Peer Abbas" (p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated).
- Faheem's ancestor story is "a subtle critique of the hegemony that the Syed typically have over spiritual matters", elevating indigenous castes; "Mirs deriving their ancestry from Kashmir and Kharals from Punjab"; "The Mirs trace back to Hindu Brahmins and Kharal to Hindu Rajputs" (author) (p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated).
- The women's lion/snake stories are "a counter-narrative" to the elders' intervention; "Of course, the women of this village believed in this cult" (p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated).
- Sufis' knowledge of different cultures elevated them "in a caste-ridden society" (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- The author is surprised that "Naqshbandis are considered to be the most puritanical", yet a Naqshbandi led the malamati's funeral (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Cats in Islam: Abu-Hurayra, the Prophet's cat "Muizza", "the Siddi Haddi and the Mevlevis" (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- The mentally disturbed: citing "Jürgen Wasim", historically Muslim societies regarded "fools and the mentally disturbed as God's favorites"; "In South Asia, people treat the truly mentally disturbed with remarkable patience and indulgence" (endnotes 94, 95: Frembgen, Journey to God, 75 and 76); contrasted with the mob, concluding that "reinterpretation of religion has brought religious fundamentalism and extremism into the social fabric of Pakistan" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated; p. 19, Notes, notes 94–95, folio not stated). Endnote 7: "In the Islamic tradition people with psychological complexities are regarded to be closer to God compared to ordinary people because of their lack of interest in the material world" (p. 19, Notes, note 7, folio not stated). Anam on Tibba Haji Deen: "'this is not much different from how they treat patients at mental hospitals'"; on Raju's nephew: "'What he was suffering from was teenage angst'" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
- Tibba Haji Deen and Kothewal: the author is skeptical of the gadi nasheen ("an air of pompous authority") and of the "seven generations" claim; he praises the oral transmission at Farid's birth-house as "truly remarkable" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).

**Orthodoxy, the state and the erosion of shrine culture (author's argument unless attributed)**
- Hajra's concealment of killis reflects "concerns of modesty. Morality defined by a more urbane Islam had finally caught up with this particular practice"; television brought "uniform nationalism" and "a pure religion purged of its past corruptions" and "played a pivotal role in inviting disdain towards the shrine" (p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated).
- Police in sectarian conflict "succumbed to the pressure of the majority Muslim group"; parallel drawn with "the minaret of an Ahmadi place of worship" (p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated); endnote 16: "It would be illegal to call it a mosque according to the Constitution of Pakistan ... in Pakistan the law declares them [Ahmadis] to be non-Muslims" (p. 19, Notes, note 16, folio not stated).
- Tableeghi Jamaat set against folk Islam; its origin placed in reaction to the Arya Samaj Shuddhi movement; history recast after "jahalliya" (p. 10, ch. 4, folio not stated).
- Puritanical/Deobandi Islam eroding shrine practice (the dogs barred, the Lahore drug ban); Iqbal Qaiser counters that shrine culture "was not the antidote for Islamic militancy" and notes exploitation by "peers" (p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated). The Peer Abbas minaret: "It had to give up its unique religious practice to become the tallest minaret in the city, I thought to myself" (p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated).
- "Authenticity through foreignness as opposed to indigenousness remains a pathological problem in Pakistan"; admitting indigeneity means admitting "their ancestors were Hindus", "threatening the two-nation theory"; Vaisakhi, Basant, Mehndi "are celebrated with a sense of shame" (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- The peacock-shrine plaque "was tinged with state nationalism", reinterpreting the saint's life "to reinforce the separateness of Muslims from Hindus" (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- "Shrine culture has always been heterodox"; "under mounting pressure from the orthodoxy which receives state patronage, some shrines are willing to concede"; by barring devotional acts the Auqaf "conceded to the fact that orthodoxy is preferred over heterodoxy" (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated). "Shrines as opposed to mosques have historically gained popularity because of an acceptability they provide to people of all hues" (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Commercialization of religion ("mass production of books and CDs") produces "a uniform religion ... a major ingredient of fundamentalism" (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Folk shrines show "a sense of rootedness, which challenges the notion of nationalistic religion that wants us to believe that we are all migrants in our own land. The saint here becomes the saint of the rocks" (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Sacred animals and trees aid "the preservation of the ecosystem"; Karen Armstrong, The Case for God, on symbolic reading of myth; Chief Seattle's letter "in 1855" (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Junkies in graveyards as a legacy of "General Zia-ul-Haq" and the Afghan jihad; cities absorbed into "the global economy" "tend to shed their idiosyncrasies" (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated). The cat shrine's "tall minarets highlighted the transition that the shrine was going through, from being a syncretistic Sufi shrine to a monolithic mosque" (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Syncretism is not only rural: two urban "schools of thought ... borne out of, and became popular primarily because of their religious syncretism" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). The rise of Eid Milad-Un-Nabi is, "I believe, ... in reaction to the increase of religious puritanism" — people embrace "an interpretation that is tolerant and syncretistic" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
- Barelvi/Deoband (book's prose): "Barelvis, an offshoot of the Deoband School"; "After separating from the Deoband in the nineteenth century, and spearheaded by Ahmed Raza Khan, the Barelvis embraced folk religious practices"; "The majority of Muslims in India and Pakistan belong to this particular school"; Barelvis "have taken a clear stance against the Talibanization of society", Deobands "have failed to denounce Islamic religious terrorism in the same manner" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
- Two-nation theory: Pakistan "Carved out of India in 1947"; textbooks portray Hindus negatively; "all traces of the Hindu past are obliterated" (p. 14, ch. 8, folio not stated).
- Urban puritanism: "shrines which are located in developed areas ... are losing their idiosyncrasies much faster than those found in remote regions"; "Muslim fundamentalism (not defined as militancy but puritanism) is more popular in the urban centres" (p. 14, ch. 8, folio not stated).
- Islam "was understood in a language that was already available to the locals through existing religious practices"; the shrines "are extraordinary because they continue to survive in an environment constructed out of Muslim separatism"; "at the moment, the biggest threat to the shrines doesn't come from militancy but rather from education and development" (p. 14, ch. 8, folio not stated).
- Scholarship-panel anecdote: told of "the shrines of phallic offerings", a panelist replied "But that's not true Islam" (p. 14, ch. 8, folio not stated).
- State vs folk narrative: the Maraka shrine commemorates Abdali's victims, "casting him as a villain" — "A national hero was a folk villain"; "It highlighted how cut off the state was from the culture and history of its own people" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). This "brand of religion is now slowly fading away" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- The rising display of beard and hijab is "a new phenomenon that might have parallels in Islamic history but remains a product of historical contingencies—9/11 in particular"; the trend is towards "an Islamic tradition that is not a legacy of religious syncretism like Chistiya or Barelvi, but reactionary and exclusivist, originating out of the Deoband and Ahle-Hadith schools of thought" (p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).

### Placement and attribution doubts

- Baba Lal Hassan's shrine location is not stated in ch. 3; ch. 4's "shrines similar to the ones at Kamalia and Apal Muri" suggests Kamalia but the book does not say so (p. 9–10, chs. 3–4).
- "this small village on the banks of the river Chenab was founded by my great great grandfather" (p. 9, ch. 3): unclear whether Apal Muri or Jalap is meant.
- The Sahari Mal section starts abruptly ("When Haji Kamal lit up a cigarette"); its village is placed only by "Hardo Sahari, a few kilometres away" and Kasur "several kilometres from here" (p. 15, ch. 9).
- The Khadak mohalla's city is not stated (p. 13, ch. 7); the milk camp's city is not stated (p. 7, ch. 1).
- The "lock opens by itself" answer at Tibba Haji Deen: speaker "he" — the gadi nasheen or his brother; not resolvable. The later "guardian" is presumably the gadi nasheen; the book does not say (p. 17, ch. 11).
- Iqbal Qaiser's "After my book" at Kothewal does not name the book (p. 17, ch. 11).
- "the largest graveyard in Asia" is the Chistian guide's claim, unverified in the book (p. 16, ch. 10). The Tajuddin/Chistian lineage is the guide's and Tajul Auliya's claim, not the author's.
- Kawan Wali Sarkar's "1838" and "1929" are the author's statements, source not given (p. 12, ch. 6). Muhammad Aslam's knowledge is doubted by the author.
- "Shirazi signifying that he belonged to the city of Shiraz in Iran" is the author's gloss; Faheem says the father "migrated from Shiraz" (p. 8, ch. 2).
- The Peer Abbas donors inference (objectors to the dogs are major donors) is the author's own (p. 11, ch. 5).
- Visit dates are largely unstated: Pakpattan visit undated, so "A couple of years prior to our visit" (the entrance blast, six killed) cannot be dated (p. 13, ch. 7); "a June afternoon" (Sahari Mal), "a fine spring day" (Naulakh Hazari), "this July afternoon" (cat shrine), "Last year" (ninety lakh), "That fateful night of August" (Rimsha Masih) — no years.
