## Province-level material

- PROVINCE punjab (book's word: "the Punjab" / "the province" / "Punjab"; refers to: present day, c. the time of writing, and the attacks of 1997, 2005 and 2009 — as the book states):
  - Shanti Nagar's Christian landlords make it "a unique case study in the social fabric of the Punjab. Whereas in most of the villages across the province, Muslims are the dominant landowners, while Christians are confined to menial jobs associated with the untouchable caste" (p. 123, folio not stated); "In a regular village where Christians are on the lower rung ... they would be treated as untouchables" (p. 124, folio not stated).
  - The attacks at Sangla Hill (2005), Gojra and Bahmniwala, Kasur (2009) "all of which happen to be located in Punjab"; "The story of a few burnt pages from the Quran found in local mosques seems to happen often" (p. 125, folio not stated).
- PROVINCE punjab (book's word: "the Punjab"; refers to: "During the partition" — the book does not say which side of the later border):
  - According to Dr. Farooqi, "A lot of Hindus from the Punjab at that time who couldn't afford to migrate or belonged to the lowest rung of the caste hierarchy" passed themselves off as Christians by wearing crosses "or actually converted as a matter of convenience"; "Most of those people converted into Catholicism" (p. 132, folio not stated).

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- Sacred Heart Cathedral, Lahore (chapter continued from earlier chunk, pp. 121–126): Aslam Nayamat directs security guards to admit young Christian devotees carrying "cartons of juice and other eatables", sends them to distribute it in the grounds near "the Bishop's house" to avoid crowding the entry; the crowd scrambles "to get their share of this free offering in the name of God" (p. 121, folio not stated). The Bishop concludes the sermon: "We love Pakistan because it is our country" (pp. 125–126, folio not stated).
- Aslam Nayamat: formerly a businessman whose house was "in a Christian dominated village near Khanewal"; "In 1997 after the Shanti Nagar incident, I wrapped up my business and moved to Lahore" (p. 121, folio not stated); Ahmad Gill is his cousin (p. 122, folio not stated).
- Shanti Nagar (near Khanewal), Christian village: established "in 1916 by a Christian missionary by the name of William Youth Tucker, a representative of the Salvation Army", who bought land and "distributed it to the native Christian families" (p. 123, folio not stated); its canal water makes it "the most prosperous village in the surroundings" (p. 121, folio not stated). "The only tandoor in the village is run by a Muslim" (p. 124, folio not stated). It lies "more than three hundred kilometres from Lahore" (p. 125, folio not stated).
- Shanti Nagar attack, 1997: "On the night of 5th February 1997, a couple of days after the national elections", Muslims at an abandoned white mosque about a kilometre from the village found "charred pages from the Holy Book of Quran" with "the names of the seven culprits" written in a margin (p. 121, folio not stated); mosques in the neighbouring villages and Khanewal "started spewing hatred"; an FIR was registered against the seven, one of them Ahmad Gill (p. 122, folio not stated). Tibba, "an almost entirely Christian village", was abandoned for Shanti Nagar (p. 122, folio not stated); police from Khanewal persuaded the elders to hand over private arms (p. 123, folio not stated). The mob burned buildings at Tibba including the Salvation Army School and "a Church behind the school"; Saima Rabnawaz, 45, a teacher, puts it at "about fifty to sixty thousand people" (p. 124, folio not stated). At Shanti Nagar the mob burned a mango orchard and "half of the village" before "the army arrived from Khanewal" (pp. 124–125, folio not stated). Prime Minister "Mian Nawaz Sharif" and his brother "Mian Shahbaz Sharif, the Chief Minister of Punjab" visited; funds were allocated for reconstruction (p. 125, folio not stated). The book closes on "the events of 6th February 1997" (p. 125, folio not stated).
- According to Ahmad Gill, on "the 17th of January about fifteen days before the incident" police raided the house of "Baba Raji" alleging liquor-making and a Bible fell from a shelf; villagers then reported "a blasphemy case against a police constable"; the constable was suspended (pp. 123–124, folio not stated). Most residents believe the Quran burning "was orchestrated by the police" (p. 124, folio not stated).
- Sher Khursheed, also named in the FIR: his six-year-old son "now lives in the Lahore Mental Hospital"; for "three or four years" after the event the villagers served Muslims "in separate utensils" (p. 125, folio not stated).
- Cathedral Church of Resurrection, Lahore (chapter pp. 127–134): "Head Church of the Church of Pakistan, Lahore diocese, stands opposite the Lahore High Court"; iron gate and fence raised "much after the construction" for security (p. 127, folio not stated). The main building "was constructed in 1887, following the gothic architectural tradition", affiliated with the Anglican Church; "In 1970, the Church left that order and formed the order of the Church of Pakistan" (p. 130, folio not stated). Gatekeeper Yousuf Khan, 72 (p. 127, folio not stated); Dean "Shahid Miraj (Head of this particular Chruch)" (p. 130, folio not stated); "The current Bishop, Alexandar John Malik", per Dr. Kanwal Feroze, has been "confined to his house" since the two assassinations and organised a prayer for Salman Taseer at this church (p. 129, folio not stated).
- Dr. Ammar Farooqi: 73, poet, "PhD in Urdu literature", Protestant, lives alone in Allama Iqbal Town (p. 127, folio not stated); critic of the blasphemy laws (p. 128, folio not stated); publishes a monthly magazine, "Shama" (p. 130, folio not stated).
- Salman Taseer, "Governor of Punjab", assassinated "in January 2011" by his security guard Mumtaz Qadri; lawyers showered Qadri with rose petals, among his volunteer counsel "the former chief justice of the Lahore High Court, Khawaja Muhammad Sharif" (pp. 128–129, folio not stated). ## Practices, institutions, economy

- Food distribution at the Sacred Heart Cathedral on holy days is, according to Nayamat, "a new tradition that has started recently"; young Christians "look at how Muslims distribute free food at Islamic shrines to seek the blessings of the Saint" (p. 121, folio not stated).
- Easter at the Cathedral Church of Resurrection: people enter from "about four in the morning" (p. 127, folio not stated); a candle procession of "about three hundred people" circles the church "thrice" behind a staff "shaped like a cross", singing an Urdu song, with the call "Bolo Yesu Masih ki" / "Jai" (p. 130, folio not stated); candles placed in a niche; no statues, the cross as icon (p. 131, folio not stated); no water bowl or kneeling; women and men seated on separate sides "however there is no rigid boundary"; a banner "He has risen"; "a golden statue of an eagle, an emblem from the Church when it was a symbol of the British colonial authority" (p. 133, folio not stated); a collection presented to the Bishop funds "the expenses of the Church, the clergy and other philanthropic work" (p. 133, folio not stated); wafer and holy water (p. 134, folio not stated); a communal breakfast "open for all", followed by "an English version of the same service, a remnant from colonial days" (p. 134, folio not stated).
- Church organisation, according to Dr. Farooqi: "A Cathedral is that Church which houses the Bishop"; a particular church "is a building headed by a Dean, who is a priest" (p. 127, folio not stated); the Church of Pakistan formed in 1970 when "four different sects within Protestantism combined" (p. 130, folio not stated).
- Naming: Christians object to "Eesai"; "We are Masih" (Dr. Farooqi); "a lot of Christians use the surname of Masih" (p. 131, folio not stated).
- Zoroastrian practice in Lahore: traditionally a morning communal prayer at the fire temple before evening celebrations; with no priest "attendance at the temple has shrunk" (p. 136, folio not stated); "It has been months since we've been to the Fire Temple" (Hamdast) and "None of us sitting here went to the temple in the morning" (Saba Chinoy) (pp. 136–137, folio not stated). Prayers recited in Avestan "without understanding, reading it in the Gujarati script"; Gujarati as mother tongue; sari pallu worn on the right (pp. 137–138, folio not stated).
- Visa difficulty: Farah and Sadaf "returned three times with our visa application" to Indian officials in Islamabad and dropped the plan (p. 139, folio not stated).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- Ahmad Gill, driving through the mob, was saved when "four Christian boys emerged and shielded the car"; "I later realized that they were angels" (p. 122, folio not stated).
- According to Farah, "Once the Prophet created fire out of nothing"; she herself thinks the miracle "was in fact a natural phenomenon" of the oil-rich area "around the Capsian sea" (p. 137, folio not stated).
- "According to Parsi legend", Zoroastrians fleeing in "three boats" reached Sanjan; refused by the Rajput Rana, their leader stirred sugar into milk — the community would be "invisible yet present" — and promised to adopt local customs and "never proselytize" (p. 138, folio not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- The author: the 1997 reconstruction left "the traumatic components of the attack and underlining prejudices ... unattended" (p. 125, folio not stated).
- The author: after Salman Taseer's assassination "The myth of the silent liberal majority had burst" (p. 129, folio not stated); the demand has shifted from scrapping the blasphemy laws to their "wrong manipulation" (p. 129, folio not stated).
- The author doubts Dr. Farooqi's claim that partition converts went mostly Catholic — it "may also be a product of his own prejudices against the Catholics"; "Conversion to Catholicism on the other hand is convenient history" (p. 132, folio not stated).
- The author: Christianity "as a mainstream religion ... never existed on such a scale here before British colonialism", against Dr. Feroze's reading of the Taxila cross (p. 134, folio not stated).
- The author: the Fire Temple "functions in oblivion" because "the Parsi community tends to play down its religious identity" (p. 139, folio not stated); Mary "is a hybrid of Christianity and Hinduism with a tinge of Islam" (pp. 132–133, folio not stated).
