## Practices, institutions, economy

- Sewa: "According to the Sikh religion, everyone is obliged to spend ten percent of their time or money in the performance of sewa (service)" (p. 160, folio not stated).
- Donor inscriptions on the Panja Sahib pool stairs name donors from Canada and the USA (p. 160, folio not stated).
- State works at Panja Sahib: protective walls "recently raised by the Government of Pakistan" (p. 161); glass decoration "put up recently by the government of Pakistan as renovation work" (p. 167); "government sanctioned" documentary (p. 166) (all folio not stated).
- "Only sixteen out of one hundred and thirty-five historical Gurdwaras are functional in Pakistan, according to Iqbal Qaiser"; "The rest are being used as houses, farms, stables, or isolated make-out spots for villagers" (p. 166, folio not stated).
- Festival economy: itinerant Pakistani vendors of religious goods (pp. 166–167); goods brought from India by pilgrims (p. 167); t-shirt prices in rupees (pp. 165–166) (all folio not stated).
- Pilgrim lodging at Panja Sahib: rooms for foreign pilgrims, corridors and tents for Pakistanis (pp. 166, 169, folio not stated).
- Akhand Path: "continuous recitation of the Granth Sahib, from beginning to the end without a break"; "three or more readers"; "about two and a half days" (p. 168, folio not stated).
- The Granth Sahib as "a living Guru"; "not regarded as a God-sent book but rather as the word of holy people, divinely inspired nonetheless" (pp. 167–168, folio not stated).
- Security: Nagar Kirtan at Hassan Abdal confined to the Gurdwara (p. 169, folio not stated).
- Nankana Sahib Sikh occupations: hakeem/hikmaat, grocery, cloth trading; newcomers housed in Gurdwara pilgrim rooms (pp. 174–176, folio not stated).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- Panja Sahib, "According to the legend": when Nanak removed the stone and water gushed out, "the reservoir of the Wali dried out as all the water was directed here"; the Wali "hurled a huge rock towards the Saint which he stopped with his hand leaving a permanent mark on it. The stone in the niche is that rock that Nanak stopped" (p. 161, folio not stated).
- The sacred pool is "said to have been created after the miracle of Nanak" (p. 160, folio not stated).
- Anandpur Sahib, 1699: the five volunteers apparently beheaded, "In reality however, all were safe" (p. 164, folio not stated) — told as history staged by the Guru, not as a miracle.

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- The author: in Pakistan "The political and religious realities here don't allow any blur in religious distinctions"; "The strict compartmentalization of religious practices and identity of which Pakistan is a product of leaves no room for the historical religious syncretism that was once part of this land's religious traditions" (p. 167, folio not stated).
- The author: "The tradition of making idols and other religious items practised in this part of the world for thousands of years died an untimely death after the partition" (p. 167, folio not stated).
- The author on Hassan Abdal's Muslim onlookers: "too insecure about their own religious identity to join them like they used to before partition" (p. 169, folio not stated).
- The author: the Khalsa and five Ks are "a symbol of a separate Sikh identity, an identity that the proponents of Khalistan felt had come under threat in a post-partition India" (p. 165, folio not stated).
- The author: after partition "The small number of Sikhs that remained in Pakistan were spread over a vast geographical area and thereby unable to form a cohesive entity"; now concentration in particular cities means "the traditions which were earlier downplayed are once again being claimed"; "The migrations have infused a new energy into the community" (p. 176, folio not stated).
