## Practices, institutions, economy

- Holi: human pyramids to break matkas of coloured water, with a stone; dhol; rooftop audiences (p. 27, folio not stated); in the Christian colony, water buckets, dry colours ("orange, red, blue and purple powder"), houses opened to all, flirting and public affection "not socially permissible under normal circumstances" (p. 28, folio not stated).
- Bahawalnagar revival: Raza and "ten other boys" cleared garbage, collected door-to-door donations, bought the Durga idol, then organised Navratri with a Bahawalpur band — "the first time after partition that a Hindu religious festival had been celebrated on such a scale at the Akaliyan Mohalla" (p. 31, folio not stated).
- Festival size: "pilgrims pouring in from as far as Karachi. This is one of the largest Hindu gatherings in the country", according to Raza (p. 33, folio not stated); "more than two thousand pilgrims this year, according to Raza. Since the past five years, the festival of Navratri has emerged as the most popular event here" (p. 35, folio not stated).
- Date of the author's visit: "It's ten in the night and the date is 16th October 2010" (p. 33, folio not stated); the previous night "was the eighth night of Navratri" (p. 37, folio not stated).
- Chunri offering: devotees buy chunris from a vendor and present them to Durga's idol, "an important rite" (p. 34, folio not stated); chunris are "entrusted to the priests, who later make use of it" (p. 37, folio not stated).
- Vows: Charan Das, 42, a peon "at the DIG of Police Office in Multan", vowed no shoes and an ankle bangle for a year and to cycle to the shrine if given a job (p. 34, folio not stated); the author compares Shia vows ("Shiia Muslims in Pakistan also indulge in similar offerings") (p. 34, folio not stated).
- Langar and hospitality: five cauldrons; "The pilgrims give donations, which help us bear the expenses", says Raza; "A few years ago, they constructed a Langar hall inside the compound of the shrine, which can accommodate several hundred people at one time" (p. 35, folio not stated); no shelter arrangements — pilgrims share locals' houses (p. 35, folio not stated).
- Security: Rana asked police for security; authorities told them to cancel and "blocked all the leading ways out of the city"; the group left at six instead of eight (p. 37, folio not stated).
- Rites: aarti, silent prayer; the goddess's clothes changed (red to green) with attendees and "male priests" sent out; a priest tends a fire "lit at all times"; protection threads, vermillion, and a woman who "would then ask for some money" (p. 38, folio not stated; p. 39, folio not stated).
- Stage: "a twenty feet tall figure of Durga Mata cut out of a cardboard"; welcome to pilgrims "by the administration" and musical performances (p. 38, folio not stated).
- Kunwari procession: three girls "not older than fourteen in this case" (p. 33, folio not stated), youngest "about ten years old" (p. 40, folio not stated), dressed as brides, carried in palanquins from about eleven at night; coconut broken; the three processions enter the temple "At about midnight" (pp. 39–40, folio not stated). "This is the last rite of the festival" (p. 41, folio not stated).
- No Dussehra burning: "We do not burn Dusshera here because of space issues", says Raza (p. 41, folio not stated).
- Holidays: "Hindu festivals are not officially recognized in Pakistan, so Hindus working at offices have to ask for special holidays"; Charan's five-day leave was refused (p. 35, folio not stated).
- Katas: guides appointed by "The Archaeology Department" when the site was renovated; the haveli's artwork "recently renovated" (pp. 42–43, folio not stated).

"Bahawal Nagar … is in Southern Punjab" (p. 31) is treated as a toponym, not a province bullet.
