## Practices, institutions, economy

- Ram Thamman Baisakhi: a group of eight boys performs Dandiya Raas with dhol before about fifty people; a green chadar with Quranic verses in golden thread, "brought by the group as a gift to the Saint", receives money "a gift for the performers"; "all contributions are voluntary" (pp. 62–63, folio not stated). Ghulam Ali, 22, has played dandiya "since the past fifteen years"; "There is no invitation for such festivals. People come out of their devotion" (p. 63, folio not stated).
- Baisakhi "essentially is a celebration of the beginning of the harvest season", given religious significance by Sikh and Hindu traditions; at Ram Thamman "this celebration is conjoined with the festival of the shrine"; families cut wheat on the village outskirts (p. 63, folio not stated).
- In 2011 Bhagat Advit Bhatti's delegation of "three hundred people" arrived "on the night of 12th April", stayed in the open, prayed before sunrise "at the temple of Kali Mata" and performed "an ablution (ashnan)"; "This is the first year after partition that Hindus have performed a prayer here"; "The door of the temple was opened by Hussain"; the Hindus distributed food to the village poor and left before the festivities (p. 64, folio not stated). "A few Muslims from the village feel that having food from the donation of the Hindu community is unacceptable"; "For now the Hindus perform the religious rites, while the Muslims take part in the festivities" (p. 64, folio not stated).
- The "orthodox section of the village" has used "the force of the authorities" to curb the festival, removing loudspeakers "from the stalls and Maut ka kuan"; the festival "has managed to thrive" (p. 65, folio not stated).
- Neela Gumbad administration: Bishna Ram (Treasurer), Advit (General Secretary and Pandit, "inherited the post from his father"), Asghar Khokhar; nightly prayer "at about seven" (p. 66, folio not stated). Advit's brother's family lives in the courtyard and "look after the temple" (p. 67, folio not stated).
- Neela Gumbad fabric: faces "a narrow alley, once known as the Valmiki Street", renamed after partition; "A small blue door"; "a triangle-shaped orange flag with a golden border", "an essential feature of all Valmiki temples"; no guards (p. 66, folio not stated). Courtyard "only about twenty-fifty square yards"; two rooms with bells — one of Hindu deities (Ganesh, Krishna and Radha statues; Durga Mata, Kali Mata, Parvati, Krishna, Ram and Sita portraits), one with Valmiki's statue in a palanquin and a cradle for Krishna Janamasthami (p. 67, folio not stated). A portico painting of Valmiki with Luv and Kush faces "a small wooden cross" (p. 70, folio not stated).
- Destruction: according to Bishna Ram, the pre-partition Valmiki idol was "designed by students of the Punjab University", and it and a white-marble Krishna were "smashed and destroyed ... by a mob protesting the desecration of the Babri Mosque in India"; "The entire temple was burned down" (p. 67, folio not stated). The attack was "On the 7th of December 1992"; according to the Pandit, "thousands of them, carrying weapons, including guns"; "the temple had been knocked down and then burned. For days the fire raged" (pp. 68–69, folio not stated). A wrestling arena in the courtyard "was removed when the temple was renovated after the destruction of 1992" (p. 68, folio not stated). The community collected funds and "Within a year or so after the attack, a brand new construction came up" (p. 70, folio not stated).
- Sitla Mandir: its pool, believed to cure "people with all sorts of diseases", visited by mothers "every Thursday" and by "Many Muslims", has been replaced by a community park; the rest is apartments, one occupied by "Madrassa Noor-ul-Quran", whose maulvi's family moved in after partition and "became the owner of the property by default"; "In December 1992" the maulvi instigated an attack, a bulldozer was brought, and a boy hammering the cone fell and died and "was given the title of Shaheed"; a plate reading "Ya Allah" now rests in a niche (pp. 69–70, folio not stated).
- Valmiki's birthday, 2011: preparations "two days in advance"; on "the 21st of October" Azad Chaudary, 45, a Balmiki who became Christian at 22, practises a Mohammad Rafi bhajan with his son Yashwa, 12; Musharraf Ali, 35, a Muslim flute player whose father was a Balmiki, attends (p. 72, folio not stated). Police at the gate at Advit's request; stage, speakers; crowd "Muslims and Christians, most of whom have Balmiki roots and Balmikis"; vegetarian langar of puri and channa on holy days (pp. 72–73, folio not stated). Prayer "At about seven in the evening", call "Bolo Shri Valmiki Maharaj ki" / "Jai"; halwa as prasad (p. 73, folio not stated). Ram hosts, curtails performances; final performance by Zohaib, 30, a Muslim sarangi player of the sixth generation, invited by Mehboob (flute); function ended "around midnight" to avoid attention (pp. 74–75, folio not stated).
- According to Pandit Advit, "since the past few years we have come out of the rooms and started holding functions in the courtyard", which the author ties to General Pervez Musharraf's "'Enlightened Moderation' years" (p. 74, folio not stated); media interest in minorities since "the era of General Pervez Musharraf (2001-2008)" (p. 73, folio not stated).
- Krishna Janmashtami at Neela Gumbad: "The date is the 22nd of August 2011", falling in Ramadan; an iftar is held for Muslim guests with a maulvi calling them to break fast; a hired DJ stops music for the azaan; "about a hundred people", mostly Hindus and Christians; langar of "halwa puri and channa" later; a Christian and a Sikh representative invited (pp. 76–77, folio not stated). The Pandit arranges special prayers in the temple on Eid and Eid millad-un-Nabi and on Christian functions (p. 78, folio not stated).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- According to Advit's aunt, Hanuman dug the Ram Thamman pool "with a shovel only two and a half times"; the author calls it "a mythological claim that cannot be verified historically" (p. 64, folio not stated).
- According to Hussain, "Baisakhi is being celebrated here since twelve hundred years" (p. 65, folio not stated).
- Luv himself is believed to have built the original temple of Luv near the Alamgiri gate (p. 71, folio not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- The author argues the festival survived "because of its nature" as a harvest festival "of the farmer, irrespective of religion" (p. 63, folio not stated).
- The author notes the Hanuman myth attaches to a shrine "whose history, according to the literature, only goes back about five centuries", that "history books are glaringly quiet" on Baisakhi there, and concludes "It is likely that this was an ancient temple" before Baba Ram Thamman settled (pp. 64–65, folio not stated).
- The author argues Hindus adopt "Christian and Muslim names to avoid being noticed", "more out of prudence than actual conviction" (p. 70, folio not stated), and that the community "owes more to a dictatorship than democratic regimes for its religious freedom" (p. 74, folio not stated).
- The author criticises media coverage of minorities as "paternalistic" and driven by an "'exotic' image" (p. 73, folio not stated), and argues non-Muslims must repeatedly display loyalty to Pakistan (pp. 78–79, folio not stated).
- Converts: untouchable Hindus who converted in 1947 "are referred to as Deendars or Musalis" and are still treated as untouchables; higher-caste converts "became Sheikhs" (pp. 77–78, folio not stated).
