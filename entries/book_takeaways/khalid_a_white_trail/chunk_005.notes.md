## Practices, institutions, economy

- Krishna birthday at the Valmiki temple: prayers with "a traditional reading by the Pandit from the holy books, followed by bhajans" start "about an hour after the breaking of the fast"; "Muslim and the Christian guests are also invited to attend, along with members of the media" (p. 80, folio not stated).
- The Pandit's wife marks attendees' foreheads with "a vertical line of vermillion"; elders and men stay outside for lack of space; musicians sit under the pictures of the idols (p. 80, folio not stated); aarti rotated "in a clockwise direction", everyone invited to perform it; Prasad (oily halwa) offered (pp. 80–81, folio not stated).
- Prayers last "about thirty minutes"; the Pandit asks "his Muslim, Sikh and Christian friends to cut the cake with him"; he slows down for photographers, "shots which will highlight the religious tolerance of the occasion" (p. 81, folio not stated).
- A DJ, "waiting patiently, firstly for the azaan and then for the bhajan", plays "the latest remixed Indian songs"; dancing follows; Susheela, "one of the few better off Hindus in the city", throws candies (pp. 80–81, folio not stated).
- Dispute over custody: "Ever since the celebrations at this temple have increased in fervour, the authorities want to take over the administration of the shrine," according to Bhagat Advit Bhatti (p. 82, folio not stated).
- "The Awqaf Department was created during the tenure of General Ayub Khan in 1959, to control, administer and collect revenues from shrines all over the country" (p. 82, folio not stated).
- The Pandit says his family "has been affiliated with the governing of the temple for generations" and that he is being "'harassed' and 'threatened' by the authorities to give away control" (p. 82, folio not stated).
- Property: "There is vast property connected with the temple ... shops and residential houses around the temple"; after "the creation of Pakistan", officials asked the elders to contribute to the new government; the Pandit's father, then in charge, let the authorities "have control over the shops outside of the temple and collect revenue from there, as a contribution of the Valmikis to the Jinnah fund", according to Bhagat Advit (pp. 82–83, folio not stated).
- "Even though the Jinnah fund no longer exists, the authorities still collect the revenues from the shops and the houses, now several thousands of rupees given the prime location", while the Hindu community "continues to live in poverty" (p. 83, folio not stated); the Pandit: "We don't even know how much money they collect from our properties" (p. 83, folio not stated).
- Maryabad festival dates: "The festival begins on 9th September and will continue till the 11th" (p. 85, folio not stated); "Tomorrow (10th September) is the most important day as that is when the Bishop arrives. The day after that pilgrims will leave" (p. 95, folio not stated); on "the second day" the Bishop Sahib comes from Lahore and people report miracles on stage (p. 86, folio not stated).
- Pilgrims' journeys: Shahbaz Masih and "twenty-five other friends" leave Okara "On 7th of September 2011" by cycle, a journey of "more than one hundred and fifty kilometres", in orange T-shirts embroidered "I love Mother Mary" (p. 85, folio not stated); cycled without shoes in "two sets of socks" (p. 94, folio not stated); Shahzad, a Faisalabad rickshaw driver, travels with five relatives (pp. 85–86, folio not stated).
- Walking distances as printed: "Lahore (a distance of about 70 kms.), Gujranwala (100 kms. away), Sialkot (150 kms. away), Faisalabad (150 kms. away) and Sheikupura (20 kms. away)" (p. 88, folio not stated); pilgrims "purposefully make the journey difficult", some "without shoes", believing "God will forgive their sins and fulfill their wishes" (p. 88, folio not stated).
- Food: "devotees distributing free food at the shrine" and "many stalls" (p. 86, folio not stated); temporary restaurants in a "temporary city of tents" (pp. 92–93, folio not stated).
- Organisation and security: "The Catholic Church looks after the arrangements. The government assists them with security" (p. 86, folio not stated); the complex has "high walls, covered by iron spikes and manned by police officials", one entrance with a metal detector (pp. 86–87, folio not stated); a separate line for women (p. 94, folio not stated); recently a wall was raised around the tent ground behind the Church "at a time when religious minorities have come under threat because of a rise in Islamic fundamentalism" (p. 97, folio not stated).
- Father Mushtaq, "36 year old", "a priest from Narowal", "appointed here by the Catholic Church to look after the administration of the festival" (p. 86, folio not stated).
- The mound: "made out of stone", "about twenty feet high", with a statue of Mother Mary "her head covered in a white shawl holding baby Christ" at the summit; devotees wrap shawls, light candles, spread rose petals and stick incense between the rocks (pp. 86–88, folio not stated).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- "A little before partition, a couple of Christian men saw Mother Mary appear on that mound, situated next to the Church," according to Father Mushtaq (p. 86, folio not stated).
- "In the eighties, there was another report of a few children seeing the Mother at that particular spot again. There have been numerous other reports of Saints sighted here," according to Father Mushtaq (p. 86, folio not stated).
- On the second day people report miracles on stage; "Mothers bring their sick children and children their sick parents, and all are cured"; the water of "a small spring just behind the mound" is "considered holy and consumed by people for salvation," according to Father Mushtaq (p. 86, folio not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- The author contrasts Maryabad's heavy security with Hindu festivals, "where there is either no security at all or just a few police officials at the gate", and explains it by the Christians' political and institutional weight (pp. 87, folio not stated).
- The author argues converts gave Christianity "a Hindu interpretation, of which the pilgrimage to Maryabad is just one example"; "educated Christians look down upon this shrine and the rituals around it, terming it as corruption of original Christianity" (p. 89, folio not stated).
- On untouchability: low-caste Christians are called "chuhras"; after partition "religious identities became a symbol of untouchability ... as opposed to castes earlier"; "The term 'Christian' ... is now referred to as something akin to a caste"; "Europeans of Christian origin are not given this title" (p. 90, folio not stated).
- The author argues the conservatives in the Aasia Bibi case "are using the basis of purity and impurity, a concept that they have borrowed from the Hindu caste structure" (p. 91, folio not stated).
- The author argues that where Hindu temples, Sufi shrines and gurdwaras are tied to particular places, "Christianity is devoid of any such physical connection"; Maryabad, based on "the physical presence of Mother Mary", "localizes the religion, like Islam was prior to it" (p. 95, folio not stated).
- The author links the decline of Christian devotional recordings by popular artists to "the Islamist military dictator Zia-ul-Haq during the eighties" and to insecurity "in a post 9/11 world"; the sixties and seventies are "believed to be the secular years of the country" (pp. 95–96, folio not stated).
