# khalid_a_white_trail — chunk 011 — pp. 196–214

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **gurdwara-dera-sahib** (the book's "Gurdwara Dera Sahib"; chapter on Guru Arjan's martyrdom, continued from the previous chunk):
  - The chunk opens mid-sentence ("site."): "This is where the Sikh ruler of Punjab, Maharaja Ranjit Singh, used to meet his subjects. A Mughal baradari (a pavilion with twelve pillars) stands in the middle of the garden"; "All of these three places are famous with the tourists"; "A giant gateway leading into the yellow building from here facing the garden is permanently locked" (p. 196, folio not stated).
  - "Today, this place is deserted": the only road to the monuments "is cordoned off by the police"; a barricade "fortified with barbwire"; only people "carrying special cards, which have been made for them by the Evacuee Trust Property Board (ETPB)" may pass; "Many local tourists return disappointed at not being able to visit the fort or the other sites"; a metal detector door reads 'Out' while the 'In' gate is shut (p. 196, folio not stated).
  - Occasion and date as printed: "The date is 6th June 2011 and the occasion is the commemoration of Guru Arjan's death anniversary" (p. 196, folio not stated).
  - "About three hundred pilgrims from India have come to Lahore for this event, while several hundred have come from other parts of the country"; the Indians stay "for three days", after which some return "while others will visit sacred shrines in Nankana Sahib and Hassan Abdal" (p. 196, folio not stated).
  - On regular days "hundreds of local visitors" pass and peek in; to tourists asking to enter, the security men's answer is "If you are a Muslim, you are not allowed." (p. 196, folio not stated).
  - Kartar Singh Kartar is "temporarily residing at this Gurdwara for his education and being given free accommodation and food at the shrine"; he jokes that to curious locals "I am a langur (a species of monkey)" (p. 196, folio not stated).
  - A final security check before the courtyard: male and female police search people, bags pass "through an X-ray machine"; the inner structure is the same yellow as the protecting wall; "A couple of rooms at the entrance have been taken over by the security officials" (p. 197, folio not stated).
  - The courtyard is tented against "the intense heat of June", carpeted, facing the Granthi; the Granth Sahib recitation is heard through "a small speaker"; most pilgrims are Sikhs "bearing the 5 Ks", a few clean-shaven "Nanak Panthi Sikhs" from "South Punjab and Sindh" (p. 197, folio not stated).
  - The Nishan Sahib "stands at the edge of the courtyard" on "a circular platform about five feet from the ground"; men pray to the Khanda, touch the base and kiss their hands or touch their eyes to the platform (p. 197, folio not stated).
  - Beyond a covered space lies the Langar hall; a small room "with windows on all sides, protected by iron bars" holds "About twenty attendees" though its "capacity [is] no more than five people"; women recite from "pocket Granths"; in the centre "a small bulge which is covered by a velvet cloth ... This is the main attraction; the samadhi of Guru Arjan" (pp. 197–198, folio not stated).
  - Guru Arjan was assassinated "in Lahore, where his now Gurdwara stands by the name of Gurdwara Dera Sahib" (p. 198, folio not stated).
  - Building history as the book gives it: "a small structure was raised at the spot by his son, Guru Hargobind to mark the samadhi of the Guru and it is still present there to this day"; "The present structure was first erected by Maharaja Ranjit Singh with further extensions made by the Sikh community in the early years of the twentieth century" (p. 200, folio not stated).
  - "After partition, the Gurdwara came under the control of the Pakistan Government" through the ETPB; "the ETPB regularly undertakes renovation work here"; "In 1996, rooms were built in the complex" for pilgrims "from India and other parts of Pakistan"; "This new hostel was named after Mian Meer" (p. 200, folio not stated).
  - The Langar Hall, reached by "A small door from the samadhi of Guru Arjan", is "divided into two sections", with mats "in four lines"; food is prepared by volunteers and employees, "an overwhelming majority of who seem to be low-caste Nanak Panthis from Southern Punjab" (p. 201, folio not stated).
  - The lawn serves as "a temporary camp for pilgrims who do not have rooms", "primarily Pathan-Sikh families who have come from Nankana Sahib, Hassan Abdal, Peshawar and the Tribal Areas"; Indian pilgrims and "other important guests" have rooms in the complex (p. 201, folio not stated).
  - At the conclusion of Akhand Path "bhog (community prayer) is performed"; the audience says "Waheguru"; "the entire community prostates [as printed] to the living Guru, the Granth Sahib" (p. 201, folio not stated). Sikh boys serve cold milk, juice and "freshly cut water melons" (p. 201, folio not stated).
  - Informants at the event: "32 year old Dr. Manoj Singh", "the first MBBS doctor from the Pakistani Sikh community", "serving at the Ganga Ram Hospital" (pp. 201–202, folio not stated); Kartar, "the most highly qualified Pakistani Sikh", decided "to do my PhD in the Punjabi language" (p. 202, folio not stated); "25 year old Premjeet Singh, the first Pakistani Sikh to have been commissioned into the Pakistan Rangers", allotted a room "because of his social standing", shared "with five other family members" (p. 202, folio not stated).

- **gurdwara-baoli-sahib-guru-arjan-dev-ji-lahore**, **gurdwara-dera-sahib**, **gurdwara-panjvi-chati-patshahi** (the book's "Guru Arjan"; person-level):
  - "Guru Arjan was the fifth Sikh Guru of Sikhism and the son of the fourth Sikh Guru, Guru Ram Das"; a major contribution was "the compilation into the Granth Sahib of the writings of all the previous Sikh Gurus and other non-Sikh saints" (p. 198, folio not stated).
  - "He was assassinated on the orders of the fourth Mughal Emperor, Jahangir"; the book quotes Jahangir's Tuzuk-i-Jahangiri ("this insignificant fellow (Guru Arjan) ... commanded that he should be put to death"), via footnote 5 (Dalbir Singh Dhillon, *Sikhism: Origin and Development*, "Page 108", 1988) (p. 198, folio not stated; footnote p. 204).
  - Khusrau, "Jahangir's rebellious son", "stopped over at the Langar of the Guru in Amritsar and sought blessings for his victory"; the tilak was that blessing, "considered treason by the Emperor" (p. 199, folio not stated).
  - Baba Fareed's poetry, collected by Guru Nanak at Pak Pattan, "was later included in the Granth Sahib by Guru Arjan"; "the friendship between Mian Meer and Guru Arjan is also the subject of several folk tales" (p. 200, folio not stated).
  - "Guru Arjan's martyrdom is a low point in Sikh history" (pp. 206–207, folio not stated).

- **samadhi-of-maharaja-ranjit-singh** (the book's "samadhi of Maharaja Ranjit Singh"; chapter "COMMEMORATION OF MAHARAJA RANJIT SINGH'S DEATH ANNIVERSARY AT LAHORE", begun p. 205):
  - Site: facing the Dera Sahib across "a big lawn" is "a beautiful white building, adorned with detailed frescoes and other art work ... a grand structure compared to the modest building dedicated to Guru Arjan"; "A police official sits on top of the samadhi with his gun in front of him" (p. 201, folio not stated).
  - "Just behind the samadhi of Guru Arjan, a splendid building, white in colour, stands four floors from the ground with a small dome on the top"; the Badshahi Masjid's minaret rises behind it; "The Hazuri Bagh is situated in the middle of these monuments, with a single storey pavilion placed symmetrically at the center of the garden while four lanes lead towards it" (p. 205, folio not stated).
  - The samadhi "stands on top of another single storey building, painted yellow", in a courtyard "with rooms for the pilgrims on its sides" (p. 206, folio not stated).
  - "Two loud speakers have been placed on top of the samadhi, facing the direction of the ground, erected opposite the mosque and the city, perhaps as a respect to the Muslim religion, a respect that is only expected of the religious minorities" (author's remark) (p. 206, folio not stated).
  - The hall holds "about a hundred and fifty people, all men"; "a palanquin in the centre, constructed on a raised platform with its pillars made of white marble"; "This is the conclusion of the Akhand Path held in the honour of Maharaja Ranjit Singh, also known as Sher-e-Punjab, or the Lion of Punjab" (p. 206, folio not stated).
  - Date as printed: "The occasion is the death anniversary of the ruler and the date 29th June 2011, twelve days after the death anniversary commemoration of Guru Arjan in the same complex"; the earlier pilgrims have left "and a set of new ones have arrived from India" (p. 206, folio not stated).
  - "For the city that the Maharaja once ruled, he is an alien, his memory kept alive not by people of the land he once ruled but by people from the other side of the border" (p. 206, folio not stated).
  - The palanquin "marks the spot where the last remains of the Maharaja were buried. His cremation took place in the ground accompanying the building" (p. 209, folio not stated).
  - "On a regular day, a portrait of the Maharaja is placed at the palanquin, dressed in a white robe, sporting a long white beard", next to that of "his youngest son, Dalip Singh, who also happened to be the last Maharaja of Punjab"; "Today, the portraits have been removed" (p. 209, folio not stated).
  - The dome is "decorated with coloured glass" (green, orange, red) in geometric patterns; niches with frescoes; balcony walls show court scenes and "religious drawings from Hindu mythology", with "depictions of Krishna, Ganesh, Ram and Sita"; the paintings "have faded", some "disappeared all together", and people "have noted down their names and numbers thereby disfiguring it" (p. 209, folio not stated).
  - An adjacent hall has a raised platform with "thirteen small semi-spheres. These are the samadhis of the eleven women and two pigeons who also burned in the pyre of Maharaja Ranjit Singh", including "his wives and concubines"; the book calls this "sati" from "the ancient Rajput tradition" (p. 210, folio not stated).
  - Langar after the ceremony: two rows; dal, rice, rotis, water; tea "Boiled with sugar and ginger", "highly popular in East Punjab" (pp. 210–211, folio not stated).
  - Pilgrim informants: "Amanpreet Singh, 35, is a trader from Ludhiana", on his first visit to Pakistan: "The Maharaja was a great leader ... There was no crime" (p. 208, folio not stated); "54 year old Jaswant Singh" and a friend, first-time visitors "from the city of Qadian in East Punjab" (p. 211, folio not stated).
  - Ranjit Singh (the row's principal figure): "Starting from a small fief around Gujranwala ... the Maharaja marched triumphantly into Lahore in 1799"; "In 1801, Ranjit Singh was crowned as the Ruler of Punjab by Sahib Singh Bedi, a descendant of Guru Nanak"; his empire "extended from FATA (Federally Administered Tribal Areas) to Kashmir to Multan in the South and the River Sutlej"; he made a deal with the British (p. 207, folio not stated).
  - Claims the book reports and qualifies: "It is said that the Badshahi Masjid was used as a stable for horses"; "he removed the top structure of Emperor Jahangir's tomb and placed it in the Hazuri Bagh. The pavilion, which now stands in the middle of the ground, was part of the structure of Emperor Jahangir's tomb" (p. 207, folio not stated).
  - "Like the third Mughal Emperor Akbar, Maharaja Ranjit Singh was a secular ruler"; he "constructed a mosque at a mohalla called Gul Begum in Lahore in memory of his Muslim wife"; "Another Muslim wife of his, Jind Kaur, ordered the construction of a library" at Data Darbar; Muslim generals "General Mohammad Sultan, General Elahi Baksh and General Ghaus Mohammad Khan"; "Fakir Azizudin" held the offices of "Prime Minister and the Foreign Minister" (p. 207, folio not stated).
  - He is credited with "the construction and renovation of many Gurdwaras all over Punjab"; he brought stability "which lasted a good forty years"; "The British took over soon after his death" (pp. 208–209, folio not stated). Gurdwara properties "most of which were allotted to the shrines at the time of Ranjit Singh" (p. 203, folio not stated). He "first erected" the present Dera Sahib structure (p. 200, folio not stated).
  - "In 1802, he employed a Nihang by the name of Akali Phula Singh to capture the city of Amritsar"; Phula Singh "brought two to three thousand Nihangs into the state army" (p. 210, folio not stated).

## Province-level material

- PROVINCE punjab (book's word: "Punjab" / "South Punjab" / "Southern Punjab" / "the heart of Punjab" / "Sher-e-Punjab"; refers to: several periods, stated per sub-bullet — the book's own framing):
  - Ranjit Singh's reign (the book gives 1799 entry to Lahore, 1801 coronation): "the Sikh ruler of Punjab, Maharaja Ranjit Singh" (p. 196, folio not stated); "the Sikh king who ruled Punjab before the British took over" (p. 206, folio not stated); "crowned as the Ruler of Punjab" in 1801 (p. 207, folio not stated); "In the land of Punjab which was for over a millenium [as printed] ruled by Afghans, Mughals and other non-Punjabis, Ranjit Singh was the first Punjabi to rule Punjab" (p. 208, folio not stated); he renovated "many Gurdwaras all over Punjab" and brought stability "in Punjab which lasted a good forty years" (p. 208, folio not stated).
  - After Ranjit Singh's death (no year given): "Punjab became a British colony almost immediately after his death eventually leading to its division at the time of Partition"; Dalip Singh was "the last Maharaja of Punjab, before the British took over" (p. 209, folio not stated).
  - "the older days" (period not stated more exactly): "yellow was commonly used for buildings in Punjab during the older days" (p. 197, folio not stated).
  - Partition: "During the riots in Punjab at the time of partition, millions of people were killed, with an overwhelming majority being Sikhs and Muslims" (p. 199, folio not stated).
  - Present day (c. 2011): Nanak Panthi Sikhs "belong to the areas of South Punjab and Sindh", with temples "in Sindh and Southern Punjab" (p. 197, folio not stated); Dera Sahib langar workers are "low-caste Nanak Panthis from Southern Punjab" (p. 201, folio not stated).
  - Recent past to present (Pathan Sikh elders): "Moving from tribal areas to the heart of Punjab, an overwhelming majority of them could not even speak the language. They remained confined to their little enclaves" (p. 203, folio not stated).

- PROVINCE sindh (book's word: "Sindh" / "the province of Sindh"; refers to: present day, c. 2011):
  - Nanak Panthi Sikhs "belong to the areas of South Punjab and Sindh"; their temples "in Sindh and Southern Punjab" hold portraits of Guru Nanak with Hindu deities; "They are found in very large numbers in the province of Sindh" (p. 197, folio not stated).

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- Gurdwara Dera Chaal, "on Bedian Road, Lahore": "In April 2012, Ghulab Singh, the first Sikh traffic warden in Pakistan, filed a case against the ETPB in the Lahore High Court, challenging the 'illegal' sale of land" connected with it; "The High Court ordered a stay on the sale of land" (p. 204, folio not stated).
- Badshahi Masjid (Royal Mosque), Lahore: its minaret rises behind the Ranjit Singh samadhi, with "three bulb-like domes" (p. 205, folio not stated); "It is said that the Badshahi Masjid was used as a stable for horses" under Ranjit Singh (p. 207, folio not stated).
- Emperor Jahangir's tomb: Ranjit Singh "removed the top structure" and placed it in the Hazuri Bagh (p. 207, folio not stated).
- Gul Begum mosque, Lahore: built by Ranjit Singh "in memory of his Muslim wife" (p. 207, folio not stated).
- Amritsar: Guru Arjan's Langar where Khusrau sought blessings (p. 199, folio not stated); the SGPC "based in Amritsar" took festival collections to India before 1999 (p. 203, folio not stated); captured for Ranjit Singh by Akali Phula Singh in 1802 (p. 210, folio not stated).
- Qadian, "in East Punjab", "Situated in district Gurdaspur", "the founding place of the Ahmadiyya movement": "In 1889 ... Mirza Ghulam Ahmad, founded the Ahmadiyya sect"; after Pakistan's creation many migrated "to ... a city called Rabwah in Punjab"; "the headquarters shifted from Qadian to Rabwah" (pp. 211–212, folio not stated).
- Baba Thara Singh: "a saint from our area" (Ajay Kumar, of Sukkur), in whose memory the Sindhi Nanak Panthis held the Akhand Path at Nankana (p. 214, folio not stated).
- Tara Singh, Sikh leader, and Iqbal Qaiser, "author of the book, 'Historical Sikh shrines of Pakistan'" (pp. 199–200, folio not stated).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- ETPB: controls Dera Sahib since partition, issues the security cards, regularly renovates; "created to take care of non-Muslim temples and shrines in the country" (pp. 196, 200, folio not stated).
- PSGPC: "All the affairs of Gurdwaras in Pakistan, including arranging festivals, employment, cleanliness, property, renovation etc. are managed by the Pakistan Sikh Gurdwara Prabandhak Committee (PSGPC) that functions under the purview of the ETPB"; "formed in 1999"; it collects festival revenue, "an amount that runs into several hundreds of thousands of rupees" (p. 203, folio not stated).
- Before 1999 "the Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee (SGPC), based in Amritsar, used to take the collection at the festivals back to India" (p. 203, folio not stated).
- Gurdwara property: "about 25,00,000 acres according to an official at the ETPB", mostly allotted "at the time of Ranjit Singh"; "growing resentment" over PSGPC handling of "activities, revenues, and property" (pp. 203–204, folio not stated); allegations of further corruption and of "Gurdwaras being rented out to fill the personal coffers of the officials" (p. 204, folio not stated).
- Madan Singh (former PSGPC head): "The PSGPC are cronies of the ETPB dominated by the Muslim community"; "Even the priest (Granthis) and other employees are appointed by the government"; he resigned because "they would not let me work independently" (p. 203, folio not stated).
- Nanak Panthis: clean-shaven Hindus who follow Guru Nanak but not the Khalsa, also "Nanak Panthi Hindus" (p. 197, folio not stated); "Hindus who believe in Nanak" (p. 214, folio not stated).
- Nishan Sahib and Khanda described: saffron triangular flag, "double edged dagger", "chakker", two "kirpan" (p. 197, folio not stated).
- Nihangs: "This order trains as warriors to protect the Sikh religion"; "A Nihang calls himself sava lakh (1, 25,000)" (p. 210, folio not stated); a Nihang pilgrim at the samadhi dressed in blue with steel quoits and a long sword (p. 209, folio not stated).
- Community change: earlier Sikhs "traditionally confined to ... hikmat, cloth trading and other forms of business"; the younger generation, "fluent in Urdu and Punjabi", more aware of "religious, political and social rights" (pp. 202–203, folio not stated).
- Khalistan period: Pakistan's government "openly came out in support of Khalistan as revenge for what had happened in East Pakistan in 1971"; the struggle "gained popularity between the early seventies and late eighties"; banned expat Sikhs came to Pakistan for festivals; political speeches after ceremonies; "That era defines the trajectory of the Sikh community in Pakistan" (pp. 213–214, folio not stated).
- Premjeet Singh is "often sighted at the Wagah border during the daily ceremonial parade" and volunteers "to work on Eid" in exchange for festival leave (pp. 202–203, folio not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- Author: Arjan's persecution "defined future relations between the Mughals and the Sikhs"; "It was the assassination of Guru Arjan that transformed the Sikhs from a peaceful religious reforming group into a political community", leading to the Khalsa (p. 199, folio not stated).
- Author: Sikh anti-Mughal feeling shaped Muslim-Sikh relations up to partition; Iqbal Qaiser "is of the opinion that Guru Arjan's assassination was the starting point of a trajectory, of which the violence ... at the time of partition was the last stop, four centuries later" (pp. 199–200, folio not stated).
- Author: a counter-precedent exists in Nanak's and Arjan's ties with Sufis; "The relationship of the Sikhs and the Muslims cannot only be seen through the prism of Sikh-Mughal rivalry" (p. 200, folio not stated).
- Author: Ranjit Singh's era "is now portrayed as a repressive time for Muslims in Pakistani historiography, whose entire purpose is to keep up a nationalist agenda"; using the stable/plunder examples "to draw a universal principle is reductionist" (p. 207, folio not stated).
- Author: "Invaders and plunderers like Mahmud Ghaznvi and Ahmad Shah Abdali became national heroes due to their religious affiliation and local heroes became infidels" (p. 208, folio not stated); Amanpreet's view is "another extreme", like the Muslim perception of the "Rightly-guided Caliphs" (p. 208, folio not stated).
- Khushwant Singh quoted (footnote 6, *Ranjit Singh*, "Page 275", Penguin India, 2001): "The word Nihang in Persian means crocodile ... The order has degenerated into a sect of beggars" (p. 210, folio not stated; footnote p. 212).
- Author: the Ahmadiyya case is "a strange irony of history" — free in India, persecuted in Pakistan (pp. 211–212, folio not stated).
