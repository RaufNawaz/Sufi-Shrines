## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **gurdwara-panja-sahib** (the book's "Gurdwara Panja Sahib", "Hassan Abdal"; chapter "BAIAISAKHI [text-layer?] [BAISAKHI] AT HASSAN ABDAL", pp. 157–171):
  - "Surrounded by mountains on one side and the entire city of Hassan Abdal on the other, the Gurdwara Panja Sahib is one of the most important Sikh shrines in the country" (p. 157, folio not stated).
  - It is "home to about fifty to sixty Sikh families, who have moved here in the last few years from the tribal areas due to the rise of insurgency there, while the rest of the city is Muslim"; most of these Sikhs "only know Pashtu", which "just a handful of Punjabi/Hindko speaking people here can understand" (p. 157, folio not stated).
  - The Sikhs "are mostly confined within the boundaries of the Gurdwara, protected by police officials, tall walls and barbed wires on all sides; only occasionally do they venture out" (p. 157, folio not stated).
  - "A few pilgrims visit all year round to pay their respects to the shrine associated with the founder of the Sikh religion, Guru Nanak (1469-1539)" (p. 157, folio not stated).
  - Hassan Abdal "is a historic city which has managed to retain its character": narrow curved streets with wooden balconies; the city is "Situated on hillocks" (p. 157, folio not stated).
  - "A few Hindu temples are still present here", visible from afar, "but are now being used as improvised houses or in some cases wandering grounds for stray animals, abandoned by the people of the city" (p. 157, folio not stated).
  - "The remains of a Mughal garden in a dilapidated state still survive facing the Gurdwara", referred to in the "Ain-i-Akbari" written "during the reign of the third Mughal Emperor, Akbar (1542-1605) by Abu-Fazl" (p. 157, folio not stated).
  - Baisakhi: "For three days and nights in the month of April, everything changes here"; streets around the shrine are cordoned off, "no Muslim is allowed to venture near the Gurdwara"; local schools shut and classrooms "converted into hostels for the pilgrims" (p. 157, folio not stated).
  - Pilgrims spend three nights, "13th, 14th and 15th of April", then most go on "to the other holy Sikh shrines", some staying longer; "thousands of turbaned Sikhs" haggle in the market, "most of them Indian Punjabis" (p. 157, folio not stated).
  - "Most of the Sikhs here are fair skinned and blue eyed Pathans, from the regions of Khyber-Pakthunkhwa and tribal areas and they are more alien to this place than the Indian Punjabi Sikhs"; a third category of pilgrims are clean-shaven "Hindus from the different regions of Pakistan" (p. 158, folio not stated).
  - "The date is 13th April 2011"; some pilgrims walk the tar road "without shoes as an expression of extreme respect" (p. 158, folio not stated).
  - "According to local media reports, about seven thousand pilgrims have arrived for the festival this year- about two thousand from India and the rest from within Pakistan" (p. 158, folio not stated).
  - Entry: a police officer checks "everyone's security cards" and admits one at a time; "Even until last year" turbans or head covers sufficed, and those who "looked more 'Muslim'" were asked for identity and barred if Muslim; "This year however, the security has been intensified due to the deteriorating security conditions of the country" (p. 158, folio not stated).
  - "Even till recently, Muslims were allowed to pay their respects to the shrine of Baba Guru Nanak, much in line with the Sikh philosophy but not anymore"; all pilgrims and visitors (journalists etc.) get a security card "from the 'Special Branch'" (p. 158, folio not stated).
  - A television crew, refused without the security card, threatens to telecast its treatment and is let through (p. 158, folio not stated).
  - "more than two hundred people" in the line; "The façade of the building is pink", the inside closed off by an iron gate; a police officer watches from the top of a shop opposite (p. 158, folio not stated).
  - The book's account of the origin: "About five hundred years ago, on the same date, the 1st day of Vaisakh, Guru Nanak came here" with "Bhai Mardana – a Muslim, and Bhai Bala – a Hindu"; the spot "was not a city at that time"; he settled "under a peepal tree, the very spot where the Gurdwara was built later" (p. 159, folio not stated). ## Practices, institutions, economy

- Fire Temple (Lahore): a Muslim caretaker looks after cleanliness and errands "but he is not allowed to enter the room that contains the fire. Only members of the Parsi community tend to the sacred fire", according to Sadaf (p. 140, folio not stated).
- The temple has "one room which is reserved for the fire and another hall where non-Parsis are allowed but reluctantly"; next to it a compound where "flats are leased out" to Parsi families "at subsidized rates"; administration handled by "the Anjuman, an elected body" (p. 140, folio not stated).
- The hall holds "a picture of the Prophet Zoroaster and other senior members of the community who have passed away"; "the Zoroastrians are an ancestor worshipping community", according to Sadaf's sister (p. 140, folio not stated).
- Farah: Parsis "celebrate all festivals irrespective of religion", visiting Muslim friends on Eid and Christian friends on Christmas with a cake; members may marry and convert to any religion; "what we do not allow is for any non-Parsi to convert into Zoroastrianism. To be one, one has to have a Parsi mother and father" (p. 140, p. 141, folio not stated).
- Navroz dinner debate, according to Hamdast: as attendance fell, the community debated inviting Parsis "who have converted to other religions"; after the majority agreed, "those who were against the idea were dropped" (p. 141, folio not stated).
- Navroz games formerly held (fancy dress, musical chairs, 'Pass the Pillow', a makeup race for married couples), now only Tambola (p. 143, folio not stated).
- Baha'i institutions: Local Spiritual Assembly (LSA), "the local administrative body"; National Spiritual Assembly (NSA), "with its office in Islamabad"; Universal House of Justice, governing body "world over"; nine members each; an LSA is to be set up wherever "there are nine or more members" (p. 147, p. 148, p. 149, folio not stated). The LSA is elective and forms bodies like the Programme Committee and the Educational Committee (p. 149, folio not stated).
- Programme Committee for 2011: Haider Saeed and his father, "the only two members" (p. 147, folio not stated).
- Hazrat Bab's birthday, "20th October 2011": men on the right, women on the left; songs with Spanish guitar; no applause; reading from the Kitab-i-Aqdas; greeting "Allah-u-Abha"; "Baha'i functions do not follow a fixed agenda"; closing feast in the garage of "chicken qorma, roti and salad"; Bahaullah's birthday to follow "a few days later" (pp. 147–155, folio not stated).
- Symbols: "the ring stone symbol", designed by Abdul Baha, and the "nine sided star" (p. 149, folio not stated).
- Board in the Baha'i centre's garage: "'It is forbidden for Baha'is to take part in politics'"; statements about the community need LSA approval (p. 151, folio not stated).
- Study circles headed by Haytham Zarin, open to non-Baha'is; "Most of the children come from Muslim backgrounds"; report given "Every month at the monthly festival held at the Baha'i centre" (p. 154, folio not stated).
- Iranian Baha'i refugees after "1979": helped by the local community; "Various LSAs gathered money" for their welfare; most have since migrated (p. 153, folio not stated).
- Panja Sahib security, school hostels and Baisakhi arrangements: see the Panja Sahib bullet (pp. 157–158, folio not stated).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- As the book narrates it: at Hassan Abdal Bhai Mardana was thirsty; Nanak, "possessing special powers", knew of "a Wali, a Saint sitting on top of the mountain" with a fresh pool; Wali Qandhari at first agreed but, told Mardana was a companion of another Saint, "feeling rather insecure refused him the water" and mocked Nanak; after three refusals (the third "in the name of God"), "Nanak moved a stone from the ground close to where they were seated and miraculously water started to gush out" (p. 159, folio not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- The author: "nothing is more political in Pakistan than religion. The creation of the country was based on religious identity" (p. 151, folio not stated).
- The author on Behzadi: "Like a typical convert, he is much more scrupulous about the new religion and its laws compared to its older believers" (p. 151, folio not stated).
- The author: "It is also believed that in the recent years a lot of Ahmadiyyas have adopted the Baha'i faith to avoid social discrimination. However, that is an inconvenient 'truth' that neither of the communities is willing to admit" (p. 152, folio not stated).
- The author: "the Baha'is in general have been spared the religious persecution in Pakistan unlike most of the other minorities" (p. 153, folio not stated).
- The author: the Baha'i religion's "philosophy reflects the sensibilities of its era" (p. 149, p. 150, folio not stated).
- The author on Pakistani law: the Ahmadiyya community "was declared non-Muslim in Pakistan after the National Assembly passed a law in 1974. In 1984, further 'Ahmadiyya' laws were adopted into the constitution" (p. 151, p. 152, folio not stated).
- The author: the Pathan Sikhs "are more alien to this place than the Indian Punjabi Sikhs who are more or less at home when they visit" (p. 158, folio not stated).
