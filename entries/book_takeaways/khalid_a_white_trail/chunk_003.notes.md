## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **katas-raj-temples** (the book's "Katas Raj or Killa Katas" / "Katas"):
  - The complex has "a natural pond, fossils dating back to millions of years, ancient caves, an unexcavated Buddhist stupa, Hindu temples said to be thousands of years old, and a university which attracted scholars from other parts of the world"; "Situated near the small town of Kalar Kahar"; "a holy site for thousands of years" (p. 45, folio not stated).
  - Guru Nanak "(1469-1539) also came here, as it was a popular destination for ascetics at that time", with footnote 3: "Pg 174, Historical Sikh Shrines by Iqbal Qaiser" (p. 45, folio not stated; footnote on p. 53, folio not stated).
  - "several small temples built around the pond, dedicated to the different Hindu deities"; "an iron fence protects the complex" with "only one entrance", though one can enter by "climbing the mound" (p. 45, folio not stated).
  - Across the road, next to the mound "where the university was situated", a "newly constructed single storey building" built "a few years ago" now "acts as the Archaeology Department office" (p. 45, folio not stated).
  - "Major renovation work of the temple was undertaken by the Department in 2005": the pond cleaned, buildings "painted and restored", "blue boards were raised" highlighting particular spots (p. 45, folio not stated).
  - "Katas Raj was a popular religious site for the Hindus before partition"; "Thousands of pilgrims used to descend here from faraway places on the occasion of Shivratri" (p. 45, folio not stated).
  - "This temple was abandoned as a result of partition", buildings "falling into disrepair and the pond being polluted"; "a few cement factories opened in the surrounding area and started sucking the water out from here" (pp. 45–46, folio not stated).
  - No one guarded the shrine; visitors "leave their marks on the temple walls. Several phone numbers and names are still engraved on them"; a few Hindus visited "pretending to be tourists" (p. 46, folio not stated).
  - According to Prem Gupta, who first visited in 2003: "There were boys jumping from the windows and alleys of temples into this sacred pond. They would leave juice boxes and chips wrappers in the water" (p. 46, folio not stated).
  - "Things changed for the shrine in 2005, when the veteran Indian politician L.K Advani came here and expressed displeasure"; the Government of Pakistan "started renovation work"; "In the subsequent year" it invited Indian pilgrims for Shivratri; "About three hundred pilgrims came, which became national news" (p. 46, folio not stated).
  - Indian pilgrims stayed "for three days", with government security and shelter, residing "at the building at the site of the ancient university" with "free food"; the area was "cordoned off"; "This continued until 2008"; after the Mumbai attacks ("between the 26th and 29th of November 2008") "Pilgrims didn't come to Katas for Shivratri in 2009" (p. 46, folio not stated).
  - In 2010 Hindu organizations wrote to the Archaeology Department, ETPB, Police and Ministry of Minorities for permission and "did not receive any response"; "About two thousand Hindus came to Katas from various cities of Pakistan unannounced, including Lahore, Rahim Yar Khan, Peshawar and Nowshera"; they decided to celebrate again in 2011 (pp. 46–47, folio not stated).
  - The 2011 celebration: "The date is 2nd March 2011"; Gupta's banner reads "'Welcome Yatrees (pilgrims)'" with a picture of Lord Shiva; a canopy for pilgrims inside the complex (p. 47, folio not stated).
  - Only "five officials" of police at the gate; "The number of officials deployed is an indication of the scant importance that Hindu pilgrims are accorded" (author's judgement) (p. 47, folio not stated).
  - An NCA professor and students are stopped: "Muslims are not allowed inside today," says a police official; Hindu boys are let in with a "Maha Shivratri" identification tag (pp. 47–48, folio not stated).
  - There is "a small building, the only restaurant in the area"; "Just a few years ago, this place would have refused to serve these people"; "untouchability, at least here, has receded"; it has "the only washroom the pilgrims can use", closing "at eleven in the night" (pp. 48–49, folio not stated).
  - Pilgrims from Peshawar arrive "in two large buses", about a hundred people, led by Haroon Sarab Diyal; more buses from Nowshera, Rahim Yaar Khan and Bahawalpur (p. 49, folio not stated).
  - "There is no electricity"; according to Diyal, "The authorities have not paid the bill of this temple for the past three months. Electricity has been cut off therefore"; "Ever since the Indians have stopped coming, they have become negligent again. The temple is falling into disrepair" (pp. 49–50, folio not stated).
  - "There are about a thousand people" once all buses arrive; a DSP at first refuses to open the department hall, citing "the security conditions of the country", then opens one hall for women, children and the elderly (pp. 50–51, folio not stated).
  - The shrine of Lord Shiva is "situated next to the pool": "a small light brown building, with a small dome at the top", "a wooden door, covered with floral decorations", a marble floor, and at the centre "the Shivling"; "a small room, which can only accommodate about ten people" (p. 52, folio not stated).
  - Next morning Archaeology Department officials "find their offices invaded and the temples full of sleeping pilgrims"; junior officials throw luggage and Langar utensils and rice onto the road (pp. 52–53, folio not stated).
  - Pilgrims pour pond water on their heads as "a substitute to performing a ritual ablution or ashnan" because "It is too cold to take a bath" (p. 53, folio not stated).

## Province-level material

- PROVINCE khyber pakhtunkhwa (book's word: "the province of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KPK)"; refers to: "since partition" to the present, c. 2011, as the book states):
  - "since partition, the province of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KPK) has been home to a large proportion of religious minorities who have lived there rather peacefully. These are primarily Hindus, who form the largest minority" (p. 49, folio not stated).
  - "Thousands of them are part of the main stream society, visible often in the economic and political fabric"; "Hindu festivals are celebrated with much pomp in these areas" (p. 49, folio not stated).
  - The reason given: "unlike Punjab the riots were less intense due to the influence of the Indian National Congress there. A lot of Hindus and Sikhs continued living in their ancestral lands even after the creation of Pakistan" (partition period) (p. 49, folio not stated).
  - According to Gupta, four marriages at Shivratri 2011 were of "girls from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and FATA, who suffered during the floods of 2010" (p. 51, folio not stated).

- PROVINCE punjab (book's word: "Punjab" / "Pakistani Punjab"; refers to: several periods, each marked below as the book states):
  - Present (c. 2011): minorities visible in society "which is hardly the case in Punjab, which is much more monolithic" (p. 49, folio not stated).
  - Partition: "unlike Punjab the riots were less intense" in KPK (p. 49, folio not stated); "Partition in 1947 divided Punjab and resulted in the migration of Hindus and Sikhs from the area" (p. 56, folio not stated).
  - Present (c. 2011): "the Governor of Punjab Salman Taseer was gunned down" (p. 48, folio not stated).
  - Pre-partition: special trains came to Khalu Khara "carrying pilgrims from Amritsar, Ferozepur and other parts of Punjab" (p. 56, folio not stated).
  - Nineteenth century: the British "arrival, which in Punjab was in the middle of the nineteenth century"; "the nineteenth century, which is also known as the Sikh period" (p. 57, folio not stated).
  - Post-partition to present: refugees took over and converted into households "the hundreds of temples and Gurdwaras all over Pakistani Punjab" (p. 58, folio not stated).

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Ram Thamman shrine / samadhi of Baba Ram Thamman** (village "on the outskirts of Kasur"): Baba Ram Thamman "was a Hindu Saint", from "the neighbouring village of Khalu Khara", who settled "at this spot, where his samadhi (final resting place) was eventually built"; according to the Encyclopedia, a shrine was built after his death which "eventually expanded into a twenty-two acre complex" (pp. 55–56, folio not stated). The samadhi is "a single storey white structure", "a cone like construction emerging from the middle of the houses"; inside, a marble lump "covered with an orange cloth" under a canopy, an engraved picture of an old man with a turban and stick, "probably in memory of a generous devotee" (author's guess), and a bell at the threshold (pp. 58–59, folio not stated). An adjacent building has "a temple dedicated to the Hindu deities, Kali and Shiva", now home to Ghulam Hussain's family (pp. 56, 59, folio not stated). A tank "about eight acres in size", once fed and drained from "river Sutlej, a few kilometres from here", now blocked and polluted (p. 59, folio not stated). Old haveli facades from "the nineteenth century, which is also known as the Sikh period", subdivided by migrant families ("seven houses within this one haveli") (pp. 57–58, folio not stated).
- **Khalu Khara**: "now an abandoned station" on the road connecting Ferozepur Road to Raiwind; in pre-partition days it came "to life in April, at the time of Baisakhi" (p. 56, folio not stated).
- **Durga shrine at Bahawal Nagar**: named in comparison — pilgrims carrying flags and chunris "like the pilgrims visiting the Durga shrine at Bahawal Nagar"; Hussain's head cloth is "similar in texture to what the Hindu pilgrims at Bahawal Nagar wore" (pp. 56, 58, folio not stated).
- **Al-Beruni, *Al-Hind***: "considered to be one of the best anthropological works of all times"; he "claims that the Hindus are believers of one God" and "'Ahl-e-Kitab'" (p. 45, folio not stated).
- **Rahim Yar Khan** (also "Rahim Yaar Khan"): Hindu pilgrims came to Katas from there (pp. 47, 49, folio not stated).
- **Kalar Kahar**: "the small town" near Katas Raj (p. 45, folio not stated).
- **Amritsar, Ferozepur / Ferozepur District "(now part of India)"**: pre-partition pilgrims to Ram Thamman; Hussain's and Aslam's families migrated from Ferozepur district (pp. 56, 58, folio not stated).
- **Shahbaz Bhatti**: "a Roman Catholic", "the Federal Minister for Minorities when he was assassinated", critic of the Blasphemy Laws (p. 48, folio not stated).
- **Informants**: Prem Gupta (40, Lahore, Hindu Sudhar Sabha, NGO worker; father "Shami Lal"; son Vinod, 18, at Forman Christian College) (pp. 46–48); Haroon Sarab Diyal (42, "President of the All Pakistan Hindu Rights Movement", Masters in Islamic studies) (pp. 49–50); Shakeel (a guide at Katas, bare name) (p. 47); Ghulam Hussain (85) and son Abid Hussain (57) (pp. 56, 58–59); Aslam (43, farmer) (p. 57); Abdul Razzak (40, bangle vendor) (p. 61); Muneer Ahmad (stunt rider) (p. 54) — all folio not stated.

## Practices, institutions, economy

- ETPB: "Evacuee Trust Property Board (ETPB, – an organization that looks after the property of non-Muslim shrines. It is also referred to as Hindu Awqaf or Wakf)" (p. 46, folio not stated).
- Katas Shivratri 2011, according to Gupta: "After Langar women would take the mehndi of Shiv to the temple, as sisters and sister-in-laws do so for the grooms" (p. 51, folio not stated); "we have organized four other marriages here", funded "by the contributions of the Sangat (the Hindu community present)" (p. 51, folio not stated).
- Katas Langar: "Lentils would be cooked for dinner along with tea"; "Dinner is served at eleven in the night" on "Two white plastic sheets"; for some it starts a fast broken "at noon tomorrow" (p. 51, folio not stated).
- Katas procession: mehndi plates "containing oil lamps and lumps of henna"; a shawl held by four women with "no one standing underneath it"; elder women place mehndi "at the base of the deity and perform the aarti"; Diyal "pours [milk] onto the Shivling. This is an offering to the God by the devotees"; prayer "marks the conclusion of the ceremony" (pp. 51–52, folio not stated).
- Katas bhajans on a sound system; a band from Peshawar playing "popular Indian songs" (p. 51, folio not stated); stalls selling "small idols, pictures of deities, CDs of bhajan recordings, toys for children, bindiyas and mangal sutras" (pp. 52–53, folio not stated).
- Ram Thamman attendance: "According to Police Intelligence Reports in the archives of the Punjab Secretariat at Lahore, about thirty-five thousand people attended this festival in the year, 1946. This was also the last time Baisakhi was celebrated on such a scale" (p. 56, folio not stated); today "attracting thousands of people" (p. 56); "According to the locals, more than a thousand people come here during these three days" (p. 60, folio not stated).
- Ram Thamman calendar: the festival "begins on the 13th of April, which corresponds with the first of Baisakhi according to the local calendar"; "The third and the last day is reserved for women only" (p. 60, folio not stated); the author's visit "The date is 14th April 2011" (p. 56, folio not stated).
- Ram Thamman custodianship: kept alive after 1947 "by the Muslims"; Ghulam Hussain's "grandfather, a Muslim, was a Sadhu (hermit) here"; the family "locked up the main room, which contains the samadhi" to keep migrants out (p. 56, folio not stated); "The shrine remains locked all year round and is only opened up a few days before Baisakhi"; Hussain sweeps and "changes the chadar at the samadhi"; "Sikhs and Hindus come occasionally" (p. 58, folio not stated); Abid Hussain "looks after the shrine now" (pp. 58–59, folio not stated).
- Ram Thamman fair: "Maut ka kuan" ('Well of Death') motorbike act; "About a dozen eunuchs, or Khwaja Saras" dancing (pp. 54–55, folio not stated); stalls "spread out over an area of nearly two kilometres"; Babas selling rings and lockets ("Ya-Ali", "Love", "Friendship"); food, rides (pp. 60, folio not stated).
- Abdul Razzak has come for "the past twenty-five years" and is "the third generation from his family" selling bangles there; "In the earlier days we used to come and stay here for an entire week"; earlier "Famous wrestlers" and "Popular folk theatre companies" performing "Heer-Ranjha, Sohni-Mahiwal and Raja Rasalu" (p. 61, folio not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- By calling Hindus followers of the book, "Al-Beruni raises their status"; "in contemporary Pakistan, where nationalism is premised upon hatred for Hindus, such a claim would not only be shunned but also taken offence to" (p. 45, folio not stated).
- The Ram Thamman shrine and festival "serve to dispute stereotypes about rural Pakistan being plagued by intolerance and backwardness"; its Muslim villagers continue a tradition "which some would argue is an 'un-Islamic practice'" (p. 55, folio not stated).
- Bibi Pak Daman as part of "the religious syncretism that was once part of this land but is now vanishing" (p. 48, folio not stated).
- Educated non-Muslims learn Islam to "avoid the persecution that interpretation of Islamic laws brings" (p. 50, folio not stated).
- Without Hussain's family "this would have also been taken over by the refugees and converted into a household, like the hundreds of temples and Gurdwaras all over Pakistani Punjab" (p. 58, folio not stated).
- Dating by brick size: the bricks "are smaller than the ones preferred by the British", so the structures predate British arrival (p. 57, folio not stated).
