# khalid_a_white_trail — chunk 012 — pp. 215–232

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **gurudwara-janam-asthan-nankana-sahib** (the book's "Gurdwara Janamasthan"; also "the shrine of Guru Nanak"):
  - Sikh New Year night (chapter "Sikh New Year at Nankana Sahib"): the six Muslim qawwals' performance is placed "at Gurdwara Janamasthan"; the author says it "doesn't promise to revive" the Muslim kirtan tradition "but is still a tribute to the years of interaction between the two communities at Sikh Gurdwaras" (p. 218, folio not stated).
  - "Gurdwara Janamasthan is situated where he was born" (p. 222, folio not stated).
  - Guru Nanak's birthday festival (chapter "GURU NANAK'S BIRTHDAY AT NANKANA SAHIB"), as seen by Nadeem returning "after seven years": the main gate "was earlier made of iron bars, which permitted one to witness the activities inside. Now it is one big closed structure barring any view"; the two smaller flanking gates are closed; "The wall has been raised and protected by a barb wire on the top"; police "with guns stand at the gate, on top of walls and all the other higher structures at the complex"; a new side entrance has been built at some distance from the main gate (p. 220, folio not stated).
  - Two queues, one for women and one for men; women are frisked by female police officers in a small enclosure; men are checked in the open and pass "two other security checks" before entering the courtyard (p. 220, folio not stated).
  - A police official checks Nadeem's national identity card and turns him away: "No Muslims allowed"; Nadeem says "But I came a few years ago" and gets no reply (pp. 220–221, folio not stated).
  - According to a shopkeeper, the Nagar Kirtan round the city "hasn't happened for two years now. There is a security threat. They will take out a small procession within the precincts of the Gurdwara, where it is safe" (p. 221, folio not stated).
  - A Geo van and other media cars stand at the gate, broadcasting the proceedings live by satellite (p. 221, folio not stated).
  - Nadeem, barefoot on the road before the main gate, raises his hand "in the direction of the shrine": "I will just say hello to the Baba from here then. Good bye, Baba Ji." (p. 221, folio not stated).
  - Gurdwara Tambu Sahib is "a few hundred metres away from Gurdwara Janamasthan" and is the "second most popular destination after Janamasthan" (p. 223, folio not stated); the Railway station "is opposite the Gurdwara Janamasthan" (p. 227, folio not stated).
  - "The main focus of the pilgrims is Gurdwara Janamasthan"; more police and security inside the complex than at the entrance (p. 227, folio not stated); "Everyone has been allotted a pass, even representatives from the media"; "several blocks of hostels here, constructed during various eras" house most pilgrims (p. 228, folio not stated).
  - A separate building of communal washrooms stands left of the main shrine, with stalls for laundry, public telephones, juices and eatables (p. 228, folio not stated).
  - "Only the pool at this Gurdwara is filled with water out of all the Gurdwaras of the city"; children and a few men bathe in cold weather; the pool deepens at the centre as the stairs descend; police are posted at its entrance (p. 228, folio not stated).
  - A web of lights covers the façade of the main shrine; a banner at the main entrance shows Guru Nanak on one side and Guru Gobind Singh on the other, with all the other Gurus between (p. 228, folio not stated).
  - A blue bus decorated with yellow flowers "would take a round around the green belt at the centre of the complex during the procession called Nagar Kirtan"; tents on both sides of the shrine are for traders "from other parts of the country" (p. 228, folio not stated).
  - Meena Kumari, 54, sells eatables on a sheet under "Block 1, a residential hostel"; she says her husband has set up eatable stalls at the festival for years and that this year she bought goods "in bulk from Mardan" (pp. 228–229, folio not stated).
  - The offices of the Gurdwara administration face her stall; further on are T-shirt stalls: "Never Forget 1984," "Singh is King," "Raj Karega Khalsa" (p. 229, folio not stated).
  - "the newly created Langar Hall": food served "around the clock"; the floor wet with "water, food, tea, and mud"; "Most of the volunteers are local Pathan Sikhs"; police patrol it (p. 229, folio not stated).
  - The Langar Hall is the last building before the boundary wall, "beyond which lies the Guru Nanak Government College"; a wall with barbwire and "a large brown gate, which is a recent addition" — only officials may pass it (p. 229, folio not stated).
  - Volunteers "performing sewa" wash utensils in two large tubs behind the Langar Hall; a locked room beside the washing area "stores raw material for Langar" (pp. 229–230, folio not stated).
  - Two entrances to the courtyard of the main shrine (front and right side, facing the Langar Hall); the courtyard is covered with tents and carpets where pilgrims sleep; "The majority of these pilgrims are locals from Khyber Pakhthunkhwa and Sindh, however there are a few Indian families as well" (p. 230, folio not stated).
  - Kirtan from a stage next to the shrine by three men in white shalwar-kameez (one on tabla, two on harmonium) who "appear to be Muslims"; a screen shows the proceedings, broadcast live through the internet "and viewed by millions of people world over. This is the first time it is being done from here." (p. 230, folio not stated).
  - Darshan: a queue behind "an orange robe" controlled by a local Sikh; devotees prostrate ("prostate", as printed) before the Guru Granth Sahib, go clockwise, enter by a small door on the left, fan the fly-whisk over the Book, exit by a door on the right, and receive "sujji ka halwa" as Prasad (p. 230, folio not stated).
  - Nagar Kirtan after the Akhand Path readings: a decorated pickup with a dhol-wala distributes juice packets, causing a rush (p. 231, folio not stated); ten Sikh men in "two lines of five each" lead — the front row in white cholas and saffron turbans holding the Nishan Sahib, the row behind in saffron cholas and blue turbans holding kirpans (pp. 231–232, folio not stated); the bus carries the Guru Granth Sahib in "a golden palanquin"; devotees on the main gate throw red rose petals; the bus halting at its starting point "concludes the Nagar Kirtan, marking an end to the religious rituals of the festival" (p. 232, folio not stated).

- **gurdwara-tambo-sahib** (the book's "Gurdwara Tambu Sahib"):
  - Named as the place "where he hid after returning from the incident that came to be known as Sacha Sauda" (p. 222, folio not stated).
  - "Most of the pilgrims from the developed countries stay at Gurdwara Tambu Sahib"; "Three hostel blocks have been constructed here with the help of donations from pilgrims coming from Britain, Canada and America"; "A large amount has been donated by Kar Seva, an England based non-profit organization" (p. 223, folio not stated).
  - Security is "lax" compared to Janamasthan: metal detectors and a few guards, but "fairly easy to walk in unnoticed" (p. 223, folio not stated).
  - "The area belonging to Gurdwara Tambu Sahib is the second largest in the city, making it the second most popular destination after Janamasthan" (p. 223, folio not stated).
  - "The shrine, which is a small yellow structure, stands in the middle of the ground with a white dome on the top"; the ground is "big enough to host an international cricket match"; a newly built triple-storey residential hall by the entrance, two larger residential buildings from devotees' donations, and "a single storey Langar Hall" (p. 223, folio not stated).
  - "In the future, the Government of Pakistan plans to construct a Guru Nanak University in the vicinity of this complex", a "persistent demand of the expat Sikh community" (pp. 223–224, folio not stated).
  - A book stall of the "Dyal Singh Memorial Trust, a pre-partition trust based in Lahore" stands in the parking area by the Langar Hall; there Iqbal Qaiser, 58, sells his "Historical Sikh Shrines in Pakistan": "This is the only time in the year my book sells … somewhere between twenty to thirty copies" (p. 224, folio not stated).
  - A yellow board on the Langar Hall: "This newly constructed building has been made by Kar Seva"; police stand on the Langar Hall and the residential roofs (p. 224, folio not stated).
  - "Shaheed Hall, next to the Langar Hall", also built from Kar Seva donations, "Recently completed", the "first choice of pilgrims from developed countries", now housing many Indian pilgrims as the former have reduced (pp. 224–225, folio not stated); a "five storey building" whose roof is closed to pilgrims (p. 225, folio not stated).
  - On the roof, police constable Arshad Malik, 35, on a twelve-hour duty since eight in the morning, with the police for "eighteen years", says "the entire police force of the city has been deployed at the Gurdwaras for these three days" (p. 225, folio not stated); he will not eat from the Langar: "We are Muslims. We don't eat with the Sikhs or Hindus" (pp. 225–226, folio not stated).
  - At the Janamasthan Langar Hall, "Like Gurdwara Tambu Sahib, food is served here around the clock" (p. 229, folio not stated).

- **gurdwara-panjvi-chati-patshahi** (the book's "Gurdwara Panjvin and Chevin Patshahi", "the Gurdwara of the fifth and sixth Guru"):
  - About two hundred metres from Gurdwara Tambu Sahib towards the Railway station, marked by "a brown board on the left" (p. 227, folio not stated).
  - "This Gurdwara is the only one in the city, which is not in memory of Guru Nanak" (p. 227, folio not stated).
  - Story: Guru Arjan and his son Guru Hargobind, on pilgrimage to Nankana Sahib, left their horses outside the city, tied them "at this particular spot to a Waan tree", and walked "about seven hundred metres on foot" to the shrine (Gurdwara Janamasthan) "as a mark of respect" (p. 227, folio not stated).
  - "a fairly small Gurdwara": the main shrine a double-storey building "in the traditional mango yellow colour"; a Nishan Sahib beside it, where an old Sikh writes his name on the platform; a single-storey block of rooms allotted to pilgrims; a Thara Sahib holding the Guru Granth Sahib with a fly-whisk; pilgrims prostrate, go clockwise and fan the Book (p. 227, folio not stated).
  - A small room above the shrine, "also regarded as sacred", holds a glass showcase "containing a piece of the Waan tree that was used by the Gurus to tie their horses"; devotees touch the glass for blessings (p. 227, folio not stated).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- Calendar dispute: Sikhs used the "Bikrami calendar", whose festival dates shifted every year on the "Georgian calendar" (as printed) (p. 216, folio not stated); Pal Singh Purewal, "a Canadian Sikh", made a solar calendar, presented it "in 1998"; "In 2003 … it was implemented by the SGPC" (pp. 216–217, folio not stated); "It is believed that under the pressure of the Indian government, in 2010 the SGPC approved amendments", which Purewal rejected; Pakistani Sikhs like Kartar call the move a "sellout" (p. 217, folio not stated).
- According to Kartar, "The Indian government has banned this event all over India"; it is celebrated "in places like England, America and Canada" (p. 217, folio not stated).
- Head covering and removing shoes are compulsory to enter a Gurdwara; some walk barefoot through the whole complex or the streets (pp. 221–222, folio not stated).
- Indian pilgrims' visas are limited to particular cities; pilgrims from Britain, America and Canada travel freely (p. 223, folio not stated); the fall in Western pilgrims "takes its toll on the religious tourism industry" (p. 224, folio not stated).
- According to Iqbal Qaiser, his book is bought mostly by British or Canadian pilgrims; "The Indian pilgrims, like us, are poor and not very keen on reading" (p. 224, folio not stated).
- Refusal to share food and utensils with Sikhs by the constable Malik and the restaurateur Aslam Chaudary (pp. 225–226, folio not stated).
- Langar volunteers "performing sewa"; local Pathan Sikhs as volunteers (p. 229, folio not stated).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- Guru Arjan and Guru Hargobind tied their horses to a Waan tree and walked to Janamasthan; a piece of that tree is kept at the Gurdwara of the fifth and sixth Guru (p. 227, folio not stated).
- Nankana sites as the book lists them: Nanak's schooling (Patti), childhood play (Balila), the snake (Malji), cattle destroying a farmer's fields while he slept (Kiara), hiding after the Sacha Sauda incident (Tambu) (p. 222, folio not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- The author: by the tenth Guru the movement became institutionalized, "a distinct community, neither Muslim nor Hindu" (p. 220, folio not stated); the Janamasthan banner "shows the transition of the religion from a spiritual reformation group to an institutionalized religion" (p. 228, folio not stated).
- The author: "Years of state propaganda after the creation of Pakistan has created a sharp division between different religions, which were non-extant or at least fluid prior to partition" (p. 225, folio not stated).
- The author on the qawwali: "If that is true, then this performance has immense symbolic value" (p. 218, folio not stated).
- Iqbal Qaiser, "An expert on Sikh religion": "anyone who is yearning to learn is a Sikh. 'I can be a Muslim and also be a Sikh if defined this way.'" (p. 219, folio not stated).
- The author's claim that qawwali "was introduced by" the Chishtiyas, a sect that "promotes tolerance among members of different religious communities" (p. 218, folio not stated).

- Sikh New Year (pp. 215–218): the gurdwara of the pavilion, Granth reading and Kartar's speech is not named on those pages; p. 218 places the qawwali "at Gurdwara Janamasthan". - Person-level, not site-specific; weigh accordingly: Guru Nanak (18 rows), Bhai Mardana (gurdwara-rori-sahib), Guru Arjan (3 rows), Guru Hargobind (6 rows), Guru Ram Das (1), Guru Gobind Singh (gurdwara-dash-mesh-pita; the p. 228 banner is a picture), Baba Fareed (shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar).
