# The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual — takeaways

*Source: Shemeem Burney Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual* (Austin: University of Texas Press,
2002; Modern Middle East Series, published with the Center for Middle Eastern Studies, University of
Texas at Austin). 240 pages, transcribed `text_layer`; chunked into 11 chunk-note files; provenance in
`out/ocr/abbas_female_voice_sufi_ritual/`. Page references are PDF page indices; **this book's text
layer prints no folio anywhere**, so every reference below reads `(p. N, folio not stated)`.
Reviewed: no.*

## What this book is

A linguistic-anthropological ethnography of devotional Sufi performance in Pakistan and India, built
on the author's own field recordings and on interviews with the musicians who sing at shrines, at
melās and in concert. Abbas writes as an insider — "I bring my own understandings and experiences of
the culture that I portray" (p. 19, folio not stated) — and her stated discipline is "the ethnography
of speaking" plus conversation analysis, not ethnomusicology (p. 19, folio not stated). The thesis is
that Sufi poetry in the subcontinent is overwhelmingly sung in a female voice, by women and by men
alike, and that because "women were not allowed into the male domains of the mosque, the Sufi shrines
became centers for their devotional activities" (p. 162, folio not stated). Elizabeth Warnock Fernea's
Foreword (pp. 14–16, folios not stated) is a separate argument and must not be merged with Abbas's.

**What it is not.** It is not a study of shrines. The index's single longest entry is
`sociolinguistics`, running two columns, while the whole indexed treatment of shrines as institutions
is the headword `Sufi shrines` with two sub-entries, `dargāhs` and `khanqāhs` (pp. 238–240, folios not
stated). Shrine material arrives incidentally, through performers' lives and through where they sing.

**Reliability — the one defect that governs every use of this book.** The `text_layer` is clean for
English prose and keeps every transliteration diacritic intact, but it **silently deletes Arabic
numerals set in the book's body and display face**, leaving a gap inside surviving punctuation. So
`(d.  )`, `(– )`, `(b. )`, `(Sura :)`, `close to . billion` and `Rs ,` are all empty. **Not one date
in this book survives**: every death year, every fieldwork year, every publication year in a

### bhit-bhit-shah — Bhit (Bhit Shah)

The book's most heavily documented archive figure, present in all eleven chunks. The book's names for
him: "Shah Abdul Latif", "Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit", "Shah Latif", "Sain Latif", "Shah"; for the site,
"Bhit Shah", "Shah Abdul Latif's shrine in Bhit Shah", "the jamālī (aesthetic) shrine".

- **Saint**
  - Placed only by name and work. The book prints `Shah Abdul Latif (d.  )` [text-layer?: digits
    dropped] (p. 50, folio not stated) — **the death year is printed on the page and destroyed in the
    text layer; 1752 has NOT been taken from the archive row.** No birth date, no silsila and no
    custodian appear anywhere in the eleven chunks: all **(not stated)**.
  - Descent: "both Muslim and non-Muslim devotees venerate Shah Abdul Latif's shrine because he was a
    Syed, who claimed descent from the prophet Muhammad through his daughter Fatima and son-in-law and
    cousin Ali ibn Abi Talib" (p. 50, folio not stated).
  - Influence: "he was influenced by Rumi" — Alan Faqir (p. 157, folio not stated).
  - He is filed by the book's own index under `Suﬁ musicians, poets and scholars`, not under `Suﬁ
    Saints belonging to Pakistan-Indian silsilās` (p. 239, folio not stated).
- **Shrine / Darbar**
  - "Shah Abdul Latif at Bhit Shah" is one of five shrines where "qawwālī is performed every evening
    after the prayers", with performances that "continue well past midnight" (p. 42, folio not stated).
  - **The daily ritual, stated as continuous since the shrine was built.** "Even today, his faqīrs
    sing his traditional poetry at the shrine, in the melodies that he composed. They sing his poetry
    in the falsetto, imitating a woman's voice. The ritual of singing Shah's poetry to his melodies
    has been performed every evening since the shrine has been there" (p. 96, folio not stated); and
    "even today when his faqīrs (mendicants) sing the daily mystical concert at his shrine, they chant
    in the falsetto that imitates the female voice" (p. 50, folio not stated). The glossary restates
    it: "Shah's faqīrs at his shrine in Bhit Shah are renowned for singing the vāī in the falsetto"
    (p. 189, folio not stated).
  - **Why the shrine carries its epithet.** "It is precisely for this reason that his shrine is known
    as the jamālī (aesthetic) shrine" (p. 50, folio not stated) — the reason being the falsetto
    female-voice chanting. Restated at p. 167 (folio not stated): "Shah's shrine is known in the region
    as a jamālī (aesthetic) shrine". An endnote gives the typology: "Jamālī mysticism is attained
    through intuition and creativity, such as by Shah Abdul Latif, a poet and musician. In contrast to
    this, jalālī mysticism is achieved through rigor and discipline" (p. 197, folio not stated).
  - The faqīrs wear black: "Shah's poetry is kind of melancholic—that is why the faqīrs at his shrine
    wear black—it is the same with his followers—his faqīrs sing his poetry like females—creating the
    female voices—his poetry is sung throughout the night at his shrine—there are maqāms in his poetry
    when certain sections are sung at particular times of the night—his poetry is sung on the tambūrā
    [stringed instrument]—it is sung in rhythm" — Sikander Baloch (p. 63, folio not stated).
  - Invited singers: "In the Sind, performers like Abida Parvin are invited to sing at Shah Abdul
    Latif's shrine" (p. 55, folio not stated).
  - **A woman is caretaker of a subsidiary set of graves.** "A woman was the caretaker of the tombs of
    Shah Abdul Latif's female relatives who were buried in a compound of the shrine" (p. 20, folio not
    stated). **She is not named and the female relatives are not named: (not stated).**
  - **Negative evidence, stated as such.** The loṭevālī roving-minstrel tradition does not occur here:
    "I have not seen such a tradition at Bhit Shah" — Naseer Mirza (p. 61, folio not stated).
  - Non-Muslim devotees, with the book's own two reasons. "Their women are ardent devotees at Muslim
    shrines such as those of Shah Abdul Latif at Bhit Shah. They go there primarily because the shrine
    is located in Hala, the heart of a fertile agricultural belt, where they can make a living working
    on the large feudal farms" (p. 46, folio not stated) — **wage labour first, devotion second, in the
    book's own order**; and "This further explains why the non-Muslim Kolhi and Bhil communities are
    devotees of Shah Abdul Latif's shrine in Bhit Shah, Sind: his poetry is linguistically diverse and
    is tolerant of other beliefs" (p. 50, folio not stated). See Doubts on "located in Hala".
  - **The author's own observation in the courtyard.** "Early in [numeral dropped, text-layer], I
    photographed non-Muslim Kolhi and Bhil peasant women at Shah Abdul Latif's shrine in Bhit Shah. I
    was standing in the shrine courtyard when I suddenly saw a train of graceful, colorfully dressed
    women in their ghāgrās (long skirts). They were all clad in flowered veils. They walked toward the
    shrine exit and hastened to pickups parked by the roadside in which their menfolk waited to drive
    them back" (p. 134, folio not stated). **The year of the visit is destroyed: (not stated).**
  - A plate is captioned "Non-Muslim Kolhi women: Shah Abdul Latif's shrine, Bhit Shah, Sind" (p. 47,
    folio not stated) — **figure number dropped**.
  - A devotee-musician: Alan Faqir "claimed to be a faqīr of the Bhit Shah shrine" (p. 78, folio not
    stated); "He considered himself a faqīr (mendicant) of the Bhit Shah shrine of Shah Abdul Latif"
    (p. 211, folio not stated); "Shah Latif—he is my mentor—he is my murshid" (p. 157, folio not
    stated). Two shrine musicians are listed in the bibliography: "Shah, Syed Ghulam Ali, and Nur
    Muhammad Shah. Bhit Shah musicians. Audiocassette. Hyderabad: Institute of Sindhology." — **this
    entry prints no year at all** (p. 217, folio not stated).
  - **An ʿurs exists but is never dated or described.** It is attached only through a conference
    title: "Proceedings of Shah Abdul Latif Conference on His [numeral dropped, text-layer] Urs
    Celebrations" (p. 219, folio not stated). **The ordinal is destroyed and must not be guessed.**
  - No sajjada nashin, no Auqaf, no endowment, no land, no building date, no visitor number:
    all **(not stated)**.
- **Practices & Events**
  - "Among musician communities in the Sind, Shah's Risālo is treated with great respect because of
    its sacred Quranic context, and the book is not allowed to lie around on the floor" (p. 51, folio
    not stated); "Since large sections of the Quran are quoted in his Risālo, musicians in Sind hold
    Shah's written text in the utmost veneration" (p. 166, folio not stated).
  - "The findings at two major shrines in Sind, that is, Shah Abdul Latif at Bhit Shah and Hazrat Lal
    Shahbaz Qalandar at Sehwan Sharif, point toward significant female input in Sufi rituals" (p. 67,
    folio not stated).
  - Sufiānā-kalām "is a feature of the shrines in Sind, especially those of Hazrat Lal Shahbaz
    Qalandar and Shah Abdul Latif at Bhit" (p. 85, folio not stated).
  - **Fifty overnight camping places, as an informant's claim.** Shaikh Ghulam Hussain: "In the case
    of Shah Latif you will find at least fifty places where he used to spend the nights. Audiences
    would come there and sit there at night—there would be a paṛāo [a camp]—they would make a
    fire—and they would have a mehfil—and when he sang his narratives, there would be faqīrs and
    dervishes with him" (p. 51, folio not stated). **Reported by Abbas as the informant's claim, not
    her own count; "fifty" is spelled out and therefore intact.**
  - Outside the shrine, Manganhar women of Alan Faqir's family "would sing the arifānā-kalām of Shah
    Latif—some songs do not have a poet—you sing songs for the love of God—you sing ''Allāh hū—Allāh
    hū'' and finish off with ''and so says Shah Latif—O Latif, I say your sanā [praise]''" (p. 79,
    folio not stated).
- **The Risālo**
  - "Shah Abdul Latif's entire Risālo is based on these narratives, and he evokes mystical poetry for
    his tragic heroines to become symbols of otherworldly love" (p. 120, folio not stated). "Each one
    of these romances is sung in its own sūr. Thus, there is a ''Sūr Sohni'' in Latif's poetry to sing
    about Sohni, ''Sūr Mumal-Rano'' to sing about Mumal-Rano's tale, and ''Sūr Marvi'' to narrate
    Umar-Marvi's story" (p. 120, folio not stated).
  - **The book's count of the sūrs contradicts itself three ways and all three are kept.** "Altogether,
    there are thirty sūrs in the Risālo, and at least ten are dedicated to female heroines, or sūrmīs"
    (p. 120, folio not stated); the glossary: "It is based on thirty sūrs; **nine** of the sūrs are
    named after Shah's female heroines (sūrmīs)" (p. 187, folio not stated); an endnote: "**Ten** of
    the sūrs in the Risālo are built around female heroines (sūrmis) and thus are called Sūr Sohni,
    Sūr Sassi, Sūr Marvi, Sūr Mumal-Rano, Sūr Leela-Canesar, Sūr Sassi-Maazuri, and Sūr Sassi Desi"
    (p. 198, folio not stated) — **and that list names seven.** All three figures are spelled out in
    words, so **this is the book contradicting itself, not text-layer damage.**
  - An endnote restates the work: "A Sufi epic based on three books. In Sindhi risālo means 'the
    message.' The Risālo narrative is based on thirty sections, each of which is built around a sūr
    that is a form or mode of singing, a rhythm, or melody" (pp. 197–198, folios not stated).
  - Named sūrs in the glossary: `Sūr Maruī/Marvi`, "a melancholic sūr"; `Sūr Mumal-Rano`; `Sūr Rām
    Kalī`, "Early-morning sūr"; `Sūr Sasui/Sassi`; `Sūr Suhni/Sohni`; `Sūr Holī Kamach`, "A sūr named
    after the Holi festival" (pp. 187–188, folios not stated).
  - Heroines: "Shah's sūrmīs (heroines) such as Sassi, Sohni, and Marvi are drawn cross culturally
    from the centuries-old pre-Islamic myths of the region that he blended into the Islamic mystical
    framework. He made his sūrmīs into representations of the pining soul in search of the beloved"
    (p. 47, folio not stated). The gloss: "The male equivalent of the sūrmī is a sūrma, which means a
    hero; someone who has prowess. Therefore, Shah intends his sūrmis to be perceived as women with
    prowess" (p. 120, folio not stated). **The book's own macron is inconsistent — `sūrmīs`/`sūrmī`
    against `sūrmis` in adjacent sentences — and is carried as printed.**
  - Jogīs: "in Shah's Risālo, especially in the section called ''Sūr Rām Kalī,'' there are innumerable
    references to mendicants (faqirs) who are ascetic with slit ears and who wear large rings in their
    ears… They travel from place to place in search of divine wisdom and wear colored robes" (p. 51,
    folio not stated); "Somehow Shah's jogīs are closely interlinked with his female voices, and there
    is hardly a sūr (a melody) in his Risālo where there is not a reference to one or the other"
    (p. 53, folio not stated). The eight Sindhi terms he uses for them, as printed: "jājak, maganhār,
    atāi, pān, chaṛan, rāgī, baraṭ, and rabābī" (p. 53, folio not stated; repeated at p. 183, folio
    not stated).
  - **A transmission claim, given as report.** "Mai Naimat, a maidservant of Shah Abdul Latif, from
    whose memory his entire Risālo is said to have been reconstructed" (p. 21, folio not stated).
    **Abbas gives this as "is said to have been", with no date and no account of the loss it
    repaired: (not stated).**
  - **His editorial principle, in Alan Faqir's words, and it turns on graves.** "Latif put a message
    in his legends—whether it is Marvi or Sassi or Sohni—or Leela-Canesar—whether it is
    Shireen-Farhad—or Rano—he puts them all in his narratives—he does not talk of Hir-Ranjha—of
    Layla-Majnun—because they have no graves—Shah only wrote of that which could be verified"
    (p. 157, folio not stated).
  - A journey: Abida Parvin, "Shah Abdul Latif—he went to Rajasthan—and the person there is a
    Rajasthani—the Shah took very simple words from him… although the individual from this region is
    not literate—the Shah acquired great wisdom from him—from the people" (p. 52, folio not stated).
  - Cotton-spinning songs: "Cross culturally, the same theme is found in Shah Abdul Latif's kapāītī
    (cotton-spinning songs) in his Risālo" (p. 152, folio not stated), four lines quoted: "Not
    withstanding the spinners / Carded the strands well / And spun out very fine yarn / Accumulated
    wealth, O mother" (p. 152, folio not stated).
  - Sung outside Pakistan: "Jairazbhoy and Catlin's recent film of folk performers in Kacch of Indian
    Gujrat demonstrates that Shah Abdul Latif's poetry of the Risālo is sung there in the Sindhi
    language" (p. 155, folio not stated).
- **Significance**
  - He opened Abida Parvin's Islamabad concert: the programme's first item is "Shah Abdul Latif of
    Bhit / Invocation / Sindhi" (p. 101, folio not stated); "she initiated the performance with a
    mystical invocation from Shah Abdul Latif's poetry in Sindhi" (p. 113, folio not stated).
  - He is one of only two poets whose Sindhi only she among the performers can sing: "she is able to

  - A political reading, attributed to the informant: Alan Faqir "told me in his interview that Sassi

    that when he sang Shah's poetry he did it with a passion that would convey the exploitation of the
    land" (p. 196, folio not stated); "These are all Alan Faqir's political interpretations of the
    female myths in Shah Abdul Latif's Risālo" (p. 210, folio not stated).
  - Works on him cited (**every year destroyed**): M. Yakoob Agha, trans., *Shah Jo Risālo alias Ganje
    Latif* (Hyderabad: Shah Abdul Latif Bhit Shah Cultural Center Committee) (pp. 205, 210, 213, 219,

    (reprint, Lahore: Sang-e Meel) (p. 205, folio not stated); Tirathdas Hotechand, "The Risālo: Its
    Musical Compositions" (p. 197, folio not stated); N. A. Baloch, "Shah Abdul Latif: The Founder of
    a New Music Institution", in *Folk Music of Sind*, ed. G. A. Allana (Jamshoro: Institute of
    Sindhology) (p. 197, folio not stated); H. T. Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit* (Delhi: Ashish);

### lal-shahbaz-qalandar — Lal Shahbaz Qalandar

The book's most fully described shrine. Its names for it: "Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar", "the Hazrat
Lal Shahbaz Qalandar shrine at Sehwan Sharif", "the Lal Shahbaz Qalandar shrine in Sehwan", "the
Shahbaz Qalandar shrine", "Qalandar Lal Shahbaz".

- **Saint**
  - "Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar, a Sufi mystic of Sind" (p. 57, folio not stated). **No death date,
    no birth date, no silsila, no lineage and no other name are given anywhere in the eleven chunks:
    all (not stated).** "Usman Marwandi", 1274 and "Qalandari" appear nowhere and must not be imported.
  - "The Sufi saint is known as a jalālī pir, a mystic with intense supernatural energy" (p. 57, folio
    not stated); "Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar's shrine is reputed for the jalālī or intense
    supernatural energy of the Sufi", as against the "jamālī or aesthetic mystics" (p. 96, folio not
    stated); and, from an endnote, "Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar of Sehwan is also known for his jalālī
    or supernatural powers, and his shrine is called a jalālī shrine" (p. 197, folio not stated).
  - He is filed by the index under `Suﬁ Saints belonging to Pakistan-Indian silsilās`, with printed
    locator `xix` and sub-entries `dhammāl`, `faqīrs`, `jalāl`, `jalālī`, `''mast qalandar''
    invocation` and `Sehwan Sharif` — **every Arabic locator destroyed** (p. 239, folio not stated).
- **Shrine / Darbar**
  - Named among the five shrines where "qawwālī is performed every evening after the prayers" (p. 42,
    folio not stated); and sufiānā-kalām "is a feature of the shrines in Sind, especially those of
    Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar and Shah Abdul Latif at Bhit" (p. 85, folio not stated).
  - "Faqīrs (devotees who wear orange robes) perform the ritual dhammāl (dance) every evening at the
    Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar shrine in Sehwan Sharif" (p. 93, folio not stated). "The large drums
    (naubats), which are played for the dhammāl, can be seen lying in the large courtyard of the
    shrine during the day" (p. 93, folio not stated). The glossary confirms the instrument's use here
    by name (p. 186, folio not stated).
  - "The passionate whirling dance of the faqīrs in a circle is said to express the immense jalāl
    (energy) of Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar. His devotees believe that many prayers are answered at
    the shrine and many wishes granted" (pp. 93–94, folios not stated).
  - The comparison the book's informants draw: "Informants at Radio Pakistan in Hyderabad compare the
    dhammāl of the faqīrs at this shrine with that of the whirling dervishes of Maulana Rumi in Konya"
    (p. 93, folio not stated); "The dhammāl that the qalandars or the faqīrs perform at the shrine is
    similar to the dance of the whirling dervishes at Maulana Jalaluddin Rumi's shrine in Konya"
    (p. 64, folio not stated).
  - **Gender arrangement of the dhammāl, and its therapeutic use.** "Both men and women participate in
    the dhammāl. Women perform the dance in a certain section of the courtyard. Many times, families
    will bring women who are emotionally disturbed to participate in the dhammāl, which devotees claim
    has healing powers" (p. 66, folio not stated). **Attributed to devotees, not asserted by Abbas.**
  - "I discovered that one can buy cassettes of healing music for emotionally disturbed persons in the
    bazaar outside the shrine" (p. 66, folio not stated).
  - **A woman holds a service role here — the book's one worked example.** "For instance, at the
    Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar shrine at Sehwan Sharif, the woman faqiriānī or caretaker gives out
    water to the devotees" (p. 160, folio not stated); earlier, "women participated actively in the
    support services at the Lal Shahbaz Qalandar shrine in Sehwan: a female muridīānī gave water to
    the devotees" (p. 20, folio not stated). Abbas frames the act religiously: "In the Islamic
    tradition giving water to the thirsty is a savab (charitable deed). The act of giving water to the
    thirsty is eulogized in the oral Sufi traditions, especially in relation to the Battle of Kerbala"
    (p. 160, folio not stated). **The prose says "faqiriānī", the plate caption for the same service
    says "Murīdīānī"; the book never reconciles them — see Doubts.** Two plates, **figure numbers
    dropped**: "Serving devotees: Murīdīānī at Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar, Sehwan Sharif" (p. 161,
    folio not stated) and "Guluband of Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar, Sehwan Sharif" (p. 164, folio not
    stated).
  - **A relic and the ritual around it.** "Among the rituals that I observed was one in which women
    devotees held up glasses of water to seek ritual blessings from the guluband or heart-shaped
    necklace that belonged to Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar and that hung over his tomb in Sehwan. The
    glasses of water were then shared with their kinswomen and men. To orthodox Muslims this may seem
    to be a fetish, but devotees draw strength from the ritual" (p. 20, folio not stated).
  - **The only Sindhi shrine where the loṭevālī tradition survives**, on Naseer Mirza's testimony:
    "These were gypsy women who only sang at the Shahbaz Qalandar shrine—certainly, that is the only
    place in the Sind now where the loṭevālīs come—to sing there… no loṭevālī passes by these
    shrines—except at Qalandar Lal Shahbaz" (p. 61, folio not stated). Their takings: they "come to

    rupee—the alms they get there" (p. 61, folio not stated).
  - Abbas's guide on her first visit "was a senior librarian at the Institute of Sindhology at Sind
    University in Jamshoro, Hyderabad. Although an orthodox Muslim, he was a devotee of the Sufi
    saint" (p. 20, folio not stated). **He is not named: (not stated).**

  - No custodian is named, no sajjada nashin, no Auqaf, no endowment, no building date, no ʿurs date

- **Practices & Events**
  - **The ʿurs and what it does to the town.** "during the urs celebrations, which last for more than
    a week, it is almost impossible to walk in the narrow streets of the city", reported by "Women
    informants who live around the heavily populated area of the shrine" (p. 56, folio not stated).
  - **The shrine as a labour market.** "Sehwan, the historic city built within a large fortress from
    the time of Alexander the Great, is flooded with large numbers of female musicians who converge on
    the city to earn a living" (pp. 56–57, folios not stated). "Most of these women also sing at the
    Sufi shrines such as Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar in Sehwan, where they make a living. Here, they
    find an audience at any time of the night or day that pays them for their performance" (p. 33,
    folio not stated). At the shrine there are "other women—women who are looking for a livelihood—they
    join in the singing of sufiānā-kalām—and this happens every year" (p. 61, folio not stated).
  - Where the women come from: "most of the women musicians come from the Panjab and the neighborhoods
    of Sind itself. They perform in every nook and cranny and attract huge audiences" (p. 57, folio not
    stated).
  - **A reported disagreement between two sets of informants about when women sing, kept as a
    disagreement.** "Female informants in the city of Sehwan Sharif confirmed that many female
    performers come from the Panjab during the urs of Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar and sing at the
    shrine with the utmost devotion. Their audiences are men as well as women. My female informants did
    not have much information about women singers performing at other times of the year. However,

## Cross-cutting material

### Practices, institutions, economy

#### The ʿurs, the melā and the ritual week

- **The book's definition, given four times and always as a death anniversary.** "women's input into
  the rituals that are performed on a day-to-day basis and during the urs or melā that celebrates the
  death anniversary of the Sufi saint" (p. 23, folio not stated); "Their presence as devotees and
  caretakers is visible during the urs (the death anniversary of the Sufi saint)" (p. 160, folio not
  stated); the glossary, "urs From the word urus (wedding); death anniversary of a Sufi saint" (p. 189,
  folio not stated); and an endnote, "The word urs is derived from urus, which means a wedding. Thus,
  the urs celebrates the Sufi's union with the deity" (p. 193, folio not stated). Restated once more:
  "Their spiritual mentor's death anniversary celebrations, called a melā or urs. The death anniversary
  is celebrated as the anniversary of the saint's union with the deity" (p. 200, folio not stated).
  **The book prints "urs" without ʿayn or diacritic throughout.**
- **THE ʿURS AS A POLITICAL AND ECONOMIC EVENT — the most consequential institutional passage in the
  book, given whole in an endnote.** "Urs celebrations usually go on for several days and can be
  observed at many levels. They are performed as part of popular culture when **the caretakers of the
  shrine make arrangements to initiate the urs**, and the devotees participate in the rituals for
  several days. **At the official level the party in power in the country shows its patronage of the
  event and sets its own time frame within the same urs period to participate in the ritual.** The
  party members, **usually led by a governor or chief minister of the province**, bring wreaths and
  perform rituals. As part of the celebrations **the ruling party in government contracts with renowned
  musicians to sing at the shrines**" (p. 198, folio not stated). **Two parallel ʿurs calendars — the
  caretakers' and the ruling party's — state patronage, provincial office-holders, and paid contracts
  with musicians. No shrine is named and no date is given: (not stated).** Corroborated at p. 55 (folio
  not stated): "In the officially organized urs celebrations in Pakistan commemorating the Sufi's death
  anniversary at his shrine, key musicians are invited to sing."
- **The weekly rhythm.** "Generally, they sing at shrines during the annual urs celebration or on
  Thursday evenings, the eve of the Muslim sabbath on Friday" (p. 33, folio not stated); "As part of
  popular culture it was sung among roving minstrels at the shrines during the urs, the melās (folk
  festivals), and on Thursday evenings" (p. 40, folio not stated); "At the shrines they are performed
  on Thursday evenings in anticipation of the Muslim sabbath and during the celebrations of the death
  anniversary of the saint" (p. 42, folio not stated); "During the urs or on Thursday evenings, or at
  any time of the day or night, devotees flock to the Sufi shrines, to communicate with the mystics
  spiritually. The communication brings relief to their emotional and personal concerns" (p. 93, folio
  not stated). **The reason given for Thursday:** "The faithful in South Asian Islam believe that the
  souls of those who die visit their tombs on Thursday evenings" (p. 195, folio not stated) —
  **attributed to "the faithful", not asserted by the author.**
- **Melā as a female-accessible arena and as a talent market.** Western male scholars "cannot enter the
  culturally close-knit networks among women in the ritual participation at Sufi shrines or at the
  community festivals, called melās, where much activity takes place" (p. 19, folio not stated).
  "producer Saleem Gilani discovered Reshma—and Ustad Muhammad Khan discovered Zeena Bai—that tradition
  has died when we would go to the melās—to hunt for talent—now educated women are there in the field—"
  (p. 62, folio not stated); "we have covered all the melās at the shrines" (p. 61, folio not stated);
  "Female singers like Taj Mastani may be discovered through the radio, instead of at Sufi shrines, as
  were Reshma or Zeena Bai" (p. 80, folio not stated).
- **A melā dated by the Islamic calendar.** The Sidis "mostly sing at their pir's annual melā, which is
  on **the sixteenth day after the Muslim festival of Eid ul Azha**, when the faithful offer a

#### Who attends, and where women may and may not go

- **The congregation, by class.** "The audiences at the shrines come from a variety of socioeconomic
  backgrounds… Women come in large numbers. The majority of participants are rural peasants, factory
  workers, housewives, and middle-class devotees. A large number who come are illiterate but well versed
  in the oral culture. The shrines fulfill devotional needs and provide outlets from the chores of daily
  life" (p. 21, folio not stated). "An entire ritualistic and spiritual world grew around these shrines
  where **women and children went in large numbers to pray, to supplicate, to bring offerings, and to
  seek relief from emotional, familial, and financial concerns**" (p. 163, folio not stated). **No
  visitor number is given anywhere in the book except the fifty counted at one evening's sama at Kasur
  (p. 106, folio not stated).**
- **The book's central institutional claim, stated twice.** "**Since women were not allowed into the
  male domains of the mosque, the Sufi shrines became centers for their devotional activities.** Here,

  rituals that took place around them. Thus, **both the elitist and lay worlds of women merged in the

#### Dhammāl, sama and healing

- "dhammāl The vigorous ritual dance performed at Sufi shrines in Panjab and Sind" (p. 180, folio not
  stated); "Dhammāl is a ritual ecstatic dance performed at the Sufi shrines or in contexts where Sufi
  texts are sung" (p. 201, folio not stated); "In this dance the soul, mind, and body are said to
  function in deep contemplation of God's name" (pp. 64–65, folios not stated); "The state of ecstasy is
  induced with the rhythmic beat of the naubats (drums)" (p. 65, folio not stated).
- **Healing, and the price of it.** Families bring emotionally disturbed women to the dhammāl "which
  devotees claim has healing powers" (p. 66, folio not stated). "In Baluchistan, such healing ceremonies
  are called guāṭi. The family of the unwell woman will negotiate with the ''Sufi'' certain terms that
  will be fulfilled when the woman gets well, such as a vow that there will be a ceremony and the
  woman's husband or family will buy her an expensive gift, perhaps in gold, if she recovers from her
  illness" (p. 66, folio not stated).
- **Infertility cures as shrine specialisms, gender-divided** — at Sidi shrines in Gujarat, not in
  Pakistan: "Mai Misra's shrine in Gujrat has become known for healing female infertility, as her
  brother Bava Habash's shrine is renowned for healing male infertility. Whereas female devotees who are
  infertile are fed Mai Misra's kichri, the male devotees who are infertile are given ''Bava Habash's
  milk'' full of hot spices"; "Infertile female devotees eat the kichri and sing jikar (devotional songs)
  at the shrine" (p. 76, folio not stated).
- **Sama defined by the performers, not the author.** "At the grassroots level, samā is also understood
  as a context or environment, and the aim of every competent performer of Sufi music and discourse is
  to create the samā" (p. 92, folio not stated); Dai Haleema's usage is taken as evidence, "To create the
  samā also means to create the context" (p. 43, folio not stated); "These are storytelling devices that
  create the samā (the environment) for their audiences" (p. 131, folio not stated); the glossary,
  "samā A spiritual assembly or context of Sufi poetry and music" (p. 187, folio not stated). **A second
  sense sits two pages away and is not reconciled: "Researchers report that from the thirteenth century
  onward, Hindu mystical songs were recited at samā gatherings" (p. 132, folio not stated).**
- **An obligation that follows from ecstasy.** "They are obligated to continue singing until the affected
  overcome the state of ecstasy" (p. 45, folio not stated) — illustrated by Bakhtiyar Kaki, for whom "a
  famous couplet from the Persian mystic Ahmed Jam brought about this state… The qawwāls had to continue
  singing until finally the Sufis present asked the performers to end the singing on the first couplet,
  ''for the martyrs of the dagger of submission,'' so that the saint would rest in final union with his
  beloved" (p. 204, folio not stated). **Attributed by the author to Regula Qureshi.**
- **Ritual purity in training.** "when he taught qawwālī, he initiated his pupils into the spiritual
  rituals of zikr. As such, he trained his students to do the wudu (the ablutions of cleaning themselves
  with water) before participating in the qawwālī sessions with him" — Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan (p. 167,
  folio not stated).

#### The economy of female performance

- **The genre divide is the earnings divide.** "Qawwāli is essentially a male genre, usually sung by
  professional musicians, who come from a line of qawwālī singers, and consists of a team of five to
  twelve musicians who take turns singing a single narrative" (pp. 39–40, folios not stated); "**The
  qawwālī genre of Sufi music is essentially a male domain**" (p. 55, folio not stated); sufiānā-kalām is
  "mystical poetry usually sung by a solo musician to minimal instrumentation. Women's input in this
  tradition is significant… In Sind this form of choir singing is called sūng" (p. 40, folio not stated),
  and "Unlike the qawwālī, where the musicians have to have rigorous training in the genre, the singing
  of sufiānā-kalām requires less rigid training" (p. 40, folio not stated).
- **Four reasons the book gives for the gendered earnings gap.** (1) "Female musicians have fewer direct
  contacts with male organizers and sponsors overseas"; (2) "Due to cultural norms, female musicians have
  limited access to their male patrons and therefore their earnings are much less"; (3) "Sometimes, the
  female musicians do not have managers able to negotiate with recording companies overseas" (all p. 34,
  folio not stated); (4) genre — "This is in part because they sing sufiānā-kalām and not the more
  popular qawwālī that is sung in the classical traditions of North Indian music… Their skills are
  considered more in line with folk music" (p. 35, folio not stated). **For all three women "their
  husbands functioned as negotiators"** (p. 34, folio not stated). "Although the female performers have
  made some capital from concerts in the Middle East, Europe, and North America, their earnings are small
  compared to those of their male counterparts" (pp. 34–35, folios not stated).
- **A woman crossing into the male genre to close the gap.** "Since the early nineties she has tried to
  establish an identity comparable to that of the male qawwāls by singing the traditional qaul, ''Mun
  kunto Maulā fā Alī-un Maulā,'' in a call-and-response pattern with one of her instrumentalists. Like
  the male qawwāls she has added a formidable repertoire of Amir Khusrau's Sufi lyrics in her
  performances" (p. 35, folio not stated) — **"the early nineties" is spelled out and survives.**
- **The household economy beyond the shrine.** "Female musicians thus make a living by singing in the
  feudal households and within their own communities"; "Female musicians in the cities sing at ceremonies
  such as births, weddings, aqīqā celebrations, and circumcisions" (p. 34, folio not stated). "Female
  musicians create festivity in the zanānā (women's section), narrating events from Islamic history, and
  **they are paid well for their services**" (p. 169, folio not stated); "The Sidi, Mohana, and Manganhar
  women associated with feudal households in the Sind said that they sing devotional Sufi poetry at their
  mentor's shrine only on special occasions, such as his urs or a religious event. **These shrines are
  not far from where they live, perhaps within thirty to forty miles**" (p. 95, folio not stated), and
  "they only perform before female audiences in their murshid's shrine and **their female patrons pay
  them well**" (p. 95, folio not stated).
- **The cassette industry, and a named firm with a ritual calendar.** "the cassette culture has provided a
  source of income… this small industry has become a source of income for many women and men" (p. 35,
  folio not stated). "**Rehmat Gramophone Company in Faisalabad** provides female musicians with a
  livelihood by recording their songs during Milad-ud Nabi, which celebrates the Prophet's birthday…
  Their recordings are played on the local buses, in the restaurants, and on the radio. Rehmat Gramophone
  Company releases special audiocassette editions in which female and male performers narrate the tragedy
  of Kerbala ( ) [text-layer?: parenthetical content lost] during the annual Muharram mourning rituals"
  (pp. 36–37, folios not stated). "Lorraine Sakata reports that Rehmat Gramophone Company in Faisalabad
  contains an archive of the family's qawwālī repertoire of the Panjabi ang" (p. 203, folio not stated).
- **Corporate sponsorship.** "In the last ten years, multinational companies like Pepsi have hired young
  female and male singers for promotional purposes. These musicians, who are mostly professionals such as
  doctors and engineers, sing for television, and Pepsi sponsors the programs" (p. 80, folio not stated).
- **The stigma, stated four times and once qualified by class.** "Although Kubra is a musician, she is shy
  to admit her skill as a singer, due to negative community attitudes toward female performers… Since her
  husband holds a visible government position, her identity as a musician is not viewed favorably in the
  community" (p. 69, folio not stated); "Shame (izzat) is a concern for men, and they are silent about
  their womenfolk's singing… It reflects poorly on their own image as breadwinners" (p. 82, folio not
  stated); "There is a stigma attached to women singing because this is a skill associated with
  courtesans" (p. 202, folio not stated); "among the peasant or working classes singing in public contexts
  even among women is sometimes taboo" (p. 199, folio not stated). **The class exception:** "This seems to
  be a class phenomenon. Rubina Qureshi, a renowned female singer of Sindhi Sufi poetry and a faculty
  member at Sind University, is reportedly the wife of a top official in the Sind government organization"
  (p. 202, folio not stated).
- **Veiling as the explicit link.** "The community attitudes in relation to women singing are also linked
  with women's veiling (pardā). Women must not be seen in public, especially by unrelated men. Then, to
  sing is even more damaging socially for the position of their male relatives who struggle to change
  socioeconomic class and break out of the old community bonds of servitude to landowners" (p. 82, folio
  not stated).
- **When a woman of a caste-musician family sang.** "The woman would not sing—unless her circumstances
  forced her—if her husband was alive, she stayed at home—but if he was not there then she had to do the
  man's work—naturally she had to sing" (pp. 78–79, folios not stated). **Widowhood, not choice, is given
  as the trigger.**

#### Caste musicians, roving minstrels and occupational exit

- "In Sind there can be no ritual without the Manganhar, be it a birth, a wedding, or a death. They are
  the designated community musicians, and each community has its own Manganhars" (p. 77, folio not
  stated). The etymology, from an informant: "''Manganhar'' originated from the words mangan, which means
  ''to beg,'' and hār, which means ''a garland of flowers.'' Thus, according to an informant at the
  institute of Sindhology at Sind University, a manganhar is one who begs for flowers. In the
  roving-minstrel traditions, alms were given to the minstrels in return for their musical services"
  (pp. 77–78, folios not stated). Family titles: "The women in our families are called
  mangtī—faqīriānī—our family is called faqīr bādshāh—we beg—faqīr sāĩ—faqīr sāhib—this is our family
  title" (p. 78, folio not stated).
- **The musician as dispute-broker for landowners.** "if there was a dispute they would come to the faqīr
  and say, ''Faqīr, do a duā [a prayer], that I may be reconciled with my brother''—you see the vaḍerās
  [landowners] have disputes with each other… we lessen our life spans with our deeds" (p. 78, folio not
  stated).
- **Payment in kind, then in cash.** "in those days the money was very little—lots of grain—we used to
  get paid in grain—lots of it there was—one sack—four sacks—this was more than enough for a poor
  household" (p. 78, folio not stated); "**Over the last quarter of a century the practices have changed.
  Musicians are now paid in cash rather than agricultural produce**" (p. 80, folio not stated); "the
  vaḍerās prefer to pay in cash rather than in agricultural commodities" (p. 81, folio not stated).
- **Occupational exit, and who employs them now.** Caste musicians "want their children to get educated
  and be employed in the government service, which promises a stable job and an income, together with
  social prestige" (p. 79, folio not stated); "Male members of the immediate family of the Manganhars are
  employed in government services such as the police, education departments, or accounting services. The
  universities employ them… This the musicians call a **pucci naukri** (a permanent job) that gives them
  higher social status" (p. 81, folio not stated). "I have seen communities of workers, such as musicians,
  village barbers, and potters who had a variety of other roles in the landowning structures, disperse
  within the last twenty-five years" (p. 81, folio not stated). **The landlords' withdrawal:** "The
  younger generation of landlords no longer lives in the villages. They live in large urban centers like
  Karachi, Lahore, or Islamabad, or they study in the academies of the West, from where they return to
  join the country's political forces. **The land thus becomes a base from which political identity and
  votes come**" (p. 81, folio not stated).
- **Sidi labour, servitude and its aftermath.** "Sidis were initially brought as slaves from Africa to
  work in the subcontinent of Pakistan and India" (p. 67, folio not stated); "Although they are now free,
  some of them are attached to the feudal households in the Sind province of Pakistan" (p. 68, folio not
  stated); "now they are employed—in various professions that include work with government departments—such
  as Sind University—they carry on with their singing—" (p. 70, folio not stated). "The Hammerton Treaty
  of  banned bringing African slaves to India. Many were freed in Bombay, ." — **both years destroyed**
  (p. 199, folio not stated).
- **Other caste service roles at ritual.** "the barbers (nāis) communicated births and deaths in the
  community and were paid for their services; they performed male circumcision and cooked huge quantities
  of food for ritual celebrations; the potters (kumhārs) traditionally were also the carriers of the
  bride's palanquin or small litter called a ḍolī" (pp. 201–202, folios not stated).
- **The instrument names the profession.** "These minstrels are called loṭevālī for playing the loṭā,
  chalevālī for playing the chalā, or ghaṛevālī for playing the ghaṛā" (pp. 57–58, folios not stated); the
  glossary gives each a separate entry (pp. 180–184, folios not stated). Mendicant sound-markers: "there is
  the jingle of bells (talyoon) in the background that is indicative of the mendicant traditions. The jogīs
  (mendicants) also tied another form of bell called a ghungrū to the yak-tarā" (p. 80, folio not stated).
  **A drum held sacred by a community:** "mugarmān A tall vertical drum played in a standing position with
  thumping and stroking of both hands; **held sacred by the Sidi communities in Sind, Baluchistan,
  Rajasthan, and Gujrat who use them in their shrines and for rituals**; most likely has an African origin"
  (p. 185, folio not stated); "dādā Grandfather; a fond name that Sidis use for the giant mugarmān"
  (p. 180, folio not stated).

#### Broadcast, diaspora and the media economy

- **The radio as the institution of transmission.** Input came "from professionals such as music directors,
  producers, archivists, and scholars in Pakistan who are linked with the singing of ritual Sufi discourse
  at the shrines, in concerts, and in the media, especially the radio. **The radio seems to ensure the
  continuity of this tradition in the popular culture among the speech communities**" (p. 23, folio not
  stated). "In addition to singing at Sufi shrines, women musicians perform on the radio or television"
  (p. 34, folio not stated).
- **The ritual has moved out of the shrine — the book's own summary of the change.** "The changes have come
  about as **the rituals have gradually moved out of the shrine contexts to the more public contexts of the
  melās or folk festivals and now of course the concert settings**, where the musicians use sophisticated
  electronic media in addition to traditional instrumentation. The multimedia have played a significant
  role in the changes" (p. 139, folio not stated). But the shrine repertoire is carried into the hall: "I
  noticed a trend among musicians to reinforce the singing of the qaul and the girāhs in concert settings
  for their expatriate audiences in the same manner that they would do in the shrines" (p. 140, folio not
  stated). And the agents are the musicians: "the musicians themselves have largely been responsible for
  the changes in the rituals. While on the one hand they are involved in the modernization process, on the
  other hand they uphold the traditions" (p. 139, folio not stated).
- **The diaspora as patron.** "In the last two decades, large expatriate speech communities have given
  immense patronage to qawwālī and sufiānā-kalām sung in concert. The musicians recreate it as a performing
  art, outside the shrine culture, where they still uphold the sanctity of the ritual" (p. 115, folio not
  stated); "In the last three decades a large number of professionals and skilled workers from the
  subcontinent have moved to the West and to the Gulf States" (p. 169, folio not stated) — **"two decades"
  and "three decades" both spelled out and intact, both relative to the book's own undated present.**
  "**The patrons showered the musicians with pound sterling currency notes to express their
  appreciation**", seen "in the video footage that I obtained in the United Kingdom, from concerts
  performed in the eighties" (p. 170, folio not stated). At one such performance the audience "fell into
  an ecstatic state and performed dhammāl, which showed their rapture" (p. 114, folio not stated).
- **The class composition of the two audiences, argued from the migration statistics.** "**The statistics
  show that the majority of groups comprise unskilled workers, and they are the ones who come in large
  numbers to the concerts. These groups also form the lay populations at the Sufi shrines in their own
  countries**" (p. 171, folio not stated); by contrast, "In the United States, the audiences at the Sufi
  ritual concerts in cities like New York, Chicago, San Francisco, Los Angeles, and Houston belong to
  higher socioeconomic strata" (p. 171, folio not stated). **The five statistical tables behind this
  argument (pp. 171–173, folios not stated) are gutted — table numbers, column-year headers and every data
  cell are empty, several rows surviving only as thousands-separator commas — so the claim cannot be
  checked from the text layer at all. The source lines do survive and are citable** (Salt and Ford; M.
  Fahim Khan; Nayyar; Osmani; U.S. Census Bureau).
- **A media economy grown out of ritual.** "They created the demand not only for the concerts but also for
  spiritual music on CDs and audiocassettes. In the United Kingdom, videocassettes of live qawwālī
  performances further fulfilled the needs of the audiences. **Thus, an entire electronic culture developed
  around Sufi rituals**, drawing a large number of western audiences as well" (p. 170, folio not stated);
  "**Recording companies have capitalized on this interest and have built substantial archives of excellent
  Sufi music on CDs.** Through their efforts sacrosanct and secretive Sufi rituals such as those in Iran are
  now available to listeners" (p. 174, folio not stated).
- **Commissioning of lyrics — a direct statement of the qawwali economy.** "In my personal interview with
  Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan, he said that **he commissioned Bari Nizami, Ahmed Nadeem Qasmi, and other national
  poets to write his lyrics**. His songwriters recreated the traditional, indigenous myths and texts for
  him" (p. 151, folio not stated); at Kasur, "Sometimes we get poets like Bedam Arsi to write songs for us"
  (p. 111, folio not stated).

#### Opposition to shrine ritual

- "Many in the Muslim world object to the rituals at the shrines" (p. 37, folio not stated); "**The Wahabi
  followers of Islam disdain the Sufi practices at the shrines as a form of biddat (dissent)**" (p. 43,
  folio not stated). **`Wahabi` is the last headword in the book's index** (p. 240, folio not stated), and
  `biddat` has its own (p. 236, folio not stated).
- "the devotees who visit the shrines do not claim to be Sufis… they firmly believe that they are Muslims

### Arguments and interpretations

- **The book's thesis, stated twice.** "There is enough evidence to support my argument that substantial
  Sufi poetry in the subcontinent is sung in the female voice. Male musicians use inflection, and by
  default, the female voices are heard in their syntax and semantics" (p. 141, folio not stated); and the
  conclusion from code-switching, "which confirms the hypothesis that cross culturally the female voice is
  dominant in Sufi poetry" (p. 147, folio not stated).
- **Why, in the performers' own words, and they disagree.** Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan: "The Sufi poets—in the
  tradition of Sufi mystical poetry—these mystic poets—when they speak they do it in the female voice—they
  present themselves as the female—for them their beloved—their mentor—their shaikh—is the male—whereas
  their own voice is that of the female" (p. 98, folio not stated), because "There is an elegance in
  it—there is an elegance—a humility in the female voice—which is lacking in the male voice" (p. 98, folio
  not stated). **Against him, the Kasur qawwāl Muhammad Bakhsh:** "It is actually not the ''female.'' **The
  voice is that of the spirit.** This is the subject of human existence. Life is a spinning, that is why it
  is the female. If human beings lived the way they are expected to live, all would be fine. We must live in
  humility. The female, who spins, lives in humility" (p. 111, folio not stated). **This is a flat
  disagreement about what the female voice *is*, between two of the book's own informants, and the author
  does not adjudicate it.**
- **The mechanism, as the author sets it out.** "The murid becomes the lover and the murshid the beloved.
  The former is represented as the submissive female. The metaphor becomes more complex with the infusion of
  bridal imagery into the relationship. The beloved becomes the bride or sometimes the bridegroom wearing a
  veil. Musicians sing devotional songs to this bride or to the groom asking them to lift the veil" (p. 96,
  folio not stated); and the doctrine, "iśq-e majāzī (personal love) transforms itself into iśq-e haqīqī
  (divine love)" (p. 125, folio not stated).
- **Three explanations for the female voice, the third not adopted.** (1) The forms themselves are gendered:
  "Generally, it is the woman who professes divine love, particularly in the dohā and gīt composed in Khari
  Boli or Hindawi" (p. 140, folio not stated). (2) Audience literacy forced the vernacular: "since this
  poetry was meant to be sung for ordinary people who were barely literate, there was no point in using the
  canonical Perso-Arabic forms in the assemblies where it was sung. For the same reasons then it can be said
  that the Sufi poets used women's speech, as in the cakkī-nāmā and the carkhi-nāmā" (p. 150, folio not
  stated). (3) "Some native critics claim that the use of the female narrator in the Sufi poetry of North
  India could partially be an influence from the Persian poetic genre called the cāmā" (p. 149, folio not
  stated) — **attributed to others; the author's own summary is cautious.**
- **Women as the constituency that made the idiom.** "**For the Sufi poets women were the major participants
  or consumers of shrine activity and speech events where Sufi discourse was disseminated. Thus, the
  linguistic medium and metaphor had to be in voices that were meaningful to women and their networks**"
  (pp. 145–146, folios not stated). "Images in Sufi poetry were drawn from woman's domain, such as that of
  her work. The sabr (perseverance) in the musicians' cakkī-namās and carkhī-namās is derived from the story
  of Job and the story of Jonah, applied through the toiling female" (p. 168, folio not stated).
- **Orthodoxy: the book's claim that this practice is orthodox, not marginal.** "**Sufism is clearly a
  legitimate part of the Islamic tradition. Sufi poetry using the female voice is within the same Islamic
  traditions**" (p. 166, folio not stated); "**In terms of their faith (imān), men and women are considered
  equal**" (p. 165, folio not stated); "All the musicians whom I interviewed who sing Sufi poetry invoke the
  Quran, or a Quranic saying, or the hadīth to initiate a performance… **The musicians' oral sources are
  built within the written traditions of Islamic mysticism**" (p. 166, folio not stated). Against the idea
  that the great qawwals are untutored: they "create the female voices in their poetry with complete
  knowledge and understanding of the Quranic and Islamic sources of their discourse… because of their
  training for several generations in the mystical traditions" (pp. 166–167, folios not stated).
- **Myth as the discourse of the excluded — the book's strongest general claim.** "The Sufi poets made the
  native myths of Hir, Sassi, and Sohni into complex cultural tropes. The discourse so created and sung to
  music **articulated the concerns of marginalized social groups such as the Sufis themselves, women,
  minorities, persecuted communities, and the vast majority of the people who were excluded from the power
  structures supported by the clergy**. This was one of the reasons for this verbal art to be a strong
  component of popular culture in the Pakistan-India subcontinent" (p. 128, folio not stated).
- **Sufism and Bhakti as revolts against clerical and economic power.** "**both Sufi Islam and the Bhakti
  movement were revolts against the established clerical orders.** The established orders took upon
  themselves the interpretation of the faith, creating power structures for the ruling classes. **Economic
  hegemony could only be sustained through religious, social, and cultural structures upheld by clerics and
  patriarchs.** 'Shame' and 'honor' in this context became associated with control of women's
  sensibilities" (p. 151, folio not stated). The anti-clerical claim is made twice more (pp. 42, 45, folios
  not stated), and once inverted: "**For fear of the clergy mystic poetry sung to music remained confined to
  the minstrel class. That is why the sufiānā-kalām traditions are strong among this class of roving
  musicians**" (p. 50, folio not stated).
- **Syncretism as the mechanism of conversion, and caste as its motive.** "The Sufi version of Islam in
  Pakistan and India was based on syncretism and reconciled differing beliefs… **At that time in India many
  converted to Islam because it offered relief from caste oppression**" (p. 50, folio not stated). "Since
  some of these cultures were ''**matrimythical**'' and used the indigenous female myths, the Sufi poets
  creatively blended the female voices from these myths into their poetry that they sang to music" (p. 50,
  folio not stated) — **the book's own coinage and the spine of its thesis, indexed as `matrimythical`
  (p. 237, folio not stated).** Carried through to the shrine: "because the myths had a pre-Islamic,
  indigenous origin, non-Muslim devotees had no problem putting the female myths into their own devotional
  schemas or worldviews" (p. 51, folio not stated).
- **Shared shrines, and partition as the break.** "**The practice of non-Muslim devotees visiting Muslim
  shrines and vice versa is common when diverse religious communities coexist. This was especially so in
  undivided India before the partition of [numeral dropped, text-layer].** Islamic mysticism, that is,
  Sufism, was a shared experience with the indigenous forms of mysticism. Mughal emperors such as Akbar
  encouraged the syncreticism" (pp. 196–197, folios not stated). **Directly relevant to the archive's mixed
  Muslim, Hindu, Sikh and Jain rows.**
- **Shrines as shared acoustic space.** "These very linguistic and musical elements **must have drawn the
  laypeople to the Sufi shrines of Pakistan and India in the last several centuries**. It is not surprising
  that **the shrines provided the space for speech communities to listen to ritual, devotional poetry that
  had shared meanings. These contexts represented the shared religious worldview of the participants of the
  events, especially the women**" (p. 174, folio not stated).
- **The social conditions Sufi poetry answered.** "in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries through the
  following centuries, the region was a place of extreme contrasts. On the one hand, society witnessed the
  grandeur and splendor of the Muslim aristocracy and the opulent lifestyles of the rajas and maharajas; on
  the other hand, **most of the population was struggling under feudal tutelage. Society was crushed with
  illiteracy, poverty, disease, caste, and gender apartheid**" (p. 168, folio not stated). "**Their khanqahs
  became places where laypersons found simplifications of the Quranic teachings.** The Sufis spoke to the
  people in metaphors that they could understand, in images that came from the life around them, such as
  those of spinning, weaving, grinding, and husking" (p. 168, folio not stated).
- **Chronology, as the book gives it — all of it in words, and therefore all of it safe.** "Mystical
  performances originated in Sufism in the eighth century in the Arabian peninsula" (p. 38, folio not
  stated); samā "goes back to the middle of the ninth century—references to this event are found in Baghdad"
  (p. 39, folio not stated); "Sufi poetry outside the high tradition of the samā was used in the eleventh
  century in Iran. From there it spread to Turkey and gradually into the Pakistan-India subcontinent, where
  it became popular in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries" (p. 38, folio not stated); "Sufiānā-kalām
  evolved at the same time as qawwālī… in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries" (p. 42, folio not
  stated); "The tradition evolved around the thirteenth century [text-layer?: era marker dropped]" (p. 86,
  folio not stated); "Qawwālī performances in concert are a product of the twentieth century, a feature of
  postcolonial politics when large Muslim populations from South Asia moved to the West" (p. 84, folio not
  stated).
- **An informant who contradicts the book's own frame — kept as a contradiction.** "When I interviewed
  Surraiya Multanikar… **she said that she did not believe in any female myths. She said that the female
  narratives grew around real events in communities among young women, and these events happened a long time
  ago.** Concerning wajd (ecstasy), she said that **it may not necessarily be induced through music. This is
  an experience that anyone can attain through zikr**" (p. 168, folio not stated). **Abbas records this
  without resolving it against her own vocabulary.**
- **A correction the author refused to make, and said so.** "A native Sindhi informant corrected this term
  to mean a choir of mendicants who sing mystical poetry, but I retain the context in which I interviewed
  Baloch and Mirza" (p. 198, folio not stated).
- **The translation policy, which every user of this book must carry.** "The translations are not a literal
  rendering of the text; rather, they are done to convey the mood of the Sufi poems" (p. 10, folio not
  stated); "I have avoided a word-for-word translation but have made sure that the text is an authentic
  rendering of the poetic narrative" (p. 20, folio not stated); and four further statements in the endnotes
  — "The translation is contextual and not a word-for-word rendering"; "The translation is a poetic rendering
  in context, worked out with a native Siraiki informant, and is not a paraphrase"; "The translation is in
  the context of live speech and is therefore not a paraphrase"; "The verses are translated to reinforce the
  poetic contexts and are not a word-for-word translation" (pp. 204, 205, 208, folios not stated).
  **Anyone quoting a translation from this book must treat it as a mood rendering, not a gloss.**
- **Improvisation as the book's own warrant against treating a recorded text as authorial.** "Sometimes,
  they combined the original poem of a Sufi poet with other fragments that they had either created
  themselves or a songwriter had built into the text for them. They would even take verses from the poetry
  of different Sufi poets and use them as adjacent texts, improvising the discourse, according to the cues
  they received from the audience" (p. 99, folio not stated).
- **The author's claim of priority, and what the book does not do.** "No researcher has examined the
  sociolinguistic aspects of the female voice or some of the performance fields that I have identified in my
  study" (p. 32, folio not stated); "I do not discuss different versions of the female myths, or delve into
  male rituals, or bring in every theory or study that a scholar has done on Sufism" (p. 22, folio not
  stated); "I have only touched the periphery of the female roving traditions, and the field needs to be
  further researched" (p. 66, folio not stated); "**Much work remains to be done**" (p. 174, folio not
  stated), followed by twelve `Proposals` (pp. 174–175, folios not stated).

### 5. Attributions that must survive into any entry

- The **three-century** service claim at Kasur, the **migration from Uch Sharif** and the **exclusion of

  stated).

### 7. Structural note for the coordinator

The book prints a folio on nearly every page, but always in the dropped-digit face, so **no folio is
recoverable anywhere and none has been derived, guessed or computed.** The printing-sheet mark holds
`sheet = p − 1` across all eleven chunks and is **not** a folio. A lower-case roman folio *is* legible in
the footer of seventeen front-matter pages, giving **roman folio = p − 1** (pp. 10–31, folios not stated),
which is the only folio evidence in the book; the arabic sequence cannot be checked at all. **Because
every endnote number in the Notes section is destroyed and numbering restarts per chapter, no note in
pp. 192–218 can be paired with its callout**, and four notes that are purely cross-references ("See
n. .", "See chapter , …", "The term is used in chapter .", "See page – of this chapter") are unusable as
they stand.
