## What this book is

A methodological history of one Sufi order. "The subject of this book is the Chishti Sufi order"
(p. 16, folio not stated), and the authors state two departures from the existing literature in the
same breath: "First, we use many texts produced by the Chishtis and their supporters that have been
previously ignored or slighted. Second, we deliberately abandon the numbingly circular arguments, so
prevalent in the literature, about a privileged 'golden age' of Sufism in general and the Chishti
order in particular" (p. 16, folio not stated). The thesis is a five-cycle periodization: "a Sufi
order such as the Chishtiyya is more than a parasitical legitimation of power or a nostalgic
reverence for bygone saints; it is instead a complex of spiritual practice, historical memory, and
ethical models, which continues to evolve from its medieval Islamic origins in response to the
political, ideological, and technological transformations of the contemporary world" (p. 16, folio
not stated). The authors are explicit about what it is not: "It is not a documentary history in the
usual sense. It is a stocktaking that also includes a projection of agendas for research" (p. 17,
folio 2). Composition is joint to the sentence — "at this point there is not a single page that does
not bear the marks of both authors" (p. 14, folio not stated).

**The structure is itself a finding, and it is where this book's value to the archive lies.** The
book's stated problematic is the asymmetry between its two bibliographies: European-language
scholarship is "focused largely on the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, a period
`that-paradoxically-is[text-layer?]` [that—paradoxically—is] almost entirely lacking in contemporary
documentation and is known only from later texts", while the Persian works "expand and multiply in
number and geographical extent right up to the end of the nineteenth century" (p. 16, folio not
stated). The Persian/Urdu Select Bibliography is a genre taxonomy in thirteen numbered sections,
and its scare quotes are the book's own: `1. BIBLIOGRAPHIC` (1 entry), `2. GENERAL SURVEYS` (3),
`3· STUDIES OF HAGIOGRAPHIES` (2), `4· BIOGRAPHICAL DICTIONARIES` `(CHRONOLOGICAL BY DATE OF
COMPOSITION)` (≈37, pp. 234–236), `5· GENEALOGIES` (4), `6. MoNOGRAPHIC BIOGRAPHIES (cHRONOLOGICAL
BY SUBJECT)` (≈48, pp. 236–238 — the largest saint-by-saint section and the one that carries
Taunsa), `7· "RETROSPECTIVE" MALFUZAT` (8), `8. "ORIGINAL" MALFUZAT` (≈40, pp. 239–240), `9.LETTERS`
(13), `10. SPECULATIVE WoRKS` (≈128, pp. 241–245 — by far the largest section and the one with the
thinnest Pakistan yield), `ll. LITERATURE` — `ll[text-layer?] [11]` — (5), `12. LEGAL TEXTS` (2),
`13. DEVOTIONAL WORKS` (8) (pp. 233–245, folios 224–235). The split between `7· "RETROSPECTIVE"
MALFUZAT` and `8. "ORIGINAL" MALFUZAT` (pp. 238–239, folios 228–229) embeds a scholarly judgement —
malfuzat retrospectively ascribed to the early masters versus those compiled by a named auditor in
the shaykh's lifetime — and **the book offers no explanation of the distinction where the headings
stand: (not stated)**.

For this archive the book is overwhelmingly a **Baba Farid** book plus a **Taunsa** book: 105 of its
215 archive bullets attach to `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` and 33 to the Taunsa darbar, with
`data-darbar` (Hujwiri, 34 bullets) close behind as an authorial and doctrinal presence rather than
a shrine. What it does **not** supply is the shrine furniture an archive row usually wants: with the
exception of Pakpattan and Taunsa, ʿurs dates, custodian names, endowment figures and building dates
are absent, and each chunk records that absence as an explicit negative inventory. The book's own
European-language bibliography confirms the skew — its `5. REGIONAL STUDIES` section has sections
for Bengal (5 entries), the Deccan (5), Gujarat (1) and Jaunpur (1), **its Punjab section holds
exactly one entry** (pp. 226–227, folios 215–216), and there is **no Sindh section, no Multan
section and no Pakistan section at all: (not stated)**.

**Reliability.** The `text_layer` is legible but damaged in several recurring, *non*-mechanical
ways — digit-for-letter substitutions inside proper names (`Khu1dabad` for `Khuldabad`, `Gu1` for
`Gul`), punctuation substituted for letters (`ma!fuzat`, `~diri`), lost inter-word spaces
(`until1614`, `tasawwufbecame`), em dashes flattened to hyphens, case corruption in small-capital
headings (`MoNOGRAPHIC`, `WoRKS`), and closing-quote marks rendered as `:'`, `:>` or `:`. Every
affected word below carries its `[text-layer?]` flag; the classes are inventoried under Doubts.
**The book is heavily endnoted and heavily indexed, and no index or endnote locator anywhere below
has been converted into a `(p. N)`** — such entries are cited by the PDF page the entry is *printed*
on.

### shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar — Shrine of Fariduddin Ganjshakar, Pakpattan

The book's densest archive row by a wide margin: 105 bullets across sixteen of the seventeen chunks.
The book's names for him are **"Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar"** (running form), **"Shaykh Farid"**,
**"Shaykh Farid ad-Din"**, **"Baba Farid"**, **"Farid ad-Din Mas'ud Ajodhani [Ganj-i Shakar]"**
(square brackets the book's own, p. 159, folio 147), **"Farid al-Din Ganj-i Shakar"** in the Persian
bibliography (p. 236, folio 226), **"Shakarganj"** and **"Shakarbar"** in nineteenth-century titles

**`Ganj-i Shakzar[text-layer?]` [Ganj-i Shakar]** (p. 237, folio 227). His personal name is given as
**"Mas'ud"**, and only inside the starving-son anecdote (p. 81, folio 67; p. 168, folio 156).

    Ajmeri and Qutb ad-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki and before Nizam ad-Din Awliya' and Nasir ad-Din
    `Chiragh-i-Dihli-these[text-layer?]` [Chiragh-i Dihli—these] (p. 80, folio 66); one of "the
    three Chishti giants, Farid ad-Din, Nizam ad-Din, and Nasir ad-Din, who complete the first cycle
    of the Chishtiyya" (p. 183, folio 171); and head of the appendix's second generation, "The first
    three generations are headed by Mu'in ad-Din, Farid ad-Din, and Nasir ad-Din" (p. 161, folio
    149) — **note that Qutb ad-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki is absent from that list of three although his
    biography stands between theirs** (p. 161, folio 149).
  - **The lineage claim that matters most to the archive: "Both the major sublineages of the
    Chishtis derive from him"** (p. 167, folio 155), and "The centuries-old division between the two
    branches stems from the time of Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar, and it is his two disciples 'Ala'
    ad-Din 'Ali Sabir and Nizam ad-Din Awliya', after whom these two branches are named" (p. 131,
    folio 118). The Sabir filiation is reported as contested, not as fact: Shaykh 'Ali ibn Ahmad
    Sabir "is said to have been the Shaykh 'Ali Sabir who is briefly mentioned in Siyar al-awliya'
    as a disciple of Shaykh Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar. No less an authority than Shaykh 'Abd
    al-Haqq, however, questions the conflation of the two names and persons" (p. 74, folio 60).
  - **Initiation, dated only by an event in Multan.** "The visit of Qutb ad-Din to Multan (a
    strategic commercial center in the Punjab) became the occasion for Farid ad- Din's pledge of
    allegiance to the Chishti master" (p. 80, folio 66) — the printed form is "Farid ad- Din" with
    an intruded space. **No year is given for the visit: (not stated).**
  - **Family.** "Each of the first three Indian Chishti `masters-Mu'in[text-layer?]`
    [masters—Mu'in] ad-Din, Qutb ad-Din, and Farid `ad-Din-had[text-layer?]` [ad-Din—had] families"
    (p. 81, folio 67); "it is Farid ad-Din who poignantly attests to the tension peculiar to Muslim
    saints, the tension between bearing responsibility for one's family and abandoning all to God"
    (p. 81, folio 67). **Numbers of wives and children: (not stated)**, though "one of his wives",
    "a son of ours" and "one of Farid ad-Din's sons" (pp. 81, 87, folios 67, 73) show there was more
    than one of each. His mother is named: "Bibi Sharifa became a widow at an early age and had no
    children besides Farid ad-Din. Until her dying breath, she continued to remember God, and was
    considered to be a great perfected saint" (p. 135, folio 122) — Thanvi's account; **Bibi
    Sharifa is given no dates, no burial place and no shrine: (not stated)**. His daughter Bibi
    Fatimah is "a prominent daughter of Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar" through whose elder son Khwaja
    Muhammad Imam the Faridi blood line runs (p. 126, folio 113). The descendants are a named
    kin-group: "the descendants of Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar (the Faridis) have shaped the
    continuation of Chishti identity" (p. 126, folio 113). Shaykh Salim Chishti of Fatehpur Sikri
    belongs to "the Faridi line" (p. 111, folio 98).
  - **Sayyid status left unresolved by the book itself.** He falls inside "All but one were
    sayyids, i.e., they traced their biological ancestry back to the Prophet Muhammad", who
    "preferred to be addressed by the title \"shaykh\" rather than \"sayyid`;'\"[text-layer?]`
    [\"sayyid.\"]" (p. 80, folio 66). **The book does not say which one of the five is the
    exception: (not stated).** He also falls inside "All received a protracted, formal education",
    "They either memorized the Qur'an or knew many of its passages by heart", and a "thorough
    grounding in Muslim law (`jiqh[text-layer?]` [fiqh])" (p. 80, folio 66).
  - **Austerity, quoted twice from two different sources.** "His reputation for vigils, fasting,
    and penances extended back to his boyhood when he used to be regarded as \"crazy\" for the
    severity of his observances. Customarily he broke his fast by eating stale bread" (p. 81,
    folio 67). In the appendix: he broke his fast with "a cup of sherbet to which a small quantity
    of grapes had been added", gave away half or two-thirds, then "he was served two pieces of bread
    with oil", and "the Shaykh would not eat again till the next day at iftar"; for sleep "he used
    the rug upon which he had been sitting during the day as his bed, even though the rug did not
    cover his feet" (p. 167, folio 155). Nizam ad-Din "noted that Shaykh Farid usually ate stale
    bread at the time of iftar", Nasir ad-Din that "For years they would bring stale bread to Shaykh
    Farid ad-Din", and Nizam ad-Din that "That night when I ate delah or the fruit of the karil tree
    was like a festival to me; it was a gift of grace" (p. 167, folio 155). Scruple about borrowed
    salt: "Some flavoring was used in this `food;'[text-layer?]` [food,\"] he commented, \"and it is
    not fitting that I should eat such `food:'\"[text-layer?]` [food.\"] (p. 167, folio 155).
  - **He never performed the hajj, and the book supplies the excuse.** "While Mu'in ad-Din had
    undertaken the pilgrimage before coming to India, Qutb ad-Din, Farid ad-Din, Nizam ad-Din, and
    Nasir ad-Din all failed to fulfill this critical obligation of Islam. It must be quickly noted,
    however, that they espoused poverty and, therefore, were not technically able to go on the
    pilgrimage" (p. 81, folio 67).
  - **His death, narrated.** After evening prayers in congregation he fell unconscious, asked on
    waking whether he had prayed, said them a second and then a third time — "\"I had better say
    them one more `time;'[text-layer?]` [time,\"] he said: \"Who knows what may happen?\"" — and
    "At the end, he exclaimed \"`0[text-layer?]` [O] Living one, `0[text-layer?]` [O] Eternal one!\"
    and gave up his soul to God" (p. 169, folio 157).

    Ajodhan (modern Pakpattan)" (p. 105, folio 92); "Ajmer became one of the main points on a
    pilgrimage itinerary, along with Delhi and Pakpattan" (p. 98, folio not stated); Hasan Nizami's
    "trip on foot (partially) to Pakpattan in emulation of the saint's trip to find his master
    Farid" (p. 127, folio 114); and the index's `Pakpattan 2, 5, 92, 103, 114` (p. 249, folio 240).

  - **Two chilla-gahs of his outside Pakistan** — "one chillah of Baba Farid abuts the shrine of
    Khwaja Mu'in ad-Din in Ajmer, while another associated with Farid is found outside the tomb of
    Qutb ad-Din in Delhi" (p. 109, folio 96). **No Pakistani chilla-gah is attached to him:

  - **A relic of his is held at Khuldabad, not at Pakpattan** — a staff, "which is called the staff

- **Practices & Events**
  - **The ʿurs, described as a multi-day festival with cowries thrown over the crowd.** "Today, at
    the multi-day annual festival celebrating his 'marriage' with God, cowrie shells (once used as
    the smallest units of currency) are thrown over the crowd, to imitate the moment when Shaykh
    Farid ad-Din went into ecstasy, on realizing that Nizam ad-Din Awliya' had just attained a
    status rare even among the great ones, the nearly unprecedented state of becoming the beloved of
    God Himself" (p. 18, folio 3). **The book names no month, no date, no custodian and no crowd
    figure in this passage: (not stated).**
  - **The ʿurs date is given elsewhere, and it heads the appendix's Chishti calendar** —
    "Muharram / 5. Farid ad-Din Mas'ud Ajodhani [Ganj-i Shakar] (d. 664/1265)" (p. 159, folio 147),
    i.e. the fifth of Muharram, which is also the day of death given at p. 169, folio 157.
  - **State control of the ʿurs — the book's single statement of a Pakistani government managing a
    named shrine's festival.** "Government manipulation, as in Pakistan's control of the 'urs of
    Baba Farid, may present an institutional, ideological face to this practice that many
    \"pure-minded\" mystics would resist or resent." (p. 157, folio 145). The sentence continues
    into a limit on that control: "Still, no state-controlled `apparatus—whether` in Pakistan,
    India, or Bangladesh, Saudi Arabia or Iran, or the United `States—can` have ultimate control."
    (p. 157, folio 145). **No Auqaf department, no ministry, no date, no revenue figure, no visitor
    number and not even the town of Pakpattan is named in this passage: all (not stated).**
  - **A named pilgrimage to the tomb, dated by a named traveller.** "The famous Moroccan traveler
    Ibn Battuta made such a pilgrimage to the tomb of Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar (d. 1265) at
    Ajodhan (modern Pakpattan) around the year 1340" (p. 105, folio 92) — the earliest date the
    book gives for the tomb functioning as a pilgrimage destination.
  - **The 1930s Pakpattan education dispute — the book's most concrete colonial-succession
    episode.** "a debate raged in Chishti circles in the 1930s over whether or not the British could
    compel a minor who had become diwan at Pakpattan to obtain education at a British college in
    Lahore" (p. 116, folio 103). The protest is quoted: "All the sajjada nishins from other major
    Chishti shrines in North India protested 'that the sajjada nishin should be a Darwesh and has no
    need of receiving the education which is imparted to the sons of rich families.'" (p. 116,
    folio 103). **The book does not name the minor diwan, the college, or the outcome:
    (not stated).**
  - **Zikr formulas in Indian languages are traced to him.** "Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar is the
    source of specific zikr formulas, including some in Indian languages" (p. 45, folio 31);
    Awrangabadi "cites zikr formulas in Punjabi and Hindi, such as the following, attributed to the
    famous Chishti saint Farid ad-Din: 'Say *wuhi hi* upwards, *hi hi* to the left side of the
    breast, *hin hi* toward the heart.'" (p. 47, folio 33). Their circulation and background:
    "Variations on this Hindi `zikrofFarid[text-layer?]` [zikr of Farid] ad-Din are well known in a
    number `oflndian[text-layer?]` [of Indian] Sufi texts, particularly among Chishti and Shattari
    masters. These phrases belong to a long tradition of adapting `hath a yoga[text-layer?]` [hatha
    yoga] mantras to Islamic themes and Sufi practices, as illustrated in the many translations of
    the curious yogic text known as *The Pool of Nectar*" (p. 47, folio 33).
  - **Samaʿ — he is listed among the Indian shaykhs who favoured it, and his own ruling is
    quoted.** Ashraf Jahangir Simnani "extends the list of bygone saints who favored music to
    include Indian shaykhs, viz., Qutb ad-Din, Qazi Hamid ad-Din, Farid ad-Din, and, of course,
    Nizam ad-Din", and "describes these saints not merely as advocates of `sama'but[text-layer?]`
    [samaʿ but] as those who 'experienced empathetic ecstasy and danced'" (p. 57, folio 43). Asked
    "about the permissibility or illegality of sama', a topic disputed among the 'ulama", he
    replied "\"Praise be to God!\"… \"One group has been burnt to ashes, while the other is still
    arguing.\"" (p. 170, folio 158).
  - **Learning versus devotion — his ruling, and the authors' warning about it.** Nizam ad-Din
    asked "What is your command? I will abandon scholarship and pursue my devotions and
    supererogatory `prayers:'\"[text-layer?]` [prayers.\"]"; "Shaykh Farid ad-Din replied: \"I do
    `notre-strain[text-layer?]` [not restrain] anyone from learning. Do both till one predominates.
    A dervish should have some measure of `learning:'\"[text-layer?]` [learning.\"]" (p. 87,
    folio 73). **Ernst and Lawrence immediately caution: "This anecdote is not found in Fawa'id
    al-fu'ad. It may or may not be true" (p. 87, folio 73)** — the appendix gives the same exchange
    again, with Nizam ad-Din "twenty years old" (p. 171, folio 159).
  - **He is the precedent for the khilafat-nama as a document** — of Nizam ad-Din choosing his
    leading disciples from a list of 32 candidates, "He drew up their diplomas (khilafat-namas)
    according to a strict procedure that Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar employed" (p. 194, folio 182).
  - **The succession rule for women, quoted through Thanvi.** "Her son, Baba Farid, used to say,
    \"If *khilafat* and *sajjada* [deputyship and successorship] could be given to a woman, then
    undoubtedly she was worthy of getting the *khilafat*.\" See how precisely he followed the
    *shari'a*, observes Thanvi, alluding to the *sunna* that says that a woman can not be given the
    succession" (p. 135, folio 122). The book prints **"can not"** as two words; kept as printed.
  - **His malfuzat and who recorded them.** "Some of the sayings [malfuzat] of Shaykh Farid ad-Din
    Ganj-i Shakar have been recorded by Shaykh Nizam ad-Din Awliya in a letter" (p. 169, folio 157),
    with further dicta at p. 170, folio 158; in the Persian bibliography he is the subject of
    `Farid al-Din Ganj-i Shakar. Rahat al-qulub. Comp. Nizam al-Din Awliya'. Monzavi, III, no. 2656
    (22 MSS); no. 3696 (3 MSS)` and the compiler of `Qutb al-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki (d. 633/1235).

    it is performed as a practice stipulated for drawing a person closer to God then it is not

    (p. 135, folio 122) — note **"was reputed to have practiced"**, not that he did. The appendix

    well of the Hajj mosque `ofUcch[text-layer?]` [of Ucch]. For 40 days they would suspend him from

- **Significance**
  - **He is the archive-facing face of the book's transcreedal argument.** The order's audiences are
    listed in ascending distance beginning with "South Asian Muslim devotees, pilgrims at the shrine
    of Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar in Pakpattan (Pakistani Punjab)" (pp. 17–18, folios 2–3); and "The
    same Shaykh Farid ad-Din is so highly respected by Sikhs that they have incorporated his Punjabi
    verses for their holy scripture, the Guru Granth Sahib" (p. 18, folio 3). He is also the root of
    the book's twentieth-century Western line: Hazrat Inayat Khan is "from the lineage of Farid
    ad-Din and Nizam ad-Din" (p. 18, folio 3).
  - **He is the book's extreme case of withdrawal from kings — and the case that broke.** "Perhaps
    no Chishti master was as renowned for his withdrawal from society at large and the company of
    kings in particular as Shaykh Farid ad-Din of Pakpattan… Yet even 'Shaykh Farid Ganj-i Shakar
    was forced by his circumstances to permit his disciples to circulate a begging bowl and collect
    food, [even though] no Chishti saint before him had permitted such a `practice:'[text-layer?]`
    [practice.\"]" (p. 20, folio 5 — the inner quotation is the authors' quotation of another
    writer, **unnamed in the chunk: (not stated)**).
  - **He refused land grants, and his family starved — the row's single most useful fact for the
    economics of this archive.** "Farid ad-Din later refused land grants that were offered to him,
    and routinely gave away all money and other gifts brought to his lodge. As a consequence, his
    family is said to have literally faced starvation on numerous occasions" (p. 81, folio 67).
    **Who offered the grants, when, and of what size: all (not stated).** He also refused to lobby a
    sultan: asked for a letter to "Sultan Ghiyas ad-Din Balban", he wrote "I entrust this man's fate
    to God and then to you. If you give him something, God is the giver but you will be thanked for
    it. If you give him nothing, God is the preventer, and you are `excused:'\"[text-layer?]`
    [excused.\"]" (p. 170, folio 158).
  - **Mughal legitimacy is anchored on him.** Akbar at Fatehpur Sikri drew "through him on the
    spiritual blessing that derived from his ascetic patron, Shaykh Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar in
    the Punjab" (p. 112, folio 99), so that "Through a twofold, redoubled Chishti loyalty, Akbar
    could anchor his imperial legitimacy in two provinces adjacent to Uttar Pradesh, the Punjab and
    Rajasthan" (pp. 112–113, folios 99–100). A Mughal grandee's devotion is recorded separately:
    "Though Khan-i Khanan Muhammad Bayram Khan enjoyed a high position in the Mughal court and had
    indescribable wealth and power at his command, nonetheless, with respect to the dervishes, he
    may be described as one who had faith in, and love of, them", with a four-line poem on Farid's
    name that he "is said to have composed" (p. 168, folio 156).
  - **The editors' own headnote.** He is "an extraordinary ascetic, noted for his controlled
    ecstasy; yet unlike Qutb ad-Din, he was also a loyal father and husband", "Reckoned among the
    greatest of the saints and the foremost of the Sufis, according to 'Abd al-Haqq" (p. 167,
    folio 155), and his life yields "the three characteristics of pious Chishtis: eschewing all
    stable income, engaging in meditation, often abetted by music, and training worthy disciples"
    (p. 167, folio 155). In the structural parallel: "The third Shaykh in each case (Farid ad-Din in
    the thirteenth century, Fakhr ad-Din in the eighteenth century) consolidates and extends the
    order" (p. 124, folio 111).

### darbar-hazrat-khawaja-shah-muhammad-sulaiman-taunsvi-r-a — Darbar Hazrat Khawaja Shah Muhammad Sulaiman Taunsvi, Taunsa Sharif

Thirty-three bullets across seven chunks, and the only Pakistani shrine besides Pakpattan that the
book treats institutionally. The book's forms are **"Shaykh Sulayman Taunsawi"** (running form),
**"Shah Sulayman"**, **"Shaykh Sulayman"**, **"Shah Sulayman Tawnsawi"**, **`Sulayman Tawnsawi`** in
the Persian bibliography, and once, in the index, **`Sui[text-layer?]` [Sul-] `Tawnsvi`** (p. 249,
folio 240). The place is written **"Taunsa"** and, in one manuscript note, **`Tawnsah`** (p. 243,
folio 233).

- **Saint**
  - **Death year, printed four times and consistent.** "(d. 1850)" (p. 33, folio 19); "(d. 1850)"
    (p. 121, folio 108); "Sulayman Taunsawi (1267/1850)" (p. 124, folio 111); and, as a
    bibliography heading, `Sulayman Tawnsawi (d. `I267/1850)[text-layer?]` [1267/1850)]` (p. 237,
    folio 227) and `Sulayman Tawnsawi (d. 1267/1850)` (p. 240, folio 230). **The archive row's
    `figure_died` 1850 and the book agree.**
  - **Silsila, refined by the book into a three-part name.** "One of the main branchings within the
    Chishtiyya, for instance, is the Nizami-Chishtiyya, derived from Shaykh Nizam ad-Din Awliya'
    (d. 1325); a sub-subbranch is the `Sulay-maniyya`, linked to Shaykh Sulayman Taunsawi (d. 1850)"
    (p. 33, folio 19), and "Hence a master of the latter subbranch would be properly identified as a
    Sulaymani-Nizami-Chishti shaykh" (p. 33, folio 19). Elsewhere it is "the Chishti Nizami
    Sulaymaniyya lineage in the colonial period" (p. 124, folio 111). **Directly usable against the
    archive row's recorded "Chishti-Nizami".**
  - **His pir, and the full printed line above him.** "he himself was the major successor to Nur
    Muhammad Maharvi (d. 1790)" (p. 121, folio 108), and Nur Muhammad "was himself the fourth in
    line of succession from Shah Kalim Allah" (p. 123, folio 110). The sequence as printed: "Kalim
    Allah `Jahan-abadi` (1142/1729), Nizam ad-Din Awrangabadi (1142/1730), Fakhr ad-Din Dihlawi
    (1199/1784), Nur Muhammad Maharvi (1205/1790), Sulayman Taunsawi (1267/1850)" (pp. 123–124,
    folios 110–111). **Fakhr ad-Din Dihlawi's Common Era year is printed four different ways across
    the book — see Doubts.**
  - **The authors refuse the "Chishti revival" label for him.** "Rather than categorize him as part
    of the so-called Chishti revival, we prefer to locate him in the third cycle of the Chishti
    Nizamiyya, a historical period (`eighteenth-twenty-first` centuries)" (p. 121, folio 108); they
    add that "numerous scholars cite him as a founding figure of the Chishti recuperation in
    eighteenth century North India" but that "we lack a complete picture of him" (p. 121, folio
    108). **Note the book's own "eighteenth century North India" for a man it dates to 1850.**
  - **Successors, named and dated, with one internal contradiction.** Khayrabadi "became one of the
    major successors of Shaykh Sulayman" (p. 71, folio 57). "The same pattern of educational effort
    was followed by his successor Muhammad 'Aqil (d. 1814), and the latter's disciple Gul Muhammad
    Ahmadpuri (d. 1827), both of whom founded religious schools in the southern Punjab that provided
    free instruction and lodging to hundreds of `students.''[text-layer?]` [students."]"
    (pp. 121–122, folios 108–109). **The book gives Muhammad 'Aqil's death as 1814 here and as 1813
    three pages later — "since his master was Muhammad 'Aqil (d. 1813)" (p. 124, folio 111). Both
    are recorded; neither is preferred.** The lineage travels: "Sufi Saheb (d. 1916), the Chishti
    responsible for introducing Chishti Nizami Sulaymaniyya into South Africa" (p. 124, folio 111).
- **Shrine / Darbar**
  - **Location, given three ways and all on the Indus.** "the shrine of Sulayman at Taunsa on the
    upper Indus is remote, and difficult of access" (p. 99, folio 86); "branch shrines, like Taunsa
    on the west bank of the Indus" (p. 111, folio 98); "his principal lodge in Taunsa on the upper
    Indus" (p. 121, folio 108). He is also placed there alive, receiving a returning disciple:
    Khayrabadi's circuit of tombs ended "before returning to the living master, Shaykh Sulayman in
    Taunsa" (p. 71, folio 57).
  - **Classified as a branch shrine of Ajmer, and the practice in question is hereditary
    custodianship.** "Variations of this practice can be traced from the principal Chishti shrine in
    Ajmer to branch shrines, like Taunsa on the west bank of the Indus" (p. 111, folio 98) — the
    practice being hereditary custodianship under the title *gaddi nishin* (pp. 110–111, folios

    Sulayman Tawnsawi, both discussed at length by David Gilmartin" (p. 116, folio 103).
  - **The shrine holds a library, and the book recommends it to future scholars.** "we can say with
    confidence that future scholarship will benefit from the library built up and retained at his
    principal lodge in Taunsa on the upper Indus" (p. 121, folio 108). **No catalogue, no holdings
    count and no access arrangement is given: (not stated).**

- **Practices & Events**
  - **The institutions he founded — the row's core economic and educational material.** "It is,
    above all, the combination of spiritual valence and political activism that characterizes
    Shaykh Sulayman. He consolidated the order by stressing learning through establishment of
    institutions: not only the academy called `Dar al- 'Ulum` at Taunsa on the upper Indus, but also
    other religious schools throughout the Punjab, linking each to instruction in the mystical
    insights of the Nizami Chishti branch of Sufism" (p. 121, folio 108). **No founding year, no

  - **He is the channel by which Nizami Chishti Sufism expanded into the upper Punjab** — Hafiz
    Sayyid Muhammad 'Ali Khayrabadi marks "the expansion of the Nizami branch of Chishti Sufism

    row.** "Consider the shrine at Taunsa, which witnessed a conflict between two of the branches of

    other to Mihr 'Ali Shah (d. 1937). Political power struggles internal to the Sulaymaniyya can be
    traced to British manipulation and also the rivalry of local Sikhs, Hindus, and Muslim

    political contestation is only part of the narrative" (p. 124, folio 111). **Allah Bakhsh is

  - **Partisanship against the Sikhs, claimed as a first in Chishti history.** "He urged his

    (p. 121, folio 108). Immediately qualified: "Notable, however, is the effort of Shah Sulayman to
    maintain cordial relations with Punjabi Hindus even while he opposed their Sikh neighbors"
    (p. 121, folio 108).

    original Persian works "expand and multiply in number and geographical extent right up to the
    end of the nineteenth century (see, for example, the numerous biographies of Shaykh Sulayman
    Taunsawi)" (p. 16, folio not stated).

- **Practices & Events**
  - **Samaʿ — he is a theorist of it, he criticises it, and he practises it.** Mole "cites four
    Indian writers as important theorists of `sama'[text-layer?]` [samāʿ]: 'Ali Hujwiri, Gisu Daraz,

    conflicting views toward `sama'` that were voiced by contemporary and earlier Sufis, but he
    severely criticizes sensual gratification through empathetic ecstasy" (p. 52, folio 38). When "a
    contemporary traditionist reports to Hujwiri that he has written a book on the permissibility of

    goes on to admit that he himself practices `sama:[text-layer?]` [samāʿ.]" (p. 52, folio 38). The
    constraint: "To the extent that Hujwiri affirms empathetic ecstasy, he is constrained by the
    well-attested hadith of the Prophet, 'When you recite the `Qur'an`, you should weep, and if you
    cannot weep, then you should try to weep.'" (p. 52, folio 38).
  - **The rebuke he records against himself** — a "venerable shaykh who indulged Hujwiri's desire

    your nature and keep you back from higher things.'" (p. 52, folio 38) — and "it is perhaps with
    the shaykh's reprimand in mind that Hujwiri closes his section on `sama'` and concludes his book

    `sama:[text-layer?]` [samāʿ.]" (p. 52, folio 38).
  - **He is twice the benchmark for how a treatise on samaʿ should be built.** Mas'ud Bakk "Like
    Hujwiri, … proceeds from a general description of hearing and its role in Islamic revelation to

    equivocal posture of Hujwiri and `~shayri[text-layer?]` [Qushayri] vis-a-vis sama'" (p. 205,

- **Significance**
  - **The authors' verdict on his historical importance, and the most citable thing in this row.**
    "Almost despite himself, Hujwiri gives some of the claims made on behalf of `sama'` and for the
    first time links the topic of `sama'` to the discussion of the grades of ecstasy. It is the
    linkage of `sama'` to empathetic ecstasy that made possible the later and distinctive Chishti
    contribution to `sama'` theory" (p. 53, folio 39).
  - **He is the first of the two texts on which the whole "rhetoric of decline" rests.** "consider
    Shaykh 'Ali Hujwiri (d. 1074), who quoted the truism of Abu al-Hasan of Fushanja: that Sufism
    was in the beginning a reality without a name, but it had become over time a name without a
    reality. Hujwiri offers no commentary on this startling observation" (p. 22, folio 7).
  - **His classification of the orders, and its afterlife.** "Some of the more `inclusive-and
    often unwieldy-tazkiras[text-layer?]` [inclusive—and often unwieldy—tazkiras] took their cue
    from the eleventh-century theorist of Persian Sufism, Shaykh 'Ali Hujwiri" (p. 38, folio 24);
    "He classified the Sufi orders into 12, linking each to a famous early Sufi master, despite the
    fact that there was seldom a correspondence between these early ascetics and the well-known Sufi
    orders `oflater[text-layer?]` [of later] times" (p. 38, folio 24). The afterlife: "Both
    Sultanate and Mughal tazkira writers increased the number of families by two, and most of the
    major Chishti and non-Chishti tazkiras liked to categorize the Sufi orders as dispersed through
    \"14 `families:'[text-layer?]` [families,\"] of which the Chishtiyya were prominent. Even here,
    however, there were major disagreements about how to project \"the 14 families.\"" (p. 38,
    folio 24). **A second count is given elsewhere: "Hujwiri's theoretical list of ten approved and
    two heretical orders", juxtaposed by Abu al-Fazl with the 14 families of Lata'if-i Ashrafi
    (p. 72, folio 58), and "the list of ten acceptable Sufi schools or teachings compiled by
    Hujwiri in the eleventh century, which he acknowledged was a theoretical construct based on
    individual masters, not a current institutional reality" (p. 72, folio 58). 12, 14 and "ten
    approved and two heretical" all appear; the book does not say they are the same count.**
  - **Reading his book is the stated cause of a documented conversion.** The Lennard brothers "had
    converted to Islam in 1936 after reading the Sufi classic *The Unveiling of the Concealed*, by
    'Ali Hujwiri (d. 1072)", then "left their well-to-do English family and taken the Muslim names
    Faruq Ahmad and Shahid Allah", and "traveled to the Middle East and India in search of a Sufi
    master" (p. 138, folio 125).
  - **A Persian biography of him survives in one manuscript, held at Gulra** — `Muhammad 'Alim.
    Ahwal-i 'Ali Hujwiri, biography of Hujwiri (d. 465/1073). Chs. 4-6 concern his authority over

### Practices, institutions, economy

*Merged from all seventeen chunks. Almost none of it is attached to a Pakistani site — the book's
institutional case studies are Ajmer, Delhi (Nizam ad-Din), Khuldabad and Kalyar. It is included
because it is the fullest account in this corpus of how a Chishti shrine is financed, inherited and
regulated, and because the book itself says the pattern generalises: "The same process operates in
other shrines. At Ajmer it is simply cast in its broadest, and most complex, pattern" (p. 115,
folio 102).*

- **ʿUrs — definition, etymology, dating and dispute.** The book glosses *ʿurs* from *Fawa'id
  al-fu'ad*, "p. 209, gives the etymology of 'urs as 'getting married,' but also mentions another
  meaning, 'the alighting of a caravan at night'" (p. 210, folio 199); dates the term's currency —
  "Jurists such as the Baghdadian Ibn al-Jawzi (d. 1201) criticized Sufi 'urs festivities, and the
  Mevlevi Sufis in Anatolia used the term 'urs in the thirteenth century" (p. 210, folio 199); and
  makes it constitutive of the order — "They celebrate the death anniversaries of their masters"
  (p. 21, folio 6). There is a collective feast: "there is a Muslim equivalent of the Christian All
  Saints' Day on the first Thursday in the month of `Rajah[text-layer?]` [Rajab], when one may
  commemorate all the saints' festivals at once" (p. 106, folio 93). **A reformer describes his own
  ʿurs, and it is an ʿurs without samaʿ:** "Every year at the time of the passing of the blessed
  spirit of my master and guide, I perform a virtuous act. First there is Qur'an recitation, then
  sometimes if time permits, a birthday poem in honor of the Prophet Muhammad is recited, food is
  prepared for those present, and the virtue of these deeds along with other things is dedicated to
  the shaykh. My custom has been that no sama' ever occurs, whether with voice alone or with
  instruments." (p. 145, folio 133). ʿUrs attendance is a measure of a suborder's cohesion: those in
  the Nizami line "do not all attend the `'ursofNizam[text-layer?]` ['urs of Nizam] ad-Din, whereas
  the Sabiri Chishti shaykhs all attend the 'urs in Kalyar in the company of their disciples"
  (p. 130, folio 117).
- **ʿUrs income, and the lunar-calendar numeral that must be checked.** "The biggest income from
  offerings comes at the time of the annual 'urs, celebrated during the first six days of the month
  of Rajab (as with all observances in the **356**-day Muslim lunar calendar, this occurs nine days
  earlier each solar year, so it has no fixed date in the Gregorian calendar)" (p. 115, folio 102)
  — Ajmer. **The figure "356" is reported exactly as printed and no reading is asserted here; see
  Doubts.**
- **Printed ʿurs calendars are a commodity, and one is sold with the shajara.** "today one can still
  acquire current Urdu almanacs printed in Bombay and Lahore that prominently feature the death
  anniversaries of Sufi saints of the Indian subcontinent" (p. 105, folio 92); and, on the facing
  page of a ready-made printed lineage document, "a grid forms a calendar of saints' death
  anniversaries, with some spaces filled in but others left blank for the owner to fill in with the
  names of favorite saints." (p. 150, folio 138). Three modern printed calendars are cited with
  imprints, the last a Lahore one: `Hakim Mawlawi Muhammad Barakat 'Ali, Asrar-i 'alam jantri 1987
  (Lahore: Maktaba-i Rafiq-i Ruzgar, 1986), pp. 19-24` (p. 211, folio 200).
- **The Appendix is itself an ʿurs calendar, transcribed from a Taunsa manuscript.** Its provenance:
  "The following list is given as an addendum to a manuscript of Adab al-muridin by Muhammad
  Chishti, copied in Taunsa ca. 1790 (personal collection of Carl Ernst; thanks to Mr. Khalil
  al-Rahman Dawoodi of Lahore). It lists the death-anniversary dates of important saints of the
  Chishti lineage, arranged in sequence according to the months and days of the Muslim lunar
  calendar." (p. 159, folio 147). Its Pakistan-facing entries are "Muharram / 5. Farid ad-Din
  `Mas'udAjodhani[text-layer?]` [Mas'ud Ajodhani] [Ganj-i Shakar] (d. 664/1265)" (p. 159, folio 147)
  and "`Rajah[text-layer?]` [Rajab] / 6. Mu'in ad-Din Chishti (d. 633/1236)" (p. 160, folio 148).
  Two entries carry **no year at all** — "28. Nasir ibn Muhibb ad-Din (?)" (the question mark the
  book's own) and "22. Majd ibn Siraj" (p. 160, folio 148) — and one carries **two possible days**,
  "21 (or 17). 'Ali ibn Abi Talib (d. 40/661)" (p. 160, folio 148).
- **Offerings — what is given, to whom, and what it is worth.** "The pilgrim is also urged to offer
  'sweets, roses, and flowers' at the tomb, or a bit of money, and this is still expected of the
  visitor today" (p. 106, folio 93); "Offering food to the spirits of the saints brings good fortune
  in this life, and pilgrims may also present petitions to the saints" (p. 106, folio 93); and ritual
  is sized to the purse — "one should perform only what can be done in accordance with one's
  ability, especially in case of poverty" (p. 106, folio 93). At Ajmer the mechanism is named and
  defined: "namaz, or Islamic ritual prayer, which is the main function of all visitors to the
  shrine, became linked to nazar-o-niyaz, monies that are paid to the khuddam, or resident
  functionaries, by visitors to Ajmer Sharif" (p. 115, folio 102), formalised by a **written contract
  between pilgrim and custodian** — "His relationship with pilgrims is formalized through a contract
  called a wikalat-nama", "a sort of pledge … by which a devotee bound himself, his family members,
  relatives, clansmen … to a particular khadim", in return for which "he receives all the
  nazar-o-niyaz (offerings), whether in cash or kind, gold and silver articles, animals, and a
  one-quarter share of the cauldron food offered by the devotee" (p. 115, folio 102). Hindu clients
  are part of it: "the traffic in Hindu clients, not only in temporary nazar-o-niyaz but also
  permanent land grants, was substantial" (p. 115, folio 102).
- **Grave offerings as the endowment of a saint's dependents, stated by the saint himself.** Nizam
  ad-Din's servants ask "We are poor men. After you have gone, what will become of us?" and are told
  "The charity that will arrive at my grave will suffice for you"; asked who will divide it, he
  answers "That man who is willing to relinquish his own portion" (p. 175, folio 163). The same
  deathbed passage orders the khanqah's goods liquidated — "You must distribute everything, except
  the minimum that is nec-essary for the daily subsistence of the dervishes", then even that: "These
  are the effects of a dead man-why should they be preserved? Give it all away and sweep the room
  clean." — with the outcome reported flatly: "As soon as they cleared the storerooms, a host of
  people gathered and snatched up the goods." (p. 175, folio 163).
- **Futuh, kasb, the begging bowl and the refusal of land — the Chishti economic doctrine.** The
  early rule: "The earliest Indian Chishti masters recommended avoiding formal ties through
  endowments, yet they accepted donations in cash or kind with one stipulation: that they be quickly
  spent for appropriate purposes, such as food, modest clothes, and living quarters, as well as
  ritual necessities, including assemblies of music (majalis-i sama')" (p. 35, folio 21), phrased by
  Nizam ad-Din as "'no rejecting, no asking, no saving.'" (p. 35, folio 21). *Futuh* is named as the
  mechanism twice — "As charitable offerings [futuh] began to arrive, he also made use of them"
  (p. 165, folio 153); "the doors of futuh (charity) were opened and the world benefited from the
  outpourings of his generosity and kindness" (p. 172, folio 160). The later rule keeps the
  principle with two named exceptions, one of them a wage: "these later Chishtis made it a principle
  always to distribute the alms they received, except under special circumstances (such as personal
  illness for Shah Kalim Allah, or the desire to pay the qawwals on Friday night for Shaykh Nizam
  ad-Din Awrangabadi)" (p. 123, folio 110) — **paying the qawwals on Friday night is the only named
  recurring shrine expenditure in the book.** The doctrine on kings is eight couplets: "Never seek
  kindness and generosity from kings; / When you take a village or land, you will fall down in front
  of a door", against "Do not go to Kings uncalled; but if they summon you, go instantly" (p. 19,
  folio 4). Compromises are recorded: the begging bowl permitted under duress (p. 20, folio 5);
  "Descendants of a leading Chishti master in South India, for instance, accepted land grants that
  in effect made them rural gentry" (p. 20, folio 5); Shah Kalim Allah "had to forgo dependence on
  donations and rent out property in order to recover his health" (p. 20, folio 5). A state stipend
  is accepted under a stated test: Tatar Khan's "rescript for a daily prayer ration of 80 tankas"
  was ruled acceptable "Since the prayer ration `carne[text-layer?]` [came] without any request or
  tale on your part, it is in the category of voluntary gifts" (p. 199, folio not stated). And the
  authors' verdict on the whole: "However much these ideals were compromised, nevertheless they
  remained ideals throughout" (p. 109, folio 96). The book's bibliography makes this a field in its
  own right, with four Riazul Islam articles on *futuh*, *zanbil* and *kasb* (pp. 227–228, folios
  216–217).
- **Land grants as the origin of a shrine's long-term trouble — the book's sharpest economic claim.**
  "Had Akbar limited his largesse to the construction of buildings or even direct expenditures of
  charity for the poor or the upkeep of custodian families, long-term problems might have been
  avoided. But instead, he offered land grants for the maintenance of the dargah. They were
  continued by his son and successor, Jahangir, but also other royal visitors (maharajas, rajas, and
  nawabs of princely states), with the result that the positions of volunteer service that dated
  back to the thirteenth century became linked to real estate holdings that yielded and continue to
  yield considerable annual income" (p. 114, folio 101). The Mughal system generally: "Already by
  the sixteenth century the Mughal emperors had established elaborate bureaucratic hierarchies that
  dispensed royal funds and land revenue to Sufi shrines, often appointing the trustees and
  regulating the internal affairs of the shrines as well. Shrines were exempted from ordinary taxes,
  on the condition that attendants pray for the welfare of the ruling dynasty" (p. 35, folio 21).
  And the motive: "They foresaw more benefits and fewer conflicts with dead saints than with living
  exemplars" (p. 35, folio 21). One lodge's transition is given whole: "When Shaykh Burhan ad-Din
  Gharib's lodge ceased to be controlled by a teaching master after the death of his successor, the
  trustees and attendants sought donations and eventually land endowments from the sultans of the
  Deccan. By the eighteenth century, the shrines of Shaykh Burhan ad-Din and his disciples had
  become extensions of the authority of the court, with royal music balconies even being built into
  the shrines themselves for the performance of court ceremonies" (p. 35, folio 21).
- **Custodianship, succession and the two kinds of descendant.** "the continuation of the shrines
  under the management of biological descendants of the shaykhs, many of whom compete with each
  other for the privilege and the prestige of being addressed as gaddi nishin, 'the one who sits on
  the throne'" (pp. 110–111, folios 97–98), with the verdict that "That continuity, whether in the
  biological or spiritual line, is ultimately a more important guarantor of institutional coherence
  than any single source of outside support" (p. 111, folio 98). The two lines are named in Persian:
  "the farzandan-i nan (those who claim direct biological connection to the saint and therefore
  maintain his dargah after his 'death' through Muslim inheritance law) and the farzandan-i jan
  (those who see themselves as the saint's spiritual descendants, and often are loosely connected to
  the dargah)", and "the two offspring overlap, and interact, one with the other" (p. 117,
  folio 104). Against this the first cycle is emphatically not hereditary: "None of the initial five
  Chishti masters of North India was succeeded by a blood relative" (p. 21, folio 6). Succession is
  a documented office — "He drew up their diplomas (khilafat-namas) according to a strict procedure
  that Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar employed" (p. 194, folio 182) — and a material transfer: "clothing
  him with the robe he wore, he put him in his own place and gave him a succession document"
  (p. 199, folio not stated). Relics as inheritable property are ruled on directly: "Concerning the
  blessed objects that the disciple has received from the master, if he decides to have them buried
  with himself, that is proper. If, on the other hand, he decrees that they are to be given to some
  worthy descendant, that also is proper" (p. 176, folio 164) — **burial and hereditary transfer as
  the two licit destinations, which is exactly how shrine relics enter a sajjada-nashin family.**
  One investiture is performed by the shrine's manager out of the offerings rather than by the
  saint: Iqbal, "the manager of the dargah, brought him a cap and a shirt that had been part of a
  donation… and said, 'Now you are indeed a `successor:'[text-layer?]` [successor."] Shaykh Nizam
  ad-Din said nothing, but his silence was assumed to be an indication of his approval" (p. 191,
  folio 179).
- **State regulation, colonial and post-colonial, dated.** At Ajmer: "the British Deputy Commissioner
  took on the task of choosing between claimants to the office of chief shrine custodian, `it is
  or[text-layer?]` [that is, the] sajjada nishin. From 1867, the affairs of the shrine were regulated
  by the Religious Endowments Act of 1863" (p. 115, folio 102); "The family that was in charge of the
  shrine from 1912 to 1947 migrated to Pakistan at Partition, and the Chief Commissioner then
  selected a new incumbent" (p. 115, folio 102); appeals "resulted in the passing of the Dargah
  Khwaja Sahib Act in 1955, a law designed specifically to regulate the affairs of that Sufi shrine"
  (pp. 115–116, folios 102–103). A dated income history follows: "From 1951 to 1971 the remuneration
  of nazar-o-niyaz was very low indeed. Some khuddam complain of eating dust during this period.
  Then, with 1971, things changed for the better, for two reasons: the war for Bangladeshi
  independence, and the recycling of funds from Indians working in the Persian Gulf" (p. 116,
  folio 103). The khadim body is counted: "a group numbering perhaps 1200 adult males with families,
  most living close to the shrine", "aptly described as a 'tribe'… with many clans, each staking out
  some claim to the vast responsibilities and resources of the shrine complex" (p. 115, folio 102).
  **On the Pakistani side the book says only "Government manipulation, as in Pakistan's control of
  the 'urs of Baba Farid" (p. 157, folio 145). The word Auqaf appears nowhere in the book:
  (not stated).**
- **Langar — the practice is described, the word is not used.** "They aim at poverty and denial, and
  they keep company with the poor and beggars, giving them food… They themselves wash the hands of
  the stranger, and themselves provide fire and food to the poor. They never give the rich man a
  place higher than the poor man, and their feasts are very bountiful" (p. 21, folio 6). **The word
  *langar* occurs exactly once in the whole book, inside a bibliography title, and the book spells it
  `Lunger`: `"The Shrine and Lunger of Golra Sharif"` (p. 227, folio 216). No description of any
  langar is given anywhere: (not stated).**
- **Samaʿ and qawwali — the order's institutional signature, and the archive's richest performance
  material.** The Chishtis "followed a single ritual activity more zealously than did any other
  brotherhood in the subcontinent. That practice was sama'… `Sarna'[text-layer?]` [Sama'] cannot be
  separated from meditation through recollection (zikr)" (p. 20, folio 5). It is congregational by
  definition — "hearing chanted verse (with or without accompanying instruments) in the company of
  others also seeking to participate in the dynamic dialogue between a human lover and the Divine
  Beloved" (p. 49, folio 35) — and graded by access: "sama' should not be made available to
  beginners" (p. 49, folio 35). Assembly rules: no female vocal accompaniment; a beautiful male
  voice; the reed pipe forbidden and drum and tambourine permitted; "`sarna'` is to be conducted in
  a suitable place" (p. 51, folio 37). Two named assembly types and their schedule: "Ritual space is
  also generally set aside for Thursday evening qawwali music performance. Mahfil-i 'amm (public
  assembly) and mahfil-i khass (private assembly) are musical sessions for distinctive audiences,
  but in major shrines like Nizam ad-Din and Ajmer Sharif, qawwali occurs every day" (p. 109,
  folio 96). **The economy of shrine performance is set out in a twentieth-century qawwali manual
  the book quotes at length but does not name, date or attribute: (not stated)** (p. 59, folio 45,
  printed under the head `THE MANNERS OF THE `MusiCIAN[text-layer?]` [MUSICIAN]`). Its rules on
  payment: "the singer should not be greedy… Whatever he receives he should regard as a blessing";
  "Nor should one first of all settle on the amount of payment, in the style of courtesans… This is
  the custom of courtesans and low-caste musical performers, who first set the price for performing
  or singing, not even setting foot outside their house until the payment is made"; and the one
  permitted charge, with the liability placed on the host — "if the singer is invited out, it is no
  problem if he asks for travel expenses, rather it is the duty of the one who invites him to send
  adequate travel expenses in advance" (p. 59, folio 45). Patronage is deprecated: "the enunciation
  and attention of the singer should be directed towards the people of taste and ecstasy, not
  towards the rich and powerful because of greedy hopes" (p. 60, folio 46). The programme order is
  fixed: "One begins with praise of God and adoration of the Prophet. At the end, one recites the
  unity of God" (p. 60, folio 46). **The recording industry, dated:** "In the early part of the
  century, the British Gramophone Company of India monopolized the recording and distribution of
  qawwali music, under the catchall category of \"Urdu Muslim `Islamic:'[text-layer?]` [Islamic."]"
  (p. 149, folio 137); "Then `witl![text-layer?]` [with] the advent of film in the 1930s, live
  qawwali performances were also introduced, especially in the Bombay productions from the 1950s
  on."; "especially from `tl!e[text-layer?]` [the] 1970s on, more and more qawwali recordings
  featured textual phrases in Arabic, appealing to the re-Islamized sensibilities of Pakistan
  expatriate workers and overseas Muslims." (all p. 149, folio 137). **State policy on both sides
  of the border:** "in both Pakistan and India the state-operated radio and television did not
  promote the spiritual practices of either the Chishtiyya or other orders" (p. 155, folio 143), and
  India's post-independence use of qawwali "to promote religious tolerance" (p. 149, folio 137). A
  French state subsidy created the European market (pp. 150–151, folios 138–139).
- **Ziyarat: ritual, adab, and the argument that defends it.** "There is an emphasis on determining
  the exact hour and day of death for commemoration, though this is not indispensable. Food and
  drink also play an important role and are to be offered to the spirits of the dead and then
  distributed on whatever scale the pilgrim can afford" (p. 106, folio 93). "Like the hajj, ziyarat
  calls for circumambulation, in this case of the tomb rather than of the Ka'ba"; "Some enthusiastic
  pilgrims actually found ziyarat to be superior to the hajj" (p. 107, folio 94). Adab: "One should
  especially avoid turning one's back on the saint's tomb or turning one's feet disrespectfully in
  that direction"; pilgrims "express their love and respect for the saints by kissing and touching
  their eyes to the tombs" (p. 107, folio 94). Prostration is contested and permitted on precedent —
  "The Chishti master Nizam ad-Din Awliya' was uncomfortable with the practice but permitted it
  since it was an established custom with his predecessors" (pp. 107–108, folios 94–95), Simnani
  distinguishing "worship (`'iba-dat[text-layer?]` ['ibadat]) of the person" from "spontaneous
  respect (ta'zim)" (p. 108, folio 95). Its legal status is modest by the tradition's own account:
  "the pilgrimage rituals are harmless but not required" (p. 212, folio 201). **The authors refuse
  the "Hindu survival" explanation, and this is load-bearing for this archive's Muslim-shrine
  entries:** "Protestant British civil servants and modern Muslim reformers alike have often seen in
  this ritual the insidious influence of Indian paganism … but a closer look reveals that the case
  is not so simple. Hindu practices undoubtedly occur at some Muslim shrines. The presence of Hindu
  practices at a Muslim shrine cannot, however, explain centuries of participation of educated Sufi
  masters in pilgrimage, for they found ziyarat pilgrimage to be an authentic expression of Islamic
  piety, `Qur' anic[text-layer?]` [Qur'anic] in spirit and firmly based on the model of the Prophet
  Muhammad" (pp. 103–104, folios 90–91).
- **Shrine architecture, typology and the unrecorded mass.** "it might be more correct to describe
  Sufi shrines as mosques with funerary functions, since the tombs invariably have an orientation to
  the direction of Mecca, and large mausolea almost always feature a qibla niche in the appropriate
  wall"; "The tomb was in fact an untypical form of architecture in Hindu India, where cremation was
  the preferred method of disposal of the dead" (p. 108, folio 95). Geography is a typology: "The
  tombs that are the goals of pilgrims have widely divergent locations, from the desert to the edge
  of the city, and even occasionally in the heart of an imperial capital" (p. 99, folio 86); the
  chilla-gah is its own pilgrimage class (p. 109, folio 96). **The negative that matters most to
  this archive:** "there are countless minor Chishti shrines found all over the Asian subcontinent.
  These include tombs of saints who have never been known outside their own localities, or who have
  been described in texts though their shrines remain obscure. But there are many more whose history
  will never be known" (p. 100, folio 87). One building history is given in three undated stages, at
  Ajmer (pp. 161–162, folios 149–150); one shrine is expanded by overseas remittance — "a successful
  disciple from South Africa committed funds to expand the tomb into the structure that now sits on
  `Tara-garb` Hill" (p. 125, folio 112).
- **Initiation, shajara and regalia.** The Chishti rite is "well documented, at least from the
  fourteenth century": fast, alms, ritual prayer; "The master had to accept him with a handshake;
  his head had to be shaved; and he had to be invested with a cloak (or other emblematic garment)";
  hair cut from right then left, a hat placed, two cycles of prayer, prostration at the master's
  feet, "Rising, he presents some gift to the master and joins other companions of the assembly"
  (pp. 38–39, folios 24–25). **Women's initiation avoids contact by a named device:** "The crucial
  step was for the woman to place her hand into a cup of water, in this way avoiding inappropriate
  physical contact. The master, following the example of the Prophet, would then put his own hand in
  the water and proceed to administer the oath" (p. 39, folio 25). The *shajara* is both document
  and object: "there are shrines in India and Pakistan where genealogical scrolls extend to hundreds
  of feet. These more complicated diagrams require oral commentary to be understood" (p. 37,
  folio 23); reciting the chain is itself devotional, and "those with more links had greater merit,
  just as additional lamps provide more light to a room" (p. 37, folio 23). Print turned it into a
  commodity: "the publisher makes available a large stack of ready-made lineage documents, with a
  blank for the would-be initiate and the master to inscribe their own names." (p. 150, folio 138).
  Regalia are itemised — turban, cap, staff, patched cloak (khirqa), Qur'an, prayer carpet, wooden
  shoes (p. 166, folio 154); hat and rosary (p. 192, folio 180) — and rationed: "only four have been
  given with the intention of conferring spiritual successorship; the others have been cloaks of
  blessing" (p. 176, folio 164), **the four unnamed: (not stated)**.
- **Zikr, with counts.** "Always they sit from the end of the night until morning, and after dawn
  prayer they always recite the vocal Chishti zikr 7,000 times… most reaching 30,000 zikr; 20,000
  zikr or 18,000 zikr is their usual custom" (p. 45, folio 31), with the economic consequence the
  book draws itself: "The time demands of this practice also clearly prevented serious Chishtis from
  having regular jobs" (p. 45, folio 31). Vernacular instruction is licensed: "It is right if one
  instructs the non-Arab disciple with expressions in Hindi or Persian or whatever he understands"
  (p. 47, folio 33). The index gives `Sarna' (listening to music)` the longest run of any practice
  term (p. 249, folio 240).
- **Print, periodicals and the internet as the modern Sufi economy.** "in the principal locations for
  the introduction of printing technology on a mass basis in the nineteenth century (Cairo,
  Istanbul, Tehran, and Delhi), the main patrons of publication, aside from governments, were Sufi
  orders." (p. 149, folio 137). One master's business is described in detail: books "discussed in
  chronological order, why he composed them, how well they have sold, and their current prices and
  availability" (p. 130, folio 117); "many of his disciples have never met him and have pledged
  their allegiance by post (he is said to have initiated 60,000 disciples by 1919)" (p. 130,
  folio 117); a hostile critic "claims that Nizami rose from being an itinerant bookseller to being
  a millionaire" and that some books "were written by hacks employed for the purpose" (p. 129,
  folio 116). **Mass-printed saint imagery is sold at shrines:** "images of Sufi saints are available
  from many shrines and at every festival" (p. 150, folio 138), the practice "criticized as a
  heretical innovation" and dating "from the early nineteenth century". The book lists the Chishti
  web presence it found, with URLs as printed (p. 156, folio 144), and notes the filter: "Issues of
  cost and access to equipment at the present time limit the extensive use of the Internet in
  developing countries to elites." (p. 156, folio 144).
- **Reform movements as the standing threat to shrine practice.** "The reformist threats of the
  Jama'at-i Islami and the Tablighis overlap on this point: Even though both adopt a style of
  leadership that presumes the authority of a Sufi master, they try to annul the traditional order
  and their sites, especially at Nizamuddin in Delhi, perhaps because of its enormous symbolic
  capital" (p. 117, folio 104); with the Tablighi Jama'at "reformism amounted to a sublimation and
  simplification of Sufi piety. In the end, the Tablighis rejected institutional Sufism altogether"
  (p. 120, folio 107). **A four-part explanation of shrine decline the authors endorse:** "Claudia
  Liebeskind has described the decline of three Sufi shrines in Uttar Pradesh as a result of the
  departure of former elites to Pakistan, the rise of anti-Sufi reformism, plus the many
  secularizing tendencies of life under colonial rule" (p. 213, folio 202). The colonial structural
  change is stated directly: "From being a privileged group often supported by state patronage, the
  Nizami Chishtis had been forced to become contenders in a complex society in which they were
  threatened by powerful opponents (British, Hindu, or reformist Muslim) even as they offered new
  ways to reach mass audiences" (p. 131, folio 118).
- **An organisation of shrine custodians, dated, with its patrons and its four aims.** "In 1908 he
  gathered young hereditary shrine custodians (sajjadas), and some famous patrons such as Azad and
  Shibli Nu'mani, to form a group called the Halqa-i Masha'ikh (or 'Circle of Masters'), in order to
  reform institutional Sufism in India and support its authentic teachings. Their aims were to
  spread and preserve Sufism, to unite the Sufi shaykhs within one body, to reform customs at 'urs
  festivals and khanaqahs so as to eliminate practices outside of the shari'a and tariqa, and to
  protect the political rights of Sufi shaykhs" (p. 127, folio 114). Its cost: "In 1928 someone shot
  at him, but killed one of his relatives instead" (p. 127, folio 114), and British surveillance
  made meetings "impossible… it was only in 1917 that this surveillance was lifted by the
  commissioner of Delhi, Hayley" (p. 127, folio 114).

### Arguments and interpretations

- **The five-cycle periodization, in full, and the source criticism built into it.** "We propose five
  divisions … two early periods outside of India followed by three cycles taking place in India" —
  (1) "The formative period (seventh-tenth centuries)"; (2) "The foundational period at Chisht
  (tenth-twelfth centuries)", "known only from fragmentary testimonies in literature of the fourth
  and fifth periods"; (3) "The first cycle of the Indian Chishtiyya (twelfth-fourteenth centuries)",
  whose "primary literary sources are limited to oral traditions, whether actually recorded or later
  imagined"; (4) "The second cycle of the Indian Chishtiyya (fourteenth-eighteenth centuries)", "the
  dispersal of the Chishti order from Delhi to the far corners of the subcontinent", producing "an
  immense biographical literature that frames the narrative of the previous three periods"; (5) "The
  third cycle of the Indian Chishtiyya (eighteenth-twenty-first centuries)", where "The decline of
  Mughal hegemony, along with British ascendancy in India and Wahhabi control of Arabia, led to
  tensions over the internal reform of Sufism" (pp. 27–28, folios 13–14). **Any shrine entry built
  on a thirteenth-century hagiography is, on the authors' own scheme, built on a fourteenth- to
  eighteenth-century text.**
- **Decline is rhetoric, not diagnosis.** "we avoid taking the rhetoric of decline as the criterion
  of Sufi viability. We distinguish between a shortfall in pursuit of ideals, which happens in every
  generation, and a decline in Sufism as a spiritual option from one generation to the next"
  (p. 22, folio 7); "The 'golden age' historiography of Sufism is more related to internal
  imperatives of modernity, including those of Orientalist scholarship, than to the
  self-interpretation of Sufi theorists" (p. 22, folio 7). The charge is laid on both sides: "this
  classical approach to Sufism itself mirrors a strong golden age historiography that is deeply
  etched into Muslim piety" (p. 27, folio 13). **Renewal is distinguished from revival in a
  definition this archive can use:** "Both these metaphors suggest a reanimation of something
  defunct, but to give life to tradition is to make tradition come alive, and that is a work that is
  needed in every generation. It is not subject to rise and decline" (p. 27, folio 13).
- **The order is regional and yet transcreedal, and the tension is the book's subject.** "The
  Chishtiyya is not a pan-Islamic order that happens to have an Indo-Muslim branch. The Chishti order
  stands out, and stands apart, as the major brotherhood to be identified solely with the
  subcontinent and its multiple regional communities"; yet "the Chishtiyya is neither exclusively
  Indian nor Islamic. The Chishtiyya is at once transnational and transcreedal. And it is this
  tension, bordering on a contradiction, that we will explore in the pages that follow" (p. 23,
  folio 8).
- **Sufi orders overlap, and the overlap refutes a political reading of them.** "Unlike the Christian
  monastic orders, which were divided by firm lines of authority and sacrament, Sufi orders
  frequently could overlap one another" (p. 42, folio 28); multiple initiation is "becoming" common
  by the eighteenth century (p. 122, folio 109), and the bibliography records `Dual Qadiri and
  Chishti lineages` (p. 236, folio 226), a `Ghulam Husayn Shah Chishti Sabiri Qadiri Haydarabadi
  Dakani` (p. 244, folio 234) and an index heading `Four-school Sufism 142` (p. 247, folio not
  stated). One master held "no fewer than eight \"trees\" (*shajarat*) of `spirituallineage[text-layer?]`
  [spiritual lineage]" — **and the lines the book then lists do not add to eight; see Doubts**
  (p. 132, folio 119).
- **Genealogy is contested authority, not record.** Hujwiri's twelve, the tazkira writers' fourteen
  and Abu al-Fazl's "ten approved and two heretical" are set side by side (p. 38, folio 24; p. 72,
  folio 58), with the judgement that of five orders traced to Ibn Zayd "only the Chishtis are
  historical" (p. 72, folio 58). A named internal controversy is recorded: a Persian work is "A
  refutation of Shah Wali Allah's claim that the Chishti lineage from Hasan al-Basri to 'Ali is
  spurious" (p. 236, folio 226).
- **Women are almost absent from the hagiographical record, and the book says so as a finding.** "No
  hagiography sets forth a major saintly woman on the same par as the Chishti exemplars discussed
  elsewhere. Instead, pious Muslim women are noted in the literature only as part of the groups
  seeking advice and solace from the major masters" (p. 99, folio 86); "this is part of the famous
  problem of the 'invisibility' of Muslim women" (p. 103, folio 90), and class is the condition of
  survival — "Although relatively few non-elite Muslim women left well-attested literary legacies,
  among upper-class women there was much freer access to education as well as the possibility of
  acting as a patron" (p. 100, folio 87). Against that, Baba Farid's ruling that succession cannot
  go to a woman is quoted and endorsed by Thanvi (p. 135, folio 122), and **the book records no
  counter-instance**.
- **Two textual judgements a user of this archive should carry.** On the aphorisms the appendix
  translates: "The collection of `aphorisms-which[text-layer?]` [aphorisms—which] may or may not have
  been uttered by the `saint-establish[text-layer?]` [saint—establish] a pervasive tone for the
  entire Chishti discipline" (p. 161, folio 149). And on one widely repeated Farid anecdote: "This

- **The appendix's source, named and characterised.** The material comes from "a single, heretofore
  untranslated tazkira", whose author shows "no bias toward any one order or viewpoint", though
  "'Abd al-Haqq accents the pivotal role of the Chishtiyya"; the work "is structured by generations
  and it recounts the lives of great sufi masters from the twelfth to the late sixteenth century"
  (p. 161, folio 149). It is identified in the body as "the foremost biography of medieval Indian
  saints, Akhbar al-akhyar" (p. 207, folio 196).

## Doubts and review points

### 1. Place-level attachment — one note for the whole conversion section

**Twenty-three pre-ruling "interim convention" bullets in the Pass-1 notes have been converted under
ruling (a) (21 September 2026) and are collected in `## Cross-cutting material`, § "Place-level
attachments".** Every one of those bullets attaches to **every** archive row in the named place, and
**none of them is the book describing that site.** Lahore takes 35 rows on the strength of two
scholars' home cities, a man in an anecdote, an almanac-printing trade, a British college, a
journalist's posting, a private collector, a migration stage and a run of imprints; Karachi takes 11
rows, Multan 8, Peshawar 10 on a single mention, Islamabad 4, Uch Sharif 5, and Hyderabad, Kasur,
Gujrat and Dera Ghazi Khan one each. **A human writing a shrine entry from these lines must say what
the book actually says — "Lahore printed ʿurs almanacs" — and must never let it become a statement
about the shrine.** The heaviest and least informative are the Lahore imprint lines; the most
substantive are the Uch Sharif chilla line (p. 168, folio 156), the Multan initiation line (p. 80,
