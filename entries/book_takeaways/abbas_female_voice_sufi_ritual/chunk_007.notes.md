## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The chapter's central institutional claim: ritual has migrated out of the shrine.** "The changes
  have come about as the rituals have gradually moved out of the shrine contexts to the more public
  contexts of the melās or folk festivals and now of course the concert settings, where the musicians
  use sophisticated electronic media in addition to traditional instrumentation. The multimedia have
  played a significant role in the changes" (p. 139, folio not stated).
- **Diaspora patronage as a driver of change.** "The expatriate speech communities outside the
  subcontinent who patronize the singing of Islamic mystical poetry in western, Middle Eastern, and
  South Asian settings have further contributed to the changes in the rituals. They have made the
  contexts eclectic. Their interest has further generated the involvement of other international
  groups and world music institutions. These factors have modernized the genres that are discussed
  here" (p. 139, folio not stated). "The traditions of singing Sufi poetics grew in the last quarter
  of the twentieth century, going back and forth between the native cultures and the diaspora"
  (p. 140, folio not stated).
- **Musicians, not audiences, as the agents of change.** "However, the musicians themselves have
  largely been responsible for the changes in the rituals. While on the one hand they are involved
  in the modernization process, on the other hand they uphold the traditions. Whatever improvisations
  they make are within the linguistic frames that have existed for centuries" (p. 139, folio not
  stated).
- **Shrine repertoire transplanted into the concert hall.** "I noticed a trend among musicians to
  reinforce the singing of the qaul and the girāhs in concert settings for their expatriate audiences
  in the same manner that they would do in the shrines. This is pronounced in Abida Parvin's
  sufiānā-kalām concerts where she has introduced Amir Khusrau's qaul in Arabic. The qaul and girāh
  are used in the devotional qawwālī contexts of Sufi shrines and have been sung ever since Amir
  Khusrau's time" (p. 140, folio not stated).
- **Women as the constituency of shrine activity — the book's key economic-sociological claim.** "For
  the Sufi poets women were the major participants or consumers of shrine activity and speech events
  where Sufi discourse was disseminated. Thus, the linguistic medium and metaphor had to be in voices
  that were meaningful to women and their networks" (pp. 145–146, folios not stated).
- **The repertoire is not confined to the shrine.** "I also maintain here that these songs are not
  just articulated within restricted shrine speech events, they are chanted on all occasions when
  members of a speech community come together in social contexts, be they celebrations or death
  rituals. The style of articulation may be adjusted according to the context" (p. 146, folio not
  stated).
- **Audience composition of the performances transcribed.** "Most of the myths in this chapter are
  transliterated from concert performances in Pakistan and the United Kingdom. The audiences are
  urban and highly literate: people of the diaspora in England and the academy and bureaucrats in
  Pakistan. The singers therefore transmit metaphoric messages through myth and local color,
  'speaking' to their audience using the multimedia" (pp. 137–138, folios not stated).
- **A named concert and its patron body.** Abida Parvin's black-Layla song is sung "in her concert at
  the Open University" (p. 136, folio not stated), which the author then calls "a concert for
  bureaucrats from the government" (p. 137, folio not stated). **Whether these are the same event is
  not stated; see Doubts.**
- **A second named performance, its year deleted.** "I cite the improvised Sufi poetry that Abida
  Parvin used in her  [numeral dropped, text-layer] performance in Islamabad: There are references
  from Shah Abdul Latif's poetry and Khawaja Ghulam Farid's texts. The musician certainly used frames
  from Sufi poetry to chastise the religious establishment and to speak for marginalized groups"
  (p. 151, folio not stated).
- **State broadcasting and a state-sponsored centenary.** "Another melody, which I have transcribed
  from a video created by Pakistan Television, is called 'Bābul' and is sung as a ṭhumrī… the video
  was produced during the centennial celebrations for the poet in Pakistan" (p. 144, folio not
  stated). **The centenary year is not printed in this chunk.**
- **Partition migration as a carrier of repertoire.** "The Pakistani speech communities, especially
  those who migrated to Pakistan during the  [numeral dropped, text-layer] partition from North
  India, identify with the poetic, linguistic, and musical traditions of North India and do much to
  preserve them" (p. 144, folio not stated).
- **Commissioning of lyrics — a direct statement of the qawwali economy.** "In my personal interview
  with Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan, he said that he commissioned Bari Nizami, Ahmed Nadeem Qasmi, and other
  national poets to write his lyrics. His songwriters recreated the traditional, indigenous myths and
  texts for him" (p. 151, folio not stated).
- **Hereditary training and the qawwāl lineage.** Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan's integration of grassroots
  repertoire into world music "may very well be a part of his training with his qawwāl lineage and
  may need to be explored further by listening to the repertoire of his father, Ustad Fateh Ali Khan,
  or those of his uncles Ustad Mubarik Ali Khan and Ustad Salamat Ali Khan. All the musicians trained
  him through the classical-music traditions of the subcontinent" (pp. 150–151, folios not stated).
- **Regional performance styles as a market division.** "musicians like Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan and Mehr
  Ali and Sher Ali can be identified with the Panjabi ang of qawwālī performance. This is in contrast
  to the North Indian style of the Sabri Brothers, who sing in Urdu. The differences lie mainly in
  the linguistic resources" (p. 151, folio not stated).
- **Instrumentation.** "The female myths are generally sung in the sufiānā-kalām of the vernacular
  languages with minimal musical instruments, the most basic being the yaktārā" (p. 151, folio not
  stated). "Modern-day qawwāl parties use electronic devices in addition to the traditional
  instruments. The Faridi qawwāls' narrative is sung in the established North Indian style of music"
  (p. 148, folio not stated).
- **A silsila named in an occupational-economic setting.** "The Sufis, especially the Qadiriyya in the
  cotton-growing areas along the Indus, used kāpāītī for their poetry" (p. 152, folio not stated) —
  the kapāītī being Shah Abdul Latif's "cotton-spinning songs" (p. 152, folio not stated). **This is
  the only silsila named anywhere in the chunk.**
- **The gendered seclusion of the listening assembly.** "Jairazbhoy, in his fieldwork among the folk
  performers of Rajasthan, observed that, although such discourse was addressed to the male listeners,
  there were also female participants and listeners in the assemblies who were hidden from view 'or
  were behind the cilman or the screen.' The poetry was directed to them as well" (p. 150, folio not
  stated).
- **Holi, the Rang and the devotional festival calendar.** "The Festival of Colors is also the Indian
  festive event associated with Holi, in which individuals in a community play with each other and
  sprinkle liquid colors for fun, thus soiling their partners' clothes. The Rang in qawwālī is a
  constituent of the composition and marks a celebration: In this context it is a celebration of the
  speaker's union with the spiritual mentor, the murshid" (p. 142, folio not stated). Khusrau "adapted
  songs or gīts that women sang at seasonal festivals, to write lyrics in praise of his shaikh"
  (p. 142, folio not stated), and "created many of his compositions using bol (verses) related to the
  seasonal festivals and wedding rituals that are also used as Sufi songs according to context"
  (p. 143, folio not stated). Named rāgās: "'Rāg Bahar' (Spring melody), 'Rāg Holi Khamāch' (Holi
  melody), 'Rāg Basant' (Basant melody), 'Rāg Sarang' (Music melody), the 'Rang' (Color)" (pp. 143–144,
  folios not stated).
- **Maulud as a Sindhi devotional occasion.** "virāhīnī poetry is also sung in the Sindhi devotional
  contexts, such as the mauluds that celebrate the Prophet's birth" (p. 149, folio not stated), with
  "All kinds of bridal imagery, such as the wedding ceremonies of the mehndi (henna)" (p. 149, folio
  not stated).
- **Wedding ritual as the frame for religious lament.** "The 'Bābul' is a wedding tune that the bride's
  friends sing at the time of her departure from her father's home. The bride's lament is reported to
  have been originally sung in the rural districts of Avadh during weddings, with an emotion that
  brought even the strongest listeners to tears. Allāvālahs (people close to God) read spiritual
  meanings in the lament and wept" (p. 144, folio not stated). "The ritualistic sending away of the
  bride in the traditional contexts in the subcontinent is similar to the funeral despite the
  preceding festivities to celebrate it as an auspicious event" (p. 144, folio not stated). The
  Panjabi wedding rituals Hir asks her mother to perform for Ranjha are named in the lyric: dribbling
  oil at the doorstep, "Kar ke śagan / Javy vāle [text-layer?: Javā̃y vāle]" — "Perform thou the
  rituals / Which are done for a son-in-law" — and tying "the ceremonial wreath of flowers around his
  crown, which is done for a bridegroom" (pp. 152–153, folios not stated).
- **The technical vocabulary as the book defines it** (all p. 148, folio not stated, unless noted):
  "the kāfīs (short verses for expressing mystical thoughts)"; "The kāfī is a lyrical composition
  meant to be sung with music, its subject is mystical, and its central theme is repeated in the
  refrain to create the samā (the context). It uses folk motifs from the environment to emphasize the
  mentor-disciple relationship. Furthermore, the earthly metaphors (iśq-e majāzī) communicate iśq-e
  haqīqī (spiritual union with God)". Also: the dohā "conveys an idea or an image in two verses"
  and is "an old popular Prakrit and Hindi meter in which a couplet is put together where the two
  verses rhyme" (p. 141, folio not stated); "like the dohā, a gīt is a simple poetic construction used
  to compose a song or a hymn" (p. 142, folio not stated); "The cāmā is a poem or a song in Persian not
  exceeding seventeen couplets" (p. 149, folio not stated — **spelled out, safe**); "Rekhtī is a
  subgenre of Urdu poetry written in the ghazal form in which the narrator is a woman and where the
  narrative idiom, too, is said to be that of women: 'aurtõ kī bolī'" (p. 149, folio not stated); the
  cakkī-nāmā and carkhi-nāmā, "where the narrator is the woman who sings at the grinding or spinning
  wheel" (p. 150, folio not stated); ījz (humility) and kaśf, "the lifting of the veil in Sufi poetry"
  (p. 148, folio not stated); virāhā and virāhīnī (p. 149, folio not stated).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

**This chunk contains no karamat.** What it contains is *myth* — romance narratives the author treats
as literary and ritual material, not as miracle accounts — and one cosmological reference.

- **The Layla–Majnun romance, as narrated by the author from her sources** (p. 136, folio not stated):
  Majnun's flight to the forests where "the animals becoming his friends"; Layla's refusal to
  consummate her marriage; the exchange of love poems through messengers; her husband's death "still
  hoping that one day he would have her love"; Layla's own death, "grown old pining for Qays"; and
  Qays's final madness at her grave. "Consequently, Majnun became the model of the absolute lover in
  Arabic poetry and the legend spread to Persia and the Pakistan-India subcontinent, where the
  Layla-Majnun romance was absorbed into the poetic traditions" (p. 136, folio not stated).
- **The blackening of Layla in the subcontinent.** "By the time the legend came to the subcontinent,
  Layla had become 'black' and a whole discourse of a dark-complected Layla emerged in the indigenous
  literatures. Perhaps her dark black hair got transformed into a black Layla" (p. 136, folio not
  stated) — the "Perhaps" is the author's own speculation, marked as such.
- **Majnun's Quran parable, as the book reports Ghulam Farid reporting it.** "When folks asked Majnun
  about his black Layla, he retorted with an example from the Quran. He questioned them: If the pages
  from the Quran can be white but the ink be black, then what is so wrong for him to love a woman of
  color" (p. 137, folio not stated).
- **The mirāj.** The Rang's colour imagery "also suggest the prophet Muhammad's mirāj (ascension to
  heaven)" (p. 143, folio not stated). Attributed to the author's reading of the poetics, not to a
  hagiographic source.
- **Khusrau's lament at Nizamuddin Auliya's death, reported as report.** "It is reported that on
  hearing of his shaikh's death, Amir Khusrau tore his clothes and blackened his face to lament"
  (p. 141, folio not stated) — the two dohās that follow are given as "Amir Khusrau's spontaneous
  creations on the death of his shaikh and mentor" (p. 141, folio not stated).
- **Hir's poisoning.** "This is a moving context in the narrative as Hir realizes that she has been

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The chapter's stated programme.** "In this chapter I discuss the evolution of the female voice in
  Sufi poetry as it developed over the centuries, especially in the narratives of the musicians who
  sing it now" (p. 139, folio not stated).
- **The thesis, stated twice.** "There is enough evidence to support my argument that substantial Sufi
  poetry in the subcontinent is sung in the female voice. Male musicians use inflection, and by
  default, the female voices are heard in their syntax and semantics" (p. 141, folio not stated); and
  the conclusion drawn from code-switching, "which confirms the hypothesis that cross culturally the
  female voice is dominant in Sufi poetry" (p. 147, folio not stated).
- **The method, and its claimed difference from existing scholarship.** "Some analytical and
  ethnographic work in the field is commendable and facilitated my own research. However, that work
  is textual or deals with ethnomusical theory such as Qureshi's. The transliteration methodology of
  this research that looks at the live speech of the musicians' narratives attempts to throw into
  relief the female voices and the shifts in the rituals. The female voice is further highlighted
  through interviews with the musicians and other professionals" (p. 140, folio not stated).
- **A linguistic map and an integrative model are promised.** "I further look at how the different
  geographical locations in Pakistan and the speech communities within the country contributed to the
  changes in the rituals. I attempt to explain this through a linguistic map. Additionally, I have
  devised a model through which I try to integrate the nodes such as linguistics, themes, myths,
  ecology, and performances to understand the female voices that are discussed in this book (see Fig.
  . [numeral dropped, text-layer])" (p. 140, folio not stated). **The figure number is deleted by the
  text layer; only the separating period survives.**
- **The first explanation: the forms themselves are gendered.** "The most basic appears to be the fact
  that the poetic form of this discourse is rooted in the indigenous linguistic traditions. Generally,
  it is the woman who professes divine love, particularly in the dohā and gīt composed in Khari Boli
  or Hindawi" (p. 140, folio not stated). Restated at p. 149: "The feelings of separation expressed
  through the female narrator belong to the indigenous poetic traditions in the vernacular languages.
  The woman professes divine love" (folio not stated).
- **The second explanation: audience literacy forced the vernacular.** "since this poetry was meant to
  be sung for ordinary people who were barely literate, there was no point in using the canonical
  Perso-Arabic forms in the assemblies where it was sung. For the same reasons then it can be said
  that the Sufi poets used women's speech, as in the cakkī-nāmā and the carkhi-nāmā" (p. 150, folio
  not stated). Earlier: "When the Sufi bards communicated with their listeners, they had to speak in
  an idiom the audience could understand. Naturally, the speech had to be in the indigenous canons
  related to women's linguistic and poetic traditions and their rituals" (p. 145, folio not stated).
- **A third explanation, attributed to others and not adopted.** "Some native critics claim that the
  use of the female narrator in the Sufi poetry of North India could partially be an influence from
  the Persian poetic genre called the cāmā" (p. 149, folio not stated). The author's own summary is
  cautious: "Thus, the lyrics that are studied so far appear to belong to a variety of indigenous,
  hybrid, oral poetic traditions" (p. 149, folio not stated).
- **Canonicity and marginalization — the argument via rekhtī.** "Rekhtī is set in gendered opposition
  to rekhta (by which standard Urdu ghazal poetry is known) and seems to have become equated with any
  poetry having a female narrator, regardless of poetic diction. The presence of the female narrator
  in the Deccani poetry has made it noncanonical in the mainstream Urdu literature" (pp. 149–150,
  folios not stated). Using Petievich and Platt's definition, the author argues "that generally poetic
  conventions of canonical Perso-Arabic literatures have marginalized creations where the narrator is
  the female, thus confirming my hypothesis that the female voice is predominant in the grassroots,
  indigenous sufiānā-kalām traditions and sometimes the qawwālī discourse in indigenous contexts"
  (p. 150, folio not stated). **The canonical North Indian phrase for such poetry is given as "aurat
  kī taraf se jazbāt kā izhār karnā", "expression of passions or emotions from the woman's side"
  (p. 150, folio not stated).**
- **A research agenda the author leaves open.** "The canonical literatures of the other vernacular
  languages have to be investigated for samples, and the female voices of Sufi poetry have to be
  situated within those canons for evaluation" (p. 150, folio not stated).
- **Sufism and Bhakti as revolts against clerical and economic power — the chunk's strongest political
  claim.** "both Sufi Islam and the Bhakti movement were revolts against the established clerical
  orders. The established orders took upon themselves the interpretation of the faith, creating power
  structures for the ruling classes. Economic hegemony could only be sustained through religious,
  social, and cultural structures upheld by clerics and patriarchs. 'Shame' and 'honor' in this
  context became associated with control of women's sensibilities" (p. 151, folio not stated).
  Continued: "The clerics, especially the Brahmins, tried to annihilate the female energy cults and
  replace them with the dominant male cults. But this was prevented by the countermovements having
  their roots in the masses. Among such countermovements were the devotional discourses of the Sufis
  and the Bhakti" (p. 151, folio not stated).
- **The final formulation of the chunk.** "it appears that the isomorphisms of gender, class, caste,
  and religion are formulated in myth that is sung and in which the dominant voices are those of
  women. In using such myths, the Sufi poets were fully aware that they were creating oral discourse
  that was meant to be sung in ethnographic contexts for the people. They used verbal art to address
  social issues in accordance with radical Sufi Islam" (p. 154, folio not stated).
- **Death in the myths is not read as tragedy.** "the Sufi poets who wrote about the tragic deaths of
  the lovers in their myths did not consider them to be tragic, because they did not interpret them
  from that standpoint. Their lovers suffer so that they become free of the world and from the matter
  called the 'self' that is tied to the transitory self. For the lovers, death is the opening to the
  real world, as there can be no fulfillment for them on this earth. This is perhaps a foregone
  conclusion in Sufi mysticism" (p. 138, folio not stated).
- **Grammatical evidence for the female voice, set out as a table** (p. 143, folio not stated): "'rī'
  is the female gender marker, a term of address that establishes intimacy"; "'ḍi,' the suffix, gender
  marker"; "'ī,' the suffix, is the gender marker" (for phirnā and dekhnā); "Sakhī is an intimate
  female friend". "All these are distinct syntactic constructions for the female. The marker 'rī'
  establishes the intimacy of the relationship, very much like Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan's 'nī' in his
  Panjabi qawwālīs in which the speakers are females" (p. 143, folio not stated).
- **Grammatical gender inverted in the Faridi qawwālī.** "The gender construction of the kafanīā
  (shroud) and the ghungaṭīā (veil) in the musicians' narrative is interesting, for both are male
  nouns for objects. Additionally, the vocalists use the genderized form of the noun jogan, which
  already signifies a female mendicant, derived from the male form, the jogī. They add the 'īā' to
  jogan and make it joganīā" (p. 148, folio not stated).
- **Quranic warrant for the mother figure.** "the Sufi poets seem to follow the Quranic injunctions
  that advocate reverence for the mother as the creator of life, for example, Sura : [numerals
  dropped, text-layer], which says, 'And (reverence) the womb / (That bore you)'"; and "Sura :
  [numerals dropped, text-layer] affirms, 'It is He who brought you forth / From the wombs / Of your
  mothers / When ye knew nothing; and He / Gave you hearing and sight / And intelligence and
  affection'" (p. 152, folio not stated). **Both sura and verse numbers are deleted; the colon
  survives in both. Do not supply them.**
- **The author's own surprise, recorded as her judgement.** "I find it strange that Abida Parvin should
  sing this narrative at a concert for bureaucrats from the government, talking about issues of
  ethnicity and gender" (p. 137, folio not stated). And: "I find the aesthetic construction and the
  sociolinguistic context of this melody extremely moving, especially the words of its female
  narrator" (p. 145, folio not stated).
- **Humour in the Hir narrative.** "Although Hir's situation is a tragic one, I found immense humor in
  both the written and oral sources… Maliki makes fun of Hir when she suspects her motives for leaving
  the house to go out. She makes fun of Hir's preparations to meet Ranjha as though she were visiting
  her grandmother. I found it in a qawwālī that Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan sang" (p. 154, folio not stated).
- **Caste used inside the myth as insult.** "Maliki scolds Hir for retorting, in the manner of low-born
  women. Here, she uses caste distinctions to emphasize Hir's 'wantonness'" (p. 154, folio not stated);
  the lyric names "Bhaṭ lohār tarkhāni nī" — "Flatterers, blacksmiths, and carpenters" — and "Kuṛī
  dhoban āte mūciaṇi nī" — "daughters of washerwomen and shoemakers" (p. 154, folio not stated).
- **The child-bride's metaphors as shared cultural property.** "The song where the child-bride calls
  herself the bird in her father's courtyard or the bud in his Arabian jasmine recurs often in the
  poetry written in the vernacular languages. These were familiar metaphors for the audiences… They
  are what T. S. Eliot calls 'a cultural node'" (p. 145, folio not stated), and today they "are
  projected through dramas and documentaries produced for television that highlight gender, class, and
  socioeconomic caste issues within the fold of patriarchal paradigms" (p. 145, folio not stated).
