# abbas_female_voice_sufi_ritual — chunk 002 — pp. 33–53

Twenty-one pages of continuous chapter text (pp. 33–53, folios not stated), broken only by seven
photographic plates and one full-page comparative figure (p. 41, folio not stated). The chunk
**opens mid-sentence** at p. 33 (folio not stated) — "singing devotional poetry that I call ''Sufi''
because its content is about the Sufi saints of the Muslim world, as well as the life of the prophet
Muhammad and his family" — and **closes mid-couplet** at p. 53 (folio not stated) on the second line
of a Panjabi verse Abida Parvin quotes. Both seams run into the neighbouring chunks and must be
stitched at consolidation. The material is a single authorial exposition: Abbas defines her terms
(Sufi, samā, qawwālī, sufiānā-kalām, maqām, kefīat, hāl, wajd), sets out the economy of female
devotional singers, sketches the arrival of Sufism in the subcontinent, and then turns to Shah Abdul
Latif's Risālo and the roving-minstrel tradition, with long verbatim interview extracts from Abida
Parvin and Shaikh Ghulam Hussain (pp. 51–53, folios not stated).

**The governing text-layer fact for this chunk, stated once here and inventoried under Doubts: every
number set in Arabic digits inside the body text has been dropped, leaving an empty space inside its
punctuation, while every number spelled out in words survives intact.** So `(d.  )`, `(– )`,
`(b. )`, `(Sura :)`, `close to . billion`, `in the fall of .` and `see Fig .` are all damaged, and
"the eighth century", "five to twelve musicians", "six million Muslims", "at least fifty places" and
"more than a hundred thousand Sufi shrines" are all sound. **This means that not one of the eighteen
dates this chunk prints for a saint or poet is recoverable from the text layer, and none has been
supplied from outside (RULE 2).** Every such date below reads `(not stated in a recoverable form)`.

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **State control of shrines constrains where women may sing — the single most quotable institutional
  fact in the chunk.** "They are free to sing there at any time except during the prayers. However,
  due to state control of Sufi shrines, the areas where they can sing may at times be confined to the
  female sections" (p. 33, folio not stated). **The controlling body is not named: (not stated).**
- **When singing happens at a shrine.** "Generally, they sing at shrines during the annual urs
  celebration or on Thursday evenings, the eve of the Muslim sabbath on Friday" (p. 33, folio not
  stated); restated for sufiānā-kalām — "As part of popular culture it was sung among roving
  minstrels at the shrines during the urs, the melās (folk festivals), and on Thursday evenings"
  (p. 40, folio not stated); and again for both genres — "At the shrines they are performed on
  Thursday evenings in anticipation of the Muslim sabbath and during the celebrations of the death
  anniversary of the saint" (p. 42, folio not stated).
- **Direct payment by devotees at the shrine.** "Here, they find an audience at any time of the night
  or day that pays them for their performance" (p. 33, folio not stated); "They sing at the shrines
  and are paid by the devotees" (p. 34, folio not stated).
- **The five shrines with nightly qawwālī.** "At some shrines, such as Bulle Shah, Hazrat Lal Shahbaz
  Qalandar, Shah Abdul Latif at Bhit Shah, Bahauddin Zakariya, and Rukunud-din Shah Alam
  [text-layer?: Rukunuddin Shah Alam], qawwālī is performed every evening after the prayers.
  Musicians as well as audiences prefer evening performances that continue well past midnight"
  (p. 42, folio not stated).
- **The household economy beyond the shrine.** "in family contexts they create devotional settings
  wherever necessary, be it a birth, a wedding, or an initiation ritual such as a male child's
  circumcision" (p. 33, folio not stated); "Female musicians thus make a living by singing in the
  feudal households and within their own communities"; "Female musicians in the cities sing at
  ceremonies such as births, weddings, aqīqā celebrations, and circumcisions" (p. 34, folio not
  stated).
- **Broadcast and the state as patron.** "In addition to singing at Sufi shrines, women musicians
  perform on the radio or television" (p. 34, folio not stated). Taj Mastani "affirmed that in
  Hyderabad she can earn a living from the radio. She can further negotiate business contracts with
  television stations and her clients from the affluent feudal households in Sind" (p. 34, folio not
  stated). "She said that Lok Virsa, the Institute of Folk Heritage in Islamabad, contacted her
  through Radio Pakistan in Hyderabad. Through Lok Virsa she was able to go to international concerts
  in the Middle East and Europe; as a state organization, Lok Virsa could afford to pay for her trips
  abroad. On her own she does not have the resources to travel overseas with her entourage" (p. 34,
  folio not stated).
- **A gendered earnings gap, with four reasons given.** (1) "Female musicians have fewer direct
  contacts with male organizers and sponsors overseas" (p. 34, folio not stated). (2) "Due to
  cultural norms, female musicians have limited access to their male patrons and therefore their
  earnings are much less" (p. 34, folio not stated). (3) "Sometimes, the female musicians do not have
  managers able to negotiate with recording companies overseas" (p. 34, folio not stated). (4) Genre:
  "This is in part because they sing sufiānā-kalām and not the more popular qawwālī that is sung in
  the classical traditions of North Indian music… Their skills are considered more in line with folk
  music" (p. 35, folio not stated). **For all three women "their husbands functioned as
  negotiators"** (p. 34, folio not stated).
- **Irregular foreign income.** "earnings of women such as Abida Parvin and Reshma are not regular,
  and they depend on the whims of recording companies abroad" (p. 34, folio not stated); "Although
  the female performers have made some capital from concerts in the Middle East, Europe, and North
  America, their earnings are small compared to those of their male counterparts" (pp. 34–35, folios
  not stated).
- **A woman crossing into the male genre to close the gap.** "Since the early nineties she has tried
  to establish an identity comparable to that of the male qawwāls by singing the traditional qaul,
  ''Mun kunto Maulā fā Alī-un Maulā,'' in a call-and-response pattern with one of her
  instrumentalists. Like the male qawwāls she has added a formidable repertoire of Amir Khusrau's
  Sufi lyrics in her performances" (p. 35, folio not stated).
- **The cassette industry as a livelihood.** "the cassette culture has provided a source of income…
  within areas such as Hyderabad or Faisalabad the local recording companies have assisted women
  musicians in earning a living from recording their Sufi lyrics with music. Their songwriters are
  male relatives or men in the community. In fact, this small industry has become a source of income
  for many women and men" (p. 35, folio not stated).
- **A named firm and its ritual calendar.** "Rehmat Gramophone Company in Faisalabad provides female
  musicians with a livelihood by recording their songs during Milad-ud Nabi, which celebrates the
  Prophet's birthday… Their recordings are played on the local buses, in the restaurants, and on the
  radio. Rehmat Gramophone Company releases special audiocassette editions in which female and male
  performers narrate the tragedy of Kerbala ( ) [text-layer?: parenthetical content lost] during the
  annual Muharram mourning rituals. These elegies, called nohās and marsiyās…" (pp. 36–37, folios not
  stated).
- **Khanqahs as mixed institutions of learning.** "The Sufi khanqahs (monasteries) were institutions
  of learning where both men and women were involved in several levels of activities and continue to
  be so in present times. The female voice has always been important in these rituals" (p. 39, folio
  not stated).
- **The shrine count for Sind, as a reported claim.** "In the province of Sind alone there are said to
  be more than a hundred thousand Sufi shrines" (p. 50, folio not stated). **Attributed by the book
  only to "are said to be" — no source, no date, no method: (not stated).**
- **Qawwālī as an occupational form.** "Qawwāli is essentially a male genre, usually sung by
  professional musicians, who come from a line of qawwālī singers, and consists of a team of five to
  twelve musicians who take turns singing a single narrative. They sit during the performance. Two or
  three main vocalists lead the group" (pp. 39–40, folios not stated). Its sound: "the use of rhythm,
  created through the percussion of drums and handclaps, together with the melodies" (p. 40, folio not
  stated).
- **Sufiānā-kalām as its counterpart, and the women's entry point.** "mystical poetry usually sung by
  a solo musician to minimal instrumentation. Women's input in this tradition is significant.
  Sometimes, two or more singers will perform in a chorus. In Sind this form of choir singing is
  called sūng" (p. 40, folio not stated). "Unlike the qawwālī, where the musicians have to have
  rigorous training in the genre, the singing of sufiānā-kalām requires less rigid training" (p. 40,
  folio not stated).
- **Instruments, as printed.** "a capṛī (castanets) or a tambūr or yak-tārā (stringed instruments)";
  "the cimṭā (long tongs)"; "the rabāb (a small stringed instrument)"; "a ghaṛā (large clay pot)";
  and, for women, "Women called loṭevālī, or ghaṛevālī or challevālī, use a small aluminum pot on
  which they create percussion with the ring on the middle finger while they sing mystical poetry"
  (p. 40, folio not stated). Later, "a yak-tarā (a one-string instrument)" (p. 51, folio not stated).
- **Sacred versus secular, policed by the musicians themselves.** "During a qawwālī performance in
  Rawalpindi in , [text-layer?: year dropped] I witnessed the Sabri Brothers request the then–federal
  minister for education and his entourage not to clap during the event" (p. 42, folio not stated).
  "Almost all the Sufi musicians with whom I spoke insisted on the distinction between sacred and
  secular contexts even in concert settings… their poetry is not folk, but devotional and ritualistic,
  as they perform with the utmost sanctity (adab)" (p. 42, folio not stated). "Abida Parvin
  categorically affirmed that she sang her poetry with veneration even at public concerts" (p. 42,
  folio not stated).
- **Opposition to shrine ritual.** "Many in the Muslim world object to the rituals at the shrines"
  (p. 37, folio not stated); "The Wahabi followers of Islam disdain the Sufi practices at the shrines
  as a form of biddat (dissent)" (p. 43, folio not stated); "In addition to the traditional orthodox
  Muslim view of Sufism as peripheral to the larger community practice, men and women who sing Sufi
  poetry take into consideration the way in which local communities view the place of women in public
  performances" (p. 34, folio not stated).
- **Devotees do not claim to be Sufis.** "the devotees who visit the shrines do not claim to be Sufis…
  they firmly believe that they are Muslims and belong to the ummah of the Islamic world" (p. 43,
  folio not stated); "a musician who sings qawwālī or sufiānā-kalām… does not claim to be a Sufi but
  only a transmitter of Sufi discourse or poetry" (p. 43, folio not stated).
- **Where women can and cannot enter, compared internationally.** "Until recently, the samā
  performances of the male Sufi groups in countries like Iran were secret rituals in which ordinary
  uninitiated persons were not allowed to participate. Women's entry was even more difficult. Perhaps
  the Pakistan-India area is one of the few places where the performances are held in the public
  contexts of the shrine, the folk festival, and the concert… In all the public contexts women are
  active participants" (p. 46, folio not stated).
- **The diaspora venue.** "Now the ritual concert is performed among the expatriate speech communities
  in the West and the Gulf States, where it has evolved within the frames of world music though still
  retaining the devotional speech forms and ethnomusical content" (p. 46, folio not stated); the
  poetry is sung "not only in Pakistan and India but also in England, France, Canada, the United
  States, Japan, and the Gulf States" (p. 38, folio not stated).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **A saint's prayer produces an emperor's heir.** Akbar "was an ardent devotee of Shaikh Saleem
  Chishti, a Sufi mystic who, according to legend, prayed for him so that he would have a son. His
  prayers were answered when Saleem, later known as Jehangir, was born and succeeded Akbar to the
  Mughal throne" (p. 49, folio not stated). **Abbas marks this herself as "according to legend".**
- **Mass conversion following a saint's adoption of Hindu musical worship**, attributed to a named
  musician: "Ustad Ghulam Haider Khan, a qawwāl, reports that when Hazrat Muinuddin Chishti… came to
  Ajmer in India, he studied the Hindu style of worship. He found that music was a significant
  component of worship, and the Brahmins sang aśloks, śābāds, and bhajans with percussion and wind
  instruments. Hazrat Muinuddin Chishti adapted the same practice, and thousands of Hindus converted
  to Islam" (p. 45, folio not stated). **Attributed to the informant, not asserted by Abbas —
  "according to the musician"** (p. 45, folio not stated).
- **Mira Bai's disappearance** — "the Rajput princess who ''disappeared'' among the people" (p. 49,
  folio not stated); "who herself ''disappeared'' as a roving mendicant" (p. 51, folio not stated).
  **Abbas keeps the word in scare quotes both times.**
- **Hallaj's martyrdom for anāl-Haqq** — "the famous martyr of love who was crucified… for his
  freethinking beliefs and his famous doctrine of anāl-Haqq, which means ''I am the truth''" (p. 38,
  folio not stated).
- **Shaikh Sadi and the wall**, told by Abida Parvin: "it is said of Shaikh Sadi that he would greet a
  wall every day as he went past it… Sadi replied, ''Really, there is nothing—but once upon a time
  there was something very wise inscribed on this wall—which enriched my knowledge—''" (p. 52, folio
  not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Why Abbas calls this repertoire "Sufi".** "devotional poetry that I call ''Sufi'' because its
  content is about the Sufi saints of the Muslim world, as well as the life of the prophet Muhammad
  and his family" (p. 33, folio not stated).
- **Her working definition of Sufism.** "Sufism is associated with the Islamic traditions of
  metaphysical thought and practices. Sufis believe in intuition and creativity and less in the
  fundamental and literal interpretation of the holy scriptures" (p. 37, folio not stated).
- **The mechanism of samā.** "words have a zāhir (overt meaning) and a bātin (covert meaning) that can
  be used to alter the consciousness through zikr, which is based on deep, disciplined breathing
  exercises in which the devotee incessantly repeats God's many names from the Quran… Zikr is central
  to the samā practice, to the extent that a state of ecstasy is reached" (p. 38, folio not stated).
  The competent musician "aims to induce this state among the listeners through the boundaries of
  music, speech, and song" (p. 38, folio not stated).
- **Chronology, as the book gives it.** "Mystical performances originated in Sufism in the eighth
  century in the Arabian peninsula" (p. 38, folio not stated); "Sufi poetry outside the high tradition
  of the samā was used in the eleventh century in Iran. From there it spread to Turkey and gradually
  into the Pakistan-India subcontinent, where it became popular in the thirteenth and fourteenth
  centuries" (p. 38, folio not stated); samā itself "goes back to the middle of the ninth
  century—references to this event are found in Baghdad. In this setting verses were recited to music
  accompanied by a whirling dance" (p. 39, folio not stated); "Sufiānā-kalām evolved at the same time
  as qawwālī… in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries" (p. 42, folio not stated).
- **Etymologies she relies on.** "The context is based on a qaul, which in Arabic means a famous
  saying. The singer of such discourse is called a qawwāl, someone who speaks well" (p. 39, folio not
  stated); the institution "is based on verbal art that is central to the Sufi theory of tauhīd, which
  declares God as one" (p. 39, folio not stated).
- **The anti-clerical claim, made twice.** Qawwālī and sufiānā-kalām make "a spiritual experience
  available to the people in an idiom they understand… Such an approach enables the mystics to teach
  the core of religion without the intervention of the clergy" (p. 42, folio not stated); "In the
  subcontinent of Pakistan and India the hegemony of a clerical order was perhaps not encouraged
  because the Sufi saints, who were sometimes also poets, simplified the Quranic texts for the
  masses" (p. 45, folio not stated). **But the same clergy drove the music underground: "For fear of
  the clergy mystic poetry sung to music remained confined to the minstrel class. That is why the
  sufiānā-kalām traditions are strong among this class of roving musicians"** (p. 50, folio not
  stated).
- **Sufis as scholars and political advisers.** "Not only were the poets immersed in the centuries-old
  Islamic written and oral discourses such as the Quran, the Sharīa, and the prophet Muhammad's
  hadīth; they were able as well to interpret the texts for application to religious and practical
  matters. Because of their scholarly wisdom, even monarchs and functionaries in power sought the Sufi
  scholars' guidance. As missionaries they undertook the dissemination of Islam through an oral medium
  whose basis was the written religious discourses" (pp. 38–39, folios not stated).
- **Syncretism as the mechanism of conversion, and caste as its motive.** "The Sufi version of Islam
  in Pakistan and India was based on syncretism and reconciled differing beliefs. It was holistic as
  it blended in with the beliefs of local cultures and populations who were Davidians [text-layer?:
  reads as "Dravidians"], Aryans, Hindus, Buddhists, and all native creeds. At that time in India many
  converted to Islam because it offered relief from caste oppression" (p. 50, folio not stated).
- **The "matrimythical" argument — the book's own coinage and the spine of its thesis.** "Since some
  of these cultures were ''matrimythical'' and used the indigenous female myths, the Sufi poets
  creatively blended the female voices from these myths into their poetry that they sang to music. The
  purpose was mainly aesthetic and devotional" (p. 50, folio not stated). Carried through to the
  shrine: "because the myths had a pre-Islamic, indigenous origin, non-Muslim devotees had no problem
  putting the female myths into their own devotional schemas or worldviews" (p. 51, folio not stated).
- **Devotional movements as social critique.** "The movements that emerged at this time challenged
  hegemonic religious authority, caste boundaries, gender inequity, and the exclusive use of elitist
  languages in religion. In South Asian Islam, the trend appeared in Sufism and particularly in the
  sufiānā-kalām traditions… since this model was an indigenous one that required minimal musical
  skills and resources, women's input in the singing of this poetry is visible" (p. 49, folio not
  stated). **This is the book's structural explanation for why women sing this genre and not the
  other.**
- **Bhakti.** "The Bhakti movement (– ) [text-layer?: date range dropped] meshed with it, and many
  devotional movements emerged at this time where poetry written in the vernacular was sung to
  music—these movements emphasized a personal and emotional relationship with God" (p. 43, folio not
  stated); "Central Asian Sufism influenced the local Bhakti movement in Hindustan" (p. 48, folio not
  stated).
- **Terminology is polysemous and grassroots as well as scholarly.** "the terms used in this book are
  from scholarly literature as well as from the popular culture: These are the beliefs of the
  participants of the speech events and the grassroots musicians" (p. 43, folio not stated); "Because
  of the diverse linguistic codes, cultures, and contexts of the subcontinent, the word maqām is
  polysemous" (pp. 44–45, folios not stated). Definitions from informants: kefīat as "a state of
  altered consciousness" and, in Abbas's reading, "a refined state of spiritual awareness, induced
  through the combination of Sufi poetry and music" (p. 44, folio not stated), with Ghulam Hussain
  drawing the line at "what is vulgar… when words or music trespass the fine balance", his example
  being "rock 'n' roll, which he considered the opposite extreme of Sufi music that produces kefīat"
  (p. 44, folio not stated); maqām as "a hierarchical movement toward ecstasy in a Sufi concert", per
  Sikandar Baloch and Naseer Mirza, explained "while discussing the Sufi musical concert at Shah Abdul
  Latif's shrine in Bhit Shah" (p. 44, folio not stated); "The musicians expect to attain a maqām, the
  spiritual station that enables them to create ecstasy among the listeners" (p. 42, folio not stated).
- **Wajd, hāl and kefīat distinguished.** "The term wajd (trance) may seem pejorative to orthodox
  Muslims or to someone in the West. Kefīat and hāl are states of ecstasy in the same mystical
  experience, although there is a difference in degrees of expression. Hāl is a state of arousal
  outside the control of self. Kefīat is subtle and covert although it involves ecstasy… there is no
  samā (context) without interactional kefīat between listeners and singers" (p. 45, folio not
  stated). **An obligation follows from this: "They are obligated to continue singing until the
  affected overcome the state of ecstasy"** (p. 45, folio not stated).
- **Samā as "context".** Dai Haleema's usage is taken as evidence: "To create the samā also means to
  create the context" (p. 43, folio not stated).
- **Performance as emergent.** "The socioeconomic status of the listeners and the degree of their
  involvement in a particular context generate the structure of the event. No two performances are
  expected to be identical. The performance contexts define the emerging nature of the lyrics and the
  isomorphisms of language, culture, and society" (p. 40, folio not stated).
- **Code-switching as an authenticity device.** "To establish the authenticity of their texts, the
  musicians recite the initial verses in Arabic or Persian and then switch to the vernacular. This can
  be either Urdu, Hindi, Purbi, Brajbhasha, Sindhi, Siraiki, Panjabi, Hindko, Baluchi, or any other
  code depending on the audience and the speech communities involved in the event" (pp. 42–43, folios
  not stated). Likewise "Sufi poets and musicians adapt his [Rumi's] Persian poems in their
  compositions to establish the authenticity of their narratives" (p. 38, folio not stated).
- **A regional continuum shared across a border.** "The mystical lore of the region that includes
  Sind, Baluchistan, and the coastal belt of Iran shares myths, melodies, and linguistic codes. Among
  the melodies are the famous ''Mast Qalandar'' dhun, which most female and male minstrels sing to the
  popular text that pays tribute to Ali… The ''Mast Qalandar'' is a theme about the ecstatic mendicant
  who gives up the material world and roves from place to place in search of divine wisdom" (p. 48,
  folio not stated). The Persian "Koolee"/Sindhi "Kolhi" homophony is the hinge of the argument
  (pp. 46–48, folios not stated).
- **Abida Parvin on gender in Sufi poetry, verbatim.** "Male and female does not even come into it—what
  you call Allah is one—God is the mehver [center] of everything… it really does not matter whether it
  is male or female—in fact we can really say that in the Sufi's terminology—if someone is not a
  male—he is called a female—" (p. 53, folio not stated). **This is the informant's position, given
  without endorsement or gloss by Abbas at this point in the chunk.**
- **Abida Parvin on education and Sufism, verbatim and polemical.** "there are very few Sufis among
  ''educated'' people… look at the folks in the universities—every person who is coming out of there
  is uneducated—like the husk—they have no spiritual connections—education only teaches you to become
  slaves—the educated man denies the truth—but the Sufi is the slave of truth" (p. 52, folio not
  stated).
- **The comparative model, Figure on p. 41 (folio not stated), captioned "Comparative model: Qawwālī
  and Sufiānā-Kalām" with the footnote ". Sometimes there can be more than one singer."** Its five
  columns are Linguistics, Poetics, Myths, Ecology, Performances. **Linguistics**: elitist codes /
  vernaculars; Arabic, Persian vs. Khari Boli, Urdu/Hindi, Brajbhasa, Avadhi, Purbi, Deccani,
  Rajasthani, Panjabi, Siraiki, Sindhi, Hindko, others. **Poetics**: doha, git, kafi, vai, abiyat,
  bani, rekhti, ghazal, others, cakki-namas, carkhi-namas, kapa'iti songs, wedding songs, festival
  songs, rituals — closing with "female speech" and "female narrator". **Myths**: Layla-Majnun,
  Sassi-Punnu, Sohni-Mahival, Mumal-Rano, Hir-Ranjha, others, mothers-daughters. **Ecology**: birds,
  animals, rivers, deserts, mountains, pastures, seasons, proverbs, metaphors, images.
  **Performances**: Qawwali — "male genre / many singers / turn-taking / North Indian / elitist /
  drums+claps / Arabic / Persian / Urdu-Hindi / diverse / Sufi poets / classical"; Sufiana-Kalam —
  "female & male / solo+1 / continuous / indigenous / grassroots / strings+ / vernaculars / Sufi
  poets / indigenous / folk / classical"; both under "shrines, concerts, folk festivals / males &
  females" (p. 41, folio not stated). **Every numeral in this figure's caption and cross-reference is
  destroyed** — the caption begins ". Comparative model" and the in-text pointer reads "(see Fig .)"
  (pp. 41 and 51, folios not stated).
- **A demographic claim the book makes in its own voice, reported here exactly as printed because it
