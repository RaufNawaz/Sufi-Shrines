## Practices, institutions, economy

- **State control of shrines, stated flatly.** "In Pakistan, the shrines are now under state control
  through the Auqaf Department" (p. 212, folio not stated). **This is the single most usable
  institutional sentence in the chunk.**
- **ʿUrs and melā defined as one thing.** "Their spiritual mentor's death anniversary celebrations,
  called a *melā* or *urs*. The death anniversary is celebrated as the anniversary of the saint's
  union with the deity" (p. 200, folio not stated). The book writes *urs* here without the ʿayn.
- **Dhammāl defined.** "*Dhammāl* is a ritual ecstatic dance performed at the Suﬁ shrines or in
  contexts where Suﬁ texts are sung" (p. 201, folio not stated).
- **A shrine vow.** A whole note glosses a term as "A vow made at a shrine" — **the term itself is
  (not stated), the callout being dropped** (p. 199, folio not stated).
- **Shrine singing as a livelihood for women.** "They additionally sing around shrines in Multan and
  Sahiwal to earn a living" (p. 199, folio not stated).
- **Mendicancy as a shrine economy.** Of the Sidis of Ratanpur: "They traveled from village to
  village, entertaining with their music and dance. The *faqīrs* received alms, which were their
  primary source of livelihood" (p. 201, folio not stated).
- **Zakat.** "*Jakat* or *zakat* is an obligatory annual charitable gift that a well-to-do Muslim
  must make. It is one of the pillars of Islam" (p. 200, folio not stated).
- **Hajj.** "Millions of pilgrims from the Islamic world perform the annual ritual of Hajj at the
  Kaaba and go around the structure. Hajj and its rituals are among the pillars of Islam, an
  obligation for Muslims who can aﬀord it" (p. 212, folio not stated).
- **A mosque's endowment by a Mughal princess.** Jahan Ara's two donations, **sums `[numeral

## Arguments and interpretations

- **On interpretive openness, twice.** "I have discussed the melody with both Muslim and non-Muslim
  informants and conclude that there is no ﬁxed interpretation. The lyric is drawn cross culturally
  from the regional folklore" (p. 199, folio not stated); "Kubra's chant is simple and can be
  understood cross culturally" (p. 200, folio not stated).
- **On recitation without comprehension.** "Many Muslims in the subcontinent can read and recite
  Arabic because it is the sacred language of the Quran; they may not necessarily understand Arabic"
  (p. 200, folio not stated).
- **On a woman informant's self-suppression, and its irony.** "Throughout the interview Kubra is
  reluctant to admit her identity as a musician. Ironically, she chants for me and provides the data
  for substantial discussion in this chapter" (p. 199, folio not stated).
- **On method: conversation analysis as the book's transliteration system.** "Transliterated and
  adapted according to the conversation analysis system of Harvey Sacks, Emanuel Schegloﬀ, and Gail
  Jeﬀerson, in *Studies in the Organization of Conversational Interaction*" (p. 200, folio not
  stated); "Here I use the transliteration system from conversation analysis to portray the dialogue
  among the performers themselves and the turn-taking that occurs as the discourse emerges. It can
  be seen that the narrative is not ﬁxed and evolves within the performance context… The lines are
  numbered, and I have created the line according to the pause since there is a semantic pattern in
  the chant" (p. 203, folio not stated).
- **On translation policy, stated four times.** "The translation is contextual and not a
  word-for-word rendering" (p. 204, folio not stated); "The translation is a poetic rendering in
  context, worked out with a native Siraiki informant, and is not a paraphrase" (p. 204, folio not
  stated); "The translation is in the context of live speech and is therefore not a paraphrase"
  (p. 205, folio not stated); "The verses are translated to reinforce the poetic contexts and are
  not a word-for-word translation" (p. 208, folio not stated). **Anyone quoting this book's
  translations must carry this caveat.**
- **On performativity.** "See Della Pollock, ''Performing Writing,'' in *The Ends of Performance*…
  where she draws upon Austin's concept of performative utterances. She distinguishes between words
  that report or describe (constatives) and words that do what they say (performatives). She also
  draws upon Eve Sedgwick's example of Austin's distinction between the wedding announcement and the
  wedding vow" (pp. 200–201, folios not stated). Judith Butler's *Bodies That Matter*, Saussure and
  Austin are cited together in one note (p. 200, folio not stated).
- **On gender marking as evidence.** "''*Beṭhi*'' is the feminine marker in the text, indicating the
  act of sitting" (p. 203, folio not stated); "''Kardi'' is the female form for the verb ''to do''"
  (p. 209, folio not stated); "The gender markers for all the nouns are underlined in the
  transliteration" (p. 209, folio not stated); "The verb in Arabic is *udhkurna*, feminine gender,
  as referring to the *azwaj* (the Prophet's consorts). *Udhkurna* means not only to ''remember''
  but also to ''recite,'' ''teach,'' ''make known,'' ''publish'' the Message" (p. 212, folio not
  stated). **This is the book's core philological move, visible in the notes.**
- **On a male poet's female voice.** "The fact that Waris Shah uses this in women's discourse reveals
  his sensitivity to feminine speech forms" (p. 210, folio not stated); "''*Nī*'' is an intimate,
  feminine-gender term in Panjabi, which women use to address one another" (p. 210, folio not
  stated); "Notice that the form of her narrative is a monologue because she is a solo singer here"
  (p. 204, folio not stated).
- **On the female voice in the ghazal and the gīt.** *Suhāg-rāt* "(nuptial night) is an erotic
  metaphor in the poetry of the subcontinent" (p. 208, folio not stated); a *sakhī* is "A female
