stated); a second untitled, **head-dropped** section (p. 105, folio not stated) reporting the author's
own fieldwork visit to the Bulle Shah shrine in Kasur, with five song texts and three interview
extracts (pp. 105–112, folios not stated); the chapter's conclusion and a Jakobson-derived

Sufism`, opening on p. 116 (folio not stated) — its chapter number **dropped from the text layer** —
which runs into the Sassi–Punnu myth and **breaks off mid-sentence** on p. 117 (folio not stated) at
"he escaped to go back to Bhambor where he had left Sassi. On the" (p. 117, folio not stated).

**The chapter boundary is corroborated by the endnote numbering.** Flattened superscripts run 31, 32,

RITUAL / sheet N of 239` appears on all twenty-one pages and is **ignored as a printing-sheet index,
never cited as a folio**.

**Who is speaking in the interview transcripts.** In the Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan interview the turns are
labelled only `A:` and `K:` (pp. 97–99, folios not stated). The book itself resolves `K` — "The
musician further affirmed, / K: In sufiānā-kalām—in arifānā-kalām—these things last" (p. 98, folio not
stated) — so `K` is Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan and `A` is the author asking the questions. **The book never
glosses the two letters; see Doubts.** Separately, in the *song* transliterations `A` and `B` are
**voice parts**, not speakers — "A&B Hath vic ‘‘Il Allah’’ di mālā" (p. 97, folio not stated),
"B /th /th /th /th [audience claps]" (p. 102, folio not stated) — the same two letters doing a
different job on the same pages.

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The shrine's hereditary qawwāls and their claim of three centuries' service.** "the qawwāls of the
  shrine, Muhammad Bakhsh and his paternal uncle Karam Bakhsh" (p. 105, folio not stated). "They said
  they had inherited them orally from their ancestors who had been qawwāls at the shrine for the last
  three centuries" (p. 106, folio not stated). Muhammad Bakhsh: "We are linked to this shrine, we are
  bound to it, and we sing here out of devotion to Baba Bulle Shah. The saint will not let us leave
  this shrine, and even if we do go away to other shrines to sing for the urs, we always return here.
  Our family has served this shrine for the last three centuries—generation after generation has sung
  here. We came with Bulle Shah's family from Uch Sharif, and we have learned his poetry from our
  ancestors, who have always sung here" (pp. 111–112, folios not stated). **This is a claim of
  hereditary musical office migrating with the saint's family; no sajjada nashin, no Auqaf, no
  endowment and no land are mentioned anywhere in this chunk: all (not stated).**
- **The daily singing timetable at the Bulle Shah shrine.** "We sing here twice a day. We sing in the
  morning from eight to ten, and then throughout the evening" (p. 112, folio not stated). On the
  author's visit the qawwāls "sang the entire evening, until after the evening prayers" (p. 111, folio
  not stated).
- **Women excluded from performing at the shrine, and the household alternative.** "I sought
  information about the women in their family. The qawwāls were reluctant to talk. They said, / Our
  women have never sung at the shrine. Perhaps, almost a century ago, they would go to the Syed
  households on special invitation. They would only sing among women. They sang Baba Bulle Shah's
  poetry for the Syed women" (p. 112, folio not stated). **"Almost a century ago" is the only time
  depth given: no date.**
- **The same pattern reported elsewhere.** "This claim too was affirmed earlier by the Sidi, Manganhar,
  and Mohana musicians who sing in the Syed households in the Sind among women audiences only. They
  sing at the urs, or in social settings such as the maulud (the prophet Muhammad's birth anniversary),
  weddings, and births. Similarly, female singers of Sufi poetry in the Talagang area near Rawalpindi
  claim to perform only in the feudal households of the Maliks" (p. 112, folio not stated).
- **The shrine bazaar and what is sold in it.** "The street near the shrine was colorfully decorated
  with shops that sell all kinds of artifacts such as marigolds, rose petals, candles, and small
  earthenware lamps called divās, which are filled with mustard oil and have a cotton wick in them.
  They are lit at the shrine" (pp. 105–106, folios not stated). "The shops displayed incense sticks,
  prayer beads, and silver-plated bangles with Quranic verses inscribed on them that devotees take to
  the shrine, or home as souvenirs. The shops also sell sweets that the devotees buy as tabarruk,
  nazar, or niāz (food for distribution in the shrine)" (p. 106, folio not stated).
- **Offerings made at the tomb.** Green and red sheets with gold tinsel over the crypt: "The devotees
  bring these as offerings" (p. 106, folio not stated); candles lit and fātehā offered at the
  surrounding small tombs (p. 106, folio not stated).
- **Who was present, counted.** "All together, including the musicians, there were fifty women and men
  in the shrine" (p. 106, folio not stated). **This is the chunk's only visitor count.**
- **Women's ritual at the shrine, and their movement into the sama.** "Women devotees who until then
  were performing their rituals of prayer or lighting candles and incense sticks at the poet's shrine
  or elsewhere joined the assembly to listen to the qawwāls. They came and sat with me and my female
  companion" (p. 106, folio not stated).
- **Hijṛās at the Bulle Shah shrine — the book's stated singularity.** "Male devotees in the shrine
  made a small circle in front of the musicians and started to dance in rhythm with the music. Later,
  some hijṛās (eunuchs) also joined the group" (p. 106, folio not stated). "The hijṛas at Bulle Shah
  had ghungrū tied to their ankles. They danced to the rhythm of the music" (p. 106, folio not stated).
  **"Bulle Shah's was the only shrine where I encountered the hijṛās. Most probably they are
  traditionally linked to the shrine because of Bulle Shah's own life history of having lived among the
  dancing girls for several years"** (p. 106, folio not stated). "The hijṛās continued to dance with
  the tempo of the music" (p. 111, folio not stated); "The hijṛās dance in response to the music at the
  Bulle Shah shrine" (p. 113, folio not stated). **The author marks the link as her own inference
  ("Most probably"), not as the qawwāls' claim.**
- **Hijṛās as an institution, as the book describes it.** "The hijṛās are known as khāwājāsarā in the
  court traditions of the Muslim rulers of the subcontinent, and they held important positions at the
  court. They were also associated with burial rites such as at the Nizamuddin Auliya shrine. Today in
  Pakistan and India the hijṛās sing and dance at births, especially of male infants, and at
  circumcisions and weddings. They have their own community networks through which they find out about
  events and appear with their musicians, hijṛās like themselves, to play music while some of them

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The book's governing claim, stated by the musician rather than the author.** Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan:
  "The Sufi poets—in the tradition of Sufi mystical poetry—these mystic poets—when they speak they do
  it in the female voice—they present themselves as the female—for them their beloved—their mentor—
  their shaikh—is the male—whereas their own voice is that of the female—their own discourse is that of
  the female—" (p. 98, folio not stated); "They court the beloved in the female voice, they woo the
  beloved—themselves speaking in the female voice—you can read the entire sufiānā-kalām—the entire Sufi
  kalām—that entire discourse is in the female voice—" (p. 98, folio not stated).
- **Its aesthetic justification, in his words.** "There is an elegance in it—there is an elegance—a
  humility in the female voice—which is lacking in the male voice—all these aesthetics—it is altogether
  something very different" (p. 98, folio not stated); "they found their spirit—their soul—the essence
  of what they wanted to say in the female voice" (p. 98, folio not stated).
- **The qawwāl's counter-gloss: the voice is the spirit, not the woman.** Asked "why they sang their
  narratives in the female voice or used the aurat kī avāz", Muhammad Bakhsh said: "It is actually not
  the ‘‘female.’’ The voice is that of the spirit. This is the subject of human existence. Life is a
  spinning, that is why it is the female. If human beings lived the way they are expected to live, all
  would be fine. We must live in humility. The female, who spins, lives in humility" (p. 111, folio not
  stated). **This is a flat disagreement about what the female voice *is*, between two of the book's
  own informants; the author does not adjudicate it in this chunk.**
- **Women's absence from the record, as the musician explains it.** "Probably women have sung Sufi
  songs—off and on—on different occasions—but I don't know of any such women—probably these women never
  ever came into the limelight—were never ever acknowledged for their Sufi narratives—" (p. 99, folio
  not stated).
- **Vernacular competence as repertoire advantage — the author's own argument.** "performers like
  Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan and Abida Parvin, who sang in the vernaculars, had a much wider repertoire
  compared with performers who could only use the limited texts of the North Indian style of qawwālī in
  Urdu or Hindi. Performers who sang in the indigenous languages had more linguistic resources to draw
  from, such as the texts of the Sufi poets of their areas, the folklore, and nature, such as
  references to birds, seasons, and the landscape" (p. 99, folio not stated). "Abida Parvin therefore
  has an advantage over her male qawwāl counterparts because of her linguistic resources" (p. 105,
  folio not stated).
- **Improvisation and the composite text.** "They sang the text but with a lot of improvisation built
  into it, according to the context… Sometimes, they combined the original poem of a Sufi poet with
  other fragments that they had either created themselves or a songwriter had built into the text for
  them. They would even take verses from the poetry of different Sufi poets and use them as adjacent
  texts, improvising the discourse, according to the cues they received from the audience" (p. 99,
  folio not stated). **This is the book's warrant for treating a recorded "Bulle Shah" or "Sultan Bahu"
  text as a performance artefact rather than an authorial one.**
- **Metalanguage — the author's analytic term.** Of the heat in the auditorium: "she even referred to
  the metalanguage of the heat and the humidity in the auditorium as a context of energy for the
  seekers to find the murshid" (p. 101, folio not stated); "In the text of the song, for instance in
  line fifteen, is Abida Parvin's metalanguage of the heat in the auditorium" (p. 103, folio not
  stated). Of the dance: "Abida Parvin uses the metalanguage of the dance (nāc) as a state of ecstasy
  through which the beloved is won… Terms such as dhammāl, nāc, even qalandar are states of ecstasy
  through which the devotee seeks the murshid. The reference could even be to the Divine Being. It is
  evident, then, that those musicians who sing in concert and at the shrines utilize much metalanguage"
  (p. 105, folio not stated). "the word nacāyā is significant because it expresses the act of the
  dance, which in itself is the metalanguage for ecstasy" (p. 109, folio not stated).
- **Performance as political speech.** "That evening this woman performer sang in a fiery mood and
  blasted the orthodox government of the time in the presence of its official bureaucracy by using the
  frames of Sufi poetry" (p. 101, folio not stated). "In her narratives she ridiculed the so-called
  ‘‘knowledgeable ones’’ or the caretakers of faith. She compared them to animals, to cattle, to fish,
  and to frogs" (p. 102, folio not stated). **Which government is (not stated).**
- **The bridal and spinning tropes as disciple-mentor allegory.** The second qawwālī "was about a bride
  who is forced to leave her father's home to go and live with her husband and his family. The metaphor
  of the journey to the in-laws' home is a preparation for the hereafter, to which every human being is
  subjected. This, too, represents the disciple-mentor relationship, with the disciple playing the role
  of the submissive bride" (p. 109, folio not stated). The carkhī-nāma (spinning-wheel song): "the
  narrative is of a young damsel who spins her trousseau for the forthcoming life in her husband's
  home… The young damsel is the disciple who must toil diligently to gain the mentor's wisdom" (p. 110,
  folio not stated).
- **The veil and kaśf.** "The musicians again sing of the implied reference to the veil that covers the
  bride's face. All the toil, all the suffering is for that final state, that of the kaśf, or the
  unveiling of the beloved, that state of illumination when the disciple meets the mentor" (p. 110,
  folio not stated).
- **Gender reversal at the top of the chain.** Of the kāfī addressed to Bulle Shah as bride: "the
  beloved or mentor's image assumes multiple identities. Instead of the male, the mentor now becomes
  the female bride. Thus, the tropes in the poetry are complex" (p. 111, folio not stated).
- **The Jakobson-derived model of shrine and concert performance.** "the musicians mediate spiritual
  experience for their devotees. They are the speakers, who apply their linguistic resources of poetry,
  folklore, myth, syntax, semantics, and all forms of language play accompanied with music… The
  musicians establish communication between themselves and their speech communities through language
  that is emotive for themselves as well as for their listeners. Their goal is to fulfill the emotional
  and psychological needs of their listeners" (p. 112, folio not stated); "the performers become the
  transmitters because they use cultural semantics embedded in a shared meaning between themselves and
  their audiences, thereby making the process interactional. They add body language in the transmission
  of the discourse that further enhances the interaction" (p. 112, folio not stated).
- **Improvisation as an interactional, not an inspirational, fact.** "The improvisation on certain
  segments of a text that is largely fixed is closely linked to the interactional processes in the
  anthropology of performance. The performers continue to sing certain segments of the text for an
  extensive time period because their audiences are emotionally involved in it" (p. 113, folio not
  stated). "In such a performance, speech production becomes an ad hoc process for the performer…
  mutually achieved and ratified within a cultural code between performers and recipients" (p. 114,
  folio not stated).
- **Code switching and prestige codes.** "The major code of a performance can be Panjabi, Siraiki,
  Sindhi, Pushto, Urdu, or a regional language. Within the major code singers can either start a
  performance in Arabic or switch to it, because it is the language of the Quran. They will also switch
  to Persian… The use of these prestige codes establishes the authenticity of the discourse for the
  listeners" (p. 115, folio not stated).
- **Turn-taking in the ensemble.** "The qawwāls follow a complete organized system of social interaction
  of ‘‘taking the floor,’’ which is mapped out in the transliterations. The musicians know among each
  other, and through vocal cues, who will take the floor and when" (p. 114, folio not stated).
- **The chapter's own summary.** "This chapter then describes how qawwālī and sufiānā-kalām performers
  interact with their audiences in particular contexts. They draw upon the resources of the Quran and
  the hadīth. They seek inspiration from the lives and the poetry of the Sufis of the subcontinent and
  the Middle Eastern regions. The diverse resources, including their own linguistic and musical skills,
  are blended with local folklore, proverbs, and ecology to create an oral culture that is a visible
  component of the performing arts as well as shrine ritual in Pakistan" (p. 115, folio not stated).
- **The opening argument of the new chapter, `Female Myths in Sufism`.** "The complexities of race,
  gender, class, and caste figure prominently in the narratives of the qawwāls and sufiānā-kalām
  performers. Sufi poetry in Pakistan and India was sometimes composed in opposition to the religious
  establishment and was expressed in subtle ways. It survived through representation in myth and the
  complex tropes of the female voices" (p. 116, folio not stated).
- **The author's explicit refusal of an anachronism.** "Whether or not the Sufi poets were
  ‘‘feminists’’ cannot be claimed here as the term ‘‘feminism’’ is a fairly recent one. However, the
  female myths in Sufi poetry certainly represent the voices of marginalized groups and continue to be
  used as representative frames even today" (p. 116, folio not stated).
- **Myth as a metaphor for hegemony.** The lovers' myths are sung "because the myths have become
  metaphors for the polarities of gender, religious, sociopolitical, and economic hegemony" (p. 116,
  folio not stated). Of Sassi: "Sassi becomes the symbol of strength against patriarchy. Her death in
  the desert is in fact her struggle to seek respite against social injustice. She tries to reclaim her
  love. In the Sufi lore she becomes a paradigm of moral courage and spiritual triumph" (p. 117, folio
  not stated).
