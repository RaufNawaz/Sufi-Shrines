## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The Manganhar as the designated ritual musicians of Sind.** "In Sind there can be no ritual
  without the Manganhar, be it a birth, a wedding, or a death. They are the designated community
  musicians, and each community has its own Manganhars" (p. 77, folio not stated). They "are distinct
  from the Sidi" (p. 77, folio not stated).
- **The etymology the book gives, from an informant.** "‘‘Manganhar’’ originated from the words
  mangan, which means ‘‘to beg,’’ and hār, which means ‘‘a garland of flowers.’’ Thus, according to
  an informant at the institute of Sindhology at Sind University, a manganhar is one who begs for
  flowers. In the roving-minstrel traditions, alms were given to the minstrels in return for their
  musical services" (pp. 77–78, folios not stated).
- **The repertoire and the occasions, in Alan Faqir's own words** (p. 78, folio not stated): "The
  Syeds—when they have weddings they invite us to sing—we play the śādmānā—when the Syeds come—we
  play the haswārī when a pir comes—or if we see a murshid come"; the genres listed are "duā—śādmānā—
  haswārī—olang—or the candar". Women's genres: "the women sing gīts or gīc—they sing duā [prayer]";
  "The women sing the sehrā—they sing the lāon [wedding songs]" (p. 78, folio not stated).
- **Payment in kind, then in cash — a change the book dates only as "over the last quarter of a
  century".** Alan Faqir: "in those days the money was very little—lots of grain—we used to get paid
  in grain—lots of it there was—one sack—four sacks—this was more than enough for a poor household"
  (p. 78, folio not stated). "Over the last quarter of a century the practices have changed. Musicians
  are now paid in cash rather than agricultural produce" (p. 80, folio not stated); "the vaḍerās
  prefer to pay in cash rather than in agricultural commodities. Alan Faqir stated this in his
  interview" (p. 81, folio not stated).
- **The musician as dispute-broker for landowners.** "if there was a dispute they would come to the
  faqīr and say, ‘‘Faqīr, do a duā [a prayer], that I may be reconciled with my brother’’—you see the
  vaḍerās [landowners] have disputes with each other… Then they come to the faqīr and say, ‘‘Faqīr—I
  am your slave—faqīr—I am your faqīr—help me—reconcile me with my brother’’—you see, we lessen our
  life spans with our deeds" (p. 78, folio not stated).
- **The family's titles, male and female.** "The women in our families are called mangtī—faqīriānī—our
  family is called faqīr bādshāh—we beg—faqīr sāĩ—faqīr sāhib—this is our family title—the women are
  mangtīs"; "in India they are called manganiārī—the woman musician too is called a mangiārī—or a
  mangtī" (p. 78, folio not stated).
- **When a woman of the family sang.** "The woman would not sing—unless her circumstances forced
  her—if her husband was alive, she stayed at home—but if he was not there then she had to do the
  man's work—naturally she had to sing—they had to decorate the drums… they had to sing the sehrās
  for the weddings… they had to sing the lāon for the bride and the groom" (pp. 78–79, folios not
  stated). **Widowhood, not choice, is given as the trigger.**
- **Shame, caste and the silence about women's singing.** "Musicians like him are extremely reluctant
  to discuss the subject because of the shame associated with being caste musicians, especially where
  women were involved. It is a matter of socioeconomic caste" (p. 79, folio not stated). "Shame
  (izzat) is a concern for men, and they are silent about their womenfolk's singing" (p. 82, folio not
  stated). "It reflects poorly on their own image as breadwinners… Middle-class or bourgeois
  respectability is a significant factor" (p. 82, folio not stated).
- **Veiling as the explicit link.** "The community attitudes in relation to women singing are also
  linked with women's veiling (pardā). Women must not be seen in public, especially by unrelated men.
  Then, to sing is even more damaging socially for the position of their male relatives who struggle
  to change socioeconomic class and break out of the old community bonds of servitude to landowners"
  (p. 82, folio not stated).
- **Segregated performance as a space women make for themselves.** "Such performances are segregated
  so that women can create a context of their own. They can be themselves without being stared or
  gazed at by men who do not belong to the inner circle" (p. 77, folio not stated).
- **Occupational exit and the new employers.** Caste musicians "want their children to get educated
  and be employed in the government service, which promises a stable job and an income, together with
  social prestige"; they want them "to become professionals such as doctors, engineers, MBAs, or
  educators. They no longer wish to be associated with the stigma of only being musicians" (p. 79,
  folio not stated). "Male members of the immediate family of the Manganhars are employed in
  government services such as the police, education departments, or accounting services. The
  universities employ them… Some of them hold influential positions with the federal government in
  Islamabad. This the musicians call a pucci naukri (a permanent job) that gives them higher social
  status" (p. 81, folio not stated).
- **Dispersal of the service communities.** "I have seen communities of workers, such as musicians,
  village barbers, and potters who had a variety of other roles in the landowning structures,
  disperse within the last twenty-five years" (p. 81, folio not stated). The communities listed as
  involved in the roving-minstrel traditions are "the Manganhars, the Sidis, snake-charming jogīs,
  flute players, potters, and many more who lived on the margins of the society" (pp. 79–80, folios
  not stated).
- **The landlords' withdrawal from the village.** "The younger generation of landlords no longer lives
  in the villages. They live in large urban centers like Karachi, Lahore, or Islamabad, or they study
  in the academies of the West, from where they return to join the country's political forces. The
  land thus becomes a base from which political identity and votes come" (p. 81, folio not stated).
- **The shrine melā displaced as a talent market.** "Baloch and Mirza at Radio Pakistan in Hyderabad
  affirmed that they no longer have to go to melās at the Sufi shrines to hunt for talent. Many
  educated female and male performers come themselves to the radio and television for auditions and
  are contracted to sing" (p. 80, folio not stated). "Female singers like Taj Mastani may be
  discovered through the radio, instead of at Sufi shrines, as were Reshma or Zeena Bai" (p. 80,
  folio not stated).
- **Corporate sponsorship.** "In the last ten years, multinational companies like Pepsi have hired
  young female and male singers for promotional purposes. These musicians, who are mostly
  professionals such as doctors and engineers, sing for television, and Pepsi sponsors the programs"
  (p. 80, folio not stated).
- **The qawwālī ensemble as an institution.** "The number of musicians in a qawwālī concert can range
  from one to twenty or even more depending on the resources of the group leader"; "The key
  musicians, such as those who play the harmonium, sit in the front row, while those who clap and
  sing the chorus sit in the back row. The group leader is called the mohri, which means the
  ‘‘leading chess figure.’’ The leader orchestrates the performance" (p. 83, folio not stated).
- **Ritual order inside a Chishtiyya shrine.** The ʿurs qawwālī "is always initiated with a qaul or
  saying of the prophet Muhammad, ‘‘Mun kunto Maulā fā Alī-un Maulā,’’ in Arabic"; "During an urs
  performance at the Chishtiyya shrines the poetry is focused on Ali, on the saint buried at that
  particular shrine, and on his spiritual lineage" (pp. 84–85, folios not stated). "This eclectic form
  of poetry would not be permitted inside the shrine settings of the Chishtiyya or other orders"
  (p. 85, folio not stated).
- **Custodial authority over the performance.** At Nizamuddin Auliya's ʿurs the qawwālī "is guided by
  a shaikh or his spiritual representative, and the audience is exclusively male, primarily the
  associates of the pirzade or the sajjādā-nashīn and his Sufi associates" (p. 83, folio not stated).
- **The devotional visit and its vocabulary.** "Devotional attendance at a Sufi shrine is called
  hazrī-denā in native terms, which means paying a ritual visit. Other native terms for presenting
  oneself at a Sufi shrine are salām-karnā or doing a ziārat of the saint, achieved through the
  rituals that are performed there such as listening to qawwālī or kalām and performing prayer
  (fātehā)" (pp. 92–93, folios not stated).
- **When devotees come.** "During the urs or on Thursday evenings, or at any time of the day or night,
  devotees flock to the Sufi shrines, to communicate with the mystics spiritually. The communication
  brings relief to their emotional and personal concerns" (p. 93, folio not stated). A sufiānā-kalām
  performance "can also be held in a shrine during the urs celebrations of a Sufi saint or poet, on
  Thursday evenings, or on a daily basis" (p. 85, folio not stated).
- **The musician as murid and the performance as offering.** "If they sing at a shrine, they consider
  themselves the murids (disciples) of the Sufi, and they believe that their performance is a nazar
  (gift) to the spirit of the Sufi who is buried there. They take upon themselves the role of
  mediators" (p. 90, folio not stated).
- **Cash flowing to the performer in the concert hall.** Nusrat's listeners "were overcome with rapture

- **Payment by female patrons.** The Sidi, Mohana and Manganhar women "said that they only perform

- **Shrine attendance by women musicians tied to their mentor's ʿurs.** "The Sidi, Mohana, and

- **State promotion of qawwālī after partition.** "In Pakistan itself, after the partition of

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Why devotees come, as the book states the belief.** "Today, when devotees go to a Sufi shrine, they
  do so because of their reverence for and belief in the spiritual powers of that Sufi. They believe
  in the mystic's capability to have performed karāmāt (miracles) in his lifetime. The devotees
  continue to attach the same spiritual powers to the mystic's shrine and the mystic's ability to
  provide spiritual and emotional relief even after death" (p. 90, folio not stated).
- **Lal Shahbaz Qalandar's jalāl, and children.** The faqīrs' whirling "is said to express the immense
  jalāl (energy) of Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar. His devotees believe that many prayers are answered
  at the shrine and many wishes granted" (pp. 93–94, folios not stated); the melody Reshma sang
  "honored Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar's jalāl or spiritual powers as a bestower of children" (p. 94,
  folio not stated). **Attributed to devotees and to the singer, not asserted by the author.**
- **A singer's own claim of blessing.** Reshma "believes that Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar has blessed
  her" (p. 94, folio not stated); and generally, "Almost all the performers that I have interviewed
  for this study believe that they are divinely blessed by their murshids (spiritual mentors) for the
  devotion with which they sing" (pp. 94–95, folios not stated).
- **The mi'rāj as the root image of the veil.** "The prophet Muhammad's ascension to heaven and his
  meeting with his beloved is embedded in images of the veil. The event is also called the mi'rāj"
  (p. 96, folio not stated).
- **kaśf, the doctrine of unveiling.** "The veil in Sufi poetry is intimately related to kaśf, or the
  doctrine of lifting the veil, that is, the Divine Being revealing itself to the lover" (p. 96, folio
  not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The chapter's method, stated.** "In this chapter I describe performances of Sufi poetry sung to
  music in sociolinguistic terms, looking at both qawwālī and sufiānā-kalām… The participants of the
  events are the musicians, who are the speakers, and their audiences, who are the listeners.
  Therefore, the discussion here may be perceived in terms of speaking as a cultural system" (p. 83,
  folio not stated).
- **The three speech events analysed** (p. 85–86, folios not stated): Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan in the
  United Kingdom; Muhammad Bakhsh at the Bulle Shah shrine in Kasur; and Abida Parvin's
  sufiānā-kalām concert in Islamabad, "where I was a participant in the event". "Since qawwālī is a
  male genre I will investigate two contexts by male qawwāls and their ensembles" (p. 85, folio not
  stated).
- **The book's central claim in this chunk: men sing as women.** "In the male musicians' qawwālī, I
  discovered that many times they sang in female voices. They did it to express the disciple's
  submission to the spiritual mentor, represented as the female" (p. 86, folio not stated). The
  mechanism is set out at p. 96 (folio not stated): "The murid becomes the lover and the murshid the
  beloved. The former is represented as the submissive female. The metaphor becomes more complex with
  the infusion of bridal imagery into the relationship. The beloved becomes the bride or sometimes the
  bridegroom wearing a veil. Musicians sing devotional songs to this bride or to the groom asking them
  to lift the veil." At Bhit, "They sing his poetry in the falsetto, imitating a woman's voice"
  (p. 96, folio not stated).
- **Performance as identity and as rite.** "Performance that includes cultural criteria such as verbal,
  musical, and body language becomes a mark of identity and sometimes an initiation rite, such as
  Karima wriggling her body for the groom-to-be. The context is a mutually achieved interaction
  between performers and their audiences" (p. 77, folio not stated).
- **The researcher's own effect on the event, admitted.** "Although the performance in Karachi Mori was
  specially organized for me, it was also an opportunity for Qalandar Shah to visit his family"
  (p. 77, folio not stated).
- **Concert qawwālī as a twentieth-century, postcolonial product.** "Qawwālī performances in concert
  are a product of the twentieth century, a feature of postcolonial politics when large Muslim
  populations from South Asia moved to the West"; "In concert, qawwālī evolved as an aesthetic musical
  form, albeit generated through the singing of devotional Sufi poetry" (p. 84, folio not stated).
  "Qawwālī discourse in concert thus has become more diverse" (p. 85, folio not stated).
- **Dating of the genres.** "Qawwālī and sufiānā-kalām are both musical genres connected to the
  singing of Sufi mystical poetry in Pakistan and India. The tradition evolved around the thirteenth
  century `[text-layer?: era marker dropped]`" (p. 86, folio not stated).
- **Register distinguishes the two genres.** "Qawwālī is embedded in the structures of classical music
  using elitist Perso-Arabic linguistic forms found in Hindi and Urdu poetry or poetry in vernacular
  languages such as Panjabi. Sufiānā-Kalām is mystical poetry sung in the vernacular languages of the
  subcontinent such as Hindi, Gujrati, Sindhi, Siraiki, and Panjabi" (p. 86, folio not stated).
- **Cyclical time and the aesthetics of timelessness.** "Unlike the strict structure of a western
  concert that is linear, the concept of time in qawwālī and sufiānā-kalām concerts is cyclical; there
  are moments when time is suspended and performers and their audiences are caught in a timelessness.
  … The aesthetics of the performance is the quality of timelessness" (p. 86, folio not stated).
  "Most performances continue well into the night beyond the allocated time" (p. 87, folio not
  stated).
- **A "universe of discourse" among nonliterate listeners.** "Although at times their listeners are
  nonliterate, they are conversant with the content through the oral media around them, such as the
  festivals at the Sufi shrines" (p. 87, folio not stated); "In the events where the speech
  communities get together, a ‘‘universe of discourse’’ is created" (p. 87, folio not stated).
- **The sacred frame is what makes a flirtatious lyric mystical — the author's own analytical move.**
  "To an untrained ear it would seem like a flirtatious love poem. However, when I transliterated the
  opening verses of the qawwālī where the musicians invoke the spirit of Farid… I understood the
  mystical trope of the qawwālī" (pp. 88–89, folios not stated); "Had it not been for the very sacred
  frame at the beginning, when his ensemble invoked Baba Farid, a researcher might have taken the
  performance for a secular love song where a lover seeks a beloved and serenades her" (p. 89, folio
  not stated).
- **The performer's own explanation, when asked.** "I specifically asked him about this clandestine,
  flirtatious style of singing qawwālī in England before expatriate Pakistani and Sikh audiences that
  made them ecstatic. Khan smiled quietly and took some time to say that such a state is attained
  after much riazat (practice) in singing mystical texts" (p. 89, folio not stated).
- **kefīat and samā as things performers manufacture.** "The musicians' body movements, together with
  the rhythm of the music and language, can create a kefīat (mystical delight) among the listeners"
  (p. 90, folio not stated); "They will use their linguistic resources of poetry, myth, syntax,
  semantics, and tonal and speech patterns together with music to create the samā… At the grassroots
  level, samā is also understood as a context or environment, and the aim of every competent performer
  of Sufi music and discourse is to create the samā" (p. 92, folio not stated).
- **Gender of the performer, stated in passing as female.** "The more accomplished the performer the
  better she can accomplish this" (p. 91, folio not stated) — the author's pronoun, against a chapter
  that has just called qawwālī "a male genre" (p. 85, folio not stated). **Kept as printed; see
  Doubts.**
- **A call for further research.** "Follow-up research on the mendicant traditions and their
  transformations is bound to produce interesting results. However, practitioners will allow a glimpse
  into the community only if the right channels of trust are established" (pp. 81–82, folios not
  stated).
