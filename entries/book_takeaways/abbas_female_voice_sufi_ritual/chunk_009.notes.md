## Practices, institutions, economy

**The glossary is, in effect, this book's own lexicon of shrine practice, and is the single most
useful finding aid in the chunk. Definitions below are quoted, not paraphrased.**

- **Custodianship and succession.** `sajjādā nashīn "The one seated on the prayer carpet"; official
  successor to a saint's shrine and therefore to the saint himself` (p. 187, folio not stated) —
  carries an endnote superscript 8. `pīrzāde The descendants of a pīr` (p. 186, folio not stated).
  `pīr A Sufi spiritual teacher or guide` (p. 186, folio not stated); `murshid A spiritual guide,
  master, or mentor` (p. 185, folio not stated); `murīd A devotee or disciple` (p. 186, folio not
  stated); `Shaikh-s ekh [text-layer?: Shaikh-śekh] A spiritual guide in Sufism; a leader, a prominent
  person, and a scholar` (p. 188, folio not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **A native scholar's claim to standing.** From the long methodological endnote: `I use a variety of
  live speech samples from the extensive fieldwork I did in Pakistan and the data that I collected from
  archives. I transcribed and translated the speech samples together with my interviews with the
  musicians, both women and men who sing Sufi poetry. As a native of the culture I bring my own
  interpretations to the study` (p. 194, folio not stated).
- **The gap she claims to fill.** Surveying Abu-Lughod, Altorki and El-Solh, Visweswaran and Trix, she
  writes: `However, none of the research is in Sufi rituals, and their methodologies are different`
  (p. 194, folio not stated).
- **A methodological borrowing, stated openly.** `I refer to them as transliterations. Most descriptions
  of the conventions are quoted from Jim Schenkein, ed., Studies in the Organization of Conversational
  Interaction… However, some conventions are reworked for the audience` (p. 192, folio not stated);
  `Transliterated with some adaptations according to the conversation analysis system of Harvey Sacks,
  Emanuel A. Schegloff, and Gail Jefferson… I use a dash to represent a pause in speech` (p. 198, folio
  not stated). The glossary defines the method: `conversation analysis The methodology of Sacks,
  Schegloff, and Jefferson to study live speech; an inductive, empirical study of the interactions in
  speech, especially turn-taking` (p. 180, folio not stated), and `ethnography of speaking A methodology
  to study speech communication in a community or group…` (p. 181, folio not stated).
- **A gap in the qawwali literature she names.** Against Keil and Feld, Johnston and Manuel: `The
  research overlooks the development of qawwālī music in international settings` (p. 193, folio not
  stated).
- **Shared shrines, and partition as the break.** `The practice of non-Muslim devotees visiting Muslim
  shrines and vice versa is common when diverse religious communities coexist. This was especially so in
  undivided India before the partition of [numeral dropped, text-layer]. Islamic mysticism, that is,
  Sufism, was a shared experience with the indigenous forms of mysticism. Mughal emperors such as Akbar
  encouraged the syncreticism` (pp. 196–197, folios not stated). **The clearest interpretive claim in
  the chunk, and directly relevant to the archive's mixed Muslim/Hindu/Sikh/Jain rows.**
- **Jamālī against jalālī, as a typology of saints and of shrines.** `Jamālī mysticism is attained
  through intuition and creativity, such as by Shah Abdul Latif, a poet and musician. In contrast to
  this, jalālī mysticism is achieved through rigor and discipline. Ali, the fourth caliph, is known for
  his jalālī powers. Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar of Sehwan is also known for his jalālī or supernatural
  powers, and his shrine is called a jalālī shrine` (p. 197, folio not stated). **A shrine typology
  applied to a named archive shrine — worth carrying to both rows.**
- **Two genres of Sufi performance, distinguished.** `Briefly, classical qawwālī created by Amir Khusrau
  (d. [numeral dropped, text-layer]) starts with the qaul of the Prophet in Arabic. Sufiānā-kalām is the
  poetry of the Sufi poets sung in the vernacular languages with minimal instrumentation` (p. 195, folio
  not stated).
- **On trance vocabulary, following Qureshi.** `She distinguishes the terms adequately within the
  "frameworks of spiritual arousal." She identifies trance through degrees of ecstasy, and the terms she
  uses are behoshī, wajd, and hāl` (p. 196, folio not stated).
- **A poet's land, not only his beloved.** Alan Faqir `told me in his interview that Sassi and Marvi in
  Shah's poetry also represent the land pillaged by conquerors and invaders` (p. 196, folio not stated).
  **The author reports this as her informant's reading, not her own.**
- **A correction the author refused to make, and said so.** `A native Sindhi informant corrected this
  term to mean a choir of mendicants who sing mystical poetry, but I retain the context in which I
