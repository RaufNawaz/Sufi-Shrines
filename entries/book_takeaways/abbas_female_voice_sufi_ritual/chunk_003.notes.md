## Practices, institutions, economy

- **ʿUrs as a state-organised event with invited musicians.** "In the oﬃcially organized urs
  celebrations in Pakistan commemorating the Suﬁ's death anniversary at his shrine, key musicians are
  invited to sing", note 52 (p. 55, folio not stated). "In the Sind, performers like Abida Parvin are
  invited to sing at Shah Abdul Latif's shrine", note 53 (p. 55, folio not stated).
- **ʿUrs duration and crowding at Sehwan.** "during the urs celebrations, which last for more than a
  week, it is almost impossible to walk in the narrow streets of the city" (p. 56, folio not stated).
- **The shrine as a labour market.** Sehwan "is ﬂooded with large numbers of female musicians who
  converge on the city to earn a living" (pp. 56–57, folios not stated); at the shrine there are
  "other women—women who are looking for a livelihood—they join in the singing of suﬁānā-kalām—and
  this happens every year" (p. 61, folio not stated). "The radio in Pakistan has played a signiﬁcant
  role in identifying musicians and providing them a livelihood" (p. 55, folio not stated).
- **The economy of the roving minstrels, in detail.** "The loṭevālī or chalevālī are roving gypsy
  women who move from one shrine to another while their families trade in livestock and peddle
  inexpensive local textiles and pots and pans. Their audiences are women and men from their own
  socioeconomic background who participate in the shrine activities at the time of the urs and who
  give them small sums of money during the performance. **Women tend to pay less because they are
  unemployed, while men can be more generous**" (p. 58, folio not stated).
- **The size of the takings, stated as a figure.** The loṭevālī "come to the Shahbaz Qalandar shrine
  with a loṭa—and sing there—and make a livelihood on a rupee, two rupee—the alms they get there"
  (p. 61, folio not stated).
- **Instruments and the naming of a profession by its instrument.** "They will either play a yak-tārā
  or a ghaṛā, an earthen pot used as a drum. Their identity is a ring (chalā), which they wear around
  the middle ﬁnger and use to create percussion on a loṭā (aluminum pot) or a ghaṛa. These minstrels
  are called loṭevālī for playing the loṭā, chalevālī for playing the chalā, or ghaṛevālī for playing
  the ghaṛā", note 56 (pp. 57–58, folios not stated). "The female roving minstrels are accompanied by
  small groups of instrumentalists perhaps playing percussion or accordion. But, by and large, they
  have minimal instruments" (p. 57, folio not stated).
- **Unamplified performance.** "Performers like Reshma sing the melody in their high-pitched,
  full-throated voices at the shrine without the support of microphones" (p. 57, folio not stated).
  Of Zeena Bai: "in those days—there were no loudspeakers—people report that—in a litter—where the
  litter is—she would ride a camel—and sing—the range of her voice would resonate—around half a mile"
  (p. 62, folio not stated).
- **Mannat (vow) as the reason for coming.** Reshma: "I was young at the time when I went there and
  made a mannat—for my brother—and then I went for his mannat", note 58, for his marriage into the
  tribe; "Then you went to Shahbaz Qalandar—to fulﬁll the mannat and you became famous" (p. 58, folio
  not stated).
- **Dhammāl: definition, timing, gender segregation and healing.** "the dhammāl (the Qalandari dance
  performed at the Lal Shahbaz Qalandar shrine in Sehwan every evening)" (p. 64, folio not stated);
  "The state of ecstasy is induced with the rhythmic beat of the naubats (drums)" (p. 65, folio not
  stated); "In this dance the soul, mind, and body are said to function in deep contemplation of
  God's name" (pp. 64–65, folios not stated). "**Both men and women participate in the dhammāl. Women
  perform the dance in a certain section of the courtyard. Many times, families will bring women who
  are emotionally disturbed to participate in the dhammāl, which devotees claim has healing powers**"
  (p. 66, folio not stated).
- **The healing bargain, and its price.** "In Baluchistan, such healing ceremonies are called guāṭi.
  The family of the unwell woman will negotiate with the ''Suﬁ'' certain terms that will be fulﬁlled
  when the woman gets well, such as a vow that there will be a ceremony and the woman's husband or
  family will buy her an expensive gift, perhaps in gold, if she recovers from her illness", note 67
  (p. 66, folio not stated).
- **A shrine bazaar with a cassette trade.** "I discovered that one can buy cassettes of healing music
  for emotionally disturbed persons in the bazaar outside the shrine" (p. 66, folio not stated).
- **Melā as a talent market, now defunct.** "producer Saleem Gilani discovered Reshma—and Ustad
  Muhammad Khan discovered Zeena Bai—that tradition has died when we would go to the melās—to hunt
  for talent—now educated women are there in the ﬁeld—" (p. 62, folio not stated); "we have covered
  all the melās at the shrines" (p. 61, folio not stated).
- **A melā dated by the Islamic calendar.** The Sidis "mostly sing at their pir's annual melā, which
  is on the sixteenth day after the Muslim festival of Eid ul Azha, when the faithful oﬀer a
  sacriﬁcial animal", note 83; the "Mama Gor" composition is sung "at their pir's melā after
  midnight" (p. 71, folio not stated).
- **Gharānā as institution.** Zeena Bai's is "a Rajasthani gharānā [musical lineage]" (p. 61, folio
  not stated) and also "the Maṛichā gharānā" (p. 62, folio not stated); "there are two gharānās in
  Hyderabad—the Indore gharānā and the Gawalior gharānā" (p. 61, folio not stated); "this is a
  tradition—it is an ustād gharānā—whose sons and grandsons are all alive—this is something not very
  old" (p. 62, folio not stated).
- **Burial of musicians.** Zeena Bai "is buried in the Hyderabad graveyard where all the great
  musicians of the city are buried" (p. 62, folio not stated).
- **Qawwālī and the gendering of genre.** "The fact that now Abida Parvin has introduced the qaul in
  the suﬁānā-kalām tradition ﬁts in with this assertion. **The qawwālī genre of Suﬁ music is
  essentially a male domain**" (p. 55, folio not stated). Abida Parvin "uses the ḍohra or the bait,
  short two-line verses, within the main body of her narrative, a style she imbibed from her male
  mentors, including her late husband, Shaikh Ghulam Hussain… no other woman musician in the Sind has
  this style, which is essentially a unique male style" (p. 55, folio not stated).
- **Patronage by landowners.** Taj Mastani "is additionally engaged by large landowners for private
  events such as weddings and births" (p. 64, folio not stated). A photo caption reads "Patronized by
  a landowner: Hinda Bai, later known as Bali" (p. 68, folio not stated). Karima "gave me further
  information about the shrines at which she sang, as well as her performances in the landowners'
  households and community festivals" (p. 71, folio not stated).
- **Social sanction against women performers.** "Although Kubra is a musician, she is shy to admit her
  skill as a singer, due to negative community attitudes toward female performers. Her reluctance is
  accentuated by the fact that her husband holds a position as an ''oﬃcial,'' a clerk in the Sind
  University administration. Since her husband holds a visible government position, her identity as a
  musician is not viewed favorably in the community" (p. 69, folio not stated). Kubra herself: "No—I
  don't sing—I am ashamed to sing", note 73; "my husband will not let me sing—he says it does not
  look nice", note 74 (p. 70, folio not stated).
- **Sidi labour, servitude and its aftermath.** "Sidis were initially brought as slaves from Africa to
  work in the subcontinent of Pakistan and India" (p. 67, folio not stated); "Although they are now
  free, some of them are attached to the feudal households in the Sind province of Pakistan" (p. 68,
  folio not stated); "the Africans are present in the Sind in large numbers—because of the slave
  trade—they were brought here in large numbers—and then—with the abolition of slavery [note 71]—here
  too they were set free—", "They had been coming continuously—through the ages", note 72 (p. 69,
  folio not stated); "Their allegiances have been to the ruling families—"; "now they are employed—in
  various professions that include work with government departments—such as Sind University—they
  carry on with their singing—" (p. 70, folio not stated).
- **The Sidis as performers, and the caste contrast.** "they are not like the Manganhars—the
  traditional caste musicians that people invite to perform at rituals—but the Sidis are also invited
  to sing at celebrations such as births and weddings—they have a humor that people like—they sing
  and their audiences reward them well—" (p. 70, folio not stated). Two photo captions name
  "Manganhar musician: Mai Bhagi" and "Manganhar musician: Begam Faqiriani" (pp. 64–65, folios not

- **The mugarman and the dance around it.** "They have their African drum—it stands vertical—they
  call it the mugarman… The beat is African—the dance is African—they dance around it—both women and
  men dance around it—for them it is extremely sacred—this is their link with an African past—they
  keep it in diﬀerent places to sing" (p. 70, folio not stated). Elsewhere the instrument is the
  ḍholak: "The village women spent another hour tuning the ḍholak (double-sided drum). That in itself
  was a performance", note 82 (p. 71, folio not stated).
- **Shrine economy in Gujarat, reported from Basu.** "Supposedly until forty to ﬁfty years ago all the
  Sidis living near the shrine lived as faqīrs and religious mendicants. Some even lived in jungles
  among tigers. There seems to be a connection between escaped slaves and faqīrs. **Rajpipla princes
  invited Sidis occasionally to dance in exchange for token annual donations to the shrine. At harvest
  and the end of Ramazan they received jakat in the form of produce and handicrafts from members in
  peasant and craft communities in nearby villages**", note 91 (p. 74, folio not stated).
- **Foundation date of the Ratanpur shrine, and the author's complaint about it.** "The shrine
  originated at the time of the king of Rajpipla, who came from Rajasthan in the twelfth century.
  **Basu does not oﬀer any historical discussion on the origins of the shrine**, but her interviews
  with Sidi elders refer to their memories of the past relationship with the raja, whom they visited
  and for whom they sang. He would send Bhils to their village to deliver grains, oil, and food
  stocks", note 92 (p. 74, folio not stated).
- **Royal maintenance remembered, and its loss.** An eighty-year-old woman told Basu: "In the days of
  the raja we had ijjat (honor). He would send Bhils to Ratanpur with big containers of oil, ghee,
  rice, ﬂour. Nobody was hungry. And all the Sidi houses had rights (copadio)… We received rice and
  jovar from farmers, dishes from potters, oil from the oil-pressers and sometimes also old clothes
  the people gave us. So we had no diﬃculties. **But now things are much more diﬃcult**", note 93
  (p. 74, folio not stated). "Basu reports similar accounts from other elders of memories of travels
  from place to place, singing and dancing for rice, oil, produce, and handicrafts", note 94 (p. 74,
  folio not stated).
- **Dhammāl at the Gujarat shrine.** "There are also references to the dhammāl (the ecstatic dance at
  an urs) lasting for seven nights, in the manner of the dhammāl at the Lal Shahbaz Qalandar shrine",
  note 95; "There are references to mast faqīrs (intoxicated mendicants) in Basu's research" (p. 74,
  folio not stated).
- **Infertility cures as shrine specialisms, gender-divided.** "Mai Misra's shrine in Gujrat has become
  known for healing female infertility, as her brother Bava Habash's shrine is renowned for healing
  male infertility. Whereas female devotees who are infertile are fed Mai Misra's kichri, the male
  devotees who are infertile are given ''Bava Habash's milk'' full of hot spices", note 102; "Infertile
  female devotees eat the kichri and sing jikar (devotional songs) at the shrine", note 103 (p. 76,
  folio not stated).
- **Domestic and para-shrine performance.** "the community of more than a hundred women and their
  children created a rural concert (melā) in Qalandar Shah's large village home" (p. 75, folio not
  stated); the Sidi women "sang texts dedicated to the prophet Muhammad and his family, to the local
  Suﬁ saints of Sind, and to the events of Kerbala… The context was almost ritualistic" (pp. 75–76,
  folios not stated). Their repertoire "ranged from mystical songs to the lament poetry of marsiyā or
  nohā in the Shii traditions sung during the Muharram rituals", note 99 (p. 76, folio not stated).
  Kubra: "we sing at weddings—songs that are called sehrās—in Sindhi—we sing for ceremonials—for the
  aulīyā [Suﬁ saints]—" (p. 71, folio not stated).
- **Languages of shrine song.** Reshma sang "Mast Qalandar" "in Siraiki" (p. 57, folio not stated);
  her own tribal language is Marwari/Rajasthani (pp. 59–60, folios not stated); "The languages used to

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Karāmāt sung at Sehwan.** Performers "also sing poetic texts that glorify the karāmāt (mystical
  prowess) of Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar" (p. 57, folio not stated).
- **Lal Shahbaz Qalandar as bestower of children.** "Hazrat Lal Shahbaz is venerated in the lyric as
  the bestower of children", note 55 (p. 57, folio not stated); in the lyric itself, "M v n pira
  bachṛe denā [text-layer?: characters missing from this transliterated line] / Thou giver of children
  to mothers" and "Behn n denā-e vīr bhalā [text-layer?: characters missing] / Thou giver of brothers
  to sisters" (p. 57, folio not stated).
- **Verse attributed to the saint himself.** "Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar is reported to have said of
  the dhammāl", note 66: "For the sake of the love of the friend / I dance over ﬁre / Sometimes I roll
  in the dust / And sometimes I dance on the thorns / I have become notorious in your love / I beseech
  you to come to me / I am not afraid of this disrepute / To dance in every bazaar" (p. 65, folio not
  stated). **Attributed by Abbas as a report ("is reported to have said"), not as a documented text.**
- **Dhammāl as healing.** Families bring emotionally disturbed women to the dhammāl, "which devotees
  claim has healing powers" (p. 66, folio not stated). **Attributed to devotees, not asserted by
  Abbas.**
- **A pir's prayer as the source of a voice.** Zeena Bai "said that because of his special prayer for
  her—there was so much sūr [melody] in her voice", note 63, of the Shah of Ranipur (p. 61, folio not
  stated). Reshma is asked whether "there was a Nanga—pir or jogī who prayed for you that made you so
  famous?" and answers "We always go to pirs and jogīs—I still go to them—I believe in all of them"
  (p. 59, folio not stated).
- **The Bava Gor legend, as narrated by "Abdulkader".** He "was an Abyssinian lord named Sidi Mubarak
  Nobi, who went on a pilgrimage to the holy city of Mecca. The place pleased him and he remained in
  the service of the venerable Sallah-ul-Salam", note 87. "In those times a demonness lived in
  Hindustan, who through the power of her magic kept a butter lamp burning whose light could be seen
  all the way to Arabia. Her name was Makhan Devi (Butter Goddess). It was her habit to kill men
  daily, eat them, and from their blood make a tilak (mark) on her forehead." The elders offer to kill
  her, note 88; "One day he called Sidi Mubarak Nobi and ordered him to go to Hindustan to break the
  magic of the goddess and to light the lamp of Islam" (pp. 72–73, folios not stated). "Sidi Mubarak
  Nobi gathered a great army made entirely of Sidi people. They departed and reached Baghdad, where
  he became a disciple of Ahmed Kabir Rifa'i and received the khilafat… and his name, 'Bava Gor,'
  from his master… They came to Karachi, where they rested. In Karachi there is also a memorial
  shrine, and everywhere that Bava Gor stopped on his journey a shrine was created and each time one
  of the Sidis from his army remained in the place", note 89 (p. 73, folio not stated).
- **Mai Misra in the same legend.** "She is reported to have joined her brothers to ﬁght the female
  demonness—the Butter Goddess called Makhan Devi", note 101 (p. 76, folio not stated).
- **Descent from Bilal as a gift.** "Most Sidis are convinced that a special bakhśīś (gift) has been
  bestowed upon them by their ancestor Bilal", note 97 (p. 75, folio not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The chapter's finding, stated plainly.** "The ﬁndings at two major shrines in Sind, that is, Shah
  Abdul Latif at Bhit Shah and Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar at Sehwan Sharif, point toward signiﬁcant
  female input in Suﬁ rituals" (p. 67, folio not stated). Abbas concedes the limit of her own coverage:
  "I have only touched the periphery of the female roving traditions, and the ﬁeld needs to be further
  researched" (p. 66, folio not stated).
- **Why the chapter is organised around personal histories.** "In this section I explore some aspect
  of the personal histories of women musicians who sing devotional Suﬁ poetry. Their histories, told
  partly by themselves and partly by others, **show how the melodies that they sang at Suﬁ shrines
  became marks of their personal identities**. I present some performative contexts" (p. 56, folio not
  stated).
- **Kefīat/wajd as the analytic object.** Abbas asks Abida Parvin to explain "the kefīat (ecstasy) that
  is induced through Suﬁ speech and music. She explained it as a state produced or transmitted through
  zikr. This is repetitive chanting of devotional kalimāt (phrases) in Arabic" (p. 54, folio not
  stated). "The speech and song create ecstasy, which is a very special context of some shrines in
  Sind" (p. 55, folio not stated).
- **Sūng and manānā as an indigenous category.** Baloch and Mirza "used the indigenous term sūng for
  the practice in the Suﬁ poetics and music where the lover woos the estranged beloved through a
  serenade on the capṛī (wooden castanets)", note 51 (p. 55, folio not stated); Baloch: "that is why
  there is this school of thought in Sindhi Suﬁ poetry—the sūng [note 65]—in which the lover sings to
  win back the estranged beloved—this is called manānā—it is within these contexts and voices that the
  musicians build in the ecstasy—" (p. 63, folio not stated).
- **Why the female voice, according to Baloch.** "Why the musicians use the female alāp [narrative]—is
  because there is a beauty in her voice—there is a niāz-mandī [humility]—there is a hilm—and there is
  a modesty—when mystical poetry is sung in these voices—it creates the soul of the context—like when
  we pray—we beg—we sing His sanā [praise]—so that is what the female voices do to the context—"
  (p. 63, folio not stated). And: "The words [bol] are put in the woman's voice for artistic purposes
  and then—when the woman sings it herself, it brings out the color—the elegance—whenever women
  musicians have sung mystical poetry it has been very popular" (p. 63, folio not stated).
- **Male faqīrs singing in a female voice.** At Bhit Shah "his faqīrs sing his poetry like
  females—creating the female voices" (p. 63, folio not stated). **This is the chapter's clearest case
  of the female voice detached from female singers.**
- **Abida Parvin's exceptionality explained by male training.** "Baloch and Mirza reported that since
  she was trained within the male traditions her style is exceptional"; "Abida Parvin's evolution, they
  said, has been within these grassroots mystical traditions, later to be enhanced through her training
  in the semiclassical and classical traditions of music through her father" (p. 55, folio not stated).
- **Maidānī voices defined.** "When I asked them about the maidānī voices, they said that this was the
  poetic content and the dialects in the female musicians' speech", note 64 (p. 62, folio not stated).
- **Why Abbas chose the joganī lyric.** "Such discourse is rarely sung in the suﬁānā-kalām traditions
  in the regional languages, but I selected it for its uniqueness and humor" (p. 56, folio not stated);
  "In the song there is the metalanguage of one who is ''possessed'' or has become a divānā.
  Additionally, there is subtle humor in the verses when the musician sings of the smoldering ﬁre. The
  speaker who is the female mendicant is fearful that it is perhaps the one she loves who has set
  himself on ﬁre" (p. 56, folio not stated).
- **The reading of Kubra's chant as a chain of descent.** Abbas prints the referents as a two-way
  chain: `Allah → Muhammad the Nabi → Hazrat Bilal → Bava Gor` and the same reversed (p. 75, folio not
  stated). "The hierarchical referents in the song start with Allah or God, linked through his prophet
  (Nabi) Muhammad and further linked through Bilal and Mama Gor" (p. 75, folio not stated).
- **Performative self-blessing.** "The words are used as performative acts to ritualize the event: the
  initiatory devotional ceremony at a Sidi shrine that pays homage to the prophet Muhammad through
  Bilal and Bava Gor", note 98. "The Arabic phrase ''sal-vāle'' or ''sal-āl-e'' is a ritual chant to
  bless the prophet Muhammad and his progeny, and **through articulating her speech thus the performer
  blesses herself**. She does the same through the performative act of blessing Bava Gor/Mama Gor: she
  actually blesses herself" (p. 75, folio not stated). "The signiﬁcation in the song is communicated
  through the words, the melody, and the musician's movements of her hands as she sings. The musician
  herself is a part of the signifying process" (p. 75, folio not stated).
- **Kerbala as a covert trope.** "in South Asian Islam there is veneration for Kerbala and Hussain's
  family among both the Shii and the Sunni Muslims. Thus, in Kubra's lyric the trope of Kerbala is used
  without an overt referent except through blessing Muhammad's family, which she articulates as
  ''yal-āi-e''" (p. 75, folio not stated).
- **An explicit research gap the author names.** "more needs to be investigated to establish links
  between the Sidi minstrels in South Gujrat and their related communities in Sind in Pakistan"
  (p. 74, folio not stated); "Although I did not ﬁnd any reference to roving minstrels among the Sidis
  in Karachi Mori, I recently made some connections to roving mendicants among the Sidis in Ratanpur
  and Rajpipla in Gujrat of India", note 90 (p. 73, folio not stated).
- **An etymology offered by an informant, not by the author.** Qalandar Shah: "Sidis—which is
  linguistically derived from Syedi—Cid—the word is there in Spanish and French—Cid—"; "very
  interesting anthropologically—because although they were slaves they were called ''our Syedi'' or
  ''my lord''—" (p. 70, folio not stated). **Abbas records it without endorsing it.**
