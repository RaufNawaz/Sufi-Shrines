# abbas_female_voice_sufi_ritual — chunk 005 — pp. 97–117

Twenty-one pages spanning **the end of one chapter and the opening of the next**. Pages 97–115 close a
chapter on qawwālī and sufiānā-kalām performance — interview transcript with Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan,
Abida Parvin's Open University concert in Islamabad, and a first-person account of a **shrine
performance at Bulle Shah's shrine in Kasur** — and p. 116 opens a new chapter, `Female Myths in
Suﬁsm` (p. 116, folio not stated), whose first pages give the Sassi–Punnu myth. The chapter break is
confirmed by the flattened endnote digits, which run **31 → 43** across pp. 97–114 and then **reset to
1, 2, 3** on pp. 116–117 (folio not stated throughout). The chunk **opens mid-performance-text** on
p. 97, its verse lines carried over from chunk 004, and **breaks off mid-sentence** on p. 117: "he
escaped to go back to Bhambor where he had left Sassi. On the" (p. 117, folio not stated).

**No folio anywhere.** As measured for this book, nothing in these pages prints a recoverable page
number; the only numeric furniture is the production header `6657 Abbas / THE FEMALE VOICE IN SUFI
RITUAL / sheet N of 239`, which is not quoted as content below. Every reference is therefore
`(p. N, folio not stated)`.

**Speaker tags in this chunk, and a trap.** Two different tag systems collide here. In the interview
on pp. 97–99, `A:` (with colon) is the author, Shemeem Burney Abbas, and `K:` is the respondent, whom
the lead-in identifies: "When I met Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan and asked him about his immensely intimate
use of the Panjabi poetic and linguistic structures, he said the following" (p. 97, folio not stated).
In the transliterated performance texts on pp. 97 and 102–105 and 109–111, **`A`, `B` and `A&B`
without a colon are performance annotations marking which singer or chorus takes the line** — on
pp. 102–104 the `A` lines are **Abida Parvin singing and speaking**, not the author. Attributions
below say which system is in play.

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Hereditary custodial musicianship at the Bulle Shah shrine.** "the qawwāls of the shrine, Muhammad
  Bakhsh and his paternal uncle Karam Bakhsh, were already singing there. A young man who was said to
  be a nephew also sang with them. There were two other musicians with them" (p. 105, folio not stated).
  Their claim of tenure: "They said they had inherited them orally from their ancestors who had been
  qawwāls at the shrine for the last three centuries" (p. 106, folio not stated), repeated by Muhammad
  Bakhsh: "Our family has served this shrine for the last three centuries—generation after generation
  has sung here. We came with Bulle Shah's family from Uch Sharif, and we have learned his poetry from
  our ancestors, who have always sung here" (p. 112, folio not stated).
- **Daily schedule**: "We sing here twice a day. We sing in the morning from eight to ten, and then
  throughout the evening" (p. 112, folio not stated); on the evening of the visit "The Bulle Shah
  qawwāls sang the entire evening, until after the evening prayers" (p. 111, folio not stated).
- **Attachment to the shrine and circulation to others for ʿurs**: "We are linked to this shrine, we
  are bound to it, and we sing here out of devotion to Baba Bulle Shah. The saint will not let us leave
  this shrine, and even if we do go away to other shrines to sing for the urs, we always return here"
  (pp. 111–112, folio not stated).
- **The shrine bazaar and what is sold**: shops "sell all kinds of artifacts such as marigolds, rose
  petals, candles, and small earthenware lamps called divās, which are filled with mustard oil and have
  a cotton wick in them. They are lit at the shrine" (pp. 105–106, folio not stated); "The shops
  displayed incense sticks, prayer beads, and silver-plated bangles with Quranic verses inscribed on
  them that devotees take to the shrine, or home as souvenirs. The shops also sell sweets that the
  devotees buy as tabarruk, nazar, or niāz (food for distribution in the shrine)" (p. 106, folio not
  stated). **No prices, no revenue figures, no shop numbers: (not stated).**
- **Offerings on the tomb**: green and red sheets with gold tinsel "The devotees bring these as
  offerings" (p. 106, folio not stated); candles and fātehā at the surrounding small tombs (p. 106,
  folio not stated).
- **Attendance at the observed performance**: "All together, including the musicians, there were fifty
  women and men in the shrine" (p. 106, folio not stated).
- **Instruments at the shrine**: "a harmonium (an accordion), a ḍholak (a double-sided drum shaped like
  a barrel), and a pair of tambourines" (p. 105, folio not stated).
- **Hijṛās as shrine performers.** "Male devotees in the shrine made a small circle in front of the
  musicians and started to dance in rhythm with the music. Later, some hijṛās (eunuchs) also joined the
  group" (p. 106, folio not stated). Court background: "The hijṛās are known as khāwājāsarā in the court
  traditions of the Muslim rulers of the subcontinent, and they held important positions at the court"
  (p. 106, folio not stated). Present-day occasions: "Today in Pakistan and India the hijṛās sing and
  dance at births, especially of male infants, and at circumcisions and weddings"; "They have their own
  community networks through which they find out about events and appear with their musicians, hijṛās
  like themselves"; "The families reward them according to their means" (p. 106, folio not stated).
  Exclusivity to this shrine, and the author's explanation: "Bulle Shah's was the only shrine where I
  encountered the hijṛās. Most probably they are traditionally linked to the shrine because of Bulle
  Shah's own life history of having lived among the dancing girls for several years" (p. 106, folio not
  stated). Dress and dance: "The hijṛas at Bulle Shah had ghungrū tied to their ankles" (p. 106, folio

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Bulle Shah's reconciliation through dance** — given three times, by three different voices, and the
  three do not agree on the length or the company (see Doubts).
  - Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan (tag `K:`): "when his shaikh—his mentor got annoyed with him—got weary of
    him—his pir—he went away to spend twelve years among the dancing girls—he adopted the dancing girl's
    identity—that female's voice—her identity—and returned to dance—dance like the dancing girl—before
    the mentor—to woo him—to win him back—", answering Abbas's "Like the dancing girl—like the woman?"
    with "Like the woman!" (p. 99, folio not stated).
  - Abbas, explaining the hijṛās: "Bulle Shah's own life history of having lived among the dancing girls
    for several years. He is reported to have learned the music arts to win back his beloved murshid,
    Shah Inayat" (p. 106, folio not stated).
  - Abbas, narrating the legend at length: "Bulle Shah worked hard for several years to win back his
    murshid… he went and lived among musicians for almost seven years and became their apprentice. After
    learning the musical arts, he returned to Shah Inayat's company and won him back by playing music and
    dancing before him. The event of Bulle Shah's winning back Shah Inayat through dance and music is
    sung in the qawwālī and kalām traditions" (p. 109, folio not stated). She attributes the wider body
    of such stories to "the oral lore of the qawwāls at the shrine" and to "the written texts" (p. 108,
    folio not stated).
- **The saint's continuing agency over his servants** — Muhammad Bakhsh: "The saint will not let us leave
  this shrine" (p. 112, folio not stated).
- **The Prophet's shroud** — "The belief is that the prophet Muhammad received his revelations while
  covered by a shroud (kamlī), hence the term that means 'One with the Shroud,' the 'Kamlīvālā'" (p. 97,
  folio not stated). Attributed by Abbas to "the popular Islamic tradition".
- **The Sassi myth**, given by Abbas as myth with no fixed version: "I have not found one standard
  version of the myth nor specific dates for it as the myths have evolved over the centuries" (p. 117,
  folio not stated). "Sassi is the daughter of the king of Bhambor in Sind. When she was born, the
  astrologers predicted that she would bring shame on her family"; her father's advisers "agreed that
  rather than kill her, they would put her on a raft to float down the river Indus"; a washerman found
  her and "they adopted her and called her Sassi, which means the Moon" (p. 117, folio not stated). "In
  other versions of the story the chief of Bhambor adopted her himself and when she grew up, he gave her
  state authority and made her a ruler" (p. 117, folio not stated). The famine in Kec Mekran, the
  hostage-taking of "two men from the Kec", the marriage to Punnu, the drugging at "one of these
  nocturnal banquets", the theft of Punnu "on a camel", and her death: "she never found him and died
  tired, hungry, and thirsty in the desert. The people built a tomb over her dead body" (p. 117, folio
  not stated). **The tomb's location is not given and no shrine is named: (not stated).**
- **Hir's elopement** — "Hir violates her caste conduct to elope with a cowherd, thus bringing shame to
  her family" (p. 116, folio not stated); in Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan's version "Hir wants to go away with
  Ranjha, wearing rings in her ears, and a tilak (mark) on her forehead" (p. 97, folio not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The book's title thesis, in the performer's own mouth rather than the author's.** Nusrat Fateh Ali
  Khan (tag `K:`): "The Sufi poets—in the tradition of Sufi mystical poetry—these mystic poets—when they
  speak they do it in the female voice—they present themselves as the female—for them their beloved—their
  mentor—their shaikh—is the male—whereas their own voice is that of the female" (p. 98, folio not
  stated); "you can read the entire suﬁānā-kalām—the entire Sufi kalām—that entire discourse is in the
  female voice" (p. 98, folio not stated); "There is an elegance in it—there is an elegance—a humility in
  the female voice—which is lacking in the male voice" (p. 98, folio not stated).
- **A competing gloss from the shrine, which denies the "female" reading.** Muhammad Bakhsh, asked "why
  they sang their narratives in the female voice or used the aurat kī avāz": "It is actually not the
  'female.' The voice is that of the spirit. This is the subject of human existence. Life is a spinning,
  that is why it is the female. If human beings lived the way they are expected to live, all would be
  fine. We must live in humility. The female, who spins, lives in humility" (p. 111, folio not stated).
- **The erasure of women singers.** Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan: "I think I do not know of any other woman
  except Abida Parvin"; "Probably women have sung Sufi songs—off and on—on different occasions—but I
  don't know of any such women—probably these women never ever came into the limelight—were never ever
  acknowledged for their Sufi narratives" (pp. 98–99, folio not stated).
- **Vernacular repertoire beats the metropolitan one.** "performers like Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan and Abida
  Parvin, who sang in the vernaculars, had a much wider repertoire compared with performers who could
  only use the limited texts of the North Indian style of qawwālī in Urdu or Hindi. Performers who sang
  in the indigenous languages had more linguistic resources to draw from, such as the texts of the Sufi
  poets of their areas, the folklore, and nature, such as references to birds, seasons, and the
  landscape" (p. 99, folio not stated).
- **Abida Parvin's linguistic advantage over men.** "Abida Parvin has the additional resource of the
  Sindhi language… Abida Parvin therefore has an advantage over her male qawwāl counterparts because of
  her linguistic resources" (p. 105, folio not stated).
- **Performance as political speech.** "That evening this woman performer sang in a fiery mood and
  blasted the orthodox government of the time in the presence of its official bureaucracy by using the
  frames of Sufi poetry" (p. 101, folio not stated). **The government is not named and the year is
  dropped: (not stated).** Her targets: "she ridiculed the so-called 'knowledgeable ones' or the
  caretakers of faith. She compared them to animals, to cattle, to fish, and to frogs" (p. 102, folio not
  stated).
- **Metalanguage as the author's analytic term.** "she even referred to the metalanguage of the heat and
  the humidity in the auditorium as a context of energy for the seekers to find the murshid" (p. 101,
  folio not stated); "In the text of the song, for instance in line fifteen, is Abida Parvin's
  metalanguage of the heat in the auditorium. In lines four to eight she uses simile to describe a miser,
  and the miser in turn is the so-called scholar who is mean by nature, in her speech" (p. 103, folio not
  stated); "Abida Parvin uses the metalanguage of the dance (nāc) as a state of ecstasy through which the
  beloved is won" (p. 105, folio not stated); "In the original Panjabi text, the word nacāyā is
  significant because it expresses the act of the dance, which in itself is the metalanguage for ecstasy"
  (p. 109, folio not stated); "those musicians who sing in concert and at the shrines utilize much
  metalanguage" (p. 105, folio not stated).
- **Bridal and spinning tropes read as the disciple–mentor relation.** The second qawwālī "was about a
  bride who is forced to leave her father's home… The metaphor of the journey to the in-laws' home is a
  preparation for the hereafter… with the disciple playing the role of the submissive bride" (p. 109,
  folio not stated); "All the toil, all the suffering is for that final state, that of the kaśf, or the
  unveiling of the beloved, that state of illumination when the disciple meets the mentor" (p. 110, folio
  not stated); the carkhī-nāma's "young damsel is the disciple who must toil diligently to gain the
  mentor's wisdom" (p. 110, folio not stated); in the veil-lifting kāfī "Instead of the male, the mentor
  now becomes the female bride. Thus, the tropes in the poetry are complex" (p. 111, folio not stated).
- **A communication model adapted from Jakobson.** "They create the communicative channels that I
  demonstrate with the following model adapted from Jakobson (Fig. .)" (p. 112, folio not stated). The
  figure's cells read: "Context : samā / concert hall / shrine / melā / family settings"; "musicians /
  singers / transmitters"; "contact / code"; "audiences / devotees"; "ritual speech / ritual music";
  "keﬁat / samā / wajd / hāl" (p. 113, folio not stated). Caption: ". Performance: The
  Qawwali-Suﬁānā-Kalam communicative model. Adapted from Jakobson, 'Closing Statement: Linguistics and
  Poetics,' –." (p. 113, folio not stated).
- **Interaction and improvisation.** "the performers become the transmitters because they use cultural
  semantics embedded in a shared meaning between themselves and their audiences, thereby making the
  process interactional" (p. 112, folio not stated); "The improvisation on certain segments of a text
  that is largely fixed is closely linked to the interactional processes in the anthropology of
  performance" (p. 114, folio not stated); "speech production becomes an ad hoc process for the
  performer" (p. 114, folio not stated); on turn-taking, "The qawwāls follow a complete organized system
  of social interaction of 'taking the floor'" (p. 114, folio not stated); "I have used the
  social-interactional approaches to study the speech communication in my work" (p. 114, folio not
  stated).
- **Code switching and prestige codes.** "The major code of a performance can be Panjabi, Siraiki,
  Sindhi, Pushto, Urdu, or a regional language. Within the major code singers can either start a
  performance in Arabic or switch to it, because it is the language of the Quran. They will also switch
  to Persian because it is the language of higher intellectual thought… The use of these prestige codes
  establishes the authenticity of the discourse for the listeners" (pp. 114–115, folio not stated).
- **Chapter conclusion.** "The diverse resources, including their own linguistic and musical skills, are
  blended with local folklore, proverbs, and ecology to create an oral culture that is a visible component
  of the performing arts as well as shrine ritual in Pakistan" (p. 115, folio not stated).
- **The new chapter's opening thesis.** "The complexities of race, gender, class, and caste figure
  prominently in the narratives of the qawwāls and suﬁānā-kalām performers. Sufi poetry in Pakistan and
  India was sometimes composed in opposition to the religious establishment and was expressed in subtle
  ways. It survived through representation in myth and the complex tropes of the female voices" (p. 116,
  folio not stated). Her explicit refusal of an anachronism: "Whether or not the Sufi poets were
  'feminists' cannot be claimed here as the term 'feminism' is a fairly recent one" (p. 116, folio not
  stated). And the function of the myths: "the myths have become metaphors for the polarities of gender,
  religious, sociopolitical, and economic hegemony" (p. 116, folio not stated); "Sassi becomes the symbol
  of strength against patriarchy… In the Sufi lore she becomes a paradigm of moral courage and spiritual
  triumph" (p. 117, folio not stated).
- **The fusion of the romance with the Prophet.** "Somewhere at the end, the narrative becomes fused with
  the 'Kamlīvālā'… Here, in the text of the song, Hir's beloved Ranjha acquires a spirituality that blends
  with the shroud. Sometimes, the reference can even be to the Divine Being or perhaps a spiritual
  mentor—the murshid whose representation is a major preoccupation in Sufi poetry" (p. 97, folio not
  stated).
