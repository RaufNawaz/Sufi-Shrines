## Practices, institutions, economy

- **When women may sing at shrines** — "Generally, they sing at shrines during the annual urs
  celebration or on Thursday evenings, the eve of the Muslim sabbath on Friday. They are free to
  sing there at any time except during the prayers." (p. 33, folio not stated).
- **State control and spatial segregation** — "However, due to state control of Suﬁ shrines, the
  areas where they can sing may at times be confined to the female sections." (p. 33, folio not
  stated). The book names no department or Auqaf body here: **(not stated)**.
- **Payment at shrines** — women "find an audience at any time of the night or day that pays them
  for their performance" (p. 33, folio not stated); "They sing at the shrines and are paid by the
  devotees." (p. 34, folio not stated).
- **The wider livelihood** — "Female musicians thus make a living by singing in the feudal
  households and within their own communities… In addition to singing at Suﬁ shrines, women
  musicians perform on the radio or television. Female musicians in the cities sing at ceremonies
  such as births, weddings, aqīqā celebrations, and circumcisions." (p. 34, folio not stated); and
  in family contexts "they create devotional settings wherever necessary, be it a birth, a wedding,
  or an initiation ritual such as a male child's circumcision" (p. 33, folio not stated).
- **State patronage of touring** — Taj Mastani "said that Lok Virsa, the Institute of Folk Heritage
  in Islamabad, contacted her through Radio Pakistan in Hyderabad. Through Lok Virsa she was able to
  go to international concerts in the Middle East and Europe; as a state organization, Lok Virsa
  could afford to pay for her trips abroad. On her own she does not have the resources to travel
  overseas with her entourage." (p. 34, folio not stated).
- **Gendered earnings** — "earnings of women such as Abida Parvin and Reshma are not regular, and
  they depend on the whims of recording companies abroad"; "Compared with female performers, male
  musicians such as Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan and the Sabri Brothers have done much better as a result
  of their international networks of male professional and community contacts"; "For all three
  female musicians that I discuss here, namely, Abida Parvin, Taj Mastani, and Reshma, their
  husbands functioned as negotiators. Due to cultural norms, female musicians have limited access to
  their male patrons and therefore their earnings are much less." (p. 34, folio not stated).
- **Genre as an earnings mechanism** — women's earnings "are small compared to those of their male
  counterparts… This is in part because they sing suﬁānā-kalām and not the more popular qawwālī that
  is sung in the classical traditions of North Indian music. Their skills are considered more in
  line with folk music." (p. 35, folio not stated).
- **The cassette industry** — "the cassette culture has provided a source of income… within areas
  such as Hyderabad or Faisalabad the local recording companies have assisted women musicians in
  earning a living from recording their Suﬁ lyrics with music. Their songwriters are male relatives
  or men in the community. In fact, this small industry has become a source of income for many women
  and men." (p. 35, folio not stated).
- **Rehmat Gramophone Company, Faisalabad** — "provides female musicians with a livelihood by
  recording their songs during Milad-ud Nabi, which celebrates the Prophet's birthday… Their
  recordings are played on the local buses, in the restaurants, and on the radio." (p. 36, folio not
  stated). It "releases special audiocassette editions in which female and male performers narrate
  the tragedy of Kerbala ( ) during the annual Muharram mourning rituals" — the parenthesis after
  "Kerbala" is empty, `( )` `[text-layer?: a date, unrecoverable]` (p. 36, folio not stated). "The
  musicians who chant these narratives can therefore make a living through the local recording
  companies." (p. 37, folio not stated).
- **Khanqahs** — "The Suﬁ khanqahs (monasteries) were institutions of learning where both men and
  women were involved in several levels of activities and continue to be so in present times. The
  female voice has always been important in these rituals." (p. 39, folio not stated).
- **Scale of shrines in Sind** — "In the province of Sind alone there are said to be more than a
  hundred thousand Suﬁ shrines." (p. 50, folio not stated). The figure survives because it is
  printed in words; the book attributes it to no source ("are said to be").
- **Daily and weekly performance at shrines** — "Qawwālī and suﬁānā-kalām are performed at the
  shrines of the Suﬁ saints and in public concerts. At the shrines they are performed on Thursday
  evenings in anticipation of the Muslim sabbath and during the celebrations of the death
  anniversary of the saint." (p. 42, folio not stated); at the five shrines listed on p. 42
  "qawwālī is performed every evening after the prayers. Musicians as well as audiences prefer
  evening performances that continue well past midnight." (p. 42, folio not stated).
- **Where suﬁānā-kalām was sung** — "As part of popular culture it was sung among roving minstrels
  at the shrines during the urs, the melās (folk festivals), and on Thursday evenings." (p. 40,
  folio not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The author's naming of her object**: she calls the repertoire ''Suﬁ'' "because its content is
  about the Suﬁ saints of the Muslim world, as well as the life of the prophet Muhammad and his
  family" (p. 33, folio not stated).
- **Livelihood as the analytic frame**: "In Islamic societies that integrate religion into almost
  every aspect of social life, these women and men acquire the skills to earn a living that matches
  the devotion of their patrons and their audiences." (p. 33, folio not stated). Male musicians'
  perception of women of their own family performing at community rituals is said to be evident in
  the interviews (pp. 33–34, folio not stated).
- **Two constraints on women performers**: "the traditional orthodox Muslim view of Suﬁsm as
  peripheral to the larger community practice" and "the way in which local communities view the
  place of women in public performances, as well as their need to earn a living from those
  performances" (p. 34, folio not stated).
- **Abida Parvin's strategic shift**: "Since the early nineties she has tried to establish an
  identity comparable to that of the male qawwāls by singing the traditional qaul, ''Mun kunto Maulā
  fā Alī-un Maulā,'' in a call-and-response pattern with one of her instrumentalists. Like the male
  qawwāls she has added a formidable repertoire of Amir Khusrau's Suﬁ lyrics in her performances."
  (p. 35, folio not stated).
- **Definition of Sufism**: "Suﬁsm is associated with the Islamic traditions of metaphysical thought
  and practices. Suﬁs believe in intuition and creativity and less in the fundamental and literal
  interpretation of the holy scriptures." (p. 37, folio not stated).
- **The mechanism of ecstasy**: "words have a zāhir (overt meaning) and a bātin (covert meaning)
  that can be used to alter the consciousness through zikr, which is based on deep, disciplined
  breathing exercises in which the devotee incessantly repeats God's many names from the Quran…
  Zikr is central to the samā practice, to the extent that a state of ecstasy is reached." (p. 38,
  folio not stated).
- **Origins and diffusion, given twice and not identically**: "Mystical performances originated in
  Suﬁsm in the eighth century in the Arabian peninsula" (p. 38); samā "goes back to the middle of
  the ninth century—references to this event are found in Baghdad" (p. 39); "Suﬁ poetry outside the
  high tradition of the samā was used in the eleventh century in Iran. From there it spread to
  Turkey and gradually into the Pakistan-India subcontinent, where it became popular in the
  thirteenth and fourteenth centuries." (p. 38); "Suﬁānā-kalām evolved at the same time as qawwālī…
  in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries" (p. 42); folio not stated throughout. Kept side by
  side, not harmonised.
- **Sufis as mediators of power and text**: "Because of their scholarly wisdom, even monarchs and
  functionaries in power sought the Suﬁ scholars' guidance. As missionaries they undertook the
  dissemination of Islam through an oral medium whose basis was the written religious discourses."
  (pp. 38–39, folio not stated).
- **Vernacular as anticlerical technique**: "The use of vernacular languages in qawwālī and
  suﬁānā-kalām repertoires entails the singing to music of Quranic texts, the hadīth, and the wisdom
  of the Suﬁs. Such an approach enables the mystics to teach the core of religion without the
  intervention of the clergy." (p. 42, folio not stated); and, for Sind, "For fear of the clergy
  mystic poetry sung to music remained confined to the minstrel class." (p. 50, folio not stated).
- **Sacred vs secular context**: "the key musicians that I interviewed affirmed the sanctity of both
  contexts. Abida Parvin categorically affirmed that she sang her poetry with veneration even at
  public concerts… They also assert that their poetry is not folk, but devotional and ritualistic,
  as they perform with the utmost sanctity (adab)." (p. 42, folio not stated).
- **Who counts as a Sufi**: "the devotees who visit the shrines do not claim to be Suﬁs… a musician
  who sings qawwālī or suﬁānā-kalām or the poetry of the Suﬁ poets does not claim to be a Suﬁ but
  only a transmitter of Suﬁ discourse or poetry." (p. 43, folio not stated).
- **Terms are context-dependent, and the book takes them from practitioners as well as scholars**:
  "Terms like samā, maqām, wajd, kefīat, hāl, zikr, and qaul have meanings according to the context";
  "the terms used in this book are from scholarly literature as well as from the popular culture:
  These are the beliefs of the participants of the speech events and the grassroots musicians."
  (p. 43, folio not stated). Shaikh Ghulam Hussain's gloss of *kefīat* as "a state of altered
  consciousness", his line between ecstasy and "vulgar", and his rock 'n' roll contrast are given at
  p. 44 (folio not stated); Sikandar Baloch and Naseer Mirza's gloss of *maqām* at p. 44; "the word
  maqām is polysemous" (p. 45, folio not stated); *hāl* as "a state of arousal outside the control
  of self" against *kefīat* as "subtle and covert" (p. 45, folio not stated).
- **An obligation on the musician**: "They are obligated to continue singing until the affected
  overcome the state of ecstasy." (p. 45, folio not stated).
- **Syncretism and the "matrimythical" thesis** — the chunk's central argument for the archive:
  "The Suﬁ version of Islam in Pakistan and India was based on syncretism and reconciled differing
  beliefs… Since some of these cultures were ''matrimythical'' and used the indigenous female myths,
  the Suﬁ poets creatively blended the female voices from these myths into their poetry that they
  sang to music. The purpose was mainly aesthetic and devotional." (p. 50, folio not stated); and
  "because the myths had a pre-Islamic, indigenous origin, non-Muslim devotees had no problem
  putting the female myths into their own devotional schemas or worldviews" (p. 51, folio not
  stated).
- **Conversion and caste**: "At that time in India many converted to Islam because it offered relief
  from caste oppression." (p. 50, folio not stated); the devotional movements "challenged hegemonic
  religious authority, caste boundaries, gender inequity, and the exclusive use of elitist languages
  in religion" (p. 49, folio not stated).
- **Why women appear in this tradition and not the other**: "since this model was an indigenous one
  that required minimal musical skills and resources, women's input in the singing of this poetry is
  visible" (p. 49, folio not stated); "Qawwāli is essentially a male genre" (p. 39, folio not
  stated); "Suﬁānā-kalām… Women's input in this tradition is significant." (p. 40, folio not stated).
- **The comparative model (p. 41, folio not stated)** is printed as a table captioned "Comparative
  model: Qawwālī and Suﬁānā-Kalām", with the footnote "Sometimes there can be more than one singer."
  Its columns are Linguistics, Poetics, Myths, Ecology, Performances. Under Myths it lists
  "Layla-Majnun", "Sassi-Punnu", "Sohni-Mahival", "Mumal-Rano", "Hir-Ranjha", "others"; under
  Performances, qawwālī is "male genre / many singers / turn-taking / North Indian / elitist /
  drums+claps / classical" and suﬁānā-kalām is "female & male / solo+1 / continuous / indigenous /
  grassroots / strings+ / vernaculars / Suﬁ poets / indigenous / folk / classical", performed at
  "shrines, concerts, folk festivals" by "males & females". Under Linguistics the vernaculars listed
  include "Khari Boli, Urdu/Hindi, Brajbhasa, Avadhi, Purbi, Deccani, Rajasthani, Panjabi, Siraiki,
  Sindhi, Hindko, others"; under Poetics, "doha, git, kaﬁ, vai, abiyat, bani, rekhti, ghazal,
  cakki-namas, carkhi-namas, kapa'iti songs, wedding songs, festival songs, rituals", with "female
  speech" and "female narrator" set beneath. The table's own number is dropped, and the text refers
  to it as "The model (see Fig .)" (p. 51, folio not stated). See Doubts on column collation.
