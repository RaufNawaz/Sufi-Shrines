## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Sidi dance and its naming**: "These dances were called goma dance, a term most likely extracted
  from the Swahili ngoma. These dances got fused with the local dhammāl (ecstasy dance)" (p. 77, folio
  not stated). "The Sidi female musicians in Karachi Mori performed it for me" (p. 77, folio not
  stated).
- **Segregated female performance**: "Such performances are segregated so that women can create a
  context of their own. They can be themselves without being stared or gazed at by men who do not
  belong to the inner circle. Although the performance in Karachi Mori was specially organized for me,
  it was also an opportunity for Qalandar Shah to visit his family" (p. 77, folio not stated).
- **Roving minstrels of the Sind–Rajasthan area**: "Among them are the jogī snake charmers and ﬂute
  players"; the **Manganhar** are "distinct from the Sidi" (pp. 77–78, folio not stated). The full
  list given later: "the Manganhars, the Sidis, snake-charming jogīs, ﬂute players, potters, and many
  more who lived on the margins of the society" (pp. 79–80, folio not stated).
- **Ritual monopoly**: "In Sind there can be no ritual without the Manganhar, be it a birth, a wedding,
  or a death. They are the designated community musicians, and each community has its own Manganhars"
  (p. 77, folio not stated).
- **Etymology and alms**: "''Manganhar'' originated from the words mangan, which means ''to beg,'' and
  hār, which means ''a garland of ﬂowers.'' Thus, according to an informant at the institute of
  Sindhology at Sind University, a manganhar is one who begs for ﬂowers. In the roving-minstrel
  traditions, alms were given to the minstrels in return for their musical services" (pp. 77–78, folio
  not stated).
- **Genres and occasions, from Alan Faqir (`F:`)**: "The Syeds—when they have weddings they invite us
  to sing—we play the śādmānā—when the Syeds come—we play the haswārī when a pir comes—or if we see a
  murshid come"; the commissioned repertoire is "the songs of prayer—duā—śādmānā—haswārī—olang—or the
  candar" (p. 78, folio not stated).
- **Payment in kind, then**: "in those days the money was very little—lots of grain—we used to get
  paid in grain—lots of it there was—one sack—four sacks—this was more than enough for a poor
  household" (p. 78, folio not stated, Alan Faqir).
- **The faqīr as arbitrator**: "if there was a dispute they would come to the faqīr and say, ''Faqīr,
  do a duā [a prayer], that I may be reconciled with my brother''—you see the vaḍerās [landowners]
  have disputes with each other… ''Faqīr—I am your slave—faqīr—I am your faqīr—help me—reconcile me
  with my brother''—you see, we lessen our life spans with our deeds—" (p. 78, folio not stated, Alan
  Faqir; the bracketed glosses are the book's).
- **Women's titles and repertoire**: "The women in our families are called mangtī—faqīriānī—our family
  is called faqīr bādshāh—we beg—faqīr sāĩ—faqīr sāhib—this is our family title—the women are
  mangtīs—the women sing gīts or gīc—they sing duā [prayer]"; "The women sing the sehrā—they sing the
  lāon [wedding songs]—They get the murād—the female musician gets it—the male musician gets it"
  (p. 78, folio not stated, Alan Faqir). Sample wedding text as he sings it: "Allah has given you a
  groom—like the moon—and there will be much prosperity—or Allah has given you a bride like the
  ruby—and may you grow and prosper—" (p. 78, folio not stated).
- **When women sing at all**: `A: Do women in your setups sing now?` — "The woman would not sing—unless
  her circumstances forced her—if her husband was alive, she stayed at home—but if he was not there
  then she had to do the man's work—naturally she had to sing—they had to decorate the drums… they had
  to sing the sehrās for the weddings—they had to sing the gīts or the gīc—they had to sing the duā—
  they had to sing the lāon for the bride and the groom" (pp. 78–79, folio not stated, Alan Faqir).
- **Caste, shame and exit from the trade**: "Musicians like him are extremely reluctant to discuss the
  subject because of the shame associated with being caste musicians, especially where women were
  involved. It is a matter of socioeconomic caste"; "Today, caste musicians want their children to get
  educated and be employed in the government service"; they "further want their children to become
  professionals such as doctors, engineers, MBAs, or educators" (p. 79, folio not stated). "Shame
  (izzat) is a concern for men, and they are silent about their womenfolk's singing" (p. 82, folio not
  stated).
- **Payment in cash, now**: "Over the last quarter of a century the practices have changed. Musicians
  are now paid in cash rather than agricultural produce" (p. 80, folio not stated); "the vaḍerās prefer
  to pay in cash rather than in agricultural commodities. Alan Faqir stated this in his interview"
  (p. 81, folio not stated).
- **Media as the new patron, and the melā bypassed**: "Baloch and Mirza at Radio Pakistan in Hyderabad

## Arguments and interpretations

- Performance as identity and rite: "Performance that includes cultural criteria such as verbal,
  musical, and body language becomes a mark of identity and sometimes an initiation rite, such as
  Karima wriggling her body for the groom-to-be. The context is a mutually achieved interaction between
  performers and their audiences" (p. 77, folio not stated).
- On transformation of minstrel communities: "Presently, the many communities that were once involved
  in the roving-minstrel tradition are undergoing transformation due to the newly emerging socioeconomic
  structures" (p. 79, folio not stated); "I have seen communities of workers, such as musicians, village
  barbers, and potters who had a variety of other roles in the landowning structures, disperse within
  the last twenty-ﬁve years. This is because of socioeconomic changes and industrialization and most of
  all because of awareness among the peasant communities" (p. 81, folio not stated).
- On access to informants: "practitioners will allow a glimpse into the community only if the right
  channels of trust are established for the individuals in the services to ''keep face'' in social
  structures that are still very close and tightly knit" (pp. 81–82, folio not stated). She also
  recommends the work not yet done: "Follow-up research on the mendicant traditions and their
  transformations is bound to produce interesting results" (p. 81, folio not stated).
- Why men deny that their women sing: "It reﬂects poorly on their own image as breadwinners, for
  example. Middle-class or bourgeois respectability is a signiﬁcant factor"; "if she sings for money as
  a professional musician, the couple's trades will not blend socially—the husband has a respectable
  oﬃcial position whereas the wife is a low-paid musician"; "The community attitudes in relation to
  women singing are also linked with women's veiling (pardā)" (p. 82, folio not stated).
- Framing of the new chapter: "In this chapter I describe performances of Suﬁ poetry sung to music in
  sociolinguistic terms, looking at both qawwālī and suﬁānā-kalām… Therefore, the discussion here may
  be perceived in terms of speaking as a cultural system" (p. 83, folio not stated). Method stated:
  "In this chapter I analyze three speech events to distinguish between qawwālī and suﬁānā-kalām, two
  that look at qawwālī compositions and one that investigates a concert in Islamabad" (p. 85, folio not
  stated).
- Genre contrast: "Performers sing the suﬁānā-kalām in a monologue, and there is no turn-taking as in
  qawwālī… Moreover, in suﬁānā-kalām the musicians do not use handclaps and the vibrant percussion of
  drums that mark the identity of qawwālī" (p. 85, folio not stated). "Qawwālī is embedded in the
  structures of classical music using elitist Perso-Arabic linguistic forms found in Hindi and Urdu
  poetry or poetry in vernacular languages such as Panjabi. Suﬁānā-Kalām is mystical poetry sung in the
  vernacular languages of the subcontinent such as Hindi, Gujrati, Sindhi, Siraiki, and Panjabi"
  (p. 86, folio not stated). "The tradition evolved around the thirteenth century ." (p. 86, folio not
  stated; **era abbreviation dropped**).
- Concert qawwālī as a postcolonial artefact: "Qawwālī performances in concert are a product of the
  twentieth century, a feature of postcolonial politics when large Muslim populations from South Asia
  moved to the West" (p. 84, folio not stated); "In concert, qawwālī evolved as an aesthetic musical
  form, albeit generated through the singing of devotional Suﬁ poetry" (p. 84, folio not stated); and
  what the shrine forbids the concert allows — "This eclectic form of poetry would not be permitted
  inside the shrine settings of the Chishtiyya or other orders" (p. 85, folio not stated).
- Cyclical time and `kefīat`: "Unlike the strict structure of a western concert that is linear, the
  concept of time in qawwālī and suﬁānā-kalām concerts is cyclical; there are moments when time is
  suspended and performers and their audiences are caught in a timelessness… The quality of Hindustani
  music is its cyclic movement, somewhat like jazz" (p. 86, folio not stated); "The musicians' body
  movements, together with the rhythm of the music and language, can create a kefīat (mystical delight)
  among the listeners" (p. 90, folio not stated); "the aim of every competent performer of Suﬁ music
  and discourse is to create the samā" (p. 92, folio not stated).
- The book's central claim, restated twice in this chunk: "In the male musicians' qawwālī, I discovered
  that many times they sang in female voices. They did it to express the disciple's submission to the
  spiritual mentor, represented as the female" (p. 86, folio not stated); and "The murid becomes the
  lover and the murshid the beloved. The former is represented as the submissive female. The metaphor
  becomes more complex with the infusion of bridal imagery into the relationship" (p. 96, folio not
  stated).
- Reading the Nusrat case: "To an untrained ear it would seem like a ﬂirtatious love poem"; "Had it not
  been for the very sacred frame at the beginning, when his ensemble invoked Baba Farid, a researcher
  might have taken the performance for a secular love song where a lover seeks a beloved and serenades
  her" (pp. 88–89, folio not stated). On audience design: "he selected verses that would evoke an
  emotional response from his aﬄuent Panjabi-speaking audiences in the United Kingdom, that is, the
  Sikhs and the expatriate Pakistani and Indian listeners" (p. 89, folio not stated).
- The economic reading of mixed audiences: "This is perhaps due to economic factors: male audiences are
  bound to reward better, since the males are wage earners and most women are not" (p. 96, folio not
  stated).
- The veil as doctrine: "The veil in Suﬁ poetry is intimately related to kaśf, or the doctrine of
  lifting the veil, that is, the Divine Being revealing itself to the lover" (p. 96, folio not stated).
- On media and quality: "Performance distribution via electronic media (videos, CD-ROMs, audiocassettes,
  ''dish,'') to large expatriate speech communities in the Middle East, Japan, and the West, especially
  in the United Kingdom, has considerably enhanced the quality and the creativity of the performances"
  (pp. 91–92, folio not stated).
