## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Concert and mehfil as performance settings, and their audiences and patrons.** Nusrat Fateh Ali
  Khan "sang it with his ensemble in a mystical frame in a private mehfil in England in
  `[text-layer?: year printed but not captured]`. Intimate interactions were seen between performers
  and the audience, who knew each other closely, and the speech play evolved from that intimacy"
  (p. 125, folio not stated). **Year (not stated)** in the recoverable text.
- The Sabri Brothers sang the Mira Bai qawwali "in concert before an expatriate audience in the
  United Kingdom" (p. 128, folio not stated; endnote 28) and "in England" (p. 131, folio not
  stated). "For the qawwāls, it is most appropriate to sing of a Rajput princess for their affluent
  patrons who come from the Asian diaspora of the subcontinent" (p. 132, folio not stated) — the
  chunk's one explicit statement about who pays for the performance.
- **A concert for state officials.** Abida Parvin sang "the renowned Layla-Majnun story to talk
  about color and gender. Her intention was to entertain and also to create sociopolitical awareness
  through myth about the minority provinces or marginalized groups in the country before an august
  audience of government bureaucrats" (p. 135, folio not stated).
- **Shrine-derived ritual inside the concert hall.** "Perhaps there is a blend of the traditional
  shrine context with a modern concert setting, where the singers uphold the sanctity of the
  environment. This is evident from the closing salām (hymn) dedicated to the prophet Muhammad, when
  the qawwāls stand up and fold their hands around their waists, as in the prayer ritual, to sing"
  `Sarkār Medine vāle tum par lākhõ salām` — "May thou be blessed a thousand times, O Muhammad, Lord
  of Medina" (p. 132, folio not stated).
- **samā as gathering and as atmosphere.** "Researchers report that from the thirteenth century
  onward, Hindu mystical songs were recited at samā gatherings. This was because many of the
  talented musicians were newly converted Muslims" (p. 132, folio not stated; endnote 37). Elsewhere
  Abbas uses the term for the atmosphere a performer builds: "These are storytelling devices that
  create the samā (the environment) for their audiences" (p. 131, folio not stated).
- **Pilgrimage practice as the qawwali narrates it (a sung account, not the author's observation).**
  "The performers talk about Mira Bai's ritual, three-hundred-mile journeys to Ajmer for twenty
  years. She had to conceal her identity as a Rajput princess who could not be expected to become a
  devotee of a Muslim Sufi saint" (p. 131, folio not stated). In the sung text she stays away from
  the rauza for fear that her pearl anklets would jingle and Khwaja would call her `be adab`
  (disrespectful), and in the twenty-first year "she cried out from the temple steps" (`pehlī seṛī
  par jā kar sadā us ne dī`) (pp. 130–131, folio not stated).
- **Shrine visiting by non-Muslims, observed.** The Kolhi and Bhil peasant women at Bhit Shah, the
  shrine courtyard and shrine exit, and the waiting menfolk, above (p. 134, folio not stated). Abbas
  frames it as continuity: "The pattern of Mira Bai's devotion is not unusual, even today" (p. 134,
  folio not stated).
- **Recording and circulation.** Waris Shah's Hir circulates "both on audiocassettes and videos sung
  in the folk traditions" (p. 123, folio not stated).
- **Land, inheritance and caste economy in the Hir narrative.** "The Jats of the Panjab are
  zamindars (landowners), known for practicing widow-marriage" (p. 121, folio not stated; endnote
  8). "The Siyal tribe is more than usually considerate toward its women; it is one of the few
  tribes in the Panjab that allow women to inherit property under customary law" (p. 121, folio not
  stated; endnote 9). Ranjha's brothers "gave him the least fertile part of the ancestral land when
  they divided the inheritance" (p. 122, folio not stated). Cucak "did not think that he had the
  inheritance that would make him an eligible son-in-law or a powerful ally"; "In order to maintain
  their hegemony in the socioeconomic order, the Siyals had to marry their only daughter into a
  powerful clan like themselves" (p. 122, folio not stated). Ranjha "lowered himself by becoming a
  `kām ,` or a cāk, who was considered a menial of the Siyals" (p. 122, folio not stated).
- **Bribery of the religious establishment, twice.** Ranjha's brothers "bribed the religious
  establishment to support them in this unholy act against their youngest brother" (p. 122, folio
  not stated); and "Cucak bribed the clergyman and the two witnesses to fake her consent" (pp. 122–
  123, folio not stated).
- **Marriage law and consent.** Hir "was married against her will to Saida and sent off to the
  Kheras against the principles of the Sharia, which stresses that a woman can only be married with
  her consent. Hir refused to sign the marriage contract" (p. 122, folio not stated). Earlier the
  same body of law is called "the Shara, the Islamic legal system based on the Quran and the hadīth"
  (p. 121, folio not stated).
- **Wedding ritual.** Hir's lament in the palanquin "is traditionally sung at almost every Panjabi
  wedding at the time of the bride's departure to her husband's home" (p. 124, folio not stated).
- **Indigenous critical vocabulary the book records as technical terms.** `hūk` — "the pain of the
  heart when it laments in grief" (p. 120, folio not stated); `dohaī` — "cry in agony"; `kurlānā` —
  "squirm in pain"; "In the Siraiki language, these are the extreme degrees of physical and
  emotional pain" (p. 120, folio not stated). `dard` — "the performer's stylistic creation of pain
  is called dard" (p. 124, folio not stated). `bulī` — "dry bulī (parched lips)" (p. 120, folio not
  stated). `divānī` — "one who is ecstatic" (p. 129, folio not stated). `ghaṛā` — "a round clay
  pot" (p. 126, folio not stated); `ghaṛolī`, which Abida Parvin "explains… to her audience in one
  of her concerts, suggesting that it is a container that 'receives' the murshid's spiritual
  bounties" (p. 127, folio not stated). `brādārī` — "(extended family)" (p. 123, folio not stated).
  `jogī` (p. 123, folio not stated). `sūr`, `sūrmī`, `sūrma` (p. 120, folio not stated).
  `iśq-e majāzī` — "(personal love)"; `iśq-e haqīqī` — "(divine love)" (p. 125, folio not stated).
- **A professional standard, stated as such.** "The test for a competent singer of Sufi poetry is to
  be able to sing the myth with a passion that moves the listeners to tears" (p. 120, folio not
  stated).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Attributed to the Sabri Brothers' performance, not asserted by the author:** the qawwali
  "signified his karāmāt (spiritual powers)" — Hazrat Khawaja Muinuddin Chishti's — "with Mira Bai
  as his devotee" (p. 128, folio not stated).
- **The saint answers in person.** The sung narrative closes `Mīrā Bāī ke dīl se jo niklī sad ā /
  Un ko ban kar sahārā khud ānā paṛā`, glossed by the book as "The cry came from Mira's very heart /
  And indeed Khaja had to rescue Mira" (p. 133, folio not stated). Mira's cry is `Morī bεy pakaṛ le
  jāe` — "Thou, take me by the arm" (pp. 132–133, folio not stated; endnote 41).
- **The earth opens at a tomb.** "Punnu wept bitterly at her tomb, until the earth opened for him
  and he was buried with his beloved" (p. 118, folio not stated; endnote 4). Told as "the myth told
  in the lands of Sind and Baluchistan" (p. 118, folio not stated).
- **Self-mutilation as love's proof.** Mahival, unable to catch fish, "cut a piece of flesh from his
  thigh, roasted it, and brought it to her" (p. 126, folio not stated).
- **Death at the beloved's grave.** Ranjha "returned to find his beloved being buried. He killed
  himself at her grave" (p. 123, folio not stated); Sohni and Mahival drown together and "Their
  bodies were found and they were buried together. The entire village mourned their deaths" (p. 126,
  folio not stated; endnote 24).
- **A claimed relic landscape**, reported from informants rather than asserted: "there is a forest
  named Sohni, after her" (p. 126, folio not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The chunk's controlling claim — the female voice as the vehicle of mystical meaning, voiced by
  men.** Pathana Khan, a male singer, "sang in Sassi's inflected voice" (p. 118, folio not stated);
  Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan "sang the following qawwālī in Hir's voice" (p. 124, folio not stated); "The
  Sabri Brothers sing in this woman minstrel's inflected voice" (p. 129, folio not stated) and
  "mime Mira's voice" (p. 132, folio not stated). Abbas's conclusion: "her female voice transforms
  the earthly plane to reach a transcendental state" and "In Sufi poetry Hir's voice thus becomes a
  'frame' or a cultural complex to describe esoteric meanings through the aesthetics of poetry and
  music" (p. 125, folio not stated).
- **The mechanism she names for that transformation** is the Sufi doctrinal pair: "This is in
  keeping with the Sufi principles where iśq-e majāzī (personal love) transforms itself into iśq-e
  haqīqī (divine love)" (p. 125, folio not stated).
- **Myth as an affective technique.** "In this transliteration the Sufi troubadour casts emotion
  into the narrative through the use of myth. Sassi's voice gives the poetry an aesthetic dimension
  that involves the listener in an emotional bond with the speaker" (p. 119, folio not stated).
  "There is pain and there is sorrow in that love, because it cannot mature without suffering"
  (p. 119, folio not stated).
- **The ghaṛā read as a doctrine of discipleship.** "the ghaṛā assumes multiple voices: the role of
  Sohni's female friend; the mature murshid who would help the murid, Sohni, cross the stream to the
  spiritual path; the ghaṛā that speaks to Sohni. Sohni drowned because her ghaṛā was kacca and not
  mature" (p. 127, folio not stated). "Her myth, therefore, became a storytelling device or trope
  for the singer to talk of the difficulties on the spiritual path, the iśq-e haqīqī" (p. 127, folio
  not stated). The fire "purifies and transforms baser metals into gold," the water "is the
  cleansing element," and the ghaṛā becomes "a 'metaphorical nature of representation'" (p. 127,
  folio not stated; endnote 27).
- **The social claim.** "The Sufi poets made the native myths of Hir, Sassi, and Sohni into complex
  cultural tropes. The discourse so created and sung to music articulated the concerns of
  marginalized social groups such as the Sufis themselves, women, minorities, persecuted
  communities, and the vast majority of the people who were excluded from the power structures
  supported by the clergy" (p. 128, folio not stated).
- **A conversion claim the author attributes to others, not to herself.** "Many individuals in the
  native cultures assert that it was not the conquerors but rather the Sufis who disseminated Islam
  widely in the subcontinent through their oral traditions and the medium in which they spoke to the
  people" (p. 128, folio not stated).
- **Hir read against patriarchy and caste.** "Hir has many arguments with the clergy where she uses
  the rhetoric of the Shara… to prove its narrow-mindedness and duplicity" (p. 121, folio not
  stated). "In all these sections Hir's abandonment and her loss not only create the romance of the
  myth but also address patriarchal paradigms of religion, caste, socioeconomic class, and gender,
  in which the weak are exploited" (p. 125, folio not stated). "For the Sufi poets there is glory in
  Hir's death, like Antigone's in a Greek tragedy" (p. 125, folio not stated).
- **A cross-religious argument about qawwali.** "It is interesting that qawwāls in the Islamic
  mystical traditions should sing about a Rajput princess who was a great mystic herself" (p. 129,
  folio not stated). "The Mira Bai narrative that the Sabri Brothers sing in England breaks
  religious and cultural barriers. It further mirrors the inherent holistic nature of Sufi poetry.
  The myth becomes truly cross-cultural, sung for the diaspora in England" (pp. 131–132, folio not
  stated). Abbas poses, without answering, "Can this transformation be viewed as a development of
  transnational Islam?" (p. 132, folio not stated).
- **Code-switching as audience management.** "The linguistic codes of Urdu, devotional Hindi, and
  Rajasthani are suited for their listeners, who hail from a variety of linguistic backgrounds"
  (p. 132, folio not stated); "the singers switch codes within indigenous registers, such as Urdu,
  devotional Hindi, and Rajasthani. Mira Bai's ecstacy is conveyed in a highly melismatic style in
  devotional Hindi, with the singers stretching out Mira's cry" (p. 132, folio not stated; the book
  prints "ecstacy").
- **A formal musical analysis, jointly authored and page-referenced to a figure the layer loses.**
  "Tom Solomon, an ethnomusicologist, and I undertook a linguistic and musical analysis of the

- **Textual transmission of Mira Bai's verse.** "There does not seem to be an original manuscript of
