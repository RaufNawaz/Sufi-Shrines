## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The central claim about ritual space, and the reason it is an economic claim too**: "In the
  Islamic world, the mosque is primarily an arena for male activity, with little visible
  participation of women in the rituals. In the major mosques in Pakistan, for instance, there is a
  small space where women can go and pray on Friday or on religious festivals such as the Eid. In
  the local mohallā mosques there is no possibility for a female to offer her ritual prayers…Thus,
  the important spheres of religious and spiritual participation for women are the Sufi shrines"
  (p. 18, folio not stated; endnote 2 on "Eid").
- **ʿurs and melā, defined by the book**: "the urs or melā that celebrates the death anniversary of
  the Sufi saint" (p. 23, folio not stated, endnote 8); melās are "the community festivals, called
  melās, where much activity takes place" and are named as a female domain western male scholars
  cannot enter (p. 19, folio not stated). Women's singing of sufiānā-kalām "is noticeable in the
  shrines, at the melās, in concerts, and in the larger domains of female domestic life" (p. 21,
  folio not stated).
- **Offerings**: "There are many affluent and well-to-do devotees at the shrines who go there for
  prayer and meditation and also to make a mannat (a vow). When that vow is fulfilled, they go to
  the shrine to make an offering—usually food or charity that they give in cash" (p. 21, folio not
  stated). No sums, no shares, no custodial recipient: **(not stated)**.
- **Who attends**: "The audiences at the shrines come from a variety of socioeconomic backgrounds…
  Women come in large numbers. The majority of participants are rural peasants, factory workers,
  housewives, and middle-class devotees. A large number who come are illiterate but well versed in
  the oral culture. The shrines fulfill devotional needs and provide outlets from the chores of
  daily life" (p. 21, folio not stated). No visitor counts anywhere in the chunk: **(not stated)**.
- **State involvement and architecture**: the extension-into-mosque trend under "orthodox Islamic
  governments in Pakistan", Data Darbar's tenfold enlargement in "the most expensive Italian
  marble", Friday congregations in its courtyard, and the restored Mian Mir shrine "also used as a

  not stated); `faqīrīanī` — women singers at Sindhi shrines (p. 21, folio not stated); `faqīrs` —

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Abbas's gap claim**: samā "is poorly documented and scarcely understood among the wider
  scholarly audience. Women's contribution to this is even less known. Despite the strong gender
  component of Sufi ritual discourse, the role of women has been ignored in scholarly work" (p. 18,
  folio not stated).
- **Her two-sided explanation of the gap** — native scholars: "Among native scholars the area is
  ignored despite the fact that women have done much to educate the renowned male Sufis. Women are
  only referred to as mothers or sisters or spouses of the members of a Sufi silsilā (order)"
  (p. 18, folio not stated), evidenced by a quotation she attributes only to "A researcher of Amir
  Khusrau" (p. 18, folio not stated; endnote 4 on p. 19): "We do not know whether Khusrau had any
  sisters, for the eastern philosophers generally do not bother themselves about the female
  relatives of a person…" (p. 19, folio not stated). Western male scholars: "as men, they cannot
  access the female domains of participation…Furthermore, they have been handicapped due to their
  lack of knowledge of the indigenous languages" (p. 19, folio not stated).
- **Her positional claim**: "I bring my own understandings and experiences of the culture that I
  portray. I convey the intuitions and subtleties of an oral culture where information is passed
  through word of mouth" (p. 19, folio not stated), and at the close of the Preface, "I shall now
  let the musicians of Sufi melodies speak for themselves. My role is that of the interpreter of the
  culture" (p. 26, folio not stated, endnote 19).
- **Her methodological position, stated as a refusal of theory**: "I stated earlier that I do not
  try to fit this book into any theoretical frame. I apply the ethnography of speaking and the
  conversation analysis frame" (p. 25, folio not stated); cf. "I am aware that there are a number of
  theories about speech and performance, but I use only those that relate to my work" (p. 20, folio
  not stated).
- **Her translation policy**: "The translations are not a literal rendering of the text; rather,
  they are done to convey the mood of the Sufi poems" (p. 10, folio not stated); "I have avoided a
  word-for-word translation but have made sure that the text is an authentic rendering of the poetic
  narrative" (p. 20, folio not stated). She calls her renderings "poetic 'transmogrifications'"
  (p. 24, folio not stated).
- **The literature review in chapter 1, in her own terms**: "Qureshi discusses the Qawwāl Bachche, a
  lineage of male qawwāls (performers of Sufi music and poetry) in the subcontinent of Pakistan and
  India. Schimmel has covered areas of the female voice in the subcontinent, but hers are literary
  and theoretical studies. Likewise, Smith has researched Rabia al-Adawiyya and some other women
  mystics of Islam, but neither she nor Schimmel has investigated women's rituals from an
  ethnographic or performance perspective" (p. 32, folio not stated; endnotes 2–4). Conclusion: "No
  researcher has examined the sociolinguistic aspects of the female voice or some of the performance
  fields that I have identified in my study" (p. 32, folio not stated).
- **Her own limits, stated**: "The scope of this study is limited to its present undertaking…I do
  not discuss different versions of the female myths, or delve into male rituals" (p. 22, folio not
  stated); and on whether Sufi poets wrote in empathy for women, "Whether or not the Sufi poets of
  the subcontinent wrote in empathy for women is an area of research in itself…I leave the field
  open for further exploration" (p. 22, folio not stated). On child-marriage: "There is scant
  literature on child-marriage…There is little scholarly evidence available" (p. 19, folio not
  stated).
- **The eight-point inventory of female participation in Sufism** she says her fieldwork identified
  (p. 21, folio not stated): women as mystics; as creators of Sufi poetry; as influences on male
  Sufis "in their roles as mothers, daughters, nurses, and mentors"; as ethnographers and patrons;
  as singers/musicians/participants; as preservers of lore; the hijṛās/khawājāsarā; and "The
  aesthetics of the female voice, a poetic device in which the speaker is the female, even in the
  narratives of male musicians. The musicians play with the syntactic and semantic structures of the
  languages to speak as though they were females."
- **Her claim about the politics of the female voice**: "Their discourse challenged the patriarchy
  and the establishment through the device of the female speaker; even in the metalanguage of the
  mystic ecstasy, the musicians spoke as females" (p. 23, folio not stated); and of Abida Parvin,
  she "was framing her critiques of the orthodox establishment in public concerts, using the poetry
  of the Sufis of the subcontinent" (p. 24, folio not stated). The author names her own risk as the
  "'paradox of familiarity'" (p. 24, folio not stated).
- **Fernea's argument in the Foreword, which is hers and not Abbas's**: the "male religion"
  characterisation of Islam "arises from several misconceptions" — the comparison to Christianity,
  where "the mosque is not, like the Christian church, the center of religious observance. The focus
  of Muslim practice, like the focus of Judaic religious practice, lies in the home"; that early
  Western studies "were text-based, rather than being the result of on-the-ground research"; and
  that "the absence of a common language has also contributed to an ethnocentric view" (p. 14, folio
  not stated). She adds that Sufism "has always been regarded with some ambiguity by Muslim imams
  and mullahs, who see the rituals of the Sufi tariqas (organizations) as less than orthodox"
  (p. 15, folio not stated), that "the Sufi brotherhoods and sisterhoods have persisted, despite
  religious disfavor and government attempts to curtail their practices" (p. 15, folio not stated),
  and that French ethnographers "seldom mentioned the women auxiliaries of these same marabouts"
  (pp. 15–16, folio not stated). Her closing gap claim: "almost nothing has been published in
  English about men and women in South Asian Sufism" (p. 16, folio not stated).
- **The publisher's framing claim, on the cover and so not the author's own** (p. 1, folio not
  stated): "This book presents the first in-depth study of the female voice in Sufi practice in the
  subcontinent of Pakistan and India."
