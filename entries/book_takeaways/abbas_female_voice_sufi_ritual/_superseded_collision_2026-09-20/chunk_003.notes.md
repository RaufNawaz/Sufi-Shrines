# abbas_female_voice_sufi_ritual — chunk 003 — pp. 54–76

Twenty-three continuous pages of Shemeem Burney Abbas's own ethnographic prose interleaved with
**five blocks of interview transcript** and **one long quoted oral legend**. The chunk **opens
mid-passage** on p. 54 with an untagged block quotation already in progress ("There is only one God,
and there is his temple in each individual…", p. 54, folio not stated) and **breaks off mid-song** on
p. 76, the last line of a Sidi *jikar* reading only "Kinky" (p. 76, folio not stated). The field is
**Sind** throughout, with an excursus to **Ratanpur / Rajpipla in Gujrat, India** (pp. 73–74, folio
not stated) drawn from Basu's research.

**Speaker tags in this chunk, and who they are.** `A:` is the author, Shemeem Burney Abbas,
interviewing (first at p. 58, folio not stated, printed `A:57` with endnote 57 glued to it). `P:` is
**Abida Parvin** (p. 54, folio not stated — the lead-in "I asked Abida Parvin to explain the kefīat
(ecstasy)" immediately precedes the tag). `R:` is **Reshma** (pp. 58–61, folio not stated — lead-in
"I interviewed Reshma", p. 58). `B:` is **Sikander Baloch** — confirmed by the lead-in "Baloch
further reiterated, B:" (p. 63, folio not stated). `M:` (pp. 61–62, folio not stated) is by
elimination **Naseer Mirza**, but the book never says so; see Doubts. `Q:` is **Qalandar Shah** and
`K:` is **Kubra** (pp. 69–71, folio not stated — lead-in "The text of the interview with Qalandar
Shah and Kubra is translated into English", p. 69). Turns are separated by em-dashes.

**No folio anywhere.** Consistent with the measured fact for this book, not one of these twenty-three
pages prints a recoverable page number; every reference below therefore reads *folio not stated*. The
running header `6657 Abbas / THE FEMALE VOICE IN SUFI RITUAL / sheet N of 239` appears once per page
and holds to `sheet = PDF page − 1` across the whole chunk (sheet 53 on p. 54 through sheet 75 on
p. 76). It is print-production furniture and is never quoted below. The repeated `Tseng 2002.9.25
07:23` stamps and the isolated marginal `G` on every page are likewise ignored.

**Endnote superscripts run 51 to 104** (p. 55 through p. 76, folio not stated), so notes 1–50 of this
chapter fell in earlier chunks and the chapter continues past 104 into chunk 004.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **lal-shahbaz-qalandar** (the book's names: "Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar", "Lal Shahbaz Qalandar",
  "Shahbaz Qalandar", "Qalandar Lal Shahbaz", "Lal Qalandar"). **This is the dominant archive row in
  the chunk.**
  - The author "did fieldwork in Sind at the Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar shrine in `[text-layer?: year
    dropped]`" and there "studied women roving minstrels who sing devotional narratives to the Sufi
    saints" (p. 56, folio not stated). The year is **(not stated)** in the text layer; see Doubts.
  - She looked for them at this shrine "because Reshma, who was once a roving gypsy woman, rose to
    international fame while she sang her famous ''Mast Qalandar'' melody here in the late sixties"
    (p. 56, folio not stated).
  - The shrine stands at **Sehwan**, "the historic city built within a large fortress from the time of
    Alexander the Great" (p. 56, folio not stated); elsewhere "Sehwan Sharif where the Lal Shahbaz
    Qalandar shrine is located" (p. 63, folio not stated).
  - **Urs**: the celebrations "last for more than a week", and women informants living around "the
    heavily populated area of the shrine" reported that during them "it is almost impossible to walk
    in the narrow streets of the city" (p. 56, folio not stated). The city "is flooded with large
    numbers of female musicians who converge on the city to earn a living" (pp. 56–57, folio not
    stated).
  - He is called "a Sufi mystic of Sind" and "is venerated in the lyric as the bestower of children"
    (p. 57, folio not stated, endnote 55).
  - "The Sufi saint is known as a jalālī pir, a mystic with intense supernatural energy" (p. 57, folio
    not stated). Performers "sing poetic texts that glorify the karāmāt (mystical prowess) of Hazrat
    Lal Shahbaz Qalandar" (p. 57, folio not stated).
  - The "Mast Qalandar" *dhun* is "a tune reported to be sung all along the Rajasthan, Sind,
    Baluchistan, and Iran continuum"; "The melody is dedicated to Ali and is sung in all the regional
    languages along the continuum"; "Almost every folksinger in the region can sing it because of its
    simple mnemonics and rhythm" (p. 57, folio not stated).
  - The standard lyric as printed (p. 57, folio not stated): "Lāl merī pat rakio bhalā / Jhole Lālan /
    Sindhṛī dā / Sehwan dā / Sakhī Shāhbāz Qalandar / Damā dam mast Qalandar / Alī Shāhbāz Qalandar /
    Alī dam dam de andar", glossed by the book as "O Lal Qalandar, save my prestige / O thou of the
    cradle / O thou of Sind / Of Sehwan / Thou bounteous Shahbaz Qalandar / Thy ecstatic trance is the
    healing breath / O Ali-Shahbaz Qalandar / Ali's name is the healing breath". Two further lines are
    damaged in the text layer: `M v n pira bachṛe denā` [text-layer?: letters carrying diacritics
    dropped; reading uncertain] "Thou giver of children to mothers" and `Behn n denā-e vīr bhalā`
    [text-layer?: same class] "Thou giver of brothers to sisters" (p. 57, folio not stated).
  - Performers "sing the melody in their high-pitched, full-throated voices at the shrine without the
    support of microphones" (p. 57, folio not stated).
  - **Reshma's account of her own beginning there**, in her own words: "I first started at the Shahbaz
    Qalandar shrine—"; "Every year we go to the Shahbaz Qalandar shrine—"; "I was young at the time
    when I went there and made a mannat—for my brother—and then I went for his mannat" (R:, p. 58,
    folio not stated, endnote 58). Her brother "was arranged to be wedded… God helped us and my
    brother got married—where we all wanted him to be married" (R:, p. 58, folio not stated). The
    author puts it to her: "Then you went to Shahbaz Qalandar—to fulfill the mannat and you became
    famous", to which she answers only "There I sang—" (pp. 58, folio not stated).
  - Reshma on continuing attendance: "I go even now—I just returned from there—"; "The Sufi Shahbaz
    Qalandar calls for me at least three or four times—I go there and sing for him—I sing for my
    master—he listens to my songs—" (R:, p. 61, folio not stated). Asked whether women singing at Sufi
    shrines is more a Sind or a Rajasthan tradition, she answers "this is so at the shrines—not so
    much in Rajasthan—but it is at the Shahbaz Qalandar shrine—and it is at this shrine that I sing—"
    (R:, p. 60, folio not stated), and confirms other women there, "women who are looking for a
    livelihood—they join in the singing of sufiānā-kalām—and this happens every year" (R:, p. 61,
    folio not stated).
  - **M: makes this shrine the sole surviving site of the loṭevālī tradition in Sind**: "These were
    gypsy women who only sang at the Shahbaz Qalandar shrine—certainly, that is the only place in the
    Sind now where the loṭevālīs come—to sing there… no loṭevālī passes by these shrines—except at
    Qalandar Lal Shahbaz" (M:, p. 61, folio not stated). The loṭevālī "come to the Shahbaz Qalandar
    shrine with a loṭa—and sing there—and make a livelihood on a rupee, two rupee—the alms they get
    there" (M:, p. 61, folio not stated).
  - **Dhammāl**: "the dhammāl (the Qalandari dance performed at the Lal Shahbaz Qalandar shrine in
    Sehwan every evening)"; "The dhammāl that the qalandars or the faqīrs perform at the shrine is
    similar to the dance of the whirling dervishes at Maulana Jalaluddin Rumi's shrine in Konya"
    (p. 64, folio not stated). "The state of ecstasy is induced with the rhythmic beat of the naubats
    (drums)" (p. 65, folio not stated). "Both men and women participate in the dhammāl. Women perform
    the dance in a certain section of the courtyard" (p. 66, folio not stated).
  - **Saying attributed to the saint.** "Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar is reported to have said of the
    dhammāl" (p. 65, folio not stated, endnote 66): "For the sake of the love of the friend / I dance
    over fire / Sometimes I roll in the dust / And sometimes I dance on the thorns / I have become
    notorious in your love / I beseech you to come to me / I am not afraid of this disrepute / To
    dance in every bazaar" (p. 65, folio not stated). Printed in English only; no original script is
    given and no source is named beyond "is reported to have said".
  - **Healing at the shrine.** "Many times, families will bring women who are emotionally disturbed to
    participate in the dhammāl, which devotees claim has healing powers"; the family "will negotiate
    with the ''Sufi'' certain terms that will be fulfilled when the woman gets well, such as a vow
    that there will be a ceremony and the woman's husband or family will buy her an expensive gift,
    perhaps in gold, if she recovers from her illness" (p. 66, folio not stated, endnote 67). "I
    discovered that one can buy cassettes of healing music for emotionally disturbed persons in the
    bazaar outside the shrine" (p. 66, folio not stated).
  - Zeena Bai and other women "who sang as roving minstrels at the Lal Shahbaz Qalandar shrine,
    evolved into trained classical and semiclassical musicians in Hyderabad" (p. 63, folio not
    stated). Taj Mastani "affirmed that she belongs to Sehwan Sharif where the Lal Shahbaz Qalandar
    shrine is located, but she moved to Hyderabad to learn the music arts" (pp. 63–64, folio not
    stated).
  - Used as the comparative benchmark for Gujrat: Basu's material contains "references to the dhammāl
    (the ecstatic dance at an urs) lasting for seven nights, in the manner of the dhammāl at the Lal
    Shahbaz Qalandar shrine" (p. 74, folio not stated, endnote 95).
  - Named in the author's closing claim for the section: one of "two major shrines in Sind… Hazrat Lal
    Shahbaz Qalandar at Sehwan Sharif" that "point toward significant female input in Sufi rituals"
    (p. 67, folio not stated).
  - **Not stated anywhere in this chunk:** the saint's death year, his silsila as such (the adjective
    "Qalandari" is used only of the dance), the custodianship of the shrine, any building date, and
    any visitor figure.

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Radio as an institution of patronage.** "The radio in Pakistan has played a significant role in
  identifying musicians and providing them a livelihood" (p. 55, folio not stated). Sikander Baloch and
  Naseer Mirza were interviewed "at Radio Pakistan in Hyderabad in `[text-layer?: year dropped]`"
  (p. 55, folio not stated). M:: "I am forty-three years old—I have been associated with Radio
  Pakistan for the last twenty years—we have covered all the melās at the shrines" (M:, p. 61, folio
  not stated). Talent-scouting at melās has ended: "that tradition has died when we would go to the
  melās—to hunt for talent—now educated women are there in the field" (M:, p. 62, folio not stated).
- **Officially organized urs.** "In the officially organized urs celebrations in Pakistan
  commemorating the Sufi's death anniversary at his shrine, key musicians are invited to sing" (p. 55,
  folio not stated, endnote 52). No organizing body, department or Auqaf office is named — **(not
  stated)**.
- **The roving-minstrel economy.** "The loṭevālī or chalevālī are roving gypsy women who move from one
  shrine to another while their families trade in livestock and peddle inexpensive local textiles and
  pots and pans. Their audiences are women and men from their own socioeconomic background who
  participate in the shrine activities at the time of the urs and who give them small sums of money
  during the performance. Women tend to pay less because they are unemployed, while men can be more
  generous" (p. 58, folio not stated). Alms at the shrine amount to "a rupee, two rupee" (M:, p. 61,
  folio not stated).
- **Instruments and the naming of the minstrels.** "They will either play a yak-tārā or a ghaṛā, an
  earthen pot used as a drum. Their identity is a ring (chalā), which they wear around the middle
  finger and use to create percussion on a loṭā (aluminum pot) or a ghaṛa. These minstrels are called
  loṭevālī for playing the loṭā, chalevālī for playing the chalā, or ghaṛevālī for playing the ghaṛā"
  (pp. 57–58, folio not stated, endnote 56). They are "accompanied by small groups of instrumentalists
  perhaps playing percussion or accordion. But, by and large, they have minimal instruments" (p. 57,
  folio not stated). Elsewhere the ḍholak (double-sided drum) and the tambūrā are named (pp. 71, 63,
  76, folio not stated).
- **Where the women come from.** "Most of the women musicians come from the Panjab and the
  neighborhoods of Sind itself. They perform in every nook and cranny and attract huge audiences"
  (p. 57, folio not stated).
- **Gharānā and burial.** "there are two gharānās in Hyderabad—the Indore gharānā and the Gawalior
  gharānā"; Zeena Bai's is "a Rajasthani gharānā", also called "the Maṛichā gharānā" (M:, pp. 61–62,
  folio not stated). "she is buried in the Hyderabad graveyard where all the great musicians of the
  city are buried" (M:, p. 62, folio not stated).
- **Landowner patronage.** Taj Mastani "is additionally engaged by large landowners for private events
  such as weddings and births" (p. 64, folio not stated). A caption reads "Patronized by a landowner:
  Hinda Bai, later known as Bali" (p. 68, folio not stated).
- **The Sidi community's economy and status.** "Sidis were initially brought as slaves from Africa to
  work in the subcontinent of Pakistan and India" (p. 67, folio not stated); "Although they are now
  free, some of them are attached to the feudal households in the Sind province of Pakistan" (p. 68,
  folio not stated). Q:: "Their allegiances have been to the ruling families"; "Although now they are
  employed—in various professions that include work with government departments—such as Sind
  University—they carry on with their singing" (Q:, p. 70, folio not stated). They are "invited to
  sing at celebrations such as births and weddings—they have a humor that people like—they sing and
  their audiences reward them well" (Q:, p. 70, folio not stated), and are distinguished from "the
  Manganhars—the traditional caste musicians that people invite to perform at rituals" (Q:, p. 70,
  folio not stated).
- **The Sidi melā calendar.** Karima "said they sing the 'Mama Gor' composition at their pir's melā
  after midnight"; "they mostly sing at their pir's annual melā, which is on the sixteenth day after
  the Muslim festival of Eid ul Azha, when the faithful offer a sacrificial animal. Their pir
  (spiritual mentor) is Qutb Din Darya, whose shrine is called Tando Jahana and is in the Civil Lines
  at Hyderabad" (p. 71, folio not stated, endnote 83). The Manghopir congregation is "in April"
  (Q:, p. 70, folio not stated).
- **The Sidi drum.** Q:: "They have their African drum—it stands vertical—they call it the
  mugarman… The beat is African—the dance is African—they dance around it—both women and men dance
  around it—for them it is extremely sacred—this is their link with an African past" (Q:, p. 70, folio
  not stated).
- **Female performance under constraint.** Kubra "is shy to admit her skill as a singer, due to
  negative community attitudes toward female performers. Her reluctance is accentuated by the fact
  that her husband holds a position as an ''official,'' a clerk in the Sind University administration"
  (p. 69, folio not stated). K:: "I don't sing—I am ashamed to sing" (p. 70, folio not stated, endnote
  73); "my husband will not let me sing—he says it does not look nice" (p. 70, folio not stated,
  endnote 74); but "at weddings we sing—my sister—" (p. 70, folio not stated). They sing "in
  Sindhi—not in Urdu" (K:, p. 70, folio not stated) and sing "songs that are called sehrās—in
  Sindhi—we sing for ceremonials—for the aulīyā [Sufi saints]" (K:, p. 71, folio not stated).
- **Gujrat (India) shrine economy, from Basu.** "Supposedly until forty to fifty years ago all the
  Sidis living near the shrine lived as faqīrs and religious mendicants. Some even lived in jungles
  among tigers"; "Rajpipla princes invited Sidis occasionally to dance in exchange for token annual
  donations to the shrine. At harvest and the end of Ramazan they received jakat in the form of
  produce and handicrafts from members in peasant and craft communities in nearby villages" (p. 74,
  folio not stated, endnote 91). "The shrine originated at the time of the king of Rajpipla, who came
  from Rajasthan in the twelfth century" (p. 74, folio not stated). The raja "would send Bhils to
  their village to deliver grains, oil, and food stocks" (p. 74, folio not stated, endnote 92).
- **The eighty-year-old woman's testimony to Basu** (quoted in full by Abbas, p. 74, folio not stated,
  endnote 93): "In the days of the raja we had ijjat (honor). He would send Bhils to Ratanpur with big
  containers of oil, ghee, rice, flour. Nobody was hungry. And all the Sidi houses had rights
  (copadio)… We received rice and jovar from farmers, dishes from potters, oil from the oil-pressers
  and sometimes also old clothes the people gave us. So we had no difficulties. But now things are
  much more difficult."
- **Infertility cures at Ratanpur.** "Mai Misra's shrine in Gujrat has become known for healing female
  infertility, as her brother Bava Habash's shrine is renowned for healing male infertility. Whereas
  female devotees who are infertile are fed Mai Misra's kichri, the male devotees who are infertile
  are given ''Bava Habash's milk'' full of hot spices" (p. 76, folio not stated, endnote 102). The
  infertile women "eat the kichri and sing jikar (devotional songs) at the shrine" (p. 76, folio not
  stated, endnote 103), the song quoted ending "We dance dhammāl and have fun" (p. 76, folio not
  stated, endnote 104).
- **Healing outside Sind.** "In Baluchistan, such healing ceremonies are called guāṭi" (p. 66, folio
  not stated).
- **The Institute of Sindhology.** Eight photographs in this chunk are credited "Courtesy Institute of
  Sindhology, Sind University, Jamshoro" (pp. 64, 65, 66, 67, 68, 69, and the captions on pp. 72 and
  73, folio not stated). Karachi Mori "lies about twelve miles from Jamshoro, where Sind University is
  located" (p. 67, folio not stated).
- **The village concert.** "the community of more than a hundred women and their children created a
  rural concert (melā) in Qalandar Shah's large village home"; the Sidi women musicians and "their
  neighbors, the Mohana fisherwomen, created a performance for me" (p. 75, folio not stated), ranging
  "from mystical songs to the lament poetry of marsiyā or nohā in the Shii traditions sung during the
  Muharram rituals" (p. 76, folio not stated, endnote 99). "The village women spent another hour
  tuning the ḍholak (double-sided drum). That in itself was a performance" (p. 71, folio not stated,
  endnote 82).

- Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar "is venerated in the lyric as the bestower of children" and is "known as
  a jalālī pir, a mystic with intense supernatural energy"; performers sing texts glorifying his
  *karāmāt* (p. 57, folio not stated, endnote 55). Attributed by the author to the lyric and to the
  performers, not asserted in her own voice.
- The verse "For the sake of the love of the friend / I dance over fire… To dance in every bazaar" is
  what the saint "is reported to have said of the dhammāl" (p. 65, folio not stated, endnote 66).
- Devotees "claim" the dhammāl "has healing powers" for emotionally disturbed women (p. 66, folio not
  stated).
- Reshma's *mannat* at the Shahbaz Qalandar shrine for her brother's marriage, and its fulfilment:
  "God helped us and my brother got married—where we all wanted him to be married" (R:, p. 58, folio
  not stated). Reshma also reports being summoned: "The Sufi Shahbaz Qalandar calls for me at least
  three or four times" (R:, p. 61, folio not stated).
- Reported by unnamed people of Sahiwal and put to Reshma by the author, not confirmed by her: "there
  was a Nanga—pir or jogī who prayed for you that made you so famous" (A:, p. 59, folio not stated).
- Zeena Bai attributed her voice to a saint's prayer: "she said that because of his special prayer for
  her—there was so much sūr [melody] in her voice" — the Shah of Ranipur (M:, pp. 61–62, folio not
  stated, endnote 63).
- **The Bava Gor legend**, given as an oral account attributed to "Abdulkader" and quoted at length
  (pp. 72–73, folio not stated, endnotes 86–89): Sidi Mubarak Nobi, "an Abyssinian lord", went on
  pilgrimage to Mecca and remained "in the service of the venerable Sallah-ul-Salam"; "In those times
  a demoness lived in Hindustan, who through the power of her magic kept a butter lamp burning whose
  light could be seen all the way to Arabia. Her name was Makhan Devi (Butter Goddess). It was her
  habit to kill men daily, eat them, and from their blood make a tilak (mark) on her forehead"; he was
  ordered "to go to Hindustan to break the magic of the goddess and to light the lamp of Islam";
  "gathered a great army made entirely of Sidi people"; at Baghdad received the khilafat and the name
  Bava Gor; "everywhere that Bava Gor stopped on his journey a shrine was created and each time one of
  the Sidis from his army remained in the place."
- Mai Misra "is reported to have joined her brothers to fight the female demoness—the Butter Goddess
  called Makhan Devi" (p. 76, folio not stated, endnote 101).
- "Most Sidis are convinced that a special bakhśīś (gift) has been bestowed upon them by their
  ancestor Bilal" (p. 75, folio not stated, endnote 97).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Ecstasy as the object of study.** The author "asked Abida Parvin to explain the kefīat (ecstasy)
  that is induced through Sufi speech and music. She explained it as a state produced or transmitted
  through zikr. This is repetitive chanting of devotional kalimāt (phrases) in Arabic" (p. 54, folio
  not stated). Abida Parvin's own formulation includes: "he who is tied to prayer five times a day is
  not a Sufi—the Sufi is always in zikr of God" and "We only sing the kalām of the Sufia—as no one can
  express a khayāl [thought] like them" (P:, p. 54, folio not stated).
- **Female practice inside a male genre.** Baloch and Mirza "reported that since she was trained
  within the male traditions her style is exceptional. She uses the ḍohra or the bait, short two-line
  verses, within the main body of her narrative, a style she imbibed from her male mentors, including
  her late husband, Shaikh Ghulam Hussain. They claim that no other woman musician in the Sind has
  this style, which is essentially a unique male style" (p. 55, folio not stated). The author's own
  conclusion: "The fact that now Abida Parvin has introduced the qaul in the sufiānā-kalām tradition
  fits in with this assertion. The qawwālī genre of Sufi music is essentially a male domain" (p. 55,
  folio not stated).
- **The indigenous vocabulary of context.** Baloch and Mirza "affirmed that women musicians like Abida
  Parvin are immersed in the rūh (spirit) of Sufi poetics and music and can create the contexts
  (racāo) of that discourse. They used the indigenous term sūng for the practice… where the lover woos
  the estranged beloved through a serenade on the capṛī (wooden castanets)" (p. 55, folio not stated,
  endnote 51). B: expands: "there is this school of thought in Sindhi Sufi poetry—the sūng—in which
  the lover sings to win back the estranged beloved—this is called manānā—it is within these contexts
  and voices that the musicians build in the ecstasy" (B:, p. 63, folio not stated, endnote 65).
- **Why the female voice.** B:: "Why the musicians use the female alāp [narrative]—is because there is
  a beauty in her voice—there is a niāz-mandī [humility]—there is a hilm—and there is a modesty—when
  mystical poetry is sung in these voices—it creates the soul of the context—like when we pray—we
  beg—we sing His sanā [praise]" (B:, p. 63, folio not stated). And: "The words [bol] are put in the
  woman's voice for artistic purposes and then—when the woman sings it herself, it brings out the
  color—the elegance—whenever women musicians have sung mystical poetry it has been very popular"
  (B:, p. 63, folio not stated).
- **The joganī lyric.** "I have transcribed a mystic text about a female mendicant (joganī) that the
  musician sings in Urdu… I find allusions to the Layla-Majnun myth in the discourse, where Majnun has
  become a madman for love of Layla. People in the streets ridicule him and throw stones at him"
  (p. 55, folio not stated, endnote 54). On the text: "there is the metalanguage of one who is
  ''possessed'' or has become a divānā. Additionally, there is subtle humor in the verses… Such
  discourse is rarely sung in the sufiānā-kalām traditions in the regional languages, but I selected
  it for its uniqueness and humor" (p. 56, folio not stated).
- **The section's own scope statement.** "In this section I explore some aspect of the personal
  histories of women musicians who sing devotional Sufi poetry. Their histories, told partly by
  themselves and partly by others, show how the melodies that they sang at Sufi shrines became marks
  of their personal identities" (p. 56, folio not stated).
- **The section's own limit and conclusion.** "I have only touched the periphery of the female roving
  traditions, and the field needs to be further researched. The findings at two major shrines in Sind,
  that is, Shah Abdul Latif at Bhit Shah and Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar at Sehwan Sharif, point
  toward significant female input in Sufi rituals" (pp. 66–67, folio not stated).
- **The maidānī voice.** Baloch and Mirza "said that this was the poetic content and the dialects in
  the female musicians' speech"; Mai Bhagi "sang in the maidānī voices that are the dialects of the
  Thar region in Sind. The languages used to sing mystical poetry in the Thar region are Dhatki,
  Kacchi, Marwari, and Sindhi" (p. 62, folio not stated, endnote 64).
- **The etymology of "Sidi", advanced by Qalandar Shah, not by the author.** "Sidis—which is
  linguistically derived from Syedi—Cid—the word is there in Spanish and French—Cid… very interesting
  anthropologically—because although they were slaves they were called ''our Syedi'' or ''my lord''"
  (Q:, p. 70, folio not stated).
- **The referent chain in Kubra's chant**, the author's central analytic move in this section (p. 75,
  folio not stated, endnote 98): printed as "Allah → Muhammad the Nabi → Hazrat Bilal → Bava Gor" and
  again with the arrows reversed. "The hierarchical referents in the song start with Allah or God,
  linked through his prophet (Nabi) Muhammad and further linked through Bilal and Mama Gor… The words
  are used as performative acts to ritualize the event: the initiatory devotional ceremony at a Sidi
  shrine that pays homage to the prophet Muhammad through Bilal and Bava Gor." And: "through
  articulating her speech thus the performer blesses herself. She does the same through the
  performative act of blessing Bava Gor/Mama Gor: she actually blesses herself."
- **Kerbala as trope.** "Kerbala is a trope in Islamic discourse that marks the distinction between
  Shii Islam and Sunni Islam. Followers of Hussain are the Shii, but in South Asian Islam there is
  veneration for Kerbala and Hussain's family among both the Shii and the Sunni Muslims. Thus, in
  Kubra's lyric the trope of Kerbala is used without an overt referent except through blessing
  Muhammad's family, which she articulates as ''yal-āi-e''" (p. 75, folio not stated).
- **An explicit criticism of a cited scholar.** "Basu does not offer any historical discussion on the
  origins of the shrine, but her interviews with Sidi elders refer to their memories of the past
  relationship with the raja" (p. 74, folio not stated).
- **Two open research claims the author states as unfinished.** "There seems to be a connection
  between escaped slaves and faqīrs" (p. 74, folio not stated); and "more needs to be investigated to
  establish links between the Sidi minstrels in South Gujrat and their related communities in Sind in
  Pakistan" (p. 74, folio not stated).
