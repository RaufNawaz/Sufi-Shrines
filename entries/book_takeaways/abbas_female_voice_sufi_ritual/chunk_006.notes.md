# abbas_female_voice_sufi_ritual — chunk 006 — pp. 118–135

Continuous running text from the middle of a chapter on the female voice in Sufi myth and song.
The chunk opens mid-episode on the Sassi-Punnu legend (p. 118, folio not stated), runs through the
Hir-Ranjha and Sohni-Mahival myths (pp. 120–128, folios not stated), then a new section on the Mira
Bai qawwālī sung by the Sabri Brothers in England (pp. 128–134, folios not stated), and breaks off
mid-episode inside the Layla-Majnun story (p. 135, folio not stated). **No page of this chunk prints
a folio**, so every reference below is `(p. N, folio not stated)`, per the run's measured finding.
The running typesetting mark `6657 Abbas / THE FEMALE VOICE IN SUFI RITUAL / sheet N of 239` appears
on all eighteen pages and is a printing-sheet index, **not a folio**; it is not cited anywhere below.
A second running typesetting mark, `Tseng 2002.9.25 07:23`, appears at the foot of all eighteen
pages; it is likewise not a folio and is not cited. The chunk **has no opening seam of its own** — it
begins mid-sentence at "way he saw her tomb" (p. 118, folio not stated) — and **breaks off
mid-episode** at "until Layla's family and her tribe" (p. 135, folio not stated); both seams should
be stitched at consolidation.

**Who is speaking, as a check on attribution.** The book is single-authored (Shemeem Burney Abbas),
and the first person in this chunk is hers throughout: "In the previous chapter I discussed Nusrat
Fateh Ali Khan's singing" (p. 120, folio not stated); "I quote from a written text" (p. 124, folio
not stated); "a song that I have transcribed from Tufail Niazi's kalām" (p. 124, folio not stated);
"I photographed non-Muslim Kolhi and Bhil peasant women" (p. 134, folio not stated). So "Abbas
argues" is the correct attribution for everything here except where she reports a singer's words, an

**Voice-part labels in the transcriptions.** The song transliterations are laid out with a left-hand

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **bhit-bhit-shah** (the book's forms: "Shah Abdul Latif", "Shah Abdul Latif's Risālo", "Shah's
  poetry", "Latif's poetry", "Shah Abdul Latif's shrine in Bhit Shah"): **Named as the Sufi poet
  whose Risālo is built on these myths.** "Sufi bards have based entire sections of their
  compositions, such as Shah Abdul Latif's Risālo, on the Sassi-Punnu myth" (p. 118, folio not
  stated). "Even the melody in which it is sung in Shah's poetry is called ''Sūr Sassi,'' which means
  Sassi's melody" (p. 118, folio not stated). "In Shah Abdul Latif's poetry, Sassi is the epitome of
  the lover who seeks the beloved as though the beloved is the Divine Being" (p. 118, folio not
  stated).
- **bhit-bhit-shah** — the structure of the Risālo as the book states it: "Shah Abdul Latif's entire
  Risālo is based on these narratives, and he evokes mystical poetry for his tragic heroines to
  become symbols of otherworldly love" (p. 120, folio not stated). "Each one of these romances is
  sung in its own sūr. Thus, there is a ''Sūr Sohni'' in Latif's poetry to sing about Sohni, ''Sūr
  Mumal-Rano'' to sing about Mumal-Rano's tale, and ''Sūr Marvi'' to narrate Umar-Marvi's story"

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Shrine visiting by non-Muslims, observed first-hand at Bhit Shah.** "Early in [numeral dropped,
  text-layer], I photographed non-Muslim Kolhi and Bhil peasant women at Shah Abdul Latif's shrine in
  Bhit Shah" — the author "was standing in the shrine courtyard"; the women wore "ghāgrās (long
  skirts)" and "were all clad in flowered veils"; they "walked toward the shrine exit and hastened to
  pickups parked by the roadside in which their menfolk waited to drive them back" (p. 134, folio not
  stated). The book frames this as continuity with Mira Bai: "The pattern of Mira Bai's devotion is
  not unusual, even today" (p. 134, folio not stated). **This is the chunk's only observed shrine
  practice; everything else below is performance practice.**
- **Shrine visiting under concealment, as sung.** The qawwāls' Mira Bai narrative has her travel
  "three hundred miles… For a mere glimpse of her beloved in Ajmer", for twenty years, and "Never did
  she go near his shrine— / For fear that a Maharani she was— / Pearl anklets wore she around her
  feet / The jingle of pearls would create suspicion / And Khaja would say, ''Mira, thou art
  disrespectful''"; "In the twenty-first year Mira was unable to restrain herself / And she cried out
  from the temple steps" (p. 131, folio not stated). The author's commentary: "The performers talk
  about Mira Bai's ritual, three-hundred-mile journeys to Ajmer for twenty years. She had to conceal
  her identity as a Rajput princess who could not be expected to become a devotee of a Muslim Sufi
  saint" (p. 131, folio not stated).
- **Qawwālī performance settings, all three of them outside a shrine.** (1) "a private mehfil in
  England in [numeral dropped, text-layer]", where Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan sang Sohni-Mahival with his
  ensemble "in a mystical frame"; "Intimate interactions were seen between performers and the
  audience, who knew each other closely, and the speech play evolved from that intimacy" (p. 125,
  folio not stated). (2) The Sabri Brothers' Mira Bai qawwālī "in concert before an expatriate
  audience in the United Kingdom" (p. 128, folio not stated, note 28), "in England in [numeral
  dropped, text-layer]" (p. 132, folio not stated). (3) Abida Parvin "in concert at the Allama Iqbal
  Open University in Islamabad", singing Layla-Majnun "before an august audience of government
  bureaucrats" (pp. 134–135, folios not stated).
- **Concert as substitute shrine, stated as a question by the author.** "Can this transformation be
  viewed as a development of transnational Islam? Perhaps there is a blend of the traditional shrine
  context with a modern concert setting, where the singers uphold the sanctity of the environment"
  (p. 132, folio not stated).
- **The ritual close of a qawwālī gathering.** "This is evident from the closing salām (hymn)
  dedicated to the prophet Muhammad, when the qawwāls stand up and fold their hands around their
  waists, as in the prayer ritual, to sing, / Sarkār Medine vāle tum par lākhõ salām / May thou be
  blessed a thousand times, O Muhammad, Lord of Medina" (p. 132, folio not stated).
- **Samā as an institution and as a term.** "Researchers report that from the thirteenth century
  onward, Hindu mystical songs were recited at samā gatherings. This was because many of the talented
  musicians were newly converted Muslims" (p. 132, folio not stated, note 37). Separately the book
  gives the performers' own sense of the word: "These are storytelling devices that create the samā
  (the environment) for their audiences" (p. 131, folio not stated). **Two different senses of samā
  in adjacent pages; both kept.**
- **The patronage economy of the diaspora concert.** "For the qawwāls, it is most appropriate to sing
  of a Rajput princess for their affluent patrons who come from the Asian diaspora of the
  subcontinent. The linguistic codes of Urdu, devotional Hindi, and Rajasthani are suited for their
  listeners, who hail from a variety of linguistic backgrounds" (p. 132, folio not stated).
- **Recording and circulation.** "there is now substantial recorded material of Waris Shah's Hir
  available, both on audiocassettes and videos sung in the folk traditions" (p. 123, folio not
  stated). The Hir narrative "exists in Sindhi, Baluchi, Persian, Urdu, and even in Arabic. It has
  been further translated into English and French" (p. 120, folio not stated).
- **Hir as wedding ritual.** "As she was carried off in a palanquin, she sang her famous lament song,
  which is traditionally sung at almost every Panjabi wedding at the time of the bride's departure to
  her husband's home" (p. 124, folio not stated).
- **The indigenous vocabulary of affect, given as the culture's own metalanguage.** `hūk` — "the pain
  of the heart when it laments in grief" (p. 120, folio not stated); `dohaī` — "to ''cry in agony''";
  `kurlānā` — to "''squirm in pain''"; "In the Siraiki language, these are the extreme degrees of
  physical and emotional pain" (p. 120, folio not stated). `dard` — "the performer's stylistic
  creation of pain is called dard" (p. 124, folio not stated). `divānī` — "the metalanguage of
  divānī, or one who is ecstatic" (p. 129, folio not stated). `karāmāt` — glossed "(spiritual
  powers)" (p. 128, folio not stated). `samā` — glossed "(the environment)" (p. 131, folio not
  stated). `kalām` (p. 124, folio not stated). `salām` — glossed "(hymn)" (p. 132, folio not stated).
- **The criterion of a good Sufi singer.** "The test for a competent singer of Sufi poetry is to be
  able to sing the myth with a passion that moves the listeners to tears" (p. 120, folio not stated);
  "When a performer sings Hir, she uses all the available aesthetic, linguistic, cultural, and
  emotional resources with the result that even the strongest person is unable to hold back tears"
  (p. 124, folio not stated).
- **Pīr–murīd idiom carried by an object.** "the ghaṛā assumes multiple voices: the role of Sohni's
  female friend; the mature murshid who would help the murid, Sohni, cross the stream to the
  spiritual path"; "Sohni drowned because her ghaṛā was kacca and not mature"; "Sohni needed a
  seasoned guide or ghaṛā to help her cross the stream" (p. 127, folio not stated). "In Sufi poetry,
  many narratives are built around the ghaṛā or the ghaṛolī. Abida Parvin explains the ghaṛolī to her
  audience in one of her concerts, suggesting that it is a container that ''receives'' the murshid's
  spiritual bounties" (p. 127, folio not stated).
- **Land, caste and inheritance as the Hir story's economic frame.** "The Jats of the Panjab are
  zamindars (landowners), known for practicing widow-marriage" (p. 121, folio not stated, note 8).
  "The Siyal tribe is more than usually considerate toward its women; it is one of the few tribes in
  the Panjab that allow women to inherit property under customary law" (p. 121, folio not stated,

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The earth opens for Punnu.** "Punnu wept bitterly at her tomb, until the earth opened for him and
  he was buried with his beloved" (p. 118, folio not stated, note 4). Attributed by the book to "the
  myth told in the lands of Sind and Baluchistan" (p. 118, folio not stated).
- **Sassi dies of love.** Singers "sing about Sassi, who eventually dies from unmitigated love"
  (p. 120, folio not stated).
- **Mahival's self-mutilation.** "one night Mahival could not bring the fish for Sohni as he could not
  catch any. Instead, he cut a piece of flesh from his thigh, roasted it, and brought it to her. He
  naturally lost a lot of blood and became very weak" (p. 126, folio not stated).
- **Mahival becomes a faqīr.** He "now grazed other people's cattle but acquired a spirituality
  through which he became a recognized faqīr. Sohni went to the faqīr to seek his counsel, for she
  was still lovelorn. She found that the faqīr was none other than her own Mahival" (p. 126, folio
  not stated).
- **The half-baked pot.** Sohni's parents "removed the baked ghaṛā, and instead put a half-baked one
  in its place, knowing very well that she would drown if she used it… When she was midstream, her
  ghaṛā dissolved and she drowned"; Mahival "jumped into the water to save her, but the lovers were
  washed down with the current. Their bodies were found and they were buried together. The entire
  village mourned their deaths" (p. 126, folio not stated, note 24).
- **Khwaja Muinuddin Chishti's karāmāt, enacted in the song.** "the performers signified his karāmāt
  (spiritual powers) with Mira Bai as his devotee" (p. 128, folio not stated). The song's own
  resolution: "Mīrā Bāī ke dīl se jo niklī sad ā / Un ko ban kar sahārā khud ānā paṛā", glossed "The
  cry came from Mira's very heart / And indeed Khaja had to rescue Mira" (p. 133, folio not stated).
  **The saint is described as descending at the devotee's cry:** "Thou, descend, O Khaja, from thy
  mighty abode / Thou, take me by the arm" (p. 133, folio not stated, note 41).
- **A myth grown up around a devotion, given as such by the author.** "The connection between her and
  Hazrat Khawaja Muinuddin Chishti appears to be the proximity of her birthplace to Ajmer. A myth has
  grown up in the area about her devotion to this Sufi saint" (p. 128, folio not stated). **The book
  presents the Mira–Muinuddin link as local myth, not as history.**
- **Hir's poisoning and Ranjha's suicide.** "While Ranjha was gone, the Siyals poisoned Hir, and he
  returned to find his beloved being buried. He killed himself at her grave" (p. 123, folio not
  stated).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The chapter's governing claim.** "In connection with the argument that there is a strong female
  voice in Sufi ritual, I discuss the Mira Bai myth that the Sabri Brothers sang in concert before an
  expatriate audience in the United Kingdom" (p. 128, folio not stated, note 28).
- **Myth as the vehicle of emotion in performance.** "In this transliteration the Sufi troubadour
  casts emotion into the narrative through the use of myth. Sassi's voice gives the poetry an
  aesthetic dimension that involves the listener in an emotional bond with the speaker. Essentially,
  the poet sings of mystic love through Sassi, who carries that ''consuming fire'' for the beloved
  within her. There is pain and there is sorrow in that love, because it cannot mature without
  suffering" (p. 119, folio not stated).
- **A male singer using a female voice, as the chapter's core evidence.** Pathana Khan "sang in
  Sassi's inflected voice as she laments for Punnu" (p. 118, folio not stated, note 5); Nusrat Fateh
  Ali Khan "sang the following qawwālī in Hir's voice in which Hir informs her mother that she will
  not live with the Kheras" (p. 124, folio not stated, note 17); "The Sabri Brothers sing in this
  woman minstrel's inflected voice" (p. 129, folio not stated); "The Sabri Brothers and their
  ensemble mime Mira's voice thus" (p. 132, folio not stated); Nusrat's qawwāls sang Sohni "assuming
  the ghaṛā's voice; the ghaṛā itself became Sohni's intimate female friend and spoke to her"
  (p. 127, folio not stated, note 25).
- **The invocation of a Sufi poet as framing device.** "The singer invoked the Sufi poet Farid to
  create the mystical frame or context for his audiences" (p. 118, folio not stated, note 6).
- **Waris Shah's Hir as social critique.** "Through her he challenges patriarchy, the clergy, and
  issues of the socioeconomic caste system. Hir has many arguments with the clergy where she uses the
  rhetoric of the Shara… to prove its narrow-mindedness and duplicity" (pp. 120–121, folios not
  stated). "In all these sections Hir's abandonment and her loss not only create the romance of the
  myth but also address patriarchal paradigms of religion, caste, socioeconomic class, and gender, in
  which the weak are exploited" (p. 125, folio not stated).
- **Death as metaphysical triumph.** "For the Sufi poets there is glory in Hir's death, like
  Antigone's in a Greek tragedy. In the Hir narrative the lovers die, but eventually their struggle,
  their suffering and pain are a metaphysical triumph" (p. 125, folio not stated).
- **The doctrinal mechanism the author names.** "Her beloved assumes a spirituality and sublimity that
  transcends the divine. Thus, her female voice transforms the earthly plane to reach a
  transcendental state. This is in keeping with the Sufi principles where iśq-e majāzī (personal
  love) transforms itself into iśq-e haqīqī (divine love). In Sufi poetry Hir's voice thus becomes a
  ''frame'' or a cultural complex to describe esoteric meanings through the aesthetics of poetry and
  music" (p. 125, folio not stated). **Note: "transcends the divine" is what the text prints; it is
  odd against the argument and may be a printing or text-layer fault — see Doubts.**
- **The pot as trope of the spiritual path.** "Her myth, therefore, became a storytelling device or
  trope for the singer to talk of the difficulties on the spiritual path, the iśq-e haqīqī" (p. 127,
  folio not stated). "The fire becomes the element that purifies and transforms baser metals into
  gold. The water is the cleansing element. And so the ghaṛā in Sohni's myth becomes a ''metaphorical
  nature of representation,'' as well as contributing to the aesthetics of the narrative. It becomes
  a medium that transforms the earthly experiences to the spiritual realm by becoming a kind of
  murshid or mentor" (p. 127, folio not stated, note 27).
- **Myth as the discourse of the excluded — the chunk's strongest general claim.** "The Sufi poets
  made the native myths of Hir, Sassi, and Sohni into complex cultural tropes. The discourse so
  created and sung to music articulated the concerns of marginalized social groups such as the Sufis
  themselves, women, minorities, persecuted communities, and the vast majority of the people who were
  excluded from the power structures supported by the clergy. This was one of the reasons for this
  verbal art to be a strong component of popular culture in the Pakistan-India subcontinent" (p. 128,
  folio not stated).
- **A conversion claim, attributed and not endorsed.** "Many individuals in the native cultures assert
  that it was not the conquerors but rather the Sufis who disseminated Islam widely in the
  subcontinent through their oral traditions and the medium in which they spoke to the people"
  (p. 128, folio not stated). **Given as what "many individuals… assert", not as the author's
  finding.**
- **Cross-confessional singing as barrier-breaking.** "The Mira Bai narrative that the Sabri Brothers
  sing in England breaks religious and cultural barriers. It further mirrors the inherent holistic
  nature of Sufi poetry. The myth becomes truly cross-cultural, sung for the diaspora in England"
  (pp. 131–132, folios not stated). "It is interesting that qawwāls in the Islamic mystical
  traditions should sing about a Rajput princess who was a great mystic herself and the creator of
  poetry in the popular culture" (p. 129, folio not stated). "The Sabri Brothers uphold Mira Bai's
  devotion to a Sufi mystic in their concert in England because of shared spiritual and linguistic
  perceptions. The Bhakti poems of Mira Bai had common ideological grounds. She sang about the
  oppression of women, issues of race and caste prejudices, and religious bigotry that were used to
  intimidate the people. Mira's poetry and that of the Sufi mystics showed alternative paths for
  seeking relief" (p. 134, folio not stated).
- **Why the vernacular did the work.** "This was done through simple, aesthetic, lyrical compositions,
  framed in folktales familiar to the people. Spiritual and emotional concerns were transmitted in
  the vernaculars with which the people could identify. All in all, the Sufi approach was a
  nonthreatening way to bring spirituality to ''the folks''" (p. 134, folio not stated).
- **Gendered transmission of a text.** "There does not seem to be an original manuscript of her poems,
  except what has been documented from the oral tradition. Perhaps, as some have suggested, where
  writing has been the prerogative of men, women alone kept Mira's songs alive, passing them across
  the generations from mouth to mouth" (p. 129, folio not stated, note 31). "Mira's songs have been
  transmitted in the oral tradition for five hundred years and are not far removed from India's folk
  songs" (p. 129, folio not stated).
- **The performers' craft of audience capture.** "The qawwāls engage their listeners through the
  exquisite description of Mira Bai's apparel: the fact that she is a Maharani, or a princess,
  mentioned in formulaic form at the very outset, arouses the listeners' interest (lines [numerals
  dropped, text-layer]). The laypeople loved to hear such accounts. Her pearl anklets and their
  jingle as she walked are sensuous images derived from royalty" (p. 131, folio not stated).
- **The author on folk belief versus fact, stated in her own voice.** "I was amused to hear them sing
  of Mira Bai as Rana Sangha's Maharani when in fact she was his daughter-in-law. But, then, the
  discussion is about folk beliefs" (p. 131, folio not stated).
- **Modernization of the form.** "The Mira Bai qawwālī in England in [numeral dropped, text-layer] is
  a modernization of the traditional qawwālī formulas, updated and recreated for the cosmopolitan,
  expatriate Asian audiences in England" (p. 132, folio not stated).
- **Code-switching as technique.** "An interesting feature of the Mira Bai qawwālī is that the singers
  switch codes within indigenous registers, such as Urdu, devotional Hindi, and Rajasthani. Mira
  Bai's ecstacy [text-layer?: ecstasy] is conveyed in a highly melismatic style in devotional Hindi,
  with the singers stretching out Mira's cry" (p. 132, folio not stated).
- **Tom Solomon's musical analysis, attributed to him throughout.** "Tom Solomon, an ethnomusicologist,
  and I undertook a linguistic and musical analysis of the following section" (p. 132, folio not
  stated). "Lines [numerals dropped] to [numerals dropped] were in duple meter and the melody was
  repetitive; Solomon called it additive. Line [numeral dropped] was melismatic—Maqbool Ahmed (C)
  stretched out the vowels as shown in the transliteration. Solomon's explanation was that,
  musically, the lines from [numeral dropped] to [numeral dropped] were in duple meter on percussion.
  The voices of qawwāls B and C were nearly in free meter. According to Solomon, a disjunct
  relationship occurred between the voices and the drums. Solomon identified line [numeral dropped]
  as the beginning of strophe [numeral dropped], which is the longest strophe in the narrative, with
  an abrupt return to triple meter" (p. 134, folio not stated). **Every line and strophe number in
  this passage is lost to the text layer: all (not stated).**
- **Abida Parvin's stated intention at the Islamabad concert.** "She sang the renowned Layla-Majnun
  story to talk about color and gender. Her intention was to entertain and also to create
  sociopolitical awareness through myth about the minority provinces or marginalized groups in the
  country before an august audience of government bureaucrats" (pp. 134–135, folios not stated).
  **Given as the author's reading of the performer's intention.**
