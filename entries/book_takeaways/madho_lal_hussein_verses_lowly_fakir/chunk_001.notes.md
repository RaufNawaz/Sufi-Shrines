# madho_lal_hussein_verses_lowly_fakir — chunk 001 — pp. 1–67

Front of the book: blank leaf (p. 1), half-title/title matter (pp. 2–3), Contents (p. 4), dedication
(p. 5), the whole of the translator's introduction (pp. 6–19), the section-title page `KAFIS`
(p. 20), and **kafis 1–45** (pp. 21–67, one kafi per page, with a printed **Variation** after kafi 2
(pp. 22–23) and after kafi 10 (pp. 31–32)). The introduction is signed and dated at its foot:
"Lahore / August 2015" (p. 19, folio 19).

**Folio check.** A bare number stands alone at the foot of every page of the chunk except the blank
p. 1, and **in every case it equals the PDF page index**: p. 2 = 2, p. 3 = 3 … p. 67 = 67, with no
break anywhere in the chunk. I therefore give `folio = N` throughout, but see Doubts — because the
two numbers coincide, this chunk cannot show whether that number is a print edition's page or an
e-book pagination artifact, and no printed folio distinct from the PDF index is visible anywhere.

**The book's own spellings, used below unchanged.** "Shah Hussein", "Madho Lal Hussein", "Hussein"
(never "Hussain"); "Jehangir"; "Noor Ahmed Chisti"; "Mela Chiraga'an"; "Baghbanpura". The archive
row for this book's saint spells him "Hussain" — I have not normalised either way (see Doubts).

**No Punjabi, Urdu, Persian or Gurmukhi script appears anywhere in the chunk.** Every name, title,
date and technical term is in Roman transliteration or English, including the terms the book itself
flags as Punjabi (`kafi`, `rahau`, `fakir nimana`, `fakir Sain da`, `malamati`, `luddi`, `prem`,
`urs`). The protocol's "quote the original script" rule cannot be satisfied from these pages.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **`madho-lal-hussain`** (the book's forms: "Shah Hussein", "Madho Lal Hussein", "Hussein Dadha",
  and in the verse "Hussein" / "Shah Hussein"): **his dates and his two names, as printed** —
  "The portrait of Shah Hussein (1538–99), popularly known as Madho Lal Hussein" (p. 6, folio 6).
  The fusion of names is stated as fact and read as doctrine: "The fact that Hussein literally fused
  his name with Madho's and came to be known as Madho Lal Hussein is not only testament to the
  enduring legacy of love, but also illustrates an essential tenet of Sufism, the merger of the
  lover and the beloved" (p. 6, folio 6). The translator adds his own purpose: "This work of
  translation uses Hussein's self-given name in the title as homage to the poet's queer passion"
  (p. 6, folio 6).
- **`madho-lal-hussain`**: **the shrine, its place and what is buried in it** — "His urs, or death
  anniversary, is still celebrated at his shrine in Baghbanpura, Lahore. In the inner sanctum of the
  shrine, buried right next to Hussein, is his young beloved and disciple, Madho (pronounced
  Madh-oh) Lal" (p. 6, folio 6). Restated at the end of the introduction with its neighbour named:
  "the modest shrine of Shah Hussein in Baghbanpura near another dilapidated wonder of the Mughal
  era, the Shalimar Gardens" (pp. 18–19, folios 18–19). **No building date, no founder, no
  custodian, no Auqaf, no dimensions and no visitor count: (not stated).**
- **`madho-lal-hussain`**: **the ʿurs, named, dated and numbered** — "The last week of March 2016
  will mark the 428th urs of Shah Hussein, aka Madho Lal Hussein. The celebration is commonly known
  as `Mela Chiraga’an` [`Mela Chiragha'an` — text-layer?], the Festival of Lamps" (p. 19, folio 19).
  The book gives the count "428th" and the window "the last week of March 2016" and nothing else —
  no attendance, no programme, no organiser: **(not stated)**. See Doubts on the arithmetic.
- **`madho-lal-hussain`**: **daily offerings at the two graves** — "The side-by-side graves of
  Hussein and Madho are always heaped with fresh rose and marigold garlands offered by the daily
  throngs of devotees" (p. 19, folio 19). This is the chunk's only statement of shrine practice
  outside the ʿurs, and it names the offerings (rose and marigold garlands) but no cash, no langar
  and no donation box: **(not stated)**.
- **`madho-lal-hussain`**: **his trades and standing, in the book's own list** — "Weaver, mystic,
  fakir, Shah Hussein continues to command great reverence in Punjab as a poet–saint more than 400
  years after his death" (p. 6, folio 6). The translator repeatedly ranks the weaver above the
  saint: "Muhammad Pir and Noor Ahmed Chisti are not concerned with Hussein, the poet, or Hussein,

- **`madho-lal-hussain`**: **his family and origins** — "Hussein's father, a Rajput from the Kalsrai
  clan, converted to Islam during the Delhi Sultanate (1206–1526) and took on the Muslim name of
  Sheikh Usman" (p. 6, folio 6); and on the matriline, via Dara Shikoh's name for him: "He calls him
  Hussein Dadha, using Hussein's matrilineal family name (his mother belonged to the Dadha clan of
  Rajputs)" (p. 8, folio 8). No birthplace and no ancestral village: **(not stated)**.
- **`madho-lal-hussain`**: **his schooling, his two teachers and the two Quranic passages that turned
  him** — "As a boy, Hussein joined the local mosque which also functioned as a madrasa, where
  memorizing the Quran by rote was the main method of learning" (p. 6, folio 6); the itinerant
  dervish "Sheikh Bahlol Dariai, who took him under his wing" and who "had travelled across the wide

- **`madho-lal-hussain`**: **`malamati` is the word the book uses for his path, and it glosses it** —
  "the promising young student turned malamati—one who seeks disgrace and censure—leading his pack

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Sheikh Bahlol Dariai** — Hussein's first teacher, "an itinerant dervish and scholar" who had
  travelled "Arabia, Mesopotamia, Persia and Afghanistan", and who "broadened the parochial horizons
  of his young ward, daring Hussein to fathom the unconventional ways in which traditional texts
  could be interpreted" (pp. 6–7, folios 6–7); he later "returned to talk some sense into Hussein,
  and he received his due reverence despite Hussein's radically altered lifestyle" (p. 7, folio 7).
  No tomb, no dates, no place: (not stated).
- **Sheikh Sa'ad Ullah** — "another respected scholar of the time", Hussein's teacher at thirty-six
  (p. 7, folio 7), and a character in Najm Hosain Syed's play, where "Shah Hussein's character
  appears as a student of Sheikh Sa'ad Ullah and the son of a government contractor" (p. 14, folio
  14). No tomb or dates: (not stated).
- **Sheikh Usman** — Hussein's father, the Kalsrai Rajput convert (p. 6, folio 6).
- **Muhammad Pir (Sheikh Mahmud)** — author of Haqiqat al-Fuqra, "written around 1660", and quoted at

  finally nabbed at the hanging of 'Dullah Bhatti" (p. 15, folio 15).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The ʿurs read as a wedding — the chunk's one general statement of shrine practice** — "The urs of
  a saint is celebrated as a wedding, the union of the bride/mortal with the groom/divine" (p. 11,
  folio 11). The book presents this as the convention behind Hussein's female narrator, not as an
  observation of any particular shrine.
- **Shah Hussein's own ʿurs, named, counted and dated**: "the 428th urs", "the last week of March
  2016", "Mela Chiraga'an, the Festival of Lamps" (p. 19, folio 19). No procession, no lamp ritual
  described, no organiser, no cost: **(not stated)**.
- **Offerings**: "fresh rose and marigold garlands offered by the daily throngs of devotees" at the
  side-by-side graves (p. 19, folio 19).
- **Two Mughal funerary establishments set against each other as an argument about upkeep** — "Visit
  the mausoleums of the Mughal sovereigns, the marvels of architecture, the marble floors, the floral
  mosaics, the ornate domes, the once gem-studded graves. The tomb of Jehangir in Shahdara, Madho's
  hometown, is a vast complex of sprawling lawns imitating the geometry of a Persian paradise.
  Unfortunately, due to lack of proper conservation, the place is in a sorry state of disrepair;
  patchy repair work here and there, dry fountains, littered grounds and barely a trace of tall
  spruces along the cobbled paths treaded by the tourists around this forlorn grandeur" (p. 18, folio
  18) — against "the modest shrine of Shah Hussein in Baghbanpura" with its garlanded graves (pp.
  18–19, folios 18–19). **Tourists are named at Jehangir's tomb, devotees at Hussein's** (pp. 18–19).
- **Shrines as targets of present-day violence** — "No wonder the Sufi shrines, viewed as the
  hashish-clouded dens of singing and dancing, are high on the list of the God-opiated aspirants of
  martyrdom with a penchant for suicide blasts" (pp. 17–18, folios 17–18), attributed to "the
  puritanical Wahabi ideology fuelled by the petrodollars of Gulf sheikhdoms running amok in
  present-day Pakistan" (p. 17, folio 17). No incident, place or date: **(not stated)**.
- **The commercial afterlife of shrine music** — "the appropriation of spirituality by the slick
  Mammon of late capitalism is reflected in the popularity of Sufi music produced and marketed by
  Coke Studio, a Coca-Cola-sponsored entertainment venture. The Studio has provided a forum for
  Pakistani artists to fuse classical, folk and modern rhythms into catchy tunes, all the rage on the
  radio, fashion-show runways and in the music collections of the urban youth" (p. 17, folio 17).
- **The transmitting professions, named and defended** — "ignoramus singers and qawwals who are
  deemed responsible for circulating varying versions that undermine the authenticity of Hussein's
  work", against whom the translator sets "these devotional singers, the sole guarantors of an oral
  tradition", and "the illiterate minstrel caste, derogatorily known as the mirasis, who celebrated
  these kafis as expressions of love, loss and, let's admit it, devotion and reverence" (p. 17, folio
  17).
- **Mosque as madrasa** — "the local mosque which also functioned as a madrasa, where memorizing the
  Quran by rote was the main method of learning" (p. 6, folio 6).
- **Court patronage refused as an economic choice** — "Evidently, he was steering clear of the royal
  patronage and emoluments ensured through the use of court language" (p. 15, folio 15); and the
  vernacular is justified by audience economics — kafis "were easy to understand and memorize, and
  thus served as the ideal means to disseminate the mystical teachings among the illiterate masses
  dependent on the oral transmission of knowledge" (p. 15, folio 15).
- **Mughal agrarian and artisanal extraction, quoted from Moreland** — "Weavers, naked themselves,
  toiled to clothe others. Peasants, themselves hungry, toiled to feed the towns and cities. India
  taken as a unit, parted with the useful commodities in exchange for gold and silver or in other
  words, gave bread for stones… this road was banned effectively by the administrative methods in
  vogue which penalized production of increased consumption as a signal for fresh extortion.14"
  (p. 14, folio 14).
- **A weaving factory and a government contractor, in the play** — "the son of a government
  contractor whose weaving factory has become a hotbed of 'Dullah sympathizers" (p. 14, folio 14).
- **Weaving and spinning as the verse's working economy** — loom, spindle, spool, basket, thread and
  dye run through kafis 1, 2, 14, 26, 30, 39, 40, 41, 42: "My colourful spinning wheel I painted red"
  (p. 22, folio 22); "Bigger the wheel, heavier the basket my parents placed / on my head" (p. 22,
  folio 22); "Pretty spins my wheel, attracts / weavers far and wide" and "The handsome weavers /
  whet my lust, provoke / the complaints of neighbours" (pp. 22–23, folios 22–23); "Learn to weave a
  few yarns otherwise / You'll remain a dowry-less spinster" (p. 36, folio 36); "So busy weaving, not
  a yard I saved" (p. 48, folio 48); "Not a single woven spool in your basket" (p. 52, folio 52);
  "Smashed up the loom, lost the thread / I've kicked away the basket" (p. 61, folio 61); "All went
  home carrying their handlooms / I'm left all alone" (p. 62, folio 62); "The thread's come apart, the
  spindle / lies broken, better worry about the jenny" (p. 63, folio 63); "Go round and round, O
  Handloom / May she live, the one who spins you / May she live, the one who spools you" (p. 64,
  folio 64). The translator's gloss: "the whole business of weaving is allegorical for the
  thread-by-thread disentanglement of the self" (p. 13, folio 13), and "The loom, a common household
  item, often appears in Punjabi folk songs. In Hussein's kafis, it functions almost like a prayer
  wheel" (p. 13, folio 13).
- **Dowry and trousseau as the governing domestic institution** — the translator names the allegory
  outright: Hussein's narrator "worries about dowry (the deeds of mortal life) and the imminent
  departure from the parental home… to join the husband's family, a foreign household of in-laws
  (afterlife)" (p. 11, folio 11); "She is either spinning away her days to prepare for a proper
  trousseau… or avoiding the loom" (p. 12, folio 12). In the verse: "there goes / the one without a
  trousseau" (p. 52, folio 52); "All you seek are the wedding celebrations" (p. 63, folio 63); "Only
  a few days left at the father's house" (p. 36, folio 36); "My girlfriends, my childhood playmates,
  all left, / all settled in strangers' houses" (p. 50, folio 50).
- **Arranged marriage named as the norm** — "the helplessness as well as defiance of the bride in a
  culture where arranged marriage is the norm and the woman's assent is taken for granted" (p. 12,
  folio 12).
- **The wedding dress as a traded object with a stated provenance** — "the red wedding dress"
  carried "across the snows / all the way from Kashmir", "brought from Gujarat / to dispel the
  virgin's fear of the first night", "bought in Multan"; "My fine dress dyed red with rare pigments";
  "Every bride owns a wedding dress" (p. 51, folio 51, kafi 29).
- **Petty credit, peddling and market talk in the verse** — "At your doorstep arrive the travelling
  merchants / You purchase a few trinkets on a loan" (p. 34, folio 34, kafi 12); "The customer's
  leaving, earn something / Don't let him go empty-handed even if / you lose a penny or two in the
  bargain… You ask strangers for loans / Get something out of the friend" (p. 36, folio 36, kafi 14);
  "Those busy turning millions to billions / remain greedy as ever" (p. 25, folio 25, kafi 4); "In
  the bazaar of love, poor monkeys / put to bizarre tricks" (p. 65, folio 65, kafi 43).
- **Burial as the only property** — the translator's summary with kafi numbers: "In the end we all
  settle in dust (kafis 22, 32, 59, 70, 77); the wretched grave, your native land (kafi 124); a few
  feet of burial ground, all the property you need (kafis 48, 74)" (p. 18, folio 18). Two of those
  kafis are in this chunk: "Back into the dust we settle" (p. 44, folio 44, kafi 22) and "eventually
  we'll all settle in dust" (p. 54, folio 54, kafi 32). In kafi 22 the funeral is itemised: "Sweet
  friends sew our shroud / drive us down to the grave-end… On whose shoulders we'd be carried? …
  Limbs tied to a pole later we'll be carried" (p. 44, folio 44).
- **Named dances and festivity** — the luddi: "Come, let's dance the luddi / Grab that string and let
  high your kite" (p. 24, folio 24, kafi 3); the wedding party at the close of kafi 43: "Look! / Here
  comes the wedding party" (p. 65, folio 65); the dervish's cloak against the white robe: "Your white
  robe is fuel for fire / You may have been better off / with a dervish's grimy cloak" (p. 25, folio

- **Women's collective work named as a place to come to** — "Says Hussein, the Sain's fakir, come /
  where the women gather for work" (p. 28, folio 28, kafi 7).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

The introduction attributes these to "traditional sources", "legend" and to the two named
hagiographers, and it distances itself from them in the same breath; I keep its attributions.

- **Breaking chains, and the doubled body before Akbar** — introduced as part of the composite
  portrait "based on traditional sources": he "breaks off the chains when officials approach to
  arrest him, baffles Akbar, the reigning emperor of the time, by appearing in his private chambers
  when he's supposed to be locked up" (p. 6, folio 6). Told again in full and expressly as legend:
  "According to legend, the emperor was unimpressed by the wine-to-non-alcoholic-drinks miracle
  performed by Hussein when he was brought before Akbar for interrogation, and sent him to prison.
  While Hussein was in the dock, his likeness showed up in the harem attended upon by the royal
  ladies. Mystified by the miraculous feat, Akbar acknowledged Hussein's credentials as a saint, and
  set him free.1" (pp. 7–8, folios 7–8).
- **Transformation of wine** — "turns wine into water, sherbet, milk, tea, vinegar and back to wine
  again" (p. 6, folio 6), from traditional sources; called "the wine-to-non-alcoholic-drinks miracle"
  at p. 8 (folio 8).
- **The dance at the Quranic verse** — "The words resonated so deeply with Hussein that he broke into
  a spontaneous dance of self-liberation" (p. 7, folio 7).
- **The laughter in the middle of the prayer** — "In the middle of the prayer, Hussein broke into
  laughter and ran away when he recited these Quranic verses" (p. 7, folio 7).
- **Hearing through walls** — "He persists, and demonstrates his mystical prowess by hearing what
  Madho says within the walls of his house and repeating the words verbatim" (p. 9, folio 9).
- **The two lions** — the spy is "told to go back and verify the truth. He returns and finds two
  terrifying lions sitting face-to-face" (p. 9, folio 9). The scene he had first reported ends
  "and kept kissing each other like milk and sugar . . . and then the `two friends became one.7`
  [block-quote indentation lost across the page break — text-layer?]" (pp. 9–10, folios 9–10).
- **The doubled love at first sight**, in Muhammad Pir's words: "With one look he has made my heart
  restless… Taken the life out of my heart, and the soul out of my body… Friends, I've become a
  prisoner of his love. I shall not find peace till I see him.'6" (p. 9, folio 9).
- **Eluding the police until the hanging** — "Even hagiographers like Muhammad Pir and Chisti admit
  that Hussein kept eluding Akbar's police till he was finally nabbed at the hanging of 'Dullah
  Bhatti, though they make it sound as if he was attending the event as a mere spectator" (p. 15,
  folio 15).

## Arguments and interpretations

The introduction is careful about attribution, and it is worth recording which side of the line each
claim falls on. **Attributed to a named source:** the age gap and the "repulsive" verdict (Dr Syed
Nazir Ahmed, p. 10, folio 10); the dismissal of Madho as "an ordinary boy" (N. Hanif, p. 10, folio
10); the Dara Shikoh attribution and the "Hussein Dadha" name ("According to Muhammad Asif Khan",
p. 8, folio 8); the woman-soul symbol and the female nafs (Schimmel, p. 12, folio 12); the poet's
"concern with the temporal and transient" and the political reading of Hussein (Najm Hosain Syed,
pp. 13–14, folios 13–14); the Mughal economy (Moreland, p. 14, folio 14); the kafi as a sub-genre of
Indian classical music (Muhammad Asif Khan, p. 16, folio 16); the love-at-first-sight and kissing
scenes (Muhammad Pir, pp. 9–10, folios 9–10). **Asserted by the translator in his own voice:** the
censorship speculation, the elite-palatability argument, the Coke Studio and Wahabi arguments, and
the reading of the female voice. The specific claims:

- **The organising image is the translator's own** — "makes him out to be a Punjabi Dionysus" (p. 6,
  folio 6), built by "piecing together the details of his life based on traditional sources" and on
  "the scant biographical sources available" (pp. 6, 7; folios 6, 7).
- **The archival silence is stated as a problem, twice** — "There is no account of Hussein in the
  annals of Akbar, though the Mughals were known for their meticulous record-keeping" (p. 8, folio
  8); "No mention of Hussein is made even in Tuzk-i-Jehangiri,2 Jehangir's official memoir" (p. 8,
  folio 8). The one source that might have filled it, Baharia, "is not extant" and Chisti's claim to
  have used it is called "unverifiable" (p. 8, folio 8).
- **The hagiographers are charged with suppressing the poet and the weaver** — "What's conspicuously
  missing from all the above sources is any reference to Shah Hussein's poetry" (p. 8, folio 8);
  "Both men are unabashedly partial to Islam" (p. 8, folio 8); "Both writers seem bent upon
  justifying the blatantly heterodox lifestyle of Hussein, especially his state of perpetual
  inebriation, as the prescribed norm of a malamati mystic" (p. 9, folio 9).
- **The central hypothesis, put as a question** — "Could it be possible that the saint was much more
  palatable for the established elites, religious and political, than the rabble-rouser poet–weaver?
  Reverence, after all, lends itself to uncritical obeisance, but poetry is a powder keg, especially
  if the unschooled populace starts to quote it against the diktats of the sociopolitical order"
  (p. 9, folio 9).
- **Modern sainthood is said to have foreclosed a question** — "For many a modern historian, the
  sainthood of Hussein has precluded the possibilities of exploring the nature of his relationship
  with Madho. The sexuality is either ignored, or downplayed, or dismissed outrightly" (p. 10, folio
  10); and the scholars' recoil is then read as proof the poet succeeded — "The scholars' reaction is
  quite indicative of the success of Hussein's avowed intentions: tear up the ledger of saintly
  business (kafi 99)—achieve self-degradation, especially in the eyes of the learned and the
  powerful" (p. 10, folio 10).
- **A censorship hypothesis, explicitly flagged as speculation, with its evidence stated** — "There's
  no overt homosexuality in Hussein's work… Considering the speedy posthumous canonization of Hussein
  and the prevalence of the oral tradition, one has reason to speculate that some sort of character
  sanitization was at work. The likelihood of kafis with the 'objectionable' content being censored or
  suppressed cannot be ruled out." The evidence offered: "no one denies Hussein's preference for the
  forbidden alcohol, yet there is no mention of wine, a common symbol of divine ecstasy in Persian
  mystical poetry, in any of the kafis. Kafi 147 with details of bhang-making stands out as the only
  outré kafi when it comes to intoxicants" (pp. 10–11, folios 10–11).
- **The female narrator is argued for on three grounds** — malamati poignancy ("The feminization lends
  poignancy to the suffering, and explains why the narrator of the kafis is invariably female", p. 11,
  folio 11); the ʿurs-as-wedding convention (p. 11, folio 11); and tonal range ("the choice of
  assuming the female persona allows the poet to manipulate the tonal varieties of despair, a common
  device in the devotional poetry of South Asia", p. 11, folio 11). Schimmel's nafs scheme is then
  laid over Hussein's refrain: "Death before dying, a common refrain in Hussein's kafis, could be
  seen as the struggle between the higher and lower souls" (p. 12, folio 12), with the spinner
  aspiring to "`nafs mutma ‘inna` [`nafs mutmaʾinna` — text-layer?]" and the idler at "nafs ammara"
  (pp. 12–13, folios 12–13).
- **The weaver identity is said to break the standard Sufi reading** — "Hussein's assertive
  self-identification as a weaver complicates the run-of-the-mill Sufi interpretation of his work"
  (p. 13, folio 13), which is what licenses the turn to Moreland and to 'Dullah Bhatti, ending in a
  question: "As a historical figure, could Hussein, as Syed nudges us to think, be playing the true
  role of a fakir—the malcontent, the rebel, the subversive, the saboteur?" (p. 14, folio 14).
- **Anti-intellectualism explains the choice of language** — "Most Punjabi Sufi poets were highly
  learned men, but they looked askance at scholars. Scholarly learning generally qualified one for
  self-enhancement rather than the Sufi goal of self-negation" (p. 15, folio 15), evidenced by the
  Sultan Bahu, Bulleh Shah and kafi 11 quotations; hence "This streak of anti-intellectualism
  explains why Hussein chose Punjabi or the vernacular over Persian despite his erudition."
  The irony is noted against itself: "Ironically, his earliest full-length biography, Haqiqat
  al-Fuqra, is composed in Persian verse" (p. 15, folio 15).
- **The kafi is defined as form, performance and relation** — origins "traced to various sources: the
  Sanskrit words kav (poetry) and kama (sensuality), and the Arabic words kamil (perfection) and kafa
  (grouping)", with the hedge "It's quite possible that the word may be rooted in the pre-Islamic
  literary tradition but later Islamized" (p. 16, folio 16); the refrain "called rahau in Punjabi";
  "There is no consistent rhyming scheme… there could be a pattern of `a bb a bb a or aba aca ada`
  [rhyme-scheme notation, italics and spacing lost — text-layer?]" (p. 16, folio 16); and the
  relation — "A kafi is meant to be a conversation between the disciple (murid) and the master
  (murshid)… the narrator of a kafi deliberately blurs the distinction between divine (haqiqi) and
  temporal (mijazi) love to express the anguish of separation (faraq) and yearning for union or
  oneness (wahdat)… The erotic serves as the predicate for the numinous and vice versa" (p. 16,
  folio 16).
- **The singers are defended against the Urdu-tradition scholars** — "Modern Pakistani scholars,
  especially working in the Urdu tradition, lament the adulteration of kafis by ignoramus singers and
  qawwals… These critics and scholars overlook the contribution of these devotional singers, the sole
  guarantors of an oral tradition who helped Hussein's poetry survive the Mughal, British and
  postcolonial vicissitudes" (p. 17, folio 17).
- **The translator's own method, stated and qualified** — "what I have attempted to (re)produce is not
  a literal translation, but the poetry of Shah Hussein"; "What I found in Hussein's work was not the
  moral piety of a saint confined to a particular religious tradition, but the defiant devotion of a
  sixteenth-century poet upholding the nonconformist values of spirituality"; and the admission of
  risk, prompted by a line of the text itself — "A single wrong weave may not go well with the Master
  (kafi 136)… Did the poet in me go too far, overplaying the music over the message?… forgive me,
  Murshid" (p. 18, folio 18).

- **Kafi numbering runs 1–45 in this chunk and the Variations are unnumbered.** Kafi 2 (pp. 22–23,
  folios 22–23) and kafi 10 (pp. 31–32, folios 31–32) are each followed by a block headed
  **Variation** which carries no separate number, so "kafi 2" may mean either of two texts. Both
  Variations differ substantially from their base text, not trivially — kafi 2's Variation adds
  "Happily I reached my pubescence" and the parents' reproach, and kafi 10's Variation adds the brass
  anklets and the mirages. The book does not say where the Variations come from; given its own
  account of "the differences between the various versions of kafis, ranging from minor to major"
  (p. 17, folio 17), an entry citing a kafi from this book should cite the page, not the kafi number.
- **The kafi numbers cited in the introduction mostly point outside this chunk**, and the numbers a
  future consolidator will want are not here: kafis 48, 59, 63, 70, 74, 76, 77, 99, 101, 102, 106,
  124, 126, 136, 138 and 147 are all referenced in the introduction (pp. 10–18, folios 10–18) but
  fall in later chunks. In particular **kafi 147, the bhang kafi** (p. 11, folio 11), and **kafi 102,
  the second "Lal" kafi** (p. 10, folio 10), are the two the introduction's arguments turn on and
  neither is in pp. 1–67.
- **No page of this chunk names a custodian, a sajjada nashin, the Auqaf department, an endowment, a
  landholding, a revenue figure, a visitor count or a dispute for any shrine.** For the row
  `madho-lal-hussain` all of that is **(not stated)** in pp. 1–67, and the absence is worth recording
  so the consolidator does not go looking twice.
