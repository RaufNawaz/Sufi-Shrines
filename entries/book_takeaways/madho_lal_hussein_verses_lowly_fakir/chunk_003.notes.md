## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Bhang at Sufi shrines, tied explicitly to the ʿurs season — the single most directly usable
  institutional fact in the chunk**: "Bhang is a cheap intoxicant made with crushed marijuana leaves
  and milk. It's still readily available at many of the Sufi shrines, especially during the festive
  anniversaries. Bhangi, a bhang drinker, is also a term used for sweepers or janitors." (p. 183,
  folio 183). The book does not name which shrines: **(not stated)**.
- **Sangat as the setting in which the poetry is construed**: "During a Sangat session, Najm Hosain
  Syed offered the following meanings for the Punjabi word an't: end, result, goal, agenda, target.
  The translator's choice of 'reaping' tries to preserve the poetic integrity by drawing a link with
  the agrarian imagery of the second line, the blooming mustard fields." (p. 182, folio 182).
- **The loom, described part by part as a devotional machine**: "In this line, there's a reference to
  the specific part of the loom called bai'r, a thin rope that joins the spokes of the double wheels.
  It's covered with a sturdier rope and aids the circular motion making a to'n (means you in Punjabi)
  sound. The loom as a whole makes the sound of sain sain. The whole contraption of the loom is
  referred to as a device that constantly pays homage to the Master (You are the Sain, you are the
  Sain), a prayer wheel of sorts." (p. 182, folio 182). With "tana bana (warp and weft)" (p. 179,
  folio 179) and "khuddi" as the weaver's foot-space (p. 180, folio 180).
- **Marriage custom, named in Punjabi**: "As a patriarchal norm, a bride leaves the parents' house
  (paykay) to join the husband's family (susral). In this line, there's a reference to the particular
  custom (muklawa) of the bride returning to her susral after a short sojourn at her paykay. Literal
  translation: 'Today or tomorrow is muklawa.'" (p. 183, folio 183); "The in-laws, the muklawa givers.
  For muklawa, see kafi 55." (p. 184, folio 184); cross-referenced again at "See the notes for kafis
  55 and 80." (p. 186, folio 186). Marriage as social-equality management: the Khera marriage

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Hussein's wish to Akbar, attributed to a named hagiography**: "According to Muhammad Pir's Haqiqat
  al-Fuqra, when Emperor Akbar asked Hussein for any wish to be granted, Hussein requested he never be
  summoned to the royal court again." (p. 181, folio 181). The note frames it as evidence of a
  "note of anti-establishment".
- **Hussein's blessing of a military victory, attributed to unnamed hagiographers and then
  contradicted by the poem**: "According to some hagiographers, Hussein gave the blessing of a
  military victory to Khan-e-Khana when he came as a supplicant to the saint; however, this kafi
  points out something different: a caveat of mortality to the powerful." (p. 184, folio 184).
- **Hussein's journey to Amritsar to enter the Granth Sahib, attributed to "some sources" and marked
  unverifiable**: "Though the account is unverifiable, the thematic content of this kafi might have
  lent itself to the speculation." (p. 186, folio 186).
- **Mansoor al-Hallaj's cry and execution**, reported as history rather than miracle (p. 182,
  folio 182).
- **The two recording angels**, attributed to the community: "According to Muslims, two angels, Munkar
  and Nakeer, sit on the right and left shoulder respectively of every human, keeping account of the
  sins and good deeds. On the Day of Judgment, the fate of every individual will be decided on the
  written account presented by the duo." (p. 186, folio 186).
- **The angel of death**, glossed in one line: "The Islamic angel of death." (p. 181, folio 181).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The translator refuses "lord" for *Sain*, and gives a colonial reason**: "'Don't translate Sain
  [pronounced Sai'n] into "lord",' Najm Hosain Syed advised me. He didn't know how the plain-spoken
  kafis reminded me of the earthy American blues and gospel music… But I couldn't ignore what he was
  pointing at: the reality of the Indo-Pak subcontinent where the dark native often resorted to the
  most menial and unctuous terms to ingratiate himself with the white colonizer, the lord and master.
  Lord fails to convey what Sain evokes: the guardian, saviour, watcher, protector, the companion who
  comes to the succour of the despairing." (p. 180, folio 180).
- **The "variations" are declared interpretive, not translations**: "Variations, looser translations
  that accompany some of the kafis, are exercises in interpretive exploration. This particular
  variation attempts to bridge the sensual and spiritual elements within the folkloric theme of

## Doubts

- **Guru Arjun Dev is the principal figure of three archive rows, so I have not mapped him.** The
  candidates are `gurdwara-dera-sahib` (Guru Arjan Dev, Lahore, d. 1606),
  `gurdwara-baoli-sahib-guru-arjan-dev-ji-lahore` (Guru Arjan Dev, Dabbi Bazaar, Lahore) and
  `gurdwara-panjvi-chati-patshahi` (Guru Arjan Dev; Guru Hargobind, Nankana Sahib). The chunk gives
  only "Guru Arjun Dev (1563–1605)" with Amritsar and the Granth Sahib (p. 186, folio 186) — no
  gurdwara, no Lahore, nothing that picks one row. The Amritsar kafi bullet is therefore parked under
  "Other saints, sites and events" and a human must place it. Two further points for that human:
  the book spells him **"Guru Arjun Dev"**, not "Arjan"; and the death year it prints, **1605**,
  differs from the archive row's **1606**, which I have not reconciled.
- **Sultan Bahu and Bulleh Shah are here on citation evidence only.** Under the 18 September ruling I
  have given them `garh-maharaja-shorkot` and `mazar-of-bulleh-shah`, but the whole of the evidence is
  a bibliographic entry in each case (notes 16 and 17, p. 179, folio 179) plus one mention of a Bahu
  translation project (p. 182, folio 182). Neither man is discussed, dated, located or connected to a
  shrine anywhere in the chunk. A consolidator should weigh these as reading-list facts, not
  biographical ones.
- **Deities named in the notes have been left unmapped, deliberately and reversibly.** The 18
  September ruling covers a *person* who is a row's principal figure; it does not address a deity, and
  I did not extend it. If it is extended: **Ram** (p. 181, folio 181 — "he uses Ram quite often (see
  kafis 68, 80, 81, 89)"; also p. 186, folio 186) would take `ram-mandir-saidpur-ram-kund-mandir`;
  **Shiva**, printed as "Har, Shiva" (p. 181, folio 181) and again in the list of Shiva's names
  (p. 180, folio 180), is in any case a many-row name (`amb-temples-amb-sharif`, `bhagnari-mandir`,
  `katas-raj-temples`, `shahwala-teja-singh-mandir`, `shree-ratneshwar-mahadev-temple-karachi`,
  `shiv-mandir-chiti-ghati`, `umarkot-amarkot-shiv-mandir`) and would have to come back to Doubts;
  **Krishna** (p. 182, folio 182) is a two-row name (`krishna-mandir-kabari-bazar`,
  `krishna-mandir-ravi-road`); **Kali**, on the note's own hedge "Kal—a possible reference to Kali"
  (p. 184, folio 184), is a three-row name (`kalat-kali-temple`, `kali-bari-mandir`,
  `kalka-cave-temple-asthan-of-kalka-devi`) and the identification is the book's own guess, not a
  statement.
- **`[text-layer?]` damage inventory — four flags introduced, all marked inline above.**

  (4) **Italics are lost throughout**, which matters at note 152.3 (p. 187, folio 187), where the note
