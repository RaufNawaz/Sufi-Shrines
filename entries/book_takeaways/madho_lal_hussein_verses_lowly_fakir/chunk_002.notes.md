# madho_lal_hussein_verses_lowly_fakir — chunk 002 — pp. 68–178

Continuous text of the numbered kafis in Naveed Alam's English translation, **kafis 46–152**, opening
at kafi 46 (p. 68, folio 68) and running to kafi 152 (p. 177, folio 177), followed by the first page
of the book's apparatus: **"Notes and References"**, section **"I. The Disgraceful Saint: An
Introduction"**, notes 1–13 (p. 178, folio 178). Five kafis carry a second, separately headed
**"Variation"** (pp. 94–95, 152, 162, 169, 172); two kafis are numbered with a letter — **"95 (A)"**
(p. 119, folio 119) and **"114 (A)"** (p. 139, folio 139).

**Folio check.** The printed number stands alone at the foot of every page and equals the PDF index
exactly — **folio = PDF page, with no offset**, unbroken across all 111 pages (p. 68 = 68 … p. 178 =
178). I checked it page by page because this book also numbers its kafis, and the kafi number stands
at the **head** of the page while the folio stands at the **foot**. The two never take the same value
on the same page here (the offset between them runs 22, then 23 after the Variation spills onto
p. 95, then 24 after "95 (A)", then 25 after "114 (A)"), but the *values* do overlap between
sections — see Doubts for the pages a reader could misread.

**No Punjabi, no Perso-Arabic script, no Gurmukhi and no transliterated original appears anywhere in
pp. 68–178.** English only, on every page. The protocol's "quote the original script" rule therefore
cannot be satisfied from this chunk; every name below is available in the translator's English
spelling alone, quoted exactly as printed.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **`madho-lal-hussain`** (the chunk's forms: "Hussein", "Shah Hussein", "O Hussein, the weaver!";
  never "Madho", never "Madho Lal", never "Shah Hussain" with that spelling): **the signature is the
  closing device of nearly every kafi, and the epithet attached to it is the book's running
  self-description of the saint.** The printed epithets, each quoted as it stands: "Says Hussein, the
  Master's fakir" (p. 69, folio 69; also p. 70, folio 70); "Says Hussein, the Sain's fakir" (p. 76,
  folio 76, and on some thirty further pages); "Says Hussein, the wretched fakir" (p. 82, folio 82);
  "Says Hussein, the humble fakir" (p. 74, folio 74); "Says Hussein, the lowly fakir" (p. 92, folio
  92 — the phrase the volume is titled from); "Says Hussein, the miserable mendicant" (p. 73, folio
  73); "Says Hussein, the God's fakir" (p. 105, folio 105); "Says Hussein, God's fakir" (p. 165,
  folio 165); "Says Hussein, the wandering fakir" (p. 104, folio 104); "Says Hussein, the beggar
  fakir" (p. 133, folio 133); "Says Hussein, the helpless fakir" (p. 174, folio 174); "Says Hussein,
  the Sain's devotee" (p. 96, folio 96); "Says Hussein, the devotee of the Sain" (p. 108, folio 108);
  "Says Hussein, the host of senses" (p. 102, folio 102); "Shah Hussein, the poor fakir" (p. 84,
  folio 84).
- **`madho-lal-hussain`**: **he refuses the title of master in his own voice** — "One called Shah
  Hussein is a fakir, / don't call him a master / I'm not fond of falsehood" (p. 134, folio 134, kafi
  110, the closing verse).
- **`madho-lal-hussain`**: **the trade the book gives him is weaving, and it is attached to his name
  directly** — "O Hussein, the weaver! He earned no profit" (p. 176, folio 176, kafi 151). The same
  kafi is the chunk's only run of biographical negatives: "Never got engaged, never got married /
  Never fixed a date, never held a wedding / Neither a householder nor a traveller / Neither a man of
  faith nor an infidel / He is what he is" (p. 176, folio 176). No date, no place and no order
  accompanies any of it: **(not stated)**.
- **`madho-lal-hussain`**: **the only property named in the chunk as his** — "Blackbuck, you grazed /
  the borders of Shah Hussein's fields" (p. 71, folio 71, kafi 49). No location, extent, grant or
  revenue: **(not stated)**.
- **`madho-lal-hussain`**: **the incantation is credited to a named teacher, "Shah Jalal"** — "I
  found, I found, I found my beloved / He's settled in heaven, earth, water, air / Each thing
  encompassed, he said, I was the hidden treasure / Says Hussein, this the incantation taught by Shah
  Jalal" (p. 177, folio 177, kafi 152 — the last kafi in the chunk). This is the **only** named human
  teacher, pir or guide anywhere in pp. 68–177. The book gives no city, date, order or tomb for him,
  and does not gloss the name: all **(not stated)**. See Doubts — "Shah Jalal" bare matches two
  archive rows and is therefore not mapped.
- **`madho-lal-hussain`**: **the beloved is twice addressed or sought as "Lal"** — "Forest and
  wilderness she searches / Haven't found Lal yet" (p. 126, folio 126, kafi 102); "Lal, I am inside
  out, that's my business, / that's my occupation" (p. 138, folio 138, kafi 114). The chunk nowhere
  says who "Lal" is, and nowhere prints "Madho": see Doubts.
- **`madho-lal-hussain`**: **the speaking voice is a woman's, and specifically a working weaver
  girl's, throughout the sequence** — "This miserable one is caught in cotton" (p. 68, folio 68); "A
  weaver woman tied to the loom / I worked my spindles despite the detractors" (p. 107, folio 107);
  "I'm the lowly cleaner at the esteemed house" (p. 111, folio 111); "Shah Hussein, I'm the
  dowry-less" (p. 136, folio 136); "Do turn around sometime, take a look at this wretched girl"
  (p. 173, folio 173).
- **`madho-lal-hussain`**: **he speaks in the Heer–Ranjha idiom and is identified with Heer's
  position, then with Ranjha's** — "I must go to Ranjha's village" (p. 94, folio 94, kafi 72); "I'm
  Ranjha's, Ranjha's mine / The Kheras can wait in vain" (p. 96, folio 96, kafi 73); "Ranjha's gypsy,
  I'm the gypsy woman / known for her crazy devotion" (p. 114, folio 114); and the reversal — "I
  cried out my lover's name so often / I turned into Ranjha myself / Call me Ranjha, no one call me
  Heer / … Says Hussein, the company of dervishes allayed all doubts" (p. 152, folio 152, kafi 127).
- **`madho-lal-hussain`**: **the fakir's dress and status are ranked above the scholar's and the
  ascetic's, repeatedly and in his own signature lines** — "Better the tattered cloak of fakirs"
  against "Your pious white robe was hurled into fire" (p. 132, folio 132, kafi 108); "Fortunate are
  the ones / fated to be fakir wanderers" (p. 88, folio 88); "Why would I be called a learned
  scholar? / Beg for bread, the kind of work I'll do" (p. 138, folio 138); "Passion is the fakir's
  walking stick, a strange fact / beyond intelligence" (p. 138, folio 138).
- **`madho-lal-hussain`**: **the translator's own title for his introduction, and therefore his own
  characterisation of the saint, is printed in the apparatus** — the Notes are headed "I. The
  Disgraceful Saint: An Introduction" (p. 178, folio 178). The introduction text itself is not in
  this chunk.
- **`madho-lal-hussain`**: **the sources the translator used for that introduction are listed, and
  two of them are editions of the saint's own kafis** — "Shah Hussein, *Kafis: Shah Hussein*, ed.
  Muhammad Asif Khan (Lahore: Pakistan Punjabi Adabi Board, 2014; 6th edn), pp. 19–20" (note 3,
  p. 178, folio 178) and "Shah Hussein, *Poetry of Shah Hussein*, ed. Syed Nazir Ahmed (Lahore: Babar
  Ali Foundation, 2014; 3rd edn), p. 3" (note 9, p. 178, folio 178).

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

  145); "Again and again I ask only for Ranjha / to pull Heer out of her misery" (p. 158, folio 158).

- **Ram, Rama and Har** — invoked as the name to be recited: "Praise Ram, I pranced all the way to

  "You lose if you don't sing of Ram" (p. 104, folio 104); "Come, girl, let's praise Ram" (p. 112,

- **Izrael** — "Izrael, the angel, taking away the poor thing" (p. 149, folio 149). Named once; the

- **"The Writer of Fate"** — "The Writer of Fate ordained / Now what's the use of crying" (p. 71,
  folio 71).
- **Pathanay Khan** — named as the singer whose performance produced a translator's variant: the

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **No ʿurs, no mela, no langar, no offering, no custodian, no sajjada nashin, no Auqaf, no
  endowment, no land grant, no revenue figure, no visitor number, no dispute, no state involvement,
  no building and no building date appears anywhere in pp. 68–178.** This is an explicit negative and
  worth recording as one: 110 pages of verse plus one page of bibliography contain none of the
  archive's institutional vocabulary. The word "shrine" occurs once in the verse, in the next bullet.
- **The one shrine reference, and it is a dance at one** — "At the shrine she's the bride who stood
  up / for an unrestrained dance" (p. 132, folio 132, kafi 108), in the same kafi as "Better the
  tattered cloak of fakirs". The shrine is not named and no location is given: **(not stated)**. The
  only related phrase is "Even prophets, saints, holy men / lie entombed and enshrined" (p. 84, folio
  84).
- **Two named dances** — "Come, girl, let's dance the jhumar" (p. 112, folio 112) and "Together we
  shall dance the jhurmat" `[text-layer?]` [reading: "jhurmat" as printed; cf. "jhumar" at p. 112]
  (p. 138, folio 138). Also "she's the bride who dances / despite the muddy feet" (p. 125, folio
  125).
- **Alms and charity at holy men's doors, described as transactional — the chunk's sharpest
  institutional passage** — "It's a selfishly motivated world / That's the truth, O Fakir / Motives
  come, motives go / Motives draw the crowds / to the doorsteps of holy men / Motives make the
  generous / feed the hungry, clothe the naked / No one offers a glass of water / without an ulterior
  motive / Says Hussein, he's the shah / of shahs, the selfless one / who acts free of all motives"
  (p. 81, folio 81, kafi 59, entire).
- **"The ledger of saintly business"** — "Says Hussein, the lowly fakir, tear up / the ledger of
  saintly business" (p. 123, folio 123, kafi 99). Paired with the refusal of the title of master
  (p. 134, folio 134).
- **Forced or unpaid labour, the officers who record it, and exemption from it** — "I'm the lowly
  cleaner at the esteemed house / Attentively I sieve, meditatively I sweep / I dust away the lust,
  envy, anger / The judge knows, the ruler knows / free from the jobs I work voluntarily / The
  sheriff knows, the registrar knows / I serve no one except my Master" (p. 111, folio 111, kafi 88,
  entire). Also "Help me meet my lover, folks, and I'll be your unpaid servant" (p. 129, folio 129)
  and "I'll be the servant of truth seekers, / do the scullion jobs: cook, clean, / wash the dirty
  dishes" (p. 87, folio 87).
- **A named wage or coin, and the household budget it fails to cover** — "For the sake of warming the
  stove / I sweat for the five measly kaseeras / How can I pay for the expenses?" (p. 78, folio 78,
  kafi 56). The same kafi has "O God, I hid my weaver's tools", "With rings on my finger / how can I
  do any labour?", "With my red velvet shoes / how can I sit still and weave?", and closes "the good
  weaving girl got / kidnapped by the robbers".
- **Weaving and spinning as the governing economy of the sequence, described with its tools and
  grades of cloth** — "Not a single thread can I string … The ox sniffs / the balls of wool I brought
  to spin / The other girls disentangle and spool" (p. 68, folio 68); "The mistakes I make spinning /
  I blame on the faulty loom" (p. 71, folio 71); "you carry / your five sickly spindles" (p. 82,
  folio 82); "What would they know of plain, twill, satin? / Only the tool tells a thousand
  entanglements / I know the warp, know the weft, know the old loom" (p. 107, folio 107); "No wool
  you sheared, carded, or combed / What will you spin without a thread? / No loom you rolled, no yarn
  you spun / How do you expect the warp and weft?" (p. 136, folio 136); "Good that the handloom
  broke, freeing my life / from drudgery" (p. 146, folio 146); "The warp of wisdom, the weft of
  devotion / weave into every pelt" (p. 104, folio 104); "The crow leaps at the single spindle I
  worked all day" (p. 146, folio 146); "The spindle got stuck, the thread broke / Half-heartedly I
  spun the yarn / The crow took the rest" (p. 144, folio 144). The oil press and the waterwheel are
  the other two named machines: "The premonition of the oil press / makes the sesame seeds cry oily
  tears at birth" (p. 110, folio 110); "Don't waste your time turning the waterwheel at dusk"
  (p. 154, folio 154).
- **Market and credit vocabulary** — "You stand / in the market haggling with the merchants / Why
  shed tears after losing the bargain?" (p. 82, folio 82); "it's all / a merchandise of lies" (p. 76,
  folio 76); "the rich / collect their loans, nobles run the estates" (p. 134, folio 134); "You
  wasted life conning and collecting / No millionaire or billionaire ever escaped / death's angel"
  (p. 83, folio 83); "If the lover comes / I'll distribute sweets worth millions" (p. 117, folio
  117); "Before the Master asks for the record, do something" (p. 165, folio 165); "How will you
  answer when the lover takes account?" (p. 155, folio 155); "You'll cry blood while giving the
  account" (p. 135, folio 135).
- **Marriage, dowry and the transfer to the in-laws — the chunk's densest ritual material, used
  throughout as the figure for death** — "today or tomorrow you return to the in-laws … To the
  in-laws you must go, your parents' home an illusion" (p. 77, folio 77); "The girls get together,
  fix / trousseaus for their weddings / The lovely me remained a spinster" (p. 72, folio 72); "Why
  should I work on preparing my dowry?" is not in this chunk, but "I won't ask the parents for dowry"
  is (p. 158, folio 158); "They forced my marriage, dragged me / in circles around the wedding fire /
  I cried and screamed, they tied / my knot to the cruel Khera" (p. 158, folio 158); "Alone / I occupy
  the wedding stage" (p. 90, folio 90); "the muklaroos will be here to fetch the newly-wed" (p. 103,
  folio 103) and "Muklaroo seated at the doorstep" (p. 112, folio 112); "The parents arranged my
  marriage, / didn't give much time to prepare / The in-laws here, I go without a trousseau … I'm
  about to be raised / upon the pallet for the bridal bath / No festivities later, this our only
  chance / Mother sobs inconsolably, sister stands weeping" (p. 149, folio 149, kafi 124); "Four men
  lifted my bridal palanquin / dropped me at the groom's house" (p. 144, folio 144) and "Four men
  lifted the bridal palanquin / Mother-in-law, sisters-in-law mocked: / here comes the dowry-less"
  (p. 164, folio 164); "The in-laws mock me for being a brazen bride" (p. 90, folio 90); "Look, there
  stands your wedding party" (p. 156, folio 156).
- **Burial and cremation named side by side, without preference** — "One cremated, the other buried"
  (p. 127, folio 127); "Golden castles, silver jewels / end up at the cremation ground" (p. 97, folio
  97); "The perfumed princes, nobles, kings: / fuel for the sandalwood-scented pyres … barefoot
  they'll be carried upon the biers" (p. 100, folio 100). The grave is measured three times, and the
  measure differs: "A few feet of burial ground / is all the property you need" (p. 70, folio 70); "A
  few feet / for the grave is all you need / Why seek a greater entitlement?" (p. 97, folio 97);
  "Three and a half feet is the man's property / The wretched grave, the home" (p. 165, folio 165).
  Also "Your bones pressed six feet under" (p. 110, folio 110) and "remember the wretched grave, your
  native land" (p. 149, folio 149).
- **The monsoon swings and the rain festival** — "Monsoon arrives, girlfriends set up / the swings,
  celebrate the rain festival" (p. 73, folio 73, kafi 51); "My girlfriends sway upon the swings /
  hanging down the tall pipal trees" (p. 112, folio 112). The pipal tree also as a source of gathered
  leaves: "I collect the leaves of pipal trees / and people think I'm crazy" (p. 87, folio 87).
- **Canonical prayer and its abandonment** — "Don't ever forget God, O Woman, / that's the prayer of
  mendicants" (p. 69, folio 69); "You fall in love, you discard proprieties, / you forget the five
  times prayers" (p. 116, folio 116); "The water buffalo is milked indoors, keep your devotion
  private" (p. 151, folio 151).
- **Ascetic techniques listed and then refused as insufficient** — "One sits ash-smeared, gazing
  fixedly / The other roams the wild barefoot / Their hearts devoid of sincere longing / One stays up
  all night, praying / The other walks on coals, starves to death / Their place is not among the
  chosen / One reads the words of the Quran / The other engages in oral exegesis / It's not a matter
  of laughter and pride / Go to the door of the Perfect One / Beg the blessing for the beloved"
  (p. 133, folio 133, kafi 109). Also "the sadhu prides in nudity" (p. 153, folio 153) and "Give me
  focus, awareness, a sadhu's company" (p. 172, folio 172).
- **Intoxicants asked for as a fakir's equipment, in a whole kafi and its Variation** — "I ask for an
  earthen pot, a wooden stick, / a store of marijuana / Give me a sieve cloth, red pepper, / Don't
  make me plead for colour / Give me opium, a bowl, a basin full of sugar" and, in the Variation,
  "Give me the paraphernalia / for bhang-making: a mortar 'n' pestle, / a sieve cloth, red pepper,
  plenty of sugar / Give my intoxicant the perfect colour and strength / Give me the companionship of
  a fellow outcast / Give me the ingredients of a fakir's contentment" (p. 172, folio 172, kafi 147,
  signed "Shah Hussein, the Sain's fakir, this / a mendicant's prayer"). Also "Like an addict's opium
  / he's settled in my bones" (p. 160, folio 160) and "you are tipsy / on quite a few litres worth of
  swoon" (p. 131, folio 131).
- **Status and caste vocabulary, used to level rather than to rank** — "Judgement based on deeds /
  whether a pious Sufi or a lowly bhangi" (p. 80, folio 80, kafi 58, opening); "Who'd care for the
  family or caste? / We set out in search of the lover, folks" (p. 130, folio 130); "Setting aside
  the family and customs I go to my beloved" (p. 157, folio 157); "Here a king, there a pauper, here
  an ascetic, there a thief … cattle are better than us" (p. 127, folio 127).
- **Officials and learned men named as a class, repeatedly and dismissively** — "Where the mullah,
  the magistrate, / where the mighty armies of the past?" (p. 76, folio 76); "The mullah and scholar
  give advice / The wise and truthful show the path / Not straight and sorted are the ways of
  passion" (p. 140, folio 140); "Hundreds of mullahs, hundreds of wise men, / hundreds of scholars
  tried in vain" (p. 119, folio 119); "Why call a doctor or a wise man?" (p. 167, folio 167);
  "consulted the wise healers" and, in the Variation, "Consulted / the medicine men, bought the
  prescription" (p. 162, folio 162); "I ran into the night watchman" (p. 144, folio 144).
- **The ferry and the boatman as a paid service** — "The river's deep, the raft decrepit / Only God
  can get me across" (p. 73, folio 73); "I quarrel with the boatman / He's right, I'm wrong" (p. 120,
  folio 120); "I plead before the boatman" (p. 140, folio 140); "To the mouth of five streams I went.
  Why blame the boatman?" and, in the Variation, "Why blame the poor boatman / merely following the
  course / ordained for wanderers?" (p. 162, folio 162); "The flood lost its ferocity, the boatman
  rows away" (p. 175, folio 175); "I'll give / my fingers' precious rings if anyone / brings news of
  my friend" (p. 94, folio 94).
- **Two named games as public spectacle** — polo: "Let me stand and watch for a few days / the polo
  tournament. Who wins, who loses, / whose brisk horse makes a clever move? … Let's see who hits the
  ball / across the field. The crowd cheers and jeers" (p. 113, folio 113, kafi 90); and chaupat:
  "Chaupat has eighty-four squares / Only the winner remains unharmed" (p. 91, folio 91).
- **Wealth and building described as the thing to be renounced** — "Horses, elephants, temples,
  wealth / how long can anyone claim anything?" (p. 76, folio 76); "You raise mansions, tall and
  mighty, / the wretched grave your real home" (p. 75, folio 75); "The multistoreyed houses, the
  ornamentations / Death makes the dwellers homeless in a moment" (p. 106, folio 106); "High mansion,
  glittering balconies, a fancy entrance / The wealth you're so proud is the den of enemies"
  (p. 165, folio 165); "Tall mansion, a bed of gold" (p. 170, folio 170); "Only their profiles are
  left engraved on coins" (p. 115, folio 115).
- **The body as a nine-doored court, and the ten senses/streams** — "Nine doors to this court, the
  tenth under lock / The one reserved for the lover is the door I know not / A nice niche in the
  courtyard, a box inside the niche" (p. 150, folio 150, kafi 125); "As long as you follow the five
  senses / what fame will you find, O Vagabond?" (p. 83, folio 83); "All five aloft the flood waters
  / All five the same … The bride who knows how to / tame these five is the one / who makes happy her
  husband" (p. 102, folio 102); "My body a lyre, my veins the strings, / I chant Sain, Sain" (p. 86,
  folio 86); "Two young men, on your right and left shoulder, / keep count of your good and bad"
  (p. 155, folio 155).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

The chunk contains **no karamat narrative and no miracle attributed to any named saint or site.** The
kafis are lyric, not hagiographic. The only supernatural material is doctrinal or cosmological, and
the book attributes none of it to a shrine:

- The recording angels at the shoulders (p. 155, folio 155) and "the guards of the Almighty came"
  (p. 90, folio 90).
- "Izrael, the angel, taking away the poor thing" (p. 149, folio 149); "the angel of death intrigues
  / above your head" (p. 97, folio 97); "the goddess of death" picking "The lords of lords and
  sultans" (p. 104, folio 104).
- The hidden-treasure saying, quoted without source: "Each thing encompassed, he said, I was the
  hidden treasure" (p. 177, folio 177), immediately before the credit to Shah Jalal.
- "Watch how I cover the distances, cross / the streams, walk past the lions asleep" (p. 139, folio
  139) — the speaker's own feat, in the first person.

## Arguments and interpretations

This chunk carries almost no translator's voice: only the five "Variation" headings, the parenthetical
"(after listening to Pathanay Khan sing this kafi)" (p. 94, folio 94), the introduction's title (p. 178,
folio 178), and the thirteen bibliographic notes. What follows is the primary source's own judgement in
its own words, plus those two translator's interventions.

- **The translator prints a second, freer rendering beside the first and says where one of them came
  from: a recorded performance.** "Variation (after listening to Pathanay Khan sing this kafi)"
  (p. 94, folio 94), running to p. 95 (folio 95). The other four Variations (pp. 152, 162, 169, 172;
  folios 152, 162, 169, 172) are headed "Variation" with no source given: **(not stated)**.
- **The translator's characterisation of his subject is in his section title** — "I. The Disgraceful
  Saint: An Introduction" (p. 178, folio 178).
- **"Die before dying" is stated twice as the doctrine** — "Says Hussein, the fakir, / if you seek
  life then die before dying" (p. 75, folio 75); "Says Hussein, / if you seek life, die while still
  alive" (p. 117, folio 117); "Says Hussein, God's fakir, the world must be forsaken / Die before
  dying" (p. 165, folio 165).
- **Self-knowledge is put first, twice in near-identical lines** — "Know thyself, Fellow / Easier to
  find Sain / if you know who you are" (p. 97, folio 97, kafi 74) and "Know thyself, Fellow / Easier
  to find God if you know / who you are" (p. 170, folio 170, kafi 145). The chunk thus prints the same
  opening with "Sain" in one place and "God" in the other; both are reproduced here as printed.
- **God is said to be both one and many, and multicoloured** — "He calls himself one, he calls himself
  / many. He's a multicoloured Master / Says Hussein, she's the real bride / who dyes herself in the
  lover's colours" (p. 80, folio 80). Also "He may dye my life / into any colour he likes" (p. 88,
  folio 88); "I'm dyed / in my Master's colours" (p. 85, folio 85); "the Sain dyes my eyes red"
  (p. 116, folio 116).
- **The inner and the outer are declared identical, and disclosure therefore pointless** — "This is no
  place for words, my love / Inside and outside dwells the same Sain / To whom will you go tell? No
  other but / the same Sweetheart settled in all hearts" (p. 159, folio 159, kafi 134).
- **Open profession is set against silence, and silence wins** — "Go ask the wayfarers being driven by
  passion / who feared the overt expressions" (p. 135, folio 135); "Truly, the silent ones walk / in
  the company of truth" (p. 169, folio 169) and, in that kafi's Variation, "The lover comes for those
  who evoke him / in silence" (p. 169, folio 169). Against this: "The lover has burnt the veil, /
  embarrassed the envious rivals" (p. 125, folio 125), and "You pull down the veil for the handsome"
  (p. 131, folio 131). The gallows stand for the cost: "Tempt with your red lips, go / up the gallows,
  swing" (p. 169, folio 169) — no martyr is named.
- **Skill, not status, is what earns the lover — the sequence's most insistent claim, always in the
  weaving figure** — "Says Hussein, only the skilful girls / get to embrace the lover" (p. 68, folio
  68); "O girlfriends, says Hussein, misery / if you haven't done your work" (p. 72, folio 72); "Among
  my skilled girlfriends / I am the one without any talent" (p. 72, folio 72); "One wrong weave / may
  not go well with the Master" (p. 161, folio 161). Set against the countervailing claim in the same
  chunk: "We, the sinful, are the favourites of the Master" (p. 130, folio 130) and "Finally the meek
  had their day" (p. 132, folio 132). The book does not reconcile the two.
- **Devotion is ranked above both learning and law** — "Why would I be called a learned scholar? / Beg
  for bread, the kind of work I'll do" (p. 138, folio 138); "It's a matter of pure passion, Mister"
  against Quran-reading and exegesis (p. 133, folio 133); "the conclusion of a million sophistries: /
  the lover asks for the passion" (p. 133, folio 133); "Passion is the fakir's walking stick, a strange
  fact / beyond intelligence" (p. 138, folio 138).
- **Humility is named as the method, in a formula repeated across the chunk** — "Get rid of pride, seek
  humility" (p. 115, folio 115); "Set aside pride, get hold of humility" (p. 122, folio 122); "Get rid
  of pride, adopt kindness" (p. 123, folio 123); "Leave pride, adopt humility, you'll get there
  somehow" (p. 175, folio 175); "uproot arrogance, cultivate humility" (p. 168, folio 168); "humility
  is the path to the Sain" (p. 105, folio 105); "Leave behind / the self and arrogance, get hold of
  humility" (p. 149, folio 149).
- **Fate is invoked to close the argument, repeatedly** — "The Writer of Fate ordained / Now what's the
  use of crying" (p. 71, folio 71); "Fun and games, my fate: so ordained the God" (p. 122, folio 122);
  "only the ordained happens" (p. 167, folio 167); "let the destined happen" (p. 175, folio 175); "let
  happen what's got to happen" (p. 151, folio 151); "why argue against the divine will?" (p. 162,
  folio 162).

## Doubts

- **"Shah Jalal" (p. 177, folio 177) matches two archive rows and is therefore not mapped.** The
  archive holds `shrine-of-jalaluddin-surkh-posh-bukhari-jalaluddin-bukhari` (Sayyid Jalaluddin
  Surkh-Posh Bukhari, Uch Sharif) and `shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht` (Sayyid Jalaluddin,
  Makhdoom Jahaniyan Jahangasht, Uch Sharif) — both principal figures named Jalaluddin, both at Uch
  Sharif. The chunk prints only the bare form "Shah Jalal", with no city, no date, no order, no
  epithet and no tomb, and it does not gloss the name; kafi 152 carries no note marker. I have
  recorded the fact under `madho-lal-hussain`, where it is a statement about that row's principal
  figure, and have **not** assigned it to either Jalaluddin row. This is the single most consequential
  line in the chunk for the archive and a human must resolve it from the introduction (not in this
  chunk) or from another source. Third possibility a reviewer should rule out: "Shah Jalal" may be a
  figure with no archive row at all.
- **"Lal" (pp. 126 and 138, folios 126 and 138) is not glossed, and "Madho" is never printed.** The
  archive row is named "Shrine of Hazrat Madho Lal Hussain (Shah Hussain Darbar)" and its principal
  figure "Shah Hussain (Madho Lal Hussain)", so the reading is nearly irresistible — but the chunk
  nowhere says that "Lal" is Madho, nowhere prints "Madho Lal", and nowhere states a relationship.
  Do not write "Madho Lal" from these two pages. The bullet is filed under the row because the poems
  are that row's principal figure's; the identification of "Lal" itself is **(not stated)**.
- **"Lahore" appears in this chunk only as a publisher's city and inside a book title, never as a
  place in any kafi.** All six occurrences are in the Notes (p. 178, folio 178): "(Lahore: Pakistan
  Punjabi Adabi Board…)", "(Lahore: Panjnad Academy…)", "(Lahore: Al Faisal Nashran…)", "(Lahore:
  Babar Ali Foundation…)", "(Lahore: Farid Bhandar Trust…)" and the title "*Tarikh-i-Lahore ka
  Encyclopedia*". **Baghbanpura, Shalimar, the Ravi and any other Lahore locality are absent from all
  111 pages.** For a book on a Lahore shrine this is a negative worth carrying to consolidation: no
  topographical claim about `madho-lal-hussain`'s site can be sourced to chunk 002.
- **Bare toponyms, with the row each would take if the interim toponym convention reverses.**
  "Hazara" (pp. 130 and 137, folios 130 and 137) and "Ranjha's village" (p. 94, folio 94) correspond
  to **no archive row at all** — Takht Hazara is not in the index. There is therefore nothing to
  reverse for them. The publisher cities in the Notes (Islamabad, Lahore, London, New Delhi, New
  York, North Carolina; p. 178, folio 178) are bibliographic imprints, not toponyms in the text's own
  register, and I have not treated them as place references under any convention.
- **"Sial" (p. 130, folio 130) must not be mapped to `sial-sharif`.** The line is "If you are the Sain
  of Hazara / Then we the debutantes of Sial", where "Sial" is Heer's clan in the Heer–Ranjha story,
  paired with Ranjha's Hazara. The archive row `sial-sharif` (Sial Sharif, Sargodha District;
  principal figure Khwaja Muhammad Shamsuddin Sialvi) is a name collision only. Flagged because the
  string match is exact and would be easy to make downstream.
- **Deities named in the kafis are left unmapped, deliberately and reversibly.** The ruling of
  18 September covers a *person* who is a row's principal figure named as an author or literary
  reference; it does not address a deity invoked inside a devotional lyric, and I did not extend it
  without a ruling. If it is extended: **Ram** / **Rama** (pp. 90, 103, 104, 112, 160, 172; folios
  90, 103, 104, 112, 160, 172) would take `ram-mandir-saidpur-ram-kund-mandir`. **Har** (pp. 150 and
  170, folios 150 and 170) corresponds to no row and the chunk does not say whether it is the same
  name as "Ram": **(not stated)**. This follows the treatment used in
  `shackle_bulleh_shah_sufi_lyrics/chunk_004.notes.md` so the two books stay reversible together.
- **`[text-layer?]` damage class 1 — lost inter-word space in a proper name.** "LokVirsa Publishing
  House" (p. 178, folio 178, note 1), printed closed up. My reading is "Lok Virsa", the publisher's
  usual two-word form, but the chunk shows it closed and I have carried it closed with the flag
  attached. This affects only the bibliographic entry, not any fact above.
- **`[text-layer?]` damage class 2 — a dance name that may or may not be a second word.** "jhurmat"
  (p. 138, folio 138) against "jhumar" (p. 112, folio 112). Both are plausible Punjabi dance names
  and each occurs once, so I cannot tell from the chunk whether the book prints two words or whether
  one is a text-layer corruption of the other. Both are recorded as printed with the flag on
  "jhurmat". A reviewer with the printed page should settle it before either word is quoted in an
  entry.
- **"Ranjhra" (pp. 94–95, folios 94–95) is carried as printed and is NOT flagged as damage.** The
  form occurs three times, all inside the one Variation ("leads me to Ranjhra", "bring / some news of
  my Ranjhra", "send for Ranjhra, my ache"), while every other page prints "Ranjha". A text layer
  does not insert the same letter three times in one block, so I read this as the translator's
  printed spelling in that variant and have not normalised it. Recorded here so the consolidator does
  not silently merge the two spellings.
- **Italics are lost by the text layer, so the Punjabi and Arabic terms cannot be told from the
  English.** The volume must italicise its transliterated vocabulary, and the text layer gives it all
  unmarked: "kaseeras" (p. 78, folio 78), "jhumar" (p. 112), "jhurmat" (p. 138), "muklaroos" /
  "Muklaroo" (pp. 103, 112), "Chaupat" (p. 91), "bhang" (p. 172), "marwa" (p. 168), "sadhu" (pp. 153,
  172), "bhangi" (p. 80), "Sain" throughout, and the book titles in the Notes (p. 178), which I have
  italicised above by inference from bibliographic convention and not by reading the page. Anyone
  quoting these should check the printed page for the italic extent.
- **"Jahangueir" (p. 178, folio 178, note 2) is as printed and is not a fault.** The note reads
  "*Tuzk-i-Jehangiri (Memoirs of the Emperor Jahangueir)*, trans. David Price (London: Oriental
  Translation Committee, 1829)"; the archaic spelling belongs to the 1829 English title. Likewise
  "Tehqiqat-i-Chisti" (note 5) and "Haqiqat al-Fuqra" (note 4) are carried exactly as the book
  spells them, without normalisation to any other transliteration.
- **Kafi number versus folio: the pages a reader could misread.** The kafi number stands at the page
  head and the folio alone at the page foot, and the offset between them is 22 for pp. 68–94, 23 for
  pp. 96–118, 24 for pp. 120–138 and 25 for pp. 140–177 (it steps at each page carrying only a
  continued Variation or a lettered kafi). No page in the chunk has head and foot equal, so the two
  are separable by position, but the *values* overlap between sections — p. 90 heads kafi **68** and
  foots folio **90**, p. 112 heads kafi **90** and foots folio **112**, p. 134 heads kafi **110** and
  foots folio **134**. Every page reference above is the PDF index, i.e. the foot number, and folio =
  PDF page with no offset on all 111 pages.
- **Verse lineation above is the text layer's, not certainly the book's.** The extraction preserves
  leading whitespace that appears to represent centred or indented lines, but line breaks inside a
  kafi may be reflow rather than print. Where I have quoted across a break I have marked it with " /
  ". Anyone setting a quotation should check the printed lineation.
- **Kafi 104 (p. 128, folio 128) prints only two lines** — "Your lover is mad at you, say my scornful
  girlfriends / Your indifference is my infamy, yet my gaze fixed on you" — and is the shortest item
  in the chunk, with no "Says Hussein" signature. It may be complete as printed or may be truncated;
  the chunk gives no way to tell. Kafi 151 (p. 176, folio 176) and kafi 143 (p. 168, folio 168) are
  likewise unsigned in the usual form, which is why I did not treat the missing signature as
  evidence of loss.
- **One obscure line I have not emended.** "Hey you, listen: the death calls / Recite God's name, the
  customer leaves" (p. 106, folio 106). The second clause does not parse against the rest of the
  kafi, but the words are individually clean and I could not identify a specific corruption, so I
  have neither flagged nor altered it. Recorded so a reviewer with the printed page can check it.
- **No facing-page interleaving problem arose, because there is no facing page.** The brief warned of
  English and Punjabi lines coming through out of order; pp. 68–178 carry English recto and verso
  alike, with no original-script page anywhere, so no reordering was needed and none was performed.
- **Two things the consolidator will want that are not here.** The endnotes for the kafis themselves
  — the p. 178 page carries only section I, the introduction's notes 1–13, and stops at the foot of
  the page mid-apparatus; and the introduction text those notes belong to, which is in chunk 001. Any
  gloss of "Shah Jalal", "Lal", "kaseeras", "jhumar"/"jhurmat", "Muklaroo", "Chaupat", "marwa" or
  "Pathanay Khan" is **(not stated)** in this chunk.
