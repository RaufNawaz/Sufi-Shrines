## What this book is

An ethnography of the shrine of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar at Sehwan, Sindh, and of the *fakirs* whose
lives are organised around it, based on fifteen months of fieldwork between 2009 and 2018 (pp. 46,
48, folios 29, 31). Kasmani's subject is not the saint's biography but what he calls *qurb* —
intimacy with a deceased saint — and what such intimacy affords the people who pursue it: "Queer
Companions asks, what unfolds or takes hold as individuals draw close to deceased Islamic saints in
the present?" (p. 18, folio not stated). Its five settings are "grove, shrine, courtyard, lodge, and
graveyard" (p. 46, folio 29), one per chapter, each paired with one fakir protagonist; the book is
avowedly about gender and sexuality as well as religion, and it says so in its own terms — "queer in
this project isn't strictly a figure of sexuality" but "that capacious hermeneutic by which we come
to read… queer religiously" (p. 19, folio 2).

For this archive the book's value is institutional and economic rather than hagiographic. It is the
corpus's fullest account of what the Pakistani state did to one shrine — the 1959 West Pakistan Waqf
Properties Ordinance, the June 1960 takeover, the Department of Auqaf's daily management, the
rebuilding of the mausoleum in the early 1990s, the revenue and donation mechanism, the surviving
leverage of the former custodian families, the fourteen-*kafi* lodge system, and the securitisation
that followed the 2017 suicide attack. It is equally the corpus's fullest account of women's and
gender-variant people's access to a shrine. It is thin on exactly what an archive row usually wants:
**Lal's death year appears once in the author's own voice and nowhere else; his silsila is never
stated in the book's own voice; no ʿurs date, month or duration is given anywhere in 225 pages.**

### lal-shahbaz-qalandar — Lal Shahbaz Qalandar

The book's whole subject, present in all twelve chunks. The author's running form is **Lal**; the
fuller forms are `Laʿl Shahbaz Qalandar` (p. 20, folio 3), `Lal Shahbaz Qalandar` (p. 35, folio 18),
and, in the Library of Congress data on the copyright page, both `Qalandar Lal Shahbaz` and
`Lal Shahbaz Qalander` — with `-er` — on the same page (p. 5, folio not stated). The References
print the name four further ways: `Laʿl Shahbaz Qalandar` (Boivin 2003, p. 204, folio 187),
`La'l Shahbâz Qalandar` with a straight apostrophe and a circumflex (Boivin 2012b, p. 204), and
`Qalandar Lʿal Shahbaz`, with the ʿayn displaced into "Lal" (Andhar 2010, p. 203, folio 186). The
index carries `Hazrat Usman Marwandi. See Lal` (p. 221, folio 204), `Sayyid Usman Marwandi. See Lal`
(p. 223, folio 206) and `Laʿl Shahbaz Qalandar. See Lal` (p. 222, folio 205).

- **Saint**
  - **Death year — stated once, in the author's own voice, and nowhere else in the book.** "Laʿl
    Shahbaz Qalandar (d. 1274 ce, henceforth Lal) is a thirteenth-century mystic known for his
    radical rejection of Islamic religious institutions, norms, and practices." (p. 20, folio 3). The
    era is printed lowercase `ce` throughout. **Every other passage gives only a century** — "a
    thirteenth-century qalandar" (p. 35, folio 18), "an original thirteenth-century qalandar" (p. 62,
    folio 45), "a wandering mystic in the thirteenth century" (p. 164, folio 147), "a
    thirteenth-century antinomian mystic whose tomb-shrine is located in Sehwan" (p. 176, folio 159),
    "coming close to a thirteenth-century saintly figure" (p. 172, folio 155). Chunks covering
    pp. 50–158 and the whole index give **no death date at all: (not stated)**.
  - **A birth year the author never states.** The CIP heading prints "Qalandar Lal Shahbaz,
    1177–1274—Shrines. | Shrine of Lal Shahbaz Qalander (Sehwan, Pakistan)" (p. 5, folio not stated).
    **1177 is the Library of Congress heading's, never Kasmani's.** The index prints no dates of any
    kind for him (pp. 221–222, folios 204–205).
  - **His name is contested, and the book says so twice.** "his followers cannot quite agree on his
    doctrinal persuasion, Sufi affiliations, not even his actual name, Sayyid Usman Marwandi. The
    saint is better known by the three-part epithet Lal Shahbaz Qalandar." (p. 35, folio 18)
    "Custodian families in Sehwan take the position that the saint's actual name was most likely Shah
    Hasan and not Usman, the latter commonly associated with the Sunni. The title Lal Shahbaz as
    reference to the saint is first recorded in late seventeenth-century textual sources (Boivin
    2011, 20)." (p. 184, folio 167, intro n. 33; the same dating repeated p. 187, folio 170, ch. 1
    n. 2). "matters like the saint's origin or real name… are actively debated in Sehwan" (p. 62,
    folio 45).
  - **The two words behind the epithet.** "Lal (red, dear one) or Laʿl (ruby, gem), though different
    words and both ascribed to the saint, are indistinguishable in Sehwan since there is no
    difference in pronunciation." (p. 182, folio not stated, intro n. 5) And, as a fakir learned it,
    "red was the color associated with Lal, whose variant Laʿl means ruby, or that Shah-baz (or royal
    falcon) referred to a miracle attributed to the saint." (p. 64, folio 47)
  - **Origin and descent.** "he is most likely to have been born in Marwand, near Tabriz (in
    present-day Iran), and his lineage through Jafar al-Sadiq's son Ismaʿil makes him an important
    figure of the Ismaʿili line of the Shiʿi faith (Boivin 2011, 15 – 16)" — note the hedge "most
    likely to have been" — "This also means that he is a descendant of Ali and thus related to the
    Family of the Prophet (ahl-e bayt)" (p. 62, folio 45). The Sabzwari custodian family claim their
    ancestors "accompanied Lal on his journey from Iran" (p. 82, folio 65).
  - **Silsila — never stated in the book's own voice: (not stated).** What the book reports are
    competing claims. "Lal bears at least three genealogies — Sufi, Shiʿi, and Shivaite" (p. 35,
    folio 18); "Lal's Shiʿi descent through the Ismaʿili line is well established; however, he is
    also claimed through Suhrawardi and Qadiri Sufi registers." (p. 35, folio 18); "In Sufi terms,
    Lal is claimed by both Suhrawardi and Qadiri orders and is popularly regarded as one of a quartet
    of mystics, chahar-yar or 'four friends.'" (p. 62, folio 45); "For many fakirs in Sehwan, Lal is
    an original thirteenth-century qalandar, who they believe was initiated at the hands of the
    order's founding figure, Jamal al-Din Savi of Damascus." (p. 62, folio 45); "historical sources
    do not confirm that Lal observed chahar-zarb or the fourfold shave typical of the early qalandars
    (Boivin 2012b, 97)" (p. 62, folio 45). In the notes: "According to the Suhrawardi Sufi tradition,
    the saint was initiated in Multan by Bahaʾuddin Zakaria (Boivin 2011, 18). Boivin (2012b, 105)
    notes that custodians in Sehwan claim a loose affiliation with some Qadiri strands though no
    formal initiations into the order are made." (p. 184, folio 167, intro n. 34) The index has
    `qalandars` as a category of person, with `Lal, 3, 17, 38, 40, 45 – 46`, but prints **no silsila,
    tariqa lineage or chain** (p. 222, folio 205).
  - **Arrival and death, dated only in the apparatus and not reconciled.** "Also unsettled is the
    year of his arrival in Sehwan, of which 1272 ce is considered to be more reliable (Boivin 2012b,
    83)" (p. 62, folio 45); a whole note reads "The other date is 1251 (see Boivin 2012b, 83)"
    (p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 13) — **what 1251 dates is (not stated)**; "according to Michel
    Boivin (2012b, `80 – 83`), he is likely to have first arrived in Multan in 1263. He stopped in
    places like Makran, `Lahut-lamakan`, Panjgur, and Pasni in Balochistan as well as Naing, Lake
    Manchhar, and Sehwan in Sindh." (p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 14) "He later settled in Sehwan for
    the last year or two of his life" (p. 62, folio 45); "Equally enigmatic stories abound when it
    comes to the burial of the saint's body, a year after he arrived in town." (p. 163, folio 146)
    "Sehwan at this time was also under the influence of the Ismaʿilis (Boivin 2012b, 85), which
    might explain Lal's arrival in the region or his choice to settle in `Sewhan[text-layer?]`
    [Sehwan]." (p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 16) **These four figures — 1251, 1263, 1272, "a year or
    two" — are printed side by side and the book joins none of them; 1274 is not derived from them
    here.**
  - **Celibate and without issue, and the legal consequence.** He "was a celibate figure and thus
    without issue" (p. 82, folio 65); "A shrine without legal heirs could not be deemed familial
    inheritance, which in fact was a key line of argument in the modern state's takeover of the
    shrine from its traditional sayyid custodians" (p. 82, folio 65). Fakirs make him the type of
    celibacy: "Because he [the saint] did not marry either, he had cast it [his loins] in iron"
    (p. 139, folio 122). Against that, the index carries both `celibacy, 146` and `marriage, 174n28`
    under his headword (p. 222, folio 205).
  - **The record is thin and the book makes that a finding.** "he evades hagiographical hold and
    historical record. Mystery suits him better than history" (p. 62, folio 45); "Multiple claims
    work because historical references to Lal are few and far between, a patchwork at best of brief
    mentions found in other saints' biographical texts" (p. 62, folio 45); "the history of the saint
    of Sehwan is at best a patchwork of brief references, some found in sparse historical accounts,
    some in other saints' hagiographies." (p. 165, folio 148) **None of those accounts or
    hagiographies is named: (not stated).** "For a detailed biography of the saint (in French), see
    Boivin (2012b)." (p. 184, folio 167)
  - **Who he is to whom.** "As a descendant of Ali, he especially attracts Pakistan's Shiʿa. In the
    guise of a Shiva incarnate, the Hindus revere him. Muhammad, Ali, or Ram, his memory spans
    religious divides, extends far out from the town of his burial, and is traceable all over
    Pakistan" (pp. 57–58, folios 40–41); "Hindus consider him to be a Shivaite ascetic who epitomizes
    a particular ideal of ascetic renunciation" (p. 62, folio 45); "Lal, the saint of Sehwan, is a
    placeholder for Ali, if not an avatar of Ali himself" (p. 156, folio 139); "the saint is known as
    Jhule Lal, a name originally directed at the river deity of the Indus" (p. 162, folio 145).
- **Shrine / Darbar**
  - **Place and scale.** "the ancient pilgrimage town of Sehwan in Pakistan's Sindh Province" (p. 19,
    folio 2); "the antinomian saint is Pakistan's most beloved figure of devotion, whose tomb-shrine
    in Sehwan, a town of about a hundred thousand residents, draws millions each year." (p. 20,
    folio 3) — **both figures are printed in words, not numerals.** "hardly 300 kilometers north of
    Karachi", "a four-hour drive to the town" (p. 23, folio 6). The census figure is in a note:
    "According to the provisional data of the 2017 census, the population of greater Sehwan including
    neighboring villages was about 269,291 people, out of which 104,239 were town residents."
    (p. 182, folio not stated, intro n. 6) **The book never writes "Jamshoro District"; the district
    is (not stated), and "Sehwan Sharif" appears only in titles (pp. 187–188, folios 170–171).**
  - **Character.** "Sufi by renown, Shivaite in heritage, and increasingly Shiʿi in following, Sehwan
    is in many ways Pakistan at its complex best." (p. 20, folio 3) "just as Sehwan isn't simply a
    Shiʿi place, neither entirely Sufi" (p. 24, folio 7). "the shrine, whether formerly a temple or
    not, evidences a rich history of Hindu association and patronage" (p. 36, folio 19).
  - **Earliest notice and building sequence.** "the earliest mention of the saint's grave occurs in a
    fourteenth-century account of the North African traveler Ibn Battuta (d. 1369)" (p. 81, folio 64).
    "If one is to follow Boivin's (2011, 30) stage-wise process of the shrine's historical
    transformation, a first mausoleum was in place as early as 1357 and another was built around
    1585" (p. 81, folio 64); "Such developments suggest how the shrine grew from a nondescript grave
    site to a center of public pilgrimage" (p. 81, folio 64); "The shrine's rise in prominence during
    the Mughal period and especially its architectural development would have been concurrent with
    its ascending material prospects" (p. 81, folio 64).
  - **The present building.** "the present-day structure was built in the early 1990s, several rounds
    of repairs prior to and since have been undertaken by the Department of Auqaf" (p. 82, folio 65);
    "Plans for the construction of the new mausoleum were eventually executed during the early 1990s
    during the premiership of Benazir Bhutto… a much larger structure would replace the old Sindhi
    mausoleum" (p. 70, folio 53). "The shape of the shrine's golden dome and the form of its minarets
    replicate those of the mausoleums of Shiʿi imams in Iran and Iraq (see fig. 1.3)" (p. 71,
    folio 54) — "a curious development, especially when it comes at the hands of a predominantly
    Sunni state" (p. 71, folio 54); Kasmani marks the explanation as conjecture: "It might well have
    been influenced by the Shiʿi devotion of the Bhuttos since both father and daughter were
    personally involved in architectural and development plans." (p. 71, folio 54)
  - **The construction report — an unpublished, undated primary source.** "From the construction and
    survey report titled 'Redevelopment of the Mazar of Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar at Sehwan
    Sharif.' **No date of publication is mentioned.** I accessed a copy of this report in the
    personal collection of Michel Boivin in Chambéry, France, in July 2013." (pp. 187–188, folios
    170–171, ch. 1 n. 11) "It can be assumed that the document was prepared between 1989 and 1992,
    corresponding with the first government of Benazir Bhutto." (p. 189, folio 172, ch. 1 n. 33) Its
    declared models: "Khomeini's Square in Isfahan, Iran, the Alhambra in Granada, Spain, and the Taj
    Mahal in Agra, India, in addition to shrines of imams and saints in Iran and Iraq" (p. 189,
    folio 172, ch. 1 n. 33). **The title is printed two ways two pages apart — "at Sehwan Sharif"
    and "in Sehwan Sharif".** The report is also the source for "the architectural shift from the
    vernacular to the 'truly Islamic' — as the shrine's construction report describes it" (p. 71,
    folio 54).
  - **Fabric, as observed.** "The elaborate blue-tiled facade of the mauso-leum serves as backdrop"
    (p. 21, folio 4); the courtyard is "white marble" (p. 25, folio 8; p. 87, folio 70; p. 83,
    folio 66) and "long rectangular", flanked "along its length" by "shaded arcades" (p. 26, folio 9;
    p. 104, folio 87), where "many of the visitors, especially women and poorer families, spent the
    night" (p. 104, folio 87). The durbar is "the octagonal audience hall where hundreds circled the
    tomb of Lal" (p. 101, folio not stated); the two prayer corners "weren't really mosques but
    semiprivate sections reserved for ritual prayer, peripheral spaces of the shrine durbar located
    in the architectural excess resulting from an `octa­gonal[text-layer?]` [octagonal] layout in a
    square plan" (p. 78, folio 61), their "relative elevation from the ground" giving "an
    uninterrupted view of the saint's tomb" (p. 78, folio 61); "A separate room was designated for
    Shiʿi prayer in an adjacent part of the shrine" (p. 80, folio 63). "the tall and heavily carved
    wooden doors of the durbar" with "A metallic lock… wrapped in a red silken cloth" (p. 83,
    folio 66), three doors (p. 85, folio 68), "the drum and the metal disc placed just where the
    entrance portal gives way to the open courtyard" (p. 83, folio 66), "the large donation boxes"
    (p. 84, folio 67), "secondary entrances" with "a tiny shop right across one of" them (p. 21,
    folio 4). The book's own plan (Map 3.1) labels the components: **"Lal's tomb"**, **"Durbar"**,
    **"Courtyards"**, **"Outer yard"**, **"Qadimi 'alam"**, **"Markets"**, with a scale bar
    "0          200   400 m" and a north arrow "N" (p. 106, folio not stated), captioned "Plan and 3d
    visualization of the shrine of Lal. Drawing courtesy Yves Ubelmann. Illustration design by Rubab
    Paracha" (p. 105, folio 88).
  - **The outer yard, newly made.** "What used to be an old public square — featuring the qadimi
    ʿalam, an old structure commemorating the battle of Karbala — was now paved and enclosed with a
    low wall, creating an outer yard, remodeled as part of an ever-extending shrine complex"
    (p. 105, folio 88). **No date for the paving: (not stated).** "Such ongoing architectural and
    spatial modification of the shrine, especially at the hands of its administration, was already
    compromising its organic relation with the bazaar and had greatly altered the place's nightlife"
    (p. 105, folio 88).
  - **Continual rebuilding, 1990s to 2018.** "The constant and highly elaborate remodeling of
    Sehwan's shrine from the 1990s until its latest and ongoing expansion in 2018 is proof of its
    increasing importance. It also reveals the faith of the state in its economic worth and value"
    (p. 72, folio 55). Between 2009 and 2013 "The shrine's facade and its interior had been
    remodeled; the marble floor of the courtyard was newer and whiter than it was in 2009" (p. 102,
    folio 85); by 2018 "the shrine had turned grander in scale; its facades had become more ornate
    than before, the dome more golden than ever" (p. 105, folio 88). A healing room "has since been
    demolished to make way for the `ever-­extending` shrine" (p. 88, folio 71); "Only in later
    architectural modifications of the shrine was a prayer room introduced" (p. 99, folio 82).
    Figure 1.3 is captioned "The remodeled facade of Sehwan's shrine complex, 2018." (p. 71,
    folio 54). **No architect, contractor or cost anywhere: (not stated).**
  - **What the rebuilding removed.** "when, in a 2013 redesign of the tomb hall, the customary five
    lamps of the saint were not accommodated, it disenfranchised a local Hindu spiritual figure. His
    twice daily service of lighting lamps, which I had observed in years prior to the renovations,
    was no longer possible at the shrine. This forms part of a gradual but systematic fading away of
    the shrine's Hindu character at the hands of the department" (p. 72, folio 55) — **the Hindu
    figure is unnamed: (not stated)**. "Similar administrative measures have included the removal of
    the doorbells and, more importantly, what some locals have described as a Shiva lingam-like
    object from the shrine during early repairs in the `1970s.34[text-layer?]`" (p. 72, folio 55).
    "Such regulating of the shrine's unstraight heritage through architectural design continues to
    this `day.35[text-layer?]`" (p. 72, folio 55). "Whether such decisions were premeditated or taken
    arbitrarily is hard to tell" (p. 72, folio 55). Separately: "Explicitly Sunni inscriptions like
    names of the first four caliphs were introduced in the tomb hall in 2013. These, however, were no
    longer present when I visited the shrine in 2018, possibly removed when parts of the tomb hall
    were repaired after the suicide attack of 2017." (p. 189, folio 172, ch. 1 n. 35)
  - **The tomb itself.** "Today, hundreds of funerary sheets drape the tomb of the saint, so numerous
    that no mortal has ever seen the surface of the tomb itself, it is widely claimed in Sehwan."
    (p. 163, folio 146) "Citing this legend, many fakirs and prominent sayyid figures in Sehwan do
    not refer to the shrine as a tomb; instead they call it the throne of Lal (takht)." (p. 163,
    folio 146) At the centre of the durbar "stood Lal's tomb-shrine lined with silver, bedecked with
    silks and rose petals, and from where the saint as emperor (shahanshah or the King of kings) was
    said to hold court and grant audience" (p. 77, folio not stated).
  - **What stood there before — reported as local belief, twice, and not adjudicated.** "Many in
    Sehwan believe that where the shrine of Lal now stands is where the high temple of Shiva once
    stood." (p. 163, folio 146) "In a different version, however, the location of his tomb marks the
    stretch of land where Lal is said to have rested on his arrival in town." (p. 163, folio 146)
    "His site of burial, the present-day shrine at Sehwan, is thought to replace an ancient Shiva
    sanctuary." (p. 35, folio 18) In the notes: "Available archaeological evidence does not confirm
    the claim (see Collinet 2008). However, historians believe that overlaps are likely (see Boivin
    2011, 96; Schimmel 2003, 355)." (p. 185, folio 168, intro n. 35) "Annemarie Schimmel (2003, 355)
    mentions the presence of a Shiva lingam close to the saint's tomb (see also Boivin 2011, 96)."
    (p. 189, folio 172, ch. 1 n. 34) "Sehwan at the time of the saint's arrival was dominated by
    followers of Shiva as well as Daryapanthis, who divinized the Indus River (Boivin 2012b, 40)"
    (p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 8). "The name Sehwan is itself a remnant of the older Shiv-vahan
    (the village of Shiva)." (p. 36, folio 19) Sehwan's older names are given in a note: a
    seventh-century account portrays it "as a stronghold of the Shivaite sect
    `Pashupa-tas[text-layer?]` [Pashupatas] before the region's Arab conquest at the beginning of the
    eighth century. Arab geographers have called it Sadusan and Siwistan (variants: `Shiv-­asthan`,
    `Shiv-­astano`)"; Alexander Cunningham "identifies Sehwan as Sindomana… the Greek name Saindhuwan
    (or the abode of the Saindhavas) is the origin of the name Sehwan (Cunningham 1871, 266)."
    (p. 185, folio 168, intro n. 38)
  - **A second grave inside the durbar.** "a little grave in a cove-like corner of the space, which
    had been rather oddly incorporated since the redesign of the tomb-hall sometime in the 1990s. In
    fact, she viewed herself as the lone guardian of a forgotten grave" (p. 79, folio 62). A note
    identifies it: "The minor shrine in question is the tomb of one of the earliest disciples of
    Lal." (p. 190, folio 173, ch. 2 n. 9) **The disciple is (not stated).**
  - **Two domes and other sites of the saint.** Figure 5.3: "A view of Sehwan from the old fortress

    graveyard is one among several other locations that bespeak the memory of the saint. One of these

    therein" inside a Lakkiyari kafi (p. 84, folio 67) — all **(not stated)**.
  - **The 2017 suicide attack.** "February 16, 2017, at home in Berlin… Just hours before, and barely
    meters away from where Zaheda and I had first crossed paths, a suicide bomber had struck the
    courtyard of Sehwan's shrine. More than eighty people were killed." (p. 28, folio 11) "It fit the
    tragic pattern of recent years whereby key Sufi shrines in Pakistan have periodically been
    targeted by hardline militants and radical Islamist networks." (p. 28, folio 11) **The attackers
    are never named: (not stated).** Later chapters refer to it only as "the suicide attack of 2017"
    (p. 98, folio 81), "the suicide bomb attack" (p. 105, folio 88) and "the tragedy at Sehwan"
    (p. 184, folio 167, intro n. 19); Amma "had witnessed the bombing and survived it too" (p. 99,
    folio 82). "Some of the unidentified bodies from the deadly attack at the shrine in 2017 had been
    laid to rest here" — in the graveyard on the town's northern edge (p. 160, folio 143). Context:

- **Custodianship, the state, and the shrine economy**
  - **Nationalisation, in sequence.** "the newly formed state of Pakistan comes to grips with the
    varied resources of the many shrines that dotted its territory as early as the 1950s. A
    countrywide survey of religious endowments (waqf properties) was already under way at the time.
    However, it would take another decade before these shrines would formally come to be institutions
    of the state, eventually organized under the Department of Auqaf and governed via the Ministry of
    Religious `Affairs.25[text-layer?]`" (p. 68, folio 51). "It was through a federal ordinance in
    1959 that the Pakistani state finally began to implement its writ broadly by assuming
    custodianship of sites that were considered waqf (pl. auqaf), an inalienable endowment"
    (pp. 68–69, folios 51–52); the statute is named in a note — "This refers to the West Pakistan
    Waqf Properties Ordinance (1959)." (p. 183, folio 166, intro n. 17). "Authorized by a federal
    ordinance in 1959, saints and their places have since come under the purview of the Depart-ment
    of Auqaf, a subsidiary of the Ministry of Religious Affairs." (pp. 27–28, folios 10–11) "While it
    offered a provision for the maintenance of inherited shrines and ancestral tomb-sites, the
    ordinance made it possible for all nonprivate endowments that were not solely charitable to be
    taken legally into the hands of the state (Strothmann 2012, 55)" (p. 69, folio 52). "In fact,
    according to Malik (1990), only the endowments that were profitable were
    `nationalized.26[text-layer?]`" (p. 68, folio 51). "shrine takeovers were not without conflict.
    Some involved long legal battles with groups and families affected by nationalization, and a few
    such court appeals lasted well into the 1980s." (p. 68, folio 51)
  - **This shrine's takeover, dated three ways that agree.** "A move as radical as the shrine's
    takeover from its traditional custodians in 1960" (p. 28, folio 11); "The shrine of Sehwan,
    according to one report, was taken over by the state of Pakistan on June 24, 1960." (p. 61,
    folio 44, endnote 11) — the hedge "according to one report" is the book's own and **the report is
    not named: (not stated)**; the local sayyids' "control over the shrine was replaced by the state
    in June 1960" (p. 79, folio 62).
  - **The motive, stated as displacement plus money.** "shrine holdings and saints' places had long
    enabled a routinization of power for groups and individuals, mainly from sayyid lineages…
    Displacing such authority and gaining possession of shrine resources promised not only new
    financial means for the state but also guaranteed curbing the influence of local powerhouses"
    (p. 68, folio 51); "If one objective was indeed to harness the political reach and religious
    authority of shrine holders and in effect replace it with that of the state, its financial bounty
    couldn't have been any less appealing" (p. 68, folio 51).
  - **Who lost.** "These include sayyids, that is, families that claim descent from the Prophet
    Muhammad, and who held control over shrine revenues at least since the colonial period, as well
    as local Hindu patrons long asso-ciated with shrine services and ceremonies." (p. 28, folio 11)
    "State control and interest have also effectively disenfranchised local Hindu families who once
    enjoyed a greater role in similar services at the shrine" (pp. 83–84, folios 66–67).
  - **The two custodian families.** "In the period following the eighteenth century, British colonial
    records document duties and services at the shrine being shared between Hindu and Muslim groups.
    More prominent in such records are lengthy court disputes regarding its revenues, especially the
    issue of the shrine's custodianship involving two notable sayyid lineages of Sehwan, the
    Lakkiyari and the Sabzwari" (pp. 81–82, folios 64–65). "These families continue to hold a
    significant influence in the town, though their claims on the shrine and its revenues, both
    immediate and ancillary, can be documented only as far back as the colonial period" (p. 82,
    folio 65). "Their ancestors, as the Sabzwari claim, were select disciples who accompanied Lal on
    his journey from Iran; the Lakkiyari, from the neighboring hills of Lakki, assert that they
    descend from the saint's primary followers who received the journeying ascetic on the outskirts
    of Sehwan" (p. 82, folio 65) — **the book asserts neither; it reports both.** "Rivalries endure in
    Sehwan to this day" (p. 82, folio 65); "According to Boivin, the rift between the two families
    dates back to the British period (Michel Boivin, email message to author, September 1, 2014)."
    (p. 190, folio 173, ch. 2 n. 5) A third claim: "Many fakirs in Sehwan claim that the shrine's
    original heirs were in fact ascetic followers of the saint." (p. 190, folio 173, ch. 2 n. 2)

    the traditional custodians of the shrine at Sehwan"; "Sayyid Hasan Shah Khatdhani, a prominent

    Sehwan"; "Nazir Ahmed Sehwani" (p. 15, folio xiv). **No lineage, office title, succession or
    dates for any of them: (not stated).**
  - **What the former custodians kept.** "while the nationalization of shrines has dramatically
    reduced their roles at the shrine, limiting it to the ceremonial, former custodian families
    continue to hold considerable leverage through other forms of participation" (p. 89, folio 72);
    "Former custodians `(sajjadah-­nashin)` regularly seek some measure of say in the upkeep of the

  - **The daily ghusl, observed.** "It was around 3:30 a.m. on a Friday morning in November 2011"
    (p. 82, folio 65) that the author watched "the daily ceremony of ghusl or the ritual bathing of
    the saint's `tomb.6[text-layer?]`" (p. 83, folio 66). Present: "Department of Auqaf employees,
    contract workers and cleaners, a handful of fakirs, visitors, and pilgrims. A policeman tried to
    keep things in order. The junior manager of the shrine arrived with keys to the shrine. Also
    present were khadims, some state-employed, and other workers on contract whose duty is to serve
    the saint and his shrine. A fakir from a prominent lodge of the Lakkiyaris walked in with a large
    bundle wrapped in red cloth. Another one, representing a secondary shrine in the town, carried a
    big basket filled with rose petals" (p. 83, folio 66). The dastar: "It is the Lakkiyaris… who
    provide this most important item for the tomb's dressing. It is prepared with fresh leaves at one
    of their kafis (fakir lodges) located just outside the shrine premises. A representative is
    present every morning to deliver the dastar and to collect the old one, which is then taken back
    to the lodge and placed at a minor shrine `therein.9[text-layer?]` This changing of the dastar is
    one example of a greater system of exchange of favors that continues between the state and the
    sayyid elite of Sehwan" (p. 84, folio 67). "Ornamented jewels, decorative flowers, and peacock
    feathers add the final touch to the dressing of the crown of the saint, finished by the use of
    perfumed oils and `sprays.10[text-layer?]`" (p. 84, folio 67). "Occasionally, wealthy pilgrims
    also contribute gifts of ornamental value for the dressing of the tomb. Their participation is
    often made possible through government contacts or the intervention of local sayyid families who
    use their influence to confer favor on their disciples" (p. 84, folio 67). "As the cleaning drew
    to a close, the Auqaf manager walked over to the three doors of the durbar and sprinkled rose
    water on those witnessing the ritual from outside. Now sharing the substance of the ceremony, his
    primary role in the ritual lent his otherwise civil office a quasi-spiritual character" (p. 85,
    folio 68); "There was a loud wave of chants as people flocked to the tomb to touch and kiss the
    freshly dressed tomb of the saint. They brought new covers and fresh petals and offered sweets
    and money to the `saint.11[text-layer?]`" (p. 85, folio 68). "That it is performed by a junior
    manager of the department who possesses the keys to the shrine is an everyday staging of state
    power, a reminder that the shrine of Sehwan is no longer familial inheritance but public asset"
    (p. 83, folio 66). Access is restricted: "only those men assigned the task of washing are allowed
    to enter at this time" (p. 84, folio 67); "The ghusl or bathing of the saint is considered an
    intimate event that requires privacy. The presence of women inside the durbar during the ceremony

    my friend and fellow anthropologist Delphine Ortis's request to witness the event." (p. 190,
    folio 173, ch. 2 n. 8) "The ritual on Friday morning is more elaborate and considered auspicious,
    given the religious significance of the day." (p. 190, folio 173, ch. 2 n. 6) **It has eroded:**
    "certain services were discontinued; for instance, the cleaning and lighting of five lamps

  - **The flight, and the falcon form.** "Lal is additionally featured flying above in his popular
    falcon form (see fig. i.1)" (p. 21, folio 4); "As a single most defining image of the saint, the

    **The book calls the flight a *minor* miracle** (p. 61, folio 44).
  - **He camped in the grove before entering the town.** "Legends had it that the saint himself had
    camped in these woods prior to his arrival in the city proper, thus earning the grove its name,
    Lal-bagh, the garden of Lal." (p. 58, folio 41); "In the footsteps of Lal, who once camped in the
    grove before approaching the town, with chapter 2 this book arrives in Sehwan proper" (p. 76,

  - **The prayer mat on the river.** "According to one legend, the holy men of the town felt
    threatened by his decision and demanded that he prove the merit of his spiritual achievements.
    Lal asked them to join him for a prayer at the river. As the men arrived, they were astonished to
    see that Lal had spread open his prayer mat on the surface of the river itself while remaining
    afloat. None were able to follow suit, and so they quietly acquiesced." (p. 162, folio 145)
  - **The bowl of milk and the floating flower** — the one legend quoted verbatim from a source, "a
    special publication to mark the saint's fair in 1968": "They sent to him a bowl of milk filled up
    to its brim. This was an implicit sign that this city is already filled with people of Allah
    [saints]. There is no space left for you in the city… Qalandar Shahbaz [Lal] floated a flower in
    the bowl and had it sent across to them. His response therefore was that just as this flower has
    made its place in the bowl full of milk, for me too a similar place will emerge. Not only will
    there be space, but in fact the way in which this flower is floating above the milk, I too, God
    willing, will always remain superior to you." (pp. 162–163, folios 145–146) The brackets
    "[saints]" and "[Lal]" are the book's own. A note generalises it: "According to Michel Boivin, the
    flower in milk is a key trope in Sufi narratives of the Indus valley and also beyond and can be
    witnessed in accounts of other saintly figures like Pir Shams of the Ismaʿilis and also Guru
    Nanak, the spiritual leader and founder of Sikhism (email message to author, September 1, 2014)"
    (p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 17).
  - **The landowner struck dead, and the dog in the grave.** "In one legend, when the saint expressed
    his wish to settle down, the owner refused to give up his claim on the property. Lal struck him
    dead with `single blow[text-layer?]` [a single blow] of his staff. When questioned about the
    incident, the saint declared that he had merely killed a dog. Unconvinced, the family of the
    deceased man dug up the grave to indeed find a dead dog." (p. 163, folio 146)
  - **The night the men could not perform.** "In yet another version, he first arrived in the quarter
    of the city that at the time was inhabited by sex workers. On the night of his arrival, the women
    and their male clients were overcome by an extraordinary aura. None of the men that night, it is
    said, were able to perform sexually… The charisma of a powerful saint had started to take
    effect." (p. 163, folio 146) Kasmani declines to choose between this and the other tradition:
    "Whether one accepts that he first settled at a careful distance from the then walled city or
    that he arrived in the quarter of the sex workers, the alternate character of his choice of
    dwelling confirms a spatial inversion that is characteristic of antinomian piety" (p. 195,
    folio 178, ch. 5 n. 16).
  - **The endless layers at the groin, and the tomb that made itself** — attributed to Amma, "my
    fakir interlocutor at the shrine", and corroborated from other locals: "when the time came to
    bury the saint, the mullahs of the city decided to give him a funerary bath. As they began
    undressing him, there were layers and layers of cloth tied to his groin. Every time they would
    remove a layer, another would surface. That the truth of his genitals was never revealed is proof
    for many that the saint was fiercely celibate. Then came a mysterious voice that asked for the
    body to be veiled from all sides. When the curtains were finally lifted, a miraculous tomb was
    already in place." (p. 163, folio 146)
  - **The inverted fortress.** "Legend has it that his body was cut into pieces by the ruler of the
    time and thrown all across the neighborhood. Also, a furious Lal, on learning what had been done
    to his beloved, turned the fortress upside down" (pp. 128–129, folios 111–112). Told at length:
    Bodlo "would call out the saint's name every day while sweeping clean the alleys of Seh­wan.
    Irked by the constant nuisance, the prince residing in the overlooking fortress ordered that
    Bodlo be killed. But each time Bodlo was killed, he would return to the streets the next
    morning… After six failed attempts, the prince ordered that Bodlo be dismembered… As Lal set foot
    in the city, he called out for his beloved fakir. Returning the call of the master, each and
    every piece of Bodlo's butchered body rose from the skulls of those who had consumed him."
    (pp. 160–161, folios 143–144) "So furious was Lal that to invoke divine punishment, he turned his
    cup over, causing in effect the fortress and its environs to be turned on their head." (p. 161,
    folio 144); a variant: "In another version of the same legend, he used his staff instead of the
    cup to beckon divine punishment. Such details vary across narrations" (p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5
    n. 6). The site is "remembered as the inverted city or the fortress of the infidels" — *ulta
    sheher*, *kafir-qila*, *ulta qila* (p. 161, folio 144; p. 147, folio not stated; pp. 128–129,
    folios 111–112). "The solemn memory of Lal's wrath was etched in its topography, where he was
    believed to have turned on its head what was once an ancient settlement" (p. 156, folio 139).
    **The ruler is not named, no date is given, and the fortress is not named beyond its nickname:
    (not stated) on all three.**
  - **Precedence for Bodlo, as a saying.** "'Whoever comes to me must first go to Bodlo,' Lal is
    believed to have said, or so goes the saying in Sehwan" (p. 127, folio 110); "Most pilgrims
    therefore make their first offerings at Bodlo, making the otherwise small shrine a destination of
    great importance in Sehwan" (p. 127, folio 110).
  - **The she-camel, and the seal on rival lodges.** "According to local legend, Bodlo's `she-­camel`,
    a gift from Lal, would itself leave the lodge each evening to collect food for its fakirs."
    (p. 193, folio 176, ch. 4 n. 13) "As per local legend, since fakirs of the other lodges had
    colluded with each other and killed Bodlo's camel, Lal ordered that they henceforth be marked
    with a seal to distinguish them from fakirs of Bodlo." (p. 194, folio 177, ch. 4 n. 35)
  - **Marriage to the saint.** Murad "had in fact quite literally spoken of the possibility of
    contracting a marriage with Lal… 'When Lal [the saint] contracts a nikah with someone, that
    someone is then his wife'" (p. 138, folio 121). Zaheda held "that a fakir woman, owing to her
    gender, had the distinct potential to be close to the saint in a way that would not be possible
    for male fakirs" and that her pursuit "includes the possibility of being chosen by the saint as
    his spiritual wife" (pp. 111, 138, folios 94, 121).
  - **The saint as arbiter of movement, and as punisher.** "for most of my interlocutors, fakirs or
    otherwise, arriving in and departing from Sehwan was always contingent upon Lal's approval"
    (p. 150, folio 133); "fakirs believe that saints summon them" (p. 117, folio 100). Amma's own
    words: "It was in the durbar that the saint said, 'I will bend your foot!' My foot got sprained
    for the first time in the durbar… Then a second time, a third time, and then my foot got burned."
    (p. 91, folio 74); after she missed an ʿurs instruction, "As the ʿurs passed, my condition got
    serious. I was finished. For two months, I could not perform my duty, neither would I be able
    come to the durbar, nothing!" (p. 91, folio 74) And a grant: "I begged Lal to rid me of this
    monthly cycle of impurity" (p. 94, folio 77), after which "Amma first told me that she was like
    me, in her words, clean, and thus suggesting she was rid of menstruation" (p. 96, folio 79).
    **Kasmani reports the claim; he does not adjudicate it.**
  - **Non-human company at the shrine.** Two fairies from "the fabled mountains of Qaf (Koh-Qaf)"
    "had once arrived in Sehwan in their amorous pursuit of Lal" (p. 154, folio 137). Shah-Bibi's
    account: "There exists an atmosphere where all are present: the ones made of light, made of fire,
    angels — of the entire universe. Friends/allies of Allah, prophets, all of them . . . every qutb,
    ghaws, wali, pir, fakir [categories of saintly figures]. Let me tell you this one thing, that this
    is the main tower!" (p. 156, folio 139) — "main tower" is her English term, "a reference to
    telecommunications masts and structures". "Sehwan, according to her description, was one among
    three vertebrae that hold the earth in place, Karbala and the Hijaz being the other two" (p. 155,
    folio 138). "Locals also boast that Sehwan is a sacred geography with 125,000 shrines or that it
    has forever been a place of pilgrimage." (p. 164, folio 147)
- **Significance**
  - "Saints, even if ubiquitous, are divisive figures in Pakistan, though none is more vexing than
    the saint in question" (p. 20, folio 3). "Lal Shahbaz Qalandar is a plurivocal figure and a
    ubiquity hard to miss. He embodies a historical continuum and bridges multiple and oftentimes
    contradicting genealogies attributed to him" (pp. 57–58, folios 40–41). "Lal, I would argue, is a
    figure of historial abundance, evading as often as he recurs in Shiʿi, Sufi, or Hindu guises."
    (p. 63, folio 46) "Lal and Sehwan are inseparable figures, always in narration." (p. 165,
    folio 148)
  - "The saint is at the forefront of a struggle for spatial and imaginative dominion. He is more
    than a world-rejecting, miracle-making figure… Lal's portrayal is deeply implicated in the social
    and political life of the town." (p. 165, folio 148) "He fights the old order with miraculous
    conduct, strikes down his foes, and lays the foundation for new communities and institutions:
    fakirs, especially with their communities and lodges…" (p. 165, folio 148) "In the turning upside
    down of a fortress, in his emasculating impact in acts of sex, in the floating metaphor of the
    brimming cup, in his prevailing over the river as well as in his takeover of territory is a
    remaking of place: the place of Lal." (p. 164, folio 147)
  - "Lal's unresolved history explains why his shrine, along with its relics, revenues, services, and
    ceremonies, has remained riddled by different claims. It has historically attracted
    long-drawn-out disputes of authority involving fakirs, princely patrons, sayyid elite,
    traditional custodians, and, more recently, the Pakistani state." (p. 36, folio 19)
  - "What makes the modern story of governing shrines infinitely more intriguing in the case of
    Sehwan is how, despite the explicitly Sunni-Islamic disposition and agenda of the state, its
    infrastructures have systematically furthered a historically Shivaite and an ascendant Shiʿi site
    of devotion" (p. 72, folio 55). "It is not exactly surprising that Sehwan came to be adopted as a
    site of communal identification and resistance politics for the Pakistani Shiʿa during the 1990s,
    a decade synonymous with an intensification of sectarian violence and political division in the
    country" (p. 71, folio 54). "plural conceptions of the social and compound ideas of the di-vine
    that figures like Lal and Sehwan bear forth will always be unsettling in a Muslim-national present
    that rests on a severance from its polythe-istic Hindu past." (p. 28, folio 11)
  - "the shrine of Sehwan is also the core of its material and spatial relations" (p. 81, folio 64,
    citing Boivin 2011, 89 – 125 on devotional artifacts attributed to Lal). "Whether Shiʿi or
    Shivaite, Sunni or Sufi, Sehwan, despite its prominence, has only haltingly attracted the interest
    of studies on Pakistan." (p. 44, folio 27) The book's own bibliography of the place is given at
    p. 186, folio 169, intro n. 54.

## Cross-cutting material

### Practices, institutions, economy

#### Shrines as welfare infrastructure, and as a public genre

- "As parallel networks of social welfare, Sufi shrines provide free food, healing, and shelter to
  the poor in Pakistan. These are sites where devotees make vows and en-ter into beneficial
  transactions with saints and where saints return favors and generate revenue and work
  opportunities; where pilgrims cross reli-gious lines, share substances and rituals, meet fakirs,
  and find living spiri-tual masters." (p. 28, folio 11) "It is here that women deal with spirits;
  their radical desires can be publicly owned at shrines, their sorrows proverbially eaten away
  (Taneja 2017, 224, 266)." (p. 28, folio 11)
- "Pakistani women visit shrines for all manner of reasons: to make offerings, to sing tributes, to
  celebrate, to complain, to cry their hearts out, to find healing, and sometimes to simply chill on
  their day off — but equally to mend their circumstances, domestic or otherwise, petitioning saints
  as they remake their worlds" (p. 105, folio 88). One case is a property dispute brought to the
  shrine: Zaheda's mother came "to petition the saint regarding a pending dispute over a family
  property" (p. 119, folio 102).
- "Islamic saints as walis are entitled to wilayat, a concept that empowers their figures with
  territo-rial influence and spatial authority." (p. 29, folio 12) "It is no coincidence that
  saints' shrines, as seats of wilayat, are commonly regarded as durbars in South Asia, that is,
  royal courts or courts of law (Bellamy 2011)." (p. 29, folio 12) "Saints… are also known to act as
  judges who arbitrate conflicts and pronounce verdicts." (p. 29, folio 12) "the authority of a wali,
  which stems from the saint's intimacy with Allah, is made manifest in his ability and authority to
  protect a region or wilayat" (pp. 195–196, folios 178–179, ch. 5 n. 18); "taming the wild is a key
  characteristic of Sufis and Islamic saints as friends of Allah" (p. 196, folio 179, ch. 5 n. 22).

#### The state and shrine governance

- **What the Auqaf department says it does.** "According to the department's website, its aims and
  objectives include the management of shrines and mosques, providing pilgrim facilities like
  `com-munity[text-layer?]` [community] kitchens and rest houses." (pp. 188–189, folios 171–172,
  ch. 1 n. 25)
- **The secularising reading, and the instruments.** "in order to be modern, 'it was necessary to
  play down the shrine or at least represent it as a worldly institution and thus to take away its
  religious character' (Malik 1990, 76)" (p. 69, folio 52). "The coupling of modern hospitals and
  dispensaries with religious sites of healing or as attachments to a shrine complex can also be read
  in such terms (Ewing 2006, 73)" (p. 69, folio 52); "annual saints' fairs as public holidays and the
  listing of shrines in state-sponsored tourism booklets served to open up shrines to a differently
  motivated public, like tourists and cultural enthusiasts" (p. 69, folio 52); "public projects such
  as libraries as part of shrine complexes encouraged 'a scholarly rather than a magical approach to
  shrines and Sufism,' while similar infrastructural investment came with the assurance that in
  replacing traditional authorities, the state 'was not out to destroy the shrines themselves' (2006,
  74, 75)" (p. 69, folio 52).
- **The fair rebuilt as a state event.** "As events of the state, saints' annual fairs were
  systematically expanded and turned into elaborate settings. Here, in full public view of the
  gathered pilgrims, the state was able to stage its new symbolic role. It could now place itself
  where a traditional custodian once stood, an intermediary of sorts, facilitating contact between
  the saint and the pilgrim, and in so doing acquiring a kind of legitimacy for its own
  `authority.27[text-layer?]`" (p. 69, folio 52).
- **Colonial versus postcolonial reach.** "in the colonial period, 'the state did not try to control
  shrines directly through its bureaucracy'; however, the postcolonial state has gone further,
  especially by virtue of implementing a unique politico-religious ideology", involving "the state's
  desire to harness the pluralistic traditions of shrines in favor of 'representing a Singular Islam'
  (Ibad 2019, introduction)" (p. 69, folio 52) — **Ibad's study covers "only central Punjab"** (p. 69,
  folio 52).
- **The historical precedent the state claims.** "elite and imperial patronage of Sufis has taken
  varied forms and met various `ends.37[text-layer?]`… As Omid Safi has noted, systems of patronage
  aimed at Sufi persons, places, networks, institutions, and pilgrimages have regularly secured, for
  both Sufis and sultans, political legitimacy and popular support, a binding process of exchange he
  calls 'bargaining of baraka' (2000, 265)" (p. 72, folio 55). "By installing a bureaucratic
  structure, expanding access to the saint and viewing itself as the latest in a series of Muslim
  rulers who have acted as the shrine's patrons, the Pakistani state is able to lay claim to the
  shrine's symbolic reserves `as well its[text-layer?]` [as well as its] manifold revenues, which it
  can disperse and dispose of as it wills, often in alliance with the local sayyid elite or the
  shrine's former custodians (Kasmani 2019a; Ewing 2006, 75)" (p. 82, folio 65).
- **Disruption that accommodates.** "In placing a state official next to the saint, right where the
  sayyid custodian once stood, by collecting donations in his name, and through patronage of new or
  nonhereditary claimants, the state disrupts an already existing order of authority at the shrine"
  (p. 85, folio 68). But: "Drastic as state measures have been, its interventions at the shrine have
  not dismantled operative logics of hierarchy and power in Sehwan. Instead, the state disrupts only
  to accommodate itself." (p. 86, folio 69)
- **A shrine that beat the state in court.** Of Bodlo's shrine in Sehwan — **not an archive row** —
  "Its present-day custodians were able to fight a case against the state in the courts back in the
  1960s, successfully arguing that the shrine of Bodlo was in fact a legitimate family inheritance."
  (p. 192, folio 175, ch. 4 n. 4) "This idea of direct lineage is, more critically, the same legal
  reasoning through which the shrine of Bodlo, despite its economic viability and promise, was
  secured back through a court case when the Pakistani state had sought to take control of the shrine
  under the Department of Auqaf." (p. 142, folio 125) **No date, no court and no case name: (not
  stated).** Recorded here rather than under any archive row: the book never says this of Lal's
  shrine.
- **What falls outside Auqaf.** "compared to Baba-Akram's dwelling in the grove, Jamal and Bibi were
  not directly affected by bureaucratic decisions of the state, since the graveyard is not subject to
  Department of Auqaf administration." (p. 159, folio 142) Against which the grove was not exempt:
  "Baba had been evicted from their dwelling of forty years by the Department of Auqaf. As a site
  associated with the saint, this piece of land was public property, and the department, it now
  seemed, was taking a renewed interest in the upkeep of the grove, ridding it of what it considered
  encroachments. Where the beautiful dwelling once stood was now a characterless park, the
  department's shabby attempt at literally re-creating the garden of Lal" (p. 74, folio 57, dated to
  a return "In March 2018"); "The dwelling of Baba's dreams was real no more, the ʿalam now
  dismantled to fit the new layout, and a fakir left to cope with a future, impaired" (p. 75,
  folio 58). The cost: "a local landowner had come to the rescue, offering a temporary place of
  refuge in a vacant and walled compound. This was a much bigger plot of land for which Baba had to
  pay monthly rent. Without the open publicness of the garden, Baba could no longer perform a fakir
  persona or host passing pilgrims." (p. 75, folio 58)

#### The fakir lodge (kafi) as an institution and an economy

*All of the following is about Bodlo-kafi in Sehwan, which is **not an archive row**; it is the
book's fullest institutional description and is recorded here so it is not silently attached to
Lal's shrine.*

- "Lodges such as Bodlo-kafi are male-only fakir institutions in Sehwan" (p. 126, folio 109); it "is
  the largest in Sehwan, which, according to one estimate, had **eighty initiates, at least twenty of
  whom were long-term or permanent residents** (Ortis 2020, 316)" (p. 128, folio 111) — **the
  estimate is Ortis's and the book marks it as an estimate.**
- "The one at Bodlo is also prominent because it has a prominent shrine attached to it, though,
  **unlike the shrine of Lal, this one is privately run**" (p. 128, folio 111). "Both lodge and
  shrine are headed by a single custodian figure who is also the patron and spiritual master of all
  fakirs residing at the lodge. It is these fakirs, and not state officials, who perform ritual
  services at Bodlo's shrine and manage its everyday routines and revenues" (p. 128, folio 111).
- **The complex.** "a growing complex that includes several secondary units spread around the fabled
  hill. These include a community kitchen, a number of fireplaces associated with fakir gathering
  (machh), minor shrines and pavilions, a section of the custodian's old family home, a rest house
  for visiting pilgrims, and an underused mosque as well as a stable for horses" (p. 129, folio 112).
  **No building dates, architect or cost: (not stated).**
- **The bazaar funds the lodge.** "most shops in the bazaar leading to the shrine are in fact
  properties of the custodian, which confirm his growing influence in this part of town but also
  provide the economic means to run the lodge" (p. 129, folio 112).
- **Recruitment reaches beyond Sehwan.** "An entire network of satellite fakir dwellings can be found
  beyond Sehwan. These are reclusive settings established on the edges of towns and outside villages
  by journeying fakirs of the lodge, whose companies inspire a young and male following on the way."
  (p. 129, folio 112) "It explains to some extent why fakir initiates at Bodlo's lodge are
  predominantly Punjabi speaking, drawn from outside the region of Sindh." (p. 193, folio 176, ch. 4
  n. 8)
- **Admission, initiation, renaming.** "Fakir admission in the lodge was… conditional on the
  custodian's approval and involved rites and ceremonies of formal initiation… an aspiring fakir must
  find a fakir referee in order to be considered for initiation into the lodge. **Admission doesn't
  equal initiation**" (p. 130, folio 113). "Initiation marks an important transition from the death
  of a social subject to the birth of a new talib… The new fakir receives a wardi and with it he is
  given a new name" (p. 133, folio 116). The sequence: "the initiate must shave his body entirely,
  upon which he is given a ritual bath by a senior fakir, just as the dead are washed before burial.
  Then dressed in just a loincloth, he is presented to the murshid. He first prostrates himself,
  expressing his complete submission, then, placing one thumb on the other, the initiate stands in
  the audience of his master. He returns born anew, as it were, equipped with a red garment, a new
  name, and a reformed status as a confirmed fakir." (p. 143, folio 126) "As a first symbolic act,
  the new fakir is sent to beg in the surrounding areas, sealing his status of servitude." (p. 143,
  folio 126) "As a token of this new master-disciple relationship, she notes, the novice is given a
  secret word or unique formula for meditation (Ortis 2020, 317)." (pp. 143–144, folios 126–127)
  "At other lodges in Sehwan, a hot seal is applied on the fakir's arm. With it, a pact of hierarchy
  between the master and fakir is also sealed" (p. 143, folio 126).
- **Duties, and unwaged dependence with a stipend.** "Fakirs at the lodge are assigned different
  duties like the safekeeping of visitors' shoes at the entrance of the shrine, the preparation of
  day meals, and the daily collection of food donations from the various quarters of the city in the
  evenings" (p. 131, folio 114); "Most duties are rotated". "Fakirs also serve as the labor force of
  the custodian; some may work his lands, and others might help construct new units of the lodge.
  Fakirs of a lodge are **not employed** but are dependent on their sayyid masters for their
  subsistence; they receive a **daily stipend**" (p. 131, folio 114) — **the amount is (not
  stated)**.
- **The dachi round.** "Every evening, right after the drumming ritual (dhamal) was over at the
  shrine of Bodlo, he would perform the traditional role of Bodlo's she-camel (dachi). Ringing a
  large bell in one hand, which was hung around his neck, he would do the rounds of Sehwan for about
  forty-five minutes. Barefoot and completely mute, head and eyes lowered, his task as a dachi was to
  walk the streets of Sehwan, collecting milk in a bucket and food in a big pouch, carried on the
  shoulder and across the chest." (p. 132, folio 115)
- **Submission and its sanction.** "A key organizing principle of kafis in Sehwan is that they tie
  fakirs with their patrons in intimate bonds of dependence… The master, most often of sayyid
  descent, acts as their guarantor and protector, meaning that fakirs must profess full submission to
  his will and authority" (p. 131, folio 114) — glossed earlier as "(unwritten) contracts of patronage
  and structures of dependence" (p. 126, folio 109). "In the event of a serious dispute, the master
  reserves the right to revoke fakir privileges and provisions. Excommunication threatens a fakir's
  removal from chains of saintly access as well as from the larger social network of fakirs."
  (p. 131, folio 114) "Fakir intransigence was rarely tolerated, and patrons were known to
  excommunicate wayward fakirs, although this was not reported in Murad's case." (p. 145, folio 128)
- **The wardi as an office.** "what is referred to as wardi here is not restricted to the long red
  shirt… there were other items like scarf, rings, bracelets, necklaces, and earrings… Fakir objects
  customarily include items like the begging bowl and the baton" (p. 137, folio 120); the set is
  "hal-vaslah [fakir accessories and objects]" (p. 136, folio 119). Dost: "'when someone commits a
  mistake, the master, according to principle, takes the wardi back'" (p. 136, folio 119), and a
  "policeman on duty has to surrender his gun and his wardi if he is suspended" (p. 137, folio 120).
  "Taking off the shroud-like garment is a symbolic occasion of return to straight paths of the
  social." (p. 145, folio 128) "Fakir spiritual prowess had to be **acquired through practice and
  action (ʿamal)** and **not bestowed as an irrevocable gift (ʿata) of the sayyid pir**" (p. 137,
  folio 120).
- **Marriage rules, unequally applied.** "None of the elite sayyid figures of Sehwan I have spoken to
  over the years have categorically denied or discounted the possibility and permissibility of
  marriage for their fakir initiates. Yet each one of them stressed that an engagement with or a
  pursuing of profane and corporeal pleasures was bound to fundamentally affect the capacities and
  powers of a fakir… loyalty to one's murshid and complete devotion to a saintly figure precluded the
  pursuit of other intimacies" (p. 143, folio 126). "Celibacy wasn't a condition for residing at the
  lodge, though it was central to its ethos" (p. 127, folio 110). A marriage with permission cost
  rank but not membership: "the wado-fakir would have to give up his chief role at the lodge, settling
  for a lesser position, he had, unlike Murad, managed to salvage his membership in the fakir
  fraternity" (p. 142, folio 125); "There were fathers and married fakirs at the lodge, after all"
  (p. 145, folio 128). "Fakir conditions on marriage do not apply in equal measure to custodian
  figures like `Nur-­Shah`, who must produce male heirs in order to maintain lines and systems of
  routinized spiritual authority." (p. 194, folio 177, ch. 4 n. 32)
- **The office that handles the money.** "the lodge's everyday affairs, especially the distribution of
  tasks and the management of the shrine's daily income and fakir expenses, were in the hands of
  another fakir who held the office of wado-fakir, literally big or senior fakir" (pp. 141–142, folios
  124–125). **Which shrine's income is not specified: (not stated).**

#### Fakiri as a livelihood, and the shrine as a labour market

- "Fakir — also the word for beggar in Urdu, at times an honorific for transgenders in Sindhi — here
  refers to persons who take up voluntary poverty as an as-cetic performance of devotion." (p. 25,
  folio 8) "Many live on donations from pil-grims and devotees, but some also rely on their practice
  as spiritual guides and healers to run households and support families. Acquiring spiritual roles
  also means that fakirs earn a measure of respect, which not only comes to remedy the disadvantage
  that such persons might bear in terms of economic class and social status, but as in the case of
  women, also ad-vances their otherwise rare prospects for a public life." (p. 26, folio 9)
- "Fakirs in this book are distinctive individuals who arrive in Sehwan as a result of prophetic
  dreams and personal callings, some in search of mystical mentors; others leave home in the face of
  family feuds, accidents, court cases, illnesses, disappointment in love, or simply out of severe
  economic or social deprivation." (pp. 25–26, folios 8–9)
- "a publicly owned shrine offers fakirs like Amma opportunities for `self-­brokered` access to the
  saint, to operate without hereditary masters, to establish an independent following and earn a
  living as healers and spiritual guides — not a given if it were still a privately held heritage in
  the hands of the sayyid elite" (p. 99, folio 82). Amma, "through her reputation and daily practice
  as a spiritual guide and healer at the shrine, was the sole breadwinner for a family of five"
  (p. 86, folio 69). "Consulting fakirs and pirs in the shrine courtyard was commonplace" (p. 103,
  folio 86). **No sums, rates or income figures anywhere: (not stated).**
- **A spot in the durbar is property.** "one of the ways to also identify a fakir at the shrine of
  Sehwan was to pay attention to the consistency of a fakir's presence at a particular spot"; the
  work of "finding, establishing, and maintaining a regular spot in the durbar" is "arduous and often
  `time-­taking`" (pp. 97–98, folios 80–81). "Every time her place among men was questioned, whether
  by pilgrims or by newly arriving officials of the shrine, Amma had been quick to remind them how a
  growing shrine had overtaken her place and not the other way around." (p. 99, folio 82)
- **Hereditary healing, transmitted by cup.** A sayyid healer's "gift of healing, as his brother
  explained to me, was divinely bestowed upon their family. It has been transmitted over generations
  and, as was characteristic of local sayyids, was transferred from father to son by means of a
  ceremony that involves symbolically drinking spirituality from a cup (pyala)." (p. 88, folio 71)
  "`ʿata`, once bestowed, cannot be revoked; it can only be transmitted down" (p. 90, folio 73),
  whereas women "must strive to acquire their capacities through a tenuous process that involves
  individual exercise and sustained action (riyazat). Failure to secure its conditions could result
  in such powers being lost or, worse still, revoked." (p. 90, folio 73)
- **Sayyid power is not only spiritual.** "Sayyid power in Sehwan wasn't simply a spiritual matter";
  their positions "as pirs but also adjudicators and patrons are deeply grounded in Sehwani life"
  (p. 89, folio 72). "As descendants of the Prophet, sayyids enjoy historical, material, and
  spiritual privilege unlike other spiritual figures in Sehwan" (pp. 183–184, folios 166–167, intro
  n. 18). "In tracing their lineage to the Prophet, a predisposition to patrilineal structures of
  inheritance not only empowers sayyids but also equips them with charismatic systems to reproduce
  and consolidate their claim on the shrine and its resources over `time.3[text-layer?]`" (p. 81,
  folio 64).

#### Women, gender-variant persons and access

- **Exclusion from the lodges, and its consequence.** "Women and `khwaja-­sara` individuals are
  systemically excluded from such networks, rarely accommodated at fakir lodges in Sehwan, and must
  therefore find other, mostly independent, ways to pursue fakir lives." (p. 89, folio 72) "Sayyid
  control over fakir lodges regulates the exclusion of women from fakir ranks through retaining
  rights of admission and presiding over rites of initiation." (p. 99, folio 82) "affective
  formations at the lodge are premised on the exclusion of women and transgender fakirs" (p. 129,
  folio 112). One documented exception: "there was a brief period of time when two female-presenting
  fakirs were residing at Bodlo. Their status… was unclear at best, and their participation did not
  extend to all aspects of the lodge's daily life. They were also housed in a private section of the
  lodge. Their exceptional status was proof that maleness was in fact the norm at the lodge if not
  also a prerequisite for full membership" (p. 130, folio 113) — **neither woman is named and no
  dates are given: (not stated)**.
- **The state-run shrine as the alternative.** "In the absence of institutional patronage like that
  available to men across fakir lodges in Sehwan, women turn to saints at publicly administered
  shrines. As discussed in chapter 2, fakir paths for women are advanced under the patronage of the
  state, but their claims of extraordinariness must be validated through personal experience and the
  embodiment of endurance, and, in particular, their narrativization of dreams and visions, pain and
  suffering, powers and capacities" (p. 110, folio 93). "Women's public circumstances are less
  suspect at shrines, tempered by the dual protection of the saint and the state." (p. 183, folio
  166, intro n. 16) "The setting of the courtyard offered a measured publicness, a place where women
  guides like her could interact with unrelated people in relative safety and eventually establish a
  following. Space is indeed key for women's religious careers" (p. 107, folio 90).
- **Double disadvantage, and the workaround.** "Neither male nor sayyid, figures like Amma and
  Mastani are therefore doubly disadvantaged." (p. 89, folio 72) "An exclusion from dominant
  tradition means that women must innovate with what is available to them." (p. 90, folio 73) "With
  meager institutional support or sanction, for many women fakirs the key to and the proof of their
  fakir credentials lies in their embodied experiences and accounts of dealing with spirits from the
  saint." (p. 90, folio 73) "it is a fakir spirit, always male, that furnishes women like Amma's
  outward claims to be fakirs themselves" (p. 87, folio 70), and "messages from the saint are
  rendered intelligible via the mediating spirit" (p. 87, folio 70, note 12), recoverable "only after
  the performance is over through the explanations of her husband" (p. 87, folio 70). "when women
  cohabit with spirits to partially remedy their historical exclusion from local systems of
  patrilineal charisma" (p. 42, folio 25).
- **Motherhood as qualification, and purity as obstacle.** "The commonly adopted and given title
  `amma` (also `ma` and `mata`), in the South Asian context, lends women in godly pursuits the
  postsexual authority of a mother critical to their public circumstances (Pechilis 2012, 123)."
  (p. 95, folio 78) "Motherhood was in fact what paved the way for women's spiritual careers"
  (p. 96, folio 79, note 26). "In South Asia, menstruating and postpartum women can be considered to
  be in an active state of pollution, attributed with a capacity to defile, which prevents them from
  full participation in many ritual practices across various faiths." (p. 94, folio 77) Amma: "After
  my third child, I sat outside the durbar as I was `na-­pak` [lit. impure; unclean]; I begged Lal to
  rid me of this monthly cycle of impurity. What kind of fakiri would that be, if one had to stay away
  from him [the saint] for a week every month?" (p. 94, folio 77, note 21)
- **Celibacy demanded of women fakirs.** "Saintly instructions for women initiates to maintain bodily
  purity and integrity were not unheard of. In fact, the requirement to suspend sexual relations was
  a frequent one. Premised on ideals of purity and fidelity of the woman fakir, it was possibly also
  a sign of possessiveness on the part of the saint, though it was often articulated as a demand made
  by the cohabiting spirit." (p. 96, folio 79) "other women fakirs I knew had spoken of their
  struggles to keep their bodies clean and pure by refusing sex with their husbands" (p. 112,
  folio 95). "speaking of discontinuing their sexual relations with their husbands, women would try
  to establish that their marital ties did not correlate to those of a husband and wife" (pp. 134–135,
  folios 117–118).
- **Purdah relaxed for fakirs.** "as much as fakir figures were subject to cultural norms of
  segregation of the sexes, styles of purdah that women were expected to observe did not apply to
  women fakirs in equal measure. Mastani did not cover her head, and Amma interacted with men all
  day… their fakir roles allowed more relaxed styles of covering and eased participation in public
  contexts." (p. 97, folio 80)
- **Intercession through the vulnerable.** Amma "used the word `bechari` (one who is helpless) to
  describe a woman, though in so doing she had effectively turned a notion of vulnerability into a
  valuable trait… it was almost impossible for the saint to refuse a plea when forwarded by a woman
  fakir" (p. 93, folio 76). Her husband added the Sindhi idiom: "the concept of `niyani`, or little
  sister in Sindhi society… among locals, the greatest of conflicts can be resolved if one of the
  feuding parties is able get a niyani to intervene on their behalf. Women fakirs were therefore
  perfect instruments for intercession in his opinion." (p. 94, folio 77)
- **Terminology.** "Gender-variant persons in Sehwan refer to themselves in varying registers, like
  khadra (in Sindhi), `khwaja-­sara` (in Urdu), or simply fakir." (p. 186, folio 169, intro n. 51)
  "Intersex as well as feminine-identified, gender-variant persons assigned male at birth (locally,
  `khwaja-­sara`, hijra, khadra, khusra, transgender, fakir) assume a distinct subjectivity in
  Pakistan. Some of these terms are now disfavored in urban Pakistan and only situationally adopted
  by gender-variant persons and communities themselves." (p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 24) "Even if
  fewer than men, women fakirs were never hard to come by in Sehwan. Also, some of my fakir
  interlocutors have in fact been gender-variant persons, whether intersex or transgender." (p. 41,
  folio 24) — **no number is given: (not stated)**. Of a gender-variant fakir's household future:
  "a most likely trajectory for a child such as Baba would have eventually involved a life with the
  `khwaja-­sara.38[text-layer?]` Given male names at birth and in most cases also registered as sons,
  intersex sons do not extend the social promise of family lines that cisgender sons are expected to
  offer. If not given up by their parents, such children, once older, seek gurus and masters
  themselves and are known to leave family in favor of khwaja-sara households (Kasmani 2021)"
  (pp. 73–74, folios 56–57).
- **Religion inside Pakistani transgender activism, reported at second hand.** "In an account of
  religion within transgender circles in Pakistan, Claire Pamment (2019b) has revealed how pious
  performance works hand in hand with trans visibility and political activism… Pamment argues that
  pious acts 'offer an embodied outlet for negotiating multiple axes of exclusion' as well as
  'provide a potent force for trans activism in Pakistan, further strengthening access to rights'
  (2019b, 310)." (p. 174, folio 157)

#### Bhes, celibacy and the body

- "a fakir grammar known as `bhes` (also `ves`)", "[l]iterally meaning both guise and disguise"
  (p. 103, folio 86). "Bhes can be worn, received, or, in its most frequent fakir usage, one is in
  bhes. It refers to a collectivity of attributes that include but are not limited to styles of
  dressing, bodily adornment, and social behavior, spatial practice and place-based relationships,
  codes of diet and sexual conduct, and manners of sitting and walking as well as styles of speech"
  (p. 108, folio 91). "Fakir guises in Sehwan… are coded through forms of costume, color,
  accessories, social behavior, and habitat" (p. 41, folio 24).
- The three vernacular terms for celibacy: **jatti-satti**, "a term associated with seven celibate
  saints of Sehwan" (p. 127, folio 110), used "to refer to a person's nonsexual condition as well as
  unmarried status" (p. 135, folio 118); **lung-langot**, "a garment that covers the loins", used "to
  refer to maintaining sexual abstinence and celibacy among fakirs" (p. 134, folio 117); and
  **lung-band**, "literally the sealing of a man's loins, which suggests a refusal of all forms of
  sexual pleasure" (p. 135, folio 118). "Vows of abstinence… were not always lifelong commitments and
  could also be taken for limited periods of one's life" (p. 135, folio 118).
- The fourfold shave: "The observance of the fourfold shave (the shaving of the head and all facial
  hair, including the moustache, beard, and eyebrows) is an ascetic practice characteristic of
  qalandari piety (see Karamustafa 2006, 19)." (p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 19)
- "The fakir garment, which Murad often described as shroud-like, was both performance and reminder
  that the fakir is comparable to the dead. Though not being dead per se, the fakir is susceptible to
  sexual desire" (p. 134, folio 117); "'The fakir is not dead; he has the attributes of the dead.'"
  (p. 134, folio 117)
- Fakir accoutrements, quoted from Karamustafa: in the Middle Period (`1200 – 1550 ce`) they "could
  include 'woollen or felt garment or animal hide, cap, begging bowl, pouch, spoon, club, belt, bell,
  hatchet, lamp or candle, razor, needle, flint stone, and musical instruments (commonly tambourine,
  drum and pipe).'" (p. 194, folio 177, ch. 4 n. 26)

#### The qalandar as a historical type

- "Qalandars were part of an anarchistic ethos specific to cultures of the Persian world, and who
  resisted the institutionalization and elite formations of Sufi brotherhoods of the time. Liberated
  from social custom and often regarded as be-shar (without shariah/law), a defining characteristic
  of qalandars, as Ahmet Karamustafa (2006) notes, was their 'persistent cultural and social
  iconoclasticism and unconventionalism.' Such figures were notorious for transgressive bodily acts,
  extreme isolation, perpetual wandering, and an ostentatious rejection of social-religious
  institutions and authorities." (p. 34, folio 17) "Qalandars as a distinct group of deviant dervishes
  formed in the thirteenth century. Their existence as a mode of religiosity is evidenced in Persian
  literature (qalandar as topos) and Sufi theoretical treatises as early as the eleventh century; see
  Karamustafa (2006, 32)." (p. 184, folio 167, intro n. 31) "Qalandars are distinct in that such
  figures, marked by renunciation, celibacy, antinomianism, and itinerancy, turn the idea of such
  spatiality into a defining trait" (p. 196, folio 179, ch. 5 n. 21). "It ought to be said that fakir
  quests in the postcolonial present are shaped by conditions distinct from those of their
  predecessors." (p. 34, folio 17)

### Arguments and interpretations

- **The book's three clusters, in its own summary.** "The many elements that make up the greater
  argument of this book on saintly intimacy and futurity can be regathered here as three conceptual
  clusters, namely, saintly governance, intimate corporealities, and worlding with saints." (p. 178,
  folio 161)
- **Saintly governance.** "saintly vitality in Pakistan thrives on orders of the state; … it is
  sustained by the material-affective infrastructures of governance whose original or official intent
  might well be to curb forms of saintly intimacy or regulate transgressive and hard-to-discipline
  shrine worlds." (p. 56, folio 39) "I have pointed to the often inseparable dynamics of governing
  saints and governing with saints." (p. 178, folio 161) "Whatever the motivations, the governance of
  saints' places in Pakistan cannot be read independently of the state's efforts to popularize them.
  This is an important feature in Sehwan's story" (p. 69, folio 52).
- **Limited democratisation, unintended.** "the state's administrative presence pushes forward a
  limited democratization of shrine space, proliferating further the shrine's historical orders of
  authority" (p. 79, folio 62). "the presence of the state at shrines like Sehwan has afforded, even
  if inadvertently so, women and gender-variant persons an advanced access to saintly futures"
  (p. 72, folio 55); "the emancipatory tenors it might suggest on the part of the state are
  unintended, inadvertent at best (Philippon 2016)" (p. 81, folio 64). "This is particularly salient
  for women's spiritual careers, which, though doubly impaired, both by `patri-lineal[text-layer?]`
  [patrilineal] transmission of saintly authority and an ascribed femaleness to their bodies, are to
  some extent secured by the state's arbitrating role at public shrines." (p. 178, folio 161)
  "Saintly governance more provocatively suggests that as fakirs come close to the saintly in
  Pakistan, they also draw nearer to the state." (p. 178, folio 161)
- **Shrine attack as a contest of sovereignty.** "One could argue that at stake in recent violent
  confrontations is a contest of mediation, not interpretation of religion" (p. 29, folio 12). "In a
  historical and transcultural account of shrine destruction, Ali Azfar Moin (2015) has argued that
  confrontational acts of violence and desecration of religious sites are better read as enactments
  of sovereignty: to the extent that the attack at Sehwan by antistate actors and networks is an
  assertion of rival sovereignty through an act of shrine desecration, it also perversely confirms
  the enshrined saint as mediator and sovereign" (p. 29, folio 12). **Kasmani refuses the obvious
  framing**: folding the bombing together with a fakir's story "is not to give voice to the easy
  discourse of Sufis-under-threat or, for that matter, to pitch good Is-lam versus bad" (p. 29,
  folio 12).
- **Qurb, the book's key term.** The epigraph glosses it `| قُربqurb[text-layer?]` [قُرب  qurb]
  "(Urdu, from Arabic) Intimacy, closeness, affective nearness, trust" (p. 18, folio not stated).
  "Qurb, from which the word qarib derives, is a con-cept that stands for affective nearness and
  conditions of intimacy. Thus, to be close in this instance is not simply to be nazdik, the other
  Urdu word for proximity" (pp. 26–27, folios 9–10). "intimacy is the name I give to what blooms in
  that indistinct yet critical interstice between being near a thing and being nearly that thing."
  (p. 39, folio 22) "Qurb helps appreciate how and why fakir pursuits are not so much targeted at
  becoming the object itself, what Sufis would call fana, annihilation into the Truth" (p. 39,
  folio 22). The lexical authority is "Urdu Lughat, http://www.udb.gov.pk/, accessed March 2021."
  (p. 185, folio 168, intro n. 46)
- **Suhbet, the coda's term.** Glossed in its epigraph — `| صحبتsuhbet` — as "Companionship,
  conversation, cohabitation, spiritual meeting, sex" (p. 169, folio not stated). "it audaciously
  folds discourse with intercourse: to do suhbet means to converse or come into dialogue, especially
  in its Persian usage, though in Urdu it can additionally be a euphemism for sharing a bed, a polite
  word for sex, a suggestion of carnal knowledge." (p. 172, folio 155) "In Sufi contexts, it captures
  an intimate occasion marked by the audience of a holy figure or the transmission of spiritual love
  and knowledge between masters and disciples." (p. 173, folio 156)
- **Worlding with saints.** "Worlding with saints hence refers to an ongoing process, an ensemble of
  mutual affordances through which a world accrues significance… just as fakirs turn worldly or
  perceptive with saintly beings, saints too, through fakir acts and feelings, find their bearings in
  the world as the more-than-living." (p. 166, folio 149) Against the renunciatory reading: "fakirs
  are persons who, in everyday parlance, reject the world or renounce the worldly… Yet the graveyard
  tells a different story: fakir ties to the living and the worldly are made dense through their
  affective attachments with its deceased and displaced actors." (pp. 149–150, folios 132–133)
  "attachment to saints and their tombs… is complexly conversant with material realities so much that
  it is a means for becoming worldly in Sehwan" (p. 44, folio 27).
- **Intimacy as a public genre.** "attendant to my deliberations on saintly intimacies is the idea
  that intimacy is a public genre: in coming close to saintly objects, as inwardly moving as such
  process is, individuals do not withdraw, rather draw near to public economies and his-torical
  ecologies of affect." (p. 30, folio 13) "These are… affective formations that imbricate individuals,
  society, and the state in a public architecture of intimacy." (p. 19, folio 2)
- **Unstraight affordances, including against intimacy.** "unstraight affordances are queer affects
  that not only support otherwise ways of being in the world but can equally work against intimacy,
  especially when the pursuit of saintly objects is contracted on pacts of submission and the
  abandonment of rival intimacies. No less interesting is the insight that failures of saintly
  intimacy accompany one's return to economies of the heterosexual family" (p. 126, folio 109).
  "prospects of life that open up or are foreclosed by virtue of one's intimacy with saintly places,
  objects, and figures are in some measure also a queer surplus of governance" (p. 74, folio 57).
- **Sexuality and sacrality, argued in the book's own terms and left unsettled.** "The question of
  celibacy and sexual abstinence among fakirs in Sehwan is a hard one to settle, especially given the
  history and prevalence of nonnormative sexual behavior among qalandars and be-shar mystics or
  Sufis-without-shariah" (p. 134, folio 117); "I wasn't quite able to establish whether ideas of
  abstinence in fakir accounts referred exclusively to marital and reproductive sex, or if they indeed
  excluded nonnormative forms of sexual practice… most fakirs in Sehwan did not directly engage with
  questions of sexual practice" (p. 134, folio 117). Kasmani names and rejects one fakir's binary:
  "In equating the feminine with the sexual, he was reaffirming an easy binary, which holds that
  spirituality, intellect, and rationality are attributes of maleness and men, or that women are
  enormous threats to men's spiritual progress. By such logic… sacrality is incompatible with
  (hetero)sexuality" (pp. 135–136, folios 118–119). Against that he sets Saʿdiyya Shaikh on Ibn
  ʿArabi: "a complete interpenetration of spirituality and sexuality" (2012, 186), "love between men
  and women is an inherent aspect of loving God" (187), "sex provides the mystic with the opportunity
  for one of the most comprehensive experiences of servanthood and receptivity" (189) (p. 136,
  folio 119). And Ali A. Mian on male homosociality: it is "neither female-male nor female-female
  desire, but male-male desire that is channeled into communal formation" (2019, 143), and "despite
  the strong prohibition on anal intercourse, same-sex desire remains unencumbered and serves rather
  as the affective adhesive that pulls men together" (p. 129, folio 112) — which Kasmani applies:
  "Such male-to-male axis of desire in Sehwan is enshrined in the love between Lal and Bodlo and
  lived through fakir homosocial bonds at the lodge" (p. 129, folio 112).
- **Read queer religiously, do not queer religion.** "how might we learn to read queer religiously?
  Such ambition, I reckon, cannot be satisfied by queer-jacketing religion… This, then, is also not a
  call for Islamicizing queer." (p. 170, folio 153) "Hermeneutic efforts to cull queer value from
  diversely sacred aesthetics, Islamic-spiritual inheritances, and religious affect, as I have here
  pursued, is not the same as searching for indigenous queers or rehabilitating queers of faith."
  (p. 171, folio 154) "Unstraight in this work does not characterize fakirs or saints as queer in
  identitarian terms" (p. 196, folio 179, Coda n. 2).
- **The disciplinary indictment, which bears on how shrine entries are written.** "this book doesn't
  sit straight with the Sunni-centering drive of the anthropology of Islam, whose epistemologies are
  predominantly served by, if they have not also produced, an active minoritization of Islam's other
  histories and lifeworlds." (p. 43, folio 26) "writings on saints' places in South Asia too often
  fail to accord Shiʿi histories, beyond the descriptive, their rightful place in the analysis of
  shrine lifeworlds… In such instances, the category Sufi serves to straighten, subsume, even silence
  irregular, antinormative, largely non-Sunni pasts through academic writing (Kasmani 2017a)."
  (p. 44, folio 27) "Debates in the anthropology of Islam are comfortably oblivious to conversations
  in queer theory. On the other side of this equation is queer studies' blind spot for histories and
  figurations of queerness elsewhere, a geopolitical bias that determines why places such as Pakistan
  and settings like religious shrines do not strike us as sites for queer thinking." (p. 180,
  folio 163)
- **His critique of Scott Kugle** — relevant because *Sufis and Saints' Bodies* is a separate item in
  this queue. "Even a rich and thorough study such as Kugle's eventually succumbs to the trope of
  queer origins and their historical loss, not least the binary of an apparently queer-bodied and
  sexuality-affirming good (Sufi) Islam and a body-averse, sexuality-repressing bad Islam."
  (p. 175, folio 158) "the suhbet or grounds of meeting that I am here rooting for are not invested
  in queer's rehabilitation in religious tradition, less so in a project of queering." (p. 175,
  folio 158) "For Kugle, this loss has to do with Islamic fundamentalism and the rise of the Wahhabi
  movement in contemporary Muslim societies" (p. 197, folio 180, Coda n. 10). **This is Kasmani's
  argument about Kugle, not evidence from Kugle.**
- **Method, stated as a limit.** "This book is based on fifteen months of fieldwork. Three early field
  visits took me to Sehwan in 2009 and 2010, each lasting around four weeks." (p. 46, folio 29) "more
  concentrated long-term fieldwork was carried out in two phases. One lasted six months, from
  September 2011 to February 2012; the other was of three months' duration, between June and August
  2012. I returned for a month in November 2013 and finally for three weeks in February 2018."
  (pp. 46 and 48, folios 29 and 31) "The greater material involves `twenty-­four` fakirs, an almost
  equal number of men and women and a few transgender persons." (p. 187, folio 170, intro n. 62)
  Recording was largely abandoned — "Over time and in the company of fakirs, the default method
  shifted to impromptu conversations and sessions of exchange that transpired unrecorded." (p. 46,
  folio 29) "Carefully assembled oral fakir accounts, even if not recorded as one coherent
  biographical interview on a mechanical device, form the basis of each of the chapters in this book."
  (p. 49, folio 32) "The main language of research in the field has been Urdu." (p. 48, folio 31)
  "As a cis-male researcher, I had access to most public spaces as well as fakir lodges and sayyid
  households. At times, it limited my interactions in settings associated with female publics."
  (p. 49, folio 32) The author declined key practices: "I did not once par-ticipate in performances
  like dhamal and matam (drumming and ritual flagellation). Similarly, I chose not to consume any
  intoxicants" (p. 50, folio 33). Archival work: "Fieldwork involved a brief stint at the Sindh
  Archives in Karachi" and "a limited number of colonial historical records, which help situate and
  read the contemporary conflicts around questions of change, the arbitration of authorities, and
  everyday management of the shrine and its resources" (p. 49, folio 32) — **the records are not
  identified: (not stated)**.
- **The book's transliteration policy, which governs how every name in it should be read.** "I adopt
  fakir instead of faqir to better capture the term's vernacular in-flection and alternate etymology
  in Sehwan and not to privilege or repro-duce its colonial referencing"; "my rendering of distinct
  Islamic concepts such as dargah (threshold) as shrine and wali (friend of Allah) as saint, is far
  from ideal given their Christian connotations"; "For the sake of readability, I have also minimized
  the use of diacritics"; "All translations are my own." (p. 10, folio not stated) "My in-terlocutors
  spoke Urdu, Sindhi, Punjabi, Seraiki, and Balochi… my transliterations reflect a diverse
  sociolinguistic ethos and privilege the colloquial and the spoken over their etymological origin or
  written form." (p. 10, folio not stated)

### A. Sites with a named shrine, tomb, custodian or cult — candidates for future rows

- **Bodlo (Bodlo-bahar), Sehwan** — **the strongest candidate in the book, and the single richest
  non-archive subject in it.** "the shrine of Bodlo, the saint's primary disciple" (p. 124, folio not
  stated); "Believed to be his contemporary, Bodlo is the fakir par excellence whose shrine is the
  second most important site for pilgrims in Sehwan today." (p. 21, folio 4) "Only a few hundred
  meters from the graveyard is the shrine of Bodlo." (p. 160, folio 143) His shrine has its own dome,
  visible beside Lal's (p. 161, folio 144); it is "privately run" by "a single custodian figure"
  (p. 128, folio 111), whose family "were in fact the direct descendants of his brother" and who
  "were able to fight a case against the state in the courts back in the 1960s" (p. 142, folio 125;
  p. 192, folio 175, ch. 4 n. 4). "Bodlo, the foundational figure of the lodge, was jatti-satti, a
  term associated with seven celibate saints of Sehwan" (p. 127, folio 110); "That Bodlo was a
  celibate figure was an uncontested matter in Sehwan… it was endorsed by the official discourse of
  the lodge" (p. 142, folio 125) — until the custodian "portrayed Bodlo anew as a married saintly
  figure" by "casually mentioning the occasion of Bodlo's marriage to a noblewoman" (pp. 142–143,
  folios 125–126). His lodge "is considered foundational to the establishment of a system of fourteen
  kafis in Sehwan" (pp. 127–128, folios 110–111); pilgrims must offer to him before Lal (p. 127,
  folio 110); daily dhamal is performed facing his tomb (p. 128, folio 111). His dismemberment and
  resurrection is the town's founding legend (pp. 160–161, folios 143–144). "Bodlo, or `Bodlo-­bahar`
  (in full), is a curious figure in that he is hardly traceable through classical Sufi histories.
  Boivin (2012b, 121) is of the opinion that the Sindhi name Bodlo, meaning innocent young boy, even
  Buddha, and bahar, which means spring, are possibly signs that the figure bears other histories but
  survives in a Sufi guise." (p. 192, folio 175, ch. 4 n. 2) "Since Bodlo appears not to be linked to
  any classical form or figures in the Sufi tradition, Boivin… is of the opinion that the enigmatic
  figure is possibly a remnant of the region's ancient nature-based forms of worship and devotion"
  (p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 5). **No dates, no death year, no silsila, no personal name beyond
  "Bodlo": (not stated).** Named custodian: **Nur-Shah**, whose house is "situated right across from
  the shrine of Bodlo" (p. 142, folio 125) — **the only named shrine custodian in the book's index**.
- **Juman-Jatti, Sehwan** — "a saint in his own right" with a "water-facing shrine… walking distance
  from the graveyard"; his "river-facing shrine" is repeatedly destroyed by flooding and is
  "half-fallen… in contemporary times", explained as the river's revenge for his backing away from
  Lal's door and so turning his back to the Indus (p. 162, folio 145). The author heard the Lal
  legends "from fakirs at the shrine of Juman-Jatti" (p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 10). **No dates, no
  order, no custodian: (not stated). Its survival should be checked before any entry treats it as

### 2. `[text-layer?]` damage inventory, by class

1. **Soft hyphen (U+00AD) rendered inside words — the dominant artefact, book-wide.** Two sub-cases.
   Inside words that *are* hyphenated in print it is harmless (`Bodlo-­kafi`, `jatti-­satti`,
   `khwaja-­sara`, `sajjadah-­nashin`, `Lal-­bagh`, `Baba-­Akram`, `Juman-­Jatti`, `Koh-­Qaf`,
   `Bibiyan-­pak-­daman`, `Pak-­Pattan`, `Lahut-­lamakan`, `Shiv-­asthan`, `hal-­vaslah`,
   `more-­than-­living`, and several hundred more). **Inside words that are not hyphenated in print
   it is dangerous**, because a naive split yields two words: `Seh­wan` for **Sehwan** at pp. 61, 66,
   82, 88–89, 114, 117, 120, 149, 156, 160, 206; `octa­gonal` for **octagonal** (p. 78, folio 61);
   `anti­nomian` (p. 165, folio 148); `homo­sociality` (p. 127, folio 110); `grand­father` (p. 120,
   folio 103); `Seh­wani` and `Naj­mabadi` in citations (p. 192, folio 175; p. 186, folio 169);
   `Ser­vanthood` (p. 214, folio 197); `saintly inti­macy` and `in anthro­pologies` (pp. 220, 222,
   folios 203, 205). **Anyone copying a backticked string out of this file should strip U+00AD before
   searching for it.**
2. **Endnote numbers fused to the preceding word or numeral, with no space and no superscript.** This
   is the class that can poison a fact, and it runs through the whole body text — chapter 1's notes
   25–38, chapter 2 restarting at 1, and so on. **Every note number has been stripped from every
   quotation in this file**, and the flag left on the word where the notes left it. The specifically
   dangerous instances, kept flagged above: `1970s.34[text-layer?]` (p. 72, folio 55) — the 34 is
   note 34, not part of the decade; `on June 24, 1960.11` (p. 61, folio 44) — the date is June 24,
   1960 and 11 is the note; `Lal Shahbaz Qalandar.33` (p. 35, folio 18) — the 33 is not part of the
   epithet; `around four weeks.60` and `February 2018.61` (pp. 46, 48, folios 29, 31) — neither is a

   2017).12` (pp. 195–197, folios 178–180) — **Werbner's cited page is 27, not 2718; Karamustafa's is
   118; Pamment's is 310; the White citation is 2017**. Parenthetical citations such as `(Ortis 2020,
   316)` are genuine references and must **not** be stripped as note numbers.

### 5. Facts a human must check before they enter an entry

- **The death year.** "d. 1274 ce" is stated once, at p. 20, folio 3, and nowhere else in 225 pages;
  the rest of the book says only "thirteenth century". The LC heading's "1177–1274" (p. 5, folio not
  stated) is the Library of Congress's, **never Kasmani's**. The notes' own arrival figures — 1251,
  1263, 1272, "the last year or two of his life", "a year after he arrived in town" — are printed
  side by side and **the book joins none of them**; 1272 + "a year or two" must not be silently
  reconciled into 1274.
- **The silsila.** The archive row reads "Qalandari". **The book never states a silsila in its own
  voice**: it reports that he "is claimed by both Suhrawardi and Qadiri orders" (p. 62, folio 45),
  that Sehwan's fakirs believe he was initiated by Jamal al-Din Savi (p. 62, folio 45), that in the
  Suhrawardi tradition he was initiated in Multan by Bahaʾuddin Zakaria (p. 184, folio 167), and that
  custodians "claim a loose affiliation with some Qadiri strands though no formal initiations into
  the order are made" (p. 184, folio 167). It calls him "a qalandar" as a type of person, not as an
  order.
- **The ʿurs date.** **There is none anywhere in the book.** The fair is "three-day" (p. 114,
  folio 97) and is called *mela*, "saint's fair", "annual fair", "annual festival", *ʿurs* and
  *shadi* in different places; the index has no `ʿurs` headword at all, using `saint's fairs (mela)`
  (p. 223, folio 206). No month, no date, no duration beyond "three-day": **(not stated)**.
- **The 2017 attack.** The date "February 16, 2017" and the figure "More than eighty people were
  killed" appear only at p. 28, folio 11; every later reference gives no date, no casualty count and
  no attribution, and chunk 002 explicitly warns that "the suicide bomb attack at the shrine a year
  earlier" (p. 48, folio 31) supplies no date of its own. **No attacker is named anywhere: (not
  stated).**
- **"It was the time of the saint's festival"** (p. 65, folio 48) sits two sentences after the Data
  Darbar mention but **names no saint**, and the same paragraph also mentions "another saint's fair
  in a distant place in Sindh Prov-ince". **It must not be read as Data Ganj Bakhsh's ʿurs.**
- **The takeover dating is compatible but unreconciled by the book.** A federal ordinance in 1959
  (p. 68, folio 51), "another decade" after an early-1950s survey (p. 69, folio 52), and June 1960
  for Sehwan specifically (p. 79, folio 62; p. 61, folio 44). **Do not harmonise them in an entry.**
  The June 24, 1960 date carries the book's own hedge, "according to one report", and **the report is
  not named**.
- **Unnamed things that must not be guessed**: the Hindu lamp-lighter disenfranchised in 2013; the
  identity of the grave Amma guards, beyond "the tomb of one of the earliest disciples of Lal";
  "a secondary shrine in the town" and "a minor shrine therein"; the Lahore shrine attacked before
  2011; the Lahore shrine in Strothmann (2016); the thirteen kafis other than Bodlo's; the "new
  management", the "Auqaf manager" and the "senior police official" of 2018; the ruler who
  dismembered Bodlo; the 1968 festival publication's title; the name and date of the Punjabi film of
  1969 and of the five others between 1969 and 1975; the Bukhari brothers' first names; the amount of
  the fakirs' daily stipend; the two female-presenting fakirs at Bodlo-kafi.
- **Two printed oddities in the apparatus.** The construction report's title is printed both "at
  Sehwan Sharif" (p. 187, folio 170) and "in Sehwan Sharif" (p. 189, folio 172). A page range in the
  References runs backwards — `Andhar, Hafiz A. 2010 […] edited by Muhammad Ali Manjhi, 143 – 14.`
  (p. 203, folio 186) — and **no completion is offered here**; the companion entry in the same volume
  prints `11 – 26` cleanly (p. 202, folio not stated). The index prints `127n32` twice, identically,
  where every other note locator falls in the band 165n–180n (p. 222, folio 205; p. 223, folio 206);
  because it repeats on two pages it is more likely the book's own misprint than text-layer damage.
  **A reading of `177n32` is offered and not adopted.** `haziri` is given as a range at one headword
  and a list at the other, `70 – 71` against `70, 71` (p. 221, folio 204; p. 224, folio 207).
- **Every number in the index is a printed folio, never a PDF page.** `Bibiyan-pak-daman shrine, 108`
  means folio 108; `Lal's burial, 146` means folio 146; `177nn31 – 32` means notes 31–32 printed at
  folio 177. **Nothing in this file has been converted, and nothing should be.** The Notes section's
  running foot also mis-files whole chapters — `Notes to chapter three` is never printed, though
  chapter three's notes 1–18 are — so a chapter attribution must be taken from the section headings
  in the body of the notes, not from the running foot (p. 191, folio 174; p. 192, folio 175).
- **Internal spans the book keeps side by side and that should not be merged.** Amma is "A fakir for
  over two decades" (p. 79, folio 62) and, of 2009, "had already been a fakir for over a decade"
  (p. 86, folio 69); she has "three children" (p. 79, folio 62) and "two children" on arrival plus "a
  third child while in Sehwan" (p. 86, folio 69); she says "seventeen or eighteen years since I was
  instructed by the saint" (p. 91, folio 74) against Kasmani's "For over twenty years" (p. 97,
  folio 80). Zaheda's career is given as "almost eleven years" by 2013 (p. 122, folio 105), as
  beginning when she moved to Karachi "in the early 2000s" (p. 119, folio 102), and as "ten to
  fifteen years" (p. 119, folio 102). Jamal's tree is a *kikar* at p. 147 (folio not stated) and a
  *eucalyptus* at p. 154 (folio 137). **Shah-Bibi's own biography contradicts itself and the author
  says so** — a husband dead at pp. 153, 157 (folios 136, 140) returns at p. 158 (folio 141) "from a
  fifty-five-year period of ascetic wandering"; "This cast a long shadow of doubt on everything I had
  gathered from her" (p. 158, folio 141). **Nothing from her biography should enter an entry as
  fact.**
- **Things the book itself leaves unresolved and that should survive consolidation unresolved**:
  whether Murad surrendered his wardi or it was taken — "the question… is unsettled here at the kafi"
  and "The debate remained unresolved" (pp. 136–137, folios 119–120); whether the Shiva temple stood
  where the shrine now stands, or whether the tomb marks where Lal rested (p. 163, folio 146);
  whether "mosque", "prayer space" and "prayer room" at the shrine are the same structure (pp. 93,
  98, 99, folios 76, 81, 82); whether the celibacy doctrine at Bodlo-kafi survives the custodian's
  reversal of it (pp. 142–143, folios 125–126).
- **The book's own map of Sehwan cannot be read from the text layer.** p. 47 (folio not stated)
  survives only as loose labels in reading order, and the spatial relations between the Shrine of
  Lal, the Shrine of Bodlo, the Old Fortress, the Indus and the graveyard **are not recoverable**. A
  human should read the printed map before any topographic claim about Sehwan enters an entry. The
  same applies to Map 3.1 of the shrine itself (p. 106, folio not stated).
- **One cross-book caution.** Kasmani discusses Scott Kugle's *Sufis and Saints' Bodies* (2007) and
  *Homosexuality in Islam* (2010b) at p. 175, folio 158 and pp. 196–197, folios 179–180. That first
  title is a separate item in this queue. **What is recorded here is Kasmani's characterisation and
  critique of Kugle, which is an argument about Kugle, not evidence from him**, and the two sets of
  notes must be kept distinct.
