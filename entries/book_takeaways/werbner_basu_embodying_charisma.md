## What this book is

**An edited volume, not a single-author monograph.** Ten chapters by nine contributors, in four
parts: an editors' introduction (Werbner and Basu), "Embodying locality" (Landell Mills, Saheb,
Werth), "The performance of emotion" (Werbner, Basu, Frembgen, Ewing) and "Charisma and modernity"
(Malik, Lindholm), followed by a name index and a subject index (pp. 6–7, folios not stated). **Every
argument below is attributed to the contributor who makes it; where the notes do not name a
contributor the bullet says "(contributor not stated)". Two contributors' positions are never merged
into one "the author argues" — the volume repeatedly stages its contributors against each other.**

Its declared thesis is that charisma is embodied practice rather than doctrine: "the power of ritual
is seen to derive not from belief as a set of abstracted ideas but from ritual as a complex set of
transformative, embodied, negotiated ethical and aesthetic practices and the experiences which their
enactment generates" (p. 19, folio 8). The editors attack a set of "spurious separations: between
magic and religion…; between ritual and belief…; between folk and official or normative religion;
between syncretic practice and Islamic orthodoxy" — "imposed upon different facets of what is, we
argue here, a single, total, symbolic reality" (p. 15, folio 4). The named disciplinary targets are
Clifford Geertz and Ernest Gellner, whose "third scholarly discursive disjunction… severs Sufism from
its organisational underpinnings" (p. 15, folio 4).

**Geographical scope — this matters for the archive.** The volume's centre of gravity is *not*
Pakistan. Landell Mills's chapter is on Bangladesh, Saheb's on Nagore-e-Sharif in Tamil Nadu, Basu's
on the Sidi cult of Bava Gor in Gujarat (India), Lindholm's is a Middle East / South Asia comparison.
Five chapters are set in Pakistan: Werth on unnamed village shrines in the salt range, Werbner
on Ghamkol Sharif near Kohat, Frembgen on the majzub Mama Ji Sarkar in Rawalpindi (with Bari Imam at
Nurpur Shahan as his spiritual anchor), Ewing on the mother of a majzub, and Malik on the shrine of
Shâh Daulah Daryâ'î at Gujrat as read through an Urdu short story. **Four of the fifteen chunks
(003, 004, 005 and 008) return an explicit zero-mapping result against the archive's 169 rows, and
chunk 013 returns one for Lindholm's chapter within it.**

For this archive the book's value is concentrated in three places: **Werbner's chapter 5 on the
langar and ʿurs economy at Ghamkol Sharif**, which is the corpus's fullest account of a living
saint's lodge as a non-commodity economy; **Malik's chapter 9 on Shâh Daulah**, the only sustained
treatment of that shrine's chûhâ (rat-children) cult, its 1898 reconstruction and its 1960s Auqaf
takeover; and **Frembgen's chapter 7**, which supplies the one dated statement of Bari Imam's ʿurs
(1994, first week of May) and his dates (1617–1705/1706). Everything else that maps to an archive row
is a bibliography entry, an index headword, a plate caption or a name inside somebody's quoted speech
— thin by design, and flagged as such on every bullet.

### darbar-ghamkol-sharif-zinda-pir — Ghamkol Sharif / Zindapir

**By far the richest row in the book. Pnina Werbner's chapter 5, "Langar: pilgrimage, sacred exchange
and perpetual sacrifice in a Sufi saint's lodge" (folio 95 = PDF p. 106), is about this lodge and
nothing else, and chunks 006, 007, 001 and 015 all feed it.** The book's names for the place:
"Ghamkol Sharif", "the lodge of Zindapir", "the lodge"; for the saint: "Zindapir", "the living saint
of Ghamkol Sharif", "the saint", "the shaikh", "the pir", "Pir Sahab".

- **Saint**
  - **Zindapir is alive at the time of writing and Werbner interviewed him.** He is "the living saint
    of Ghamkol Sharif" (p. 111, folio 100), and the chapter writes of him throughout in the present
    tense (pp. 115–126, folios 104–115). He "stressed to me repeatedly" that the women's work "is all
    done out of muhabbat, the love of God" (p. 111, folio 100). **No death date is given anywhere in
    the book: (not stated).** Do not import 1999 from the archive row.
  - **No personal name, no birth date, no death date and no silsila.** All **(not stated)** across
    pp. 115–129 (folios 104–118). His order is referred to only as "Zindapir's Sufi order" and
    "Zindapir's cult" and **is never named** (pp. 116 and 124, folios 105 and 113). Do not import
    "Naqshbandi" from the archive row — the only silsila named anywhere in chunk 006 is the
    Naqshbandiyya, and it is named of a different man's pir at p. 98 (folio 87).
  - **Birthplace.** The core of the women volunteers "come from neighbouring Jungel Khel, the
    birthplace of Zindapir himself", "now a small town of 20,000 inhabitants" (p. 122, folio 111).
  - **Self-description as a faqir.** He "described himself as a faqir (an ascetic)": "A faqir is the
    friend of Allah. Even if he is offered one lakh [100,000] rupees or nothing to eat for Allah's
    sake, he would choose to go hungry" (p. 115, folio 104); "This is the way of a faqir. He fasts
    all day while making sure that everyone else is given food" (p. 115, folio 104).
  - **Ascetic and vegetarian.** "Zindapir, the saint of Ghamkol Sharif, is said never to sleep but to
    spend all his time meditating and praying" (pp. 113–114, folios 102–103). "The saint himself is a
    vegetarian who eats no meat and regards himself as the protector of all living creatures"
    (p. 118, folio 107); "He fasts all day and is a vegetarian while being the source of meat"
    (p. 118, folio 107).
  - **Physical description, dated to the fieldwork**: "an elderly man limping along slowly since his
    knees are painful and have been causing him some trouble" (p. 123, folio 112). He is "treated as
    a king or prince"; "Grown men tremble at his anger and sink into despair" (p. 123, folio 112).
  - **Household.** A son, whose wife runs the more exclusive of the two langars (p. 117, folio 106)
    and who built a water trough for wild animals (p. 118, folio 107); in 1991 "Zindapir's son's wife
    was undergoing an operation on her throat in England", news of its success arriving at 9.30 p.m.
    by telephone (p. 124, folio 113).
  - **Reputation on women.** "In the case of Zindapir, his reputation was quite immaculate" (endnote
    1, p. 127, folio 116).
  - **On the ʿulama:** "Allah will undoubtedly take the 'ulama to Paradise on the Day of Judgement.
    But Allah gives paradise to his faqirs on earth. I can give all the blessings of Allah" (p. 116,
    folio 105). **On generosity:** "I have to be generous because this [the lodge, or Mecca] is not
    my house. It is the house of Allah. I too am a guest here" (p. 116, folio 105) — Werbner reads
    this as "the close identification of the saint with Allah, his proximity to God" (p. 116,
    folio 105).
- **Shrine / Darbar**
  - **Location, exactly as printed**: "Ghamkol Sharif, the lodge of Zindapir, in a little valley
    outside the town of Kohat in north western Pakistan" (p. 110, folio 99).
  - **Site and building history**: "The lodge was built outside any established settlement, very
    gradually, over many years, in what was previously an uncultivated valley" (p. 125, folio 114).
    **No founding date and no building dates anywhere: (not stated)** (pp. 115–126, folios 104–115).
  - Sacralised space: "the sacrifice takes place in a space which has been sacralised by continuous
    prayer" (p. 113, folio 102); "The lodge itself is a place —the remembrance of God, the incantation
    of God's name" `[text-layer?: apparently a dropped phrase, e.g. "a place of zikr—the remembrance
    of God"]` (p. 114, folio 103). "The sound of zikr— Allah hu or La'illa il allah hu, both
    individually and communally sung—echoes `continously [text-layer?: continuously]` through the
    valley" (p. 114, folio 103).
  - **A transnational regional cult.** "the network of satellite lodges distributed throughout
    Pakistan and Britain which provide langar for visitors" (pp. 115–116, folios 104–105); the ʿurs
    convoys come "throughout Pakistan and beyond it, including from Britain, where Zindapir's order
    extends" (p. 114, folio 103); "the branches in both countries send tribute to the shaikh himself"
    (p. 124, folio 113). "On the annual hajj, Zindapir distributes langar in Mecca" (p. 116,
    folio 105), that langar "organised and funded by his vicegerent in Britain—Sufi Abdullah… The
    money for the hajj langar is donated by British Pakistanis, while the pots and utensils are kept
    by disciples of Zindapir living in Mecca who are labour migrants working in Saudi Arabia"
    (p. 116, folio 105).
  - **Productive endowment.** "Ghamkol Sharif has its herds of cattle, goats and other livestock, its
    orange groves and fruit orchards, its vegetable gardens, as well as storage rooms for grain, rice
    and clarified butter, brought by supplicants or purchased with their donations" (p. 118,
    folio 107). "Gifts of camels and horses, and of strange and beautiful wild birds and animals, as
    well as of goats, sheep and buffalo are not unusual. Many are dedicated to the langar and cannot
    be used for any other purpose" (p. 118, folio 107).
  - **What the state has given, and what the saint refuses.** "Land, telephone lines, water, roads and
    transport have all been provided free through official channels, and Zindapir takes great care,
    in reality, not to offend politicians" (p. 121, folio 110). Against that, he refuses political
    involvement — "God is not elected, and to become a Sufi you don't need to be elected" (p. 119,
    folio 108); of politicians, "Because they tells lies [jhut]" (p. 119, folio 108) — yet prays for
    them when they come for duʿa (p. 119, folio 108). "he is regularly visited by top civil servants,
    army `brigadeers` [text-layer?: brigadiers] and generals, and even politicians. All partake of
    the langar" (p. 117, folio 106). "The Chief Minister of Azad Kashmir visited him several times,
    but Zindapir told me, he never asked his name" (p. 121, folio 110). **In 1991** Werbner "was told
    confidentially that one of the leading political figures in Pakistan had visited the lodge and
    had wanted to write Zindapir a cheque for a very large sum of money", which he refused (p. 120,
    folio 109). **No Auqaf department, no waqf and no state custodianship is mentioned for this lodge
    anywhere: (not stated).**
- **Practices & Events**
  - **The ʿurs is a three-day festival** whose peak is the saint's final duʿa (p. 125, folio 114); it
    is "the major three-day festival" (p. 110, folio 99).
  - **Whose ʿurs it is — stated once, and contradicted by silence elsewhere.** "The occasion of the
    sacrificial feast is the ‘urs, the commemoration of the death/rebirth of Zindapir's Sufi master,
    Baba Qasim" (p. 111, folio 100). **Chunk 007 covering pp. 115–129 states that what or whom the
    ʿurs commemorates is nowhere given there.** The two statements are kept side by side; see Doubts,
    where this collides with the archive row's own principal-figure field.
  - **The 1990 sacrifice, counts exactly as printed.** "In 1990 ninety-five goats, thirteen sheep, and
    seventeen cattle and buffalo were slaughtered over a three-day period, the equivalent of over
    3,000 kg of meat, distributed to some 20,000 or more pilgrims and supplicants" (p. 110, folio 99).
  - **The crowd figure, with the author's own caveat attached.** "The 'urs is said, somewhat
    hyperbolically, to draw 300,000 pilgrims to the lodge, all of whom are fed by the saint" (p. 116,
    folio 105). **Werbner marks the figure as hyperbolical in the same sentence.** It sits beside the
    "20,000 or more" of 1990 (p. 110, folio 99) and is not reconciled with it by the book.
  - **The langar is open twenty-four hours a day, all year.** "The langar at Ghamkol Sharif is open
    twenty-four hours a day all year to all supplicants and pilgrims, from the very poor to the most
    elevated and powerful in the land" (p. 117, folio 106). "The ‘urs is the high point of a
    continuous flow of food provided at the lodge by the saint all year round… a perpetual sacrifice,
    one that provides an apparently endless supply of sacrificial meat every day throughout the year"
    (p. 111, folio 100).
  - **There are two langars.** "One is an open, general langar, and the other is run by his son's
    wife. Many of the most respected guests are fed from this more exclusive langar, although
    chappati bread usually comes from the central langar" (p. 117, folio 106). The Sayyid women
    Werbner stayed with during the ʿurs "had brought their own food with them for the festival, and
    ate almost nothing from the central langar", denying any significance to the avoidance "beyond a
    matter of taste preferences" (p. 117, folio 106).
  - **Nothing is bought.** "All the animals slaughtered for the langar are freely given by pilgrims,
    disciples and supplicants—none is purchased" (p. 113, folio 102). The convoys "bear with them not
    only animals but sacks of flour, bags of rice, large containers of clarified butter (ghee) and
    money donated for the purchase of food for the langar" (p. 114, folio 103).
  - **Three weeks of volunteer labour and building before the ʿurs.** "For three weeks before the
    'urs both men and women begin arriving at the lodge to contribute their voluntary labour… Much of
    the building of the lodge takes place during this period, including the extension of water pipes,
    sewerage and electricity. New hostels are constructed for pilgrims" (p. 122, folio 111).
  - **Women's preparatory labour, itemised and class-marked.** "local high-class Sayyid women of
    wealthy families who claim descent from the Prophet, along with a large number of women from
    other respected families throughout Pakistan, clean and scrape thousands of clay dishes, hundreds
    of flat bread baskets, about twenty or thirty giant clay pots and an equivalent number of iron
    chappati stoves (loh)" (p. 110, folio 99); the stoves "accumulate a layer of rust over the year
    and need to be scraped and polished in readiness for the thousands of chappatis that are to be
    baked on them" (p. 110, folio 99); the women then "use heavy iron picks to dig up the dry earth in
    order to install the ovens" and "smear the surface around the ovens with mud" (p. 110, folio 99).
    "While the women wash, scrape and polish, the men invest their voluntary labour in piling up vast
    quantities of wood" (p. 111, folio 100). "The labour of the women is all voluntary, an act of
    khidmat, of public service" (pp. 110–111, folios 99–100); "The women explain that they work for
    sawab, merit, and for God's forgiveness, Allah mu‘af kare" (p. 111, folio 100).
  - **Who the women volunteers are.** Mostly "from the high-ranking Sayyid family, which is the
    largest, wealthiest and most important in Jungel Khel"; its members are "scattered throughout the
    world, occupying professional positions as doctors, engineers or pilots", hold "many of the top
    administrative positions in the town", "own large houses" and employ servants — yet take on "the
    most menial, dirty, unpleasant tasks" and sleep "on mats on the floor" (p. 122, folio 111). During
    the ʿurs "they also help supervise the visits of women supplicants to the saint" (p. 122,
    folio 111).
  - **Purity of the food.** "The food is pure, I was told, because it is cooked by men of pure heart
    who chant zikr (the incantation of God's name) as they cook" (p. 111, folio 100). The men who
    slaughter and cook "do so as a meritorious, freely given act, an act of selfless service
    (khidmat). All the labour in the lodge is freely given in the name of God" (p. 114, folio 103).
  - **Slaughter arrangements.** "At Ghamkol Sharif… animals are donated on the hoof and one of the
    disciples slaughters them, assisted by several companions. The same disciple acts as chief
    slaughterer for the lodge on all major festivals" (p. 117, folio 106) — against Britain, where
    slaughter "is entrusted to the Pakistani butcher, who slaughters it at the abattoir" (pp. 116–117,
    folios 105–106).
  - **The daily supplicant flow and the audience protocol.** "a constant daily flow of supplicants
    seeking healing for their ailments or divine blessings in their jobs, careers and marital
    fortunes" (p. 122, folio 111). The saint "takes in about ten at a time", instructs supplicants to
    perform their daily prayers, throws "a rolled-up, inscribed paper amulet in their general
    direction" or tells them to collect amulets "outside", then raises his hands in duʿa (p. 123,
    folio 112). "On their visits to his room women supplicants sit behind a low wooden barrier to
    prevent them from reaching too close to him" (p. 123, folio 112); both sexes "expose their faces,
    the women drawing their veils above their heads" (p. 123, folio 112).
  - **What the saint gives back.** "white chiffon headscarves, embroidered praying caps and perfumes",
    drawn "out of the treasure house of objects he accumulates in his room, buried in the silk and
    brocade cushions on which he reclines"; "salt and amulets to the supplicants… along with du'a and
    dam"; and money "to the poor and needy who come to him with requests for help" (pp. 121–122,
    folios 110–111). Elsewhere the list is "white cotton scarves and white embroidered hats for his
    disciples, gowns for his khalifa, amulets and salts for supplicants" (p. 125, folio 114).
  - **The order's festival calendar (in Britain, run from this lodge)**: the ʿurs; "eid milad-un nabi
    (the commemoration of the Prophet's birth/death)"; the monthly *gyarvin sharif*, "the eleventh of
    the month", "held in most of Zindapir's satellite lodges and mosques throughout his regional
    cult"; and daily distribution "during the month of Ramadan… at the mosque branches of Zindapir's
    Sufi order in Manchester and throughout Britain" (p. 116, folio 105). British langar "differs
    rather little in organisation and ideology from the langar in Pakistan, except for the fact that,
    as yet, it is not a perpetual sacrifice" (p. 124, folio 113).
  - **Plate captions belonging to this shrine.** Plate 5.1, "Cooking pots and wood piled high in
    anticipation of the ‘urs at Ghamkol Sharif", printed folio 101 (p. 8, folio not stated; p. 112,
    folio 101); Plate 5.2, "A pilgrim leads his sacrificial goat for the langar", folio 102 (p. 8,
    folio not stated; p. 113, folio 102); Plate 5.3, "Distributing the langar to the pilgrims", folio
    106 (p. 8, folio not stated). **Plates 5.2 and 5.3 do not name a place**; see Doubts before
    attaching them. **Neither caption is dated: (not stated).**
- **Legends & Miracles**
  - **Command over the wild**, attributed by Werbner to "the legends about the Zindapir": "He can cure
    poisonous snake bites, honey bees are said not to sting, and wild animals do not invade the lodge
    or attack its inhabitants" (p. 118, folio 107).
  - **Feeding the ants.** "Every morning he feeds the ants with the remains of his sweet morning tea.
    Killing ants and any other creatures, however minute, is prohibited within the lodge area. This is
    also true of his conduct during the hajj, I was told" (p. 118, folio 107). "A special water trough
    has been constructed by the saint's son for the wild animals that descend from the hills at night
    to drink from" (p. 118, folio 107).
  - **The founding myth of the lodge**, told by Zindapir: on the first journey to establish it he saw
    a companion carrying a sack of flour and told him, "What will you eat when the flour is finished?
    Throw it away and trust in Allah" (p. 120, folio 109). Werbner calls this "the paradigmatic tale
    which illuminates the place of the lodge as the sole source of God's boundless nurture" (p. 120,
    folio 109). "In his final supplication for the sake of the community, the saint recalls his own
    first arduous journey to the lodge. Indeed, he repeats the story of this legendary journey almost
    verbatim every year" (p. 125, folio 114).
  - **The refused three days of langar — two versions, printed side by side and not adjudicated.** In
    one, a great landowner from Sahiwal district offered all the food for the langar for three days in
    exchange for permission to heal by *dam*, and Zindapir answered: "If I let you provide the langar
    for three days I will make you a partner with God which would be shirk [polytheism, blasphemy]" —
    refusing even to make him a murid (p. 120, folio 109). In the other, the offer came from the
    Minister of Finance's uncle after Zindapir cured him of an incurable disease — "Pir Sahab cast dam
    (blew a prayer) on him and said: 'Let him eat from the langar's food and he will be cured'" — and
    the refusal ran: "You will provide for the langar for three days but what will happen after that?
    I cannot make you a partner (sharik) with God" (p. 120, folio 109). **Werbner presents these
    explicitly as two versions of one tale and does not choose between them.**
  - **The refusal of the Saudi politician's hospitality.** Invited to stay at a Saudi politician's
    home in Jedda, Zindapir replied: "When I visit the house of God, I am the guest only of Allah"
    (p. 120, folio 109).
  - **Healing by insight into hidden causes.** "The healing powers of the saint are grounded in a
    belief in his ability to see below the surface, to the occult and social causes of illnesses, the
    thoughts, feelings and accidental transgressions which have brought about pain, chronic illness,
    infertility, depression, business failures and so forth" (pp. 122–123, folios 111–112).
- **Significance**
  - **The lodge as a non-commodity economy.** "Hence the lodge is a space set apart from the commodity
    economy, and capitalist logic does not hold there" (p. 114, folio 103). Werbner sets the saint,
    who "asks for nothing except from Allah", against a cash figure for hired religious labour: "Only
    yesterday I paid a maulvi [Islamic scholar] 400 rupees [about £15] for giving a sermon at the
    mosque" (p. 116, folio 105).
  - **The index treats the lodge as a site of ʿurs ritual.** Headword `Ghamkol Sharif 100–15` (p. 250,
    folio 239); sub-entry `Ghamkol Sharif 100, 101, 103, 105, 111` under the headword `‘urs rituals`
    (p. 254, folio 243). Headword `Zindapir 100, 102, 106; and giving 104–5, 107, 110–11; and
    politicians 108–10; relation to supplicants and disciples` (p. 254, folio 243) — **the final
    sub-entry carries no locator in the extracted text**; see Doubts. Headword `Qasim, Baba 101`
    (p. 252, folio 241), a single locator with no statement attached. **All numbers in these index
    entries are printed-page locators, not PDF pages.**
  - **Werbner's fieldwork frame for everything above**: "research in Pakistan at the lodge of Zindapir
    during several weeks in 1989 and 1991, and… research in Manchester among returning pilgrims from
    hajj during 1988–1989", "supported by the ESRC, UK, and the Leverhulme Trust" (p. 126, folio 115).

### shrine-of-hazrat-shah-daula-daryai — Shâh Daulah Daryâ'î, Gujrat

**Jamal Malik's chapter 9 (folio 187 = PDF p. 198) is a reading of Ahmad Nadîm Qâsimî's Urdu story
'Bain' ('Dirge'), set at this shrine, prefaced by a historical sketch of the saint and cult drawn
from colonial and malfûzât sources. Chunks 001, 012, 013 and 014 feed this row.** The book's forms:
"the dargâh (court, shrine) of Shâh Daulah Daryâ'î in Gujrat"; the plate caption "The shrine of Shâh
Daulah"; inside the story "Sân'în Daule Shâh", "Sân'în Daule Shâhjî", and the grave as "mazâr sharîf";
in the References `Hadrat Shâh Daulah Daryâ'î Gujrâtî`; in the index `Daulah Daryâ‘î, Shah` and
`Shâh Daulah` (pp. 204, 210, 212, 217, 249–253).

- **Saint**
  - **Birth name and death, exactly as printed**: "Shâh Daulah—whose birth name was Qâsim —lived in
    Mughal times and died at an advanced age around AH 1676 in Gujrat" (p. 205, folio 194). **"AH
    1676" cannot stand as printed and has not been corrected here — see Doubts.**
  - **Descent is given as contested**: "His ancestors were Afghans, but it is also claimed that he was
    a Sayyid (descendant of the Prophet)" (p. 205, folio 194).
  - **Early life**: "After a turbulent childhood he became supervisor of the treasury of a local
    official, and also worked as a mendicant and an ordinary labourer for some time" (p. 205,
    folio 194).
  - **The silsila is given twice and not harmonised by the book**: "He then spent twelve years at the
    feet of a Suhrawardi Sufi, but it is recorded too that he was initiated into the Qadiri order"
    (p. 205, folio 194).
  - **Migration**: "Due to some disputes with the local people of Sialkot, he migrated from there and,
    after some years, moved to Gujrat ‘in obedience to divine instructions…'" (p. 205, folio 194).
  - **The two titles glossed**: "During his life he devoted himself to works of public utility [hence
    his laqab or title ‘Daulah', ‘wealth'] and the constructions of religious buildings', especially
    bridges (hence his laqab ‘Dargâ‘î' [text-layer?: the gloss given, "‘one affiliated to water'",
    fits the form *Daryâ'î* used elsewhere in the chapter; the printed letters are not recoverable
    from the text layer], ‘one affiliated to water')" (p. 205, folio 194).
  - **Cross-community following and a refusal of land**: he was popular among "‘Muslims as well as
    Hindus, became famous for his miracles and received large gifts' in kind, but refused to accept
    land grants" (p. 205, folio 194).
  - **The nisba `Gujrâtî`** is attached to him only in Kunjâhî's book title (p. 217, folio 206).
- **Shrine / Darbar**
  - **State custodianship**: "this Sufi tomb, which was taken over by the Pakistan Auqaf Department in
    the 1960s (on the Auqaf see Malik 1990c)" (p. 204, folio 193).
  - **The takeover did not displace local authority**: "even the nationalisation policy of the Auqaf
    Department since the 1960s could not totally change the perceptions deeply rooted in the minds of
    the rural population and the authority exercised by the shrine administrator, the mujâwir, who
    ideally represents state power" (p. 206, folio 195).
  - **Building date**: the shrine "had been reconstructed (1898)" (p. 206, folio 195). **This is the
    only architectural date in the book for this shrine; no founding date, no ʿurs date, no named
    sajjâda nashin or custodian family, no visitor number and no revenue figure are given anywhere:
    all (not stated)** (pp. 204–213, folios 193–202).
  - **Endowment and revenue — the shrine has no land.** "the lack of regular income at the dargâh,
    since no land was attached to it, resulted in the holy men's total dependence on the alms and
    offering of the followers, while it was also suspected that the followers (murîdûn) were involved
    in traffic in women (Ibbetson and Maclagan 1911–1919:630)" (p. 206, folio 195). The *Indian
    Antiquary* editor stresses "the absence of landed property in possession of the band, or any
    recognized right to succession to the leadership, and the entire dependence on the earnings"
    (p. 206, folio 195).
  - **Its reputation in one phrase**: "The sanctuary of Shâh Daulah thus has a particular
    reputation—fertility, justice and punishment" (p. 206, folio 195).
  - **A female domain.** "the dargâh is a female domain, at least in quantitative terms—in contrast to
    mosques, which are frequented by men" (p. 208, folio 197), with the counter-note that "the power
    in shrines is usually held by men" (p. 214, folio 203).
- **Practices & Events**
  - **The chûhâ cult — the shrine's signature.** "Shâh Daulah's shrine is particularly known for its
    Chûhâs or Rat-children, who are without understanding or the power of speech. After some initial
    training they are passed on to a faqîr (religious mendicant) and made to beg in the name of Shâh
    Daulah" (p. 205, folio 194). "a band or order of faqírs makes a living out of a certain class of
    local microcephalous idiots, and the convenient existence of an important shrine" (p. 206,
    folio 195). A charge of deliberate mutilation is reported and not endorsed: "it is also suspected
    that these children are ‘artificially produced after their birth as ordinary infants' (Ibbetson
    and Maclagan 1911–1919:637)" (p. 206, folio 195).
  - **Malik dates the cult late and ties the date to the building.** "It therefore seems likely that
    this cult arose only later, by the end of the nineteenth century—after the shrine had been
    reconstructed (1898)" (p. 206, folio 195), the argument resting on the absence of the chûhâ cult
    from "contemporary and even later malfûzât and biographical accounts" (Kunjâhî 1985) (p. 206,
    folio 195).
  - **Offering of children as a vow.** "The custom used to be to leave the child, as soon as it was
    weaned, at Shah Daulah's khanqah [Sufi lodge], as an offering to him"; after his death devotees
    "would make a vow either to present the child when born or make an offering to the shrine"
    (p. 205, folio 194).
  - **The transaction at the tomb, as Malik analyses it**: "a kind of a bargain between the parents
    and the mujâwir, like the mahr (bridewealth): the parents are declared dwellers of paradise
    (jannatî—without intercession of any authority) by the shrine administrator. In response they
    leave the child—the present (ni‘mat) —as a trust (amânat) at the dargâh" (p. 211, folio 200).
  - **ʿUrs, as it functions inside the story**: "the girl's maturity or sexualisation and the marriage
    proposal coincide with the anniversary of the holy man's death, ‘urs, the unification of the pîr
    with God" (p. 209, folio 198); "During the ‘urs the girl falls victim to the corrupted society"
    (p. 211, folio 200). **No ʿurs date, month or duration is given: (not stated).**
  - **Langar at the shrine, as narrated inside the story**: Rano flings the skirt her parents bring
    "into the burning coals of the public kitchen (langar)" (p. 212, folio 201); her father later
    throws the mujâwir's shroud (kafan) "into the burning coals of the fire heating the water for the
    great ablution (ghusl)" (p. 212, folio 201).
- **Legends & Miracles**
  - **The chûhâ legend, quoted by Malik from colonial sources**: "Shah Daulah, like other saints,
    could procure the birth of a child for a couple desiring one, but the first child born in response
    to his intercession would be a Chuha—brainless, small-headed, long-eared and rat-faced" (p. 205,
    folio 194). Its post-mortem continuation: "After the saint's death the miracle continued, but in a
    modified way"; where a promised gift was withheld, "the spirit of the offended saint so worked on
    the parents that the next child was born a Chuha, and all subsequent children as well, until the
    original vow was fulfilled" (Ibbetson and Maclagan 1911–1919:630) (p. 205, folio 194). A
    physiological counter-explanation is reported from Kunjâhî 1985: "the bad conscience of parents,
    particularly mothers, set free certain chemical reactions that caused this peculiarity" (p. 206,
    folio 195).
  - **Control of female infanticide, attributed by Malik to "the colonial sources"**: "through his
    charismatic or miraculous powers (karâmât) Shâh Daulah was able to control female infanticide,
    which was widespread in Jammu State at the time (Ibbetson and Maclagan 1911–1919: 631)" (p. 205,
    folio 194).
  - **The holy hand, as narrated inside 'Bain'**: the shrine "had become an institution of justice,
    symbolised by the holy hand of Shâh Daulah, which, in order to judge, would rise from the grave"
    (p. 207, folio 196). In full: "The upper part [sar bâne kî taraf se] of the holy grave opened and
    the Pîr stretched out his holy hand [dast-e mubârak]. The culprit then was drawn to the shrine
    [mazâr sharîf] from wherever he was, laid his neck into the holy hand of the Sân'înjî [the pîr]
    and died on the spot [dher honâ]" (p. 210, folio 199). "Therefore, nobody dared to neglect Sân'în
    Daule Shâh's orders" (p. 210, folio 199).
  - **A dream as the instrument of the mujâwir's demand** that the girl Rano recite at the dargâh for
    three days, which "Shâh Daulah had ordered in a dream" (p. 207, folio 196), and otherwise "‘Sân'în
    Daule Shâhjî would put all villagers to fire and ash' (bhasam karnâ)" (p. 209, folio 198).
- **Significance**
  - **Malik's sources, named**: "historical and biographical accounts as well as on malfûzât
    literature (namely, the imputed speeches and utterances of holy men)", "extracted from, among
    other works, Ibettson's Glossary [text-layer?: Ibbetson's; the same sentence cites "Ibbetson and
    Maclagan 1911–1919"] and the work by Sharîf Kunjâhî (Kunjâhî 1985)" (p. 205, folio 194). Kunjâhî's
    book is published by a **Punjab Auqaf Department imprint**, printed `Markaz Ma'ârif Awliyâ
    Mahkamah Awqâf Punjâb`: "Kunjâhî, S. (1985) *Hadrat Shâh Daulah Daryâ'î Gujrâtî: Hayât o
    ta'lîmât*, Lahore: Markaz Ma'ârif Awliyâ Mahkamah Awqâf Punjâb" (p. 217, folio 206).
  - **The shrine as the setting of Qâsimî's story**: "‘Dirge' deals with the activities in and around
    the dargâh (court, shrine) of Shâh Daulah Daryâ'î in Gujrat" (p. 204, folio 193), a cult to "which
    Nadîm's story does not, however, refer directly" (p. 204, folio 193). Malik's verdict on what the
    story shows: "It shows that the shrine cult has become a falsehood void of any mysticism, or, in
    other words, an expression of corrupt political power" (p. 208, folio 197); "fear is the prime
    motivational force inducing the villagers and adepts to be loyal to the moral authority emanating
    from the shrine" (p. 210, folio 199).
  - **The indexes.** Headword `Daulah Daryâ‘î, Shah: ‘Bain' and 196, 198–202; sanctuary of 193–6`
    (p. 250, folio 239); sub-entry `sanctuary of Shâh Daulah 193–6` under `literary critiques 187–208`
    (p. 251, folio 240); sub-entry `‘Bain' and Shâh Daulah 196` under `Qâsimî, Ahmad Nadîm 192–3,
    202–3` (p. 253, folio 242); and, in the Subject Index, "‘Bain' ('Dirge'/'Separation') (Qâsimî)
    193, 196–203; mystical dimensions and gender 196–202; and Shâh Daulah 196, 198–202" (p. 249,
    folio 238). **All numbers are printed-page locators.** Plate 9.1, "The shrine of Shâh Daulah",
    printed folio 193 (p. 8, folio not stated).

### Place-level and person-level attachments (rulings of 21 and 22 September 2026)

*Ruling (a): a bare toponym takes the id of **every** archive row in that place, because place-level
material is part of the tradition of every site in that place. The many-row person rule does the same
for a person who is the principal figure of several rows. Each bullet below records the material
once, page referenced, with every id on one line. **These are place-level and person-level
attachments, not site descriptions** — a corresponding Doubts line is filed for each. Toponyms with
no archive row (Nurpur / Nurpur Shahan, Kallar Kahar, Jungel Khel, Samnabad, Kharian, Dera Ismail
Khan, Kulachi, Sahiwal district, Azad Kashmir, the Potwar, Punjab, Sindh, the NWFP, Swat, Kohistan,
Jammu State, Lucknow, Aligarh, and every non-Pakistani place in the book) return empty and stay under
"Figures and sites not in the archive". **Kohat, Gujrat and Pakpattan are not bare toponyms** — each
carries a named shrine in the sentence where it appears, so its material already sits in the
subsection for that shrine (`darbar-ghamkol-sharif-zinda-pir`, `shrine-of-hazrat-shah-daula-daryai`,

## Cross-cutting material

**Because this is an edited volume, arguments are grouped by the contributor who makes them. Nothing
below is "the author's" view: where two contributors disagree, both positions are given and neither
is merged into the other.**

### Practices, institutions, economy

#### What a shrine is, as an institution (Jamal Malik, chapter 9)

- "The shrine is an institution which is simultaneously therapeutic, social, economic and political;
  and in contrast to mosques, it also provides an alternative source of communication and identity,
  especially in the rather monotonous lives of peasants generally and women in particular" (p. 200,
  folio 189). Its services: "Here sick persons can be healed, destitutes find shelter and refuge, and,
  in political crises, sanctuaries can be used as catalysts for mobilisation" (p. 200, folio 189).
- **Custodial offices named and ranked.** "The holy man, the enlightened one and his descendants—the
  sajjâdah-nashîn and administrator (mutawallî, mujâwir) —exert absolute authority, and pious
  villagers, often illiterate, can come, with the passage of time, to be regarded by the shrineholders
  as servants, or even, indeed, as slaves" (p. 200, folio 189). The structural fault Malik names:
  "there is a significant contradiction between a postulated ritual equality and the practised
  hierarchy at the bigger shrines and lodges" (p. 200, folio 189).
- **Pir and landlord.** "In many cases, the local pîr represents the local landlord or is at least
  closely related to him" (p. 199, folio 188). Malik quotes Spencer Trimingham (1971:22), "Indian Islam
  seems to have been essentially a holy-man Islam" (p. 199, folio 188).
- **Eighteenth-century reformist critique of the shrine economy**: reformists "condemned what they
  called the exploitative and parasitical shrine cult and the monopoly of landed property mostly
  stemming from the ideology of the ‘feudal' system" and "demanded a purification of Islam from local
  ideas and rituals" (Ansari 1984; Baljon 1989; Puhlitî 1988) (p. 200, folio 189).

#### Langar, sacrifice and the gift (Pnina Werbner, chapter 5)

- **Werbner's central claim.** "The langar, conceived of as perpetual sacrifice, is the key organising
  feature of Sufi lodges in South Asia. Such lodges are centres for the collection and redistribution
  of food on a vast scale" (p. 117, folio 106); "the langar objectifies this perpetual source of
  saintly divine spiritual power" (p. 117, folio 106). Her gloss of the term: langar is "the communal
  distribution of food at a religious lodge or celebration" (p. 107, folio 96). And, from the editors'
  introduction, "The langar is the core institution of Sufi shrines, a locus of sacred exchange which
  extends the social and spatial ambit of the lodge" (p. 22, folio 11).
- **Sacrifice is a conversion of economies, not a killing.** "The conversion is from the cash economy
  to the moral economy, the good faith economy, the gift economy. Unlike ordinary halal slaughter, a
  ritual sacrifice is the slaughter of an animal freely given, removed from the cash nexus of
  commercial buying and selling" (p. 113, folio 102). "nominally in Islam every animal slaughtered is
  a sacrifice (see contributions in Brisbarre and Gokalp 1993)"; ritual sacrifices "are set apart from
  routine Islamic slaughter by additional customs related to particular festive occasions—the eid,
  ‘aqiqa (on the birth of a son) or sadaqa (sacrifices of exorcism or expiation)" (p. 111, folio 100).
- **A purchased animal must be sacralised first.** "a purchased animal must be transformed through
  rites of sacralisation before it can be sacrificed or defined as an offering. Among Pakistanis this
  sacralisation is usually achieved through a communal reading of the whole Qur'an in a cooperative
  gathering, or through a special specified prayer of dedication—such as the eid morning prayer" or
  through "an offering of vegetarian food known as tabarruk" (p. 113, folio 102).
- **The direction of giving — the formula the volume reuses.** "Gifts to God, including animal
  sacrifices and offerings of food and money, are always unilateral—given without expectation of
  return" (p. 114, folio 103); they go "either ‘downwards', to the poor, in the form of sadaqa (alms),
  or ‘upwards', as religious tribute to saints and holy men, whether alive or dead. Alternatively,
  offerings are made to communal causes such as the building of a mosque or the langar" (p. 114,
  folio 103). **Figure 5.1** is captioned "The structure of sacrificial giving" (p. 114, folio 103).
  **Basu explicitly builds on this formula in chapter 6** (p. 145, folio 134).
- **Three kinds of religious gift.** *Sadaqa* is "gifts to the poor"; *nazrana* is "a religious
  tributory [text-layer?: tributary] gift of money, valuable objects or food given as a mark of
  respect or in gratitude for a blessing bestowed"; *tabarruk* is "blessing, thanksgiving", the
  remains of communal meals "distributed… among the people to be taken home" (p. 115, folio 104).
  "Members of these castes, I was told, will not accept sadaqa… but only nazrana", and "Sayyids are
  not offered and do not usually accept tabarruk" (p. 115, folio 104).
- **Three kinds of animal sacrifice.** *Sadaqa*, "an expiatory gift at the time of extreme danger of a
  life-threatening kind, in which the victim is given to the poor in its entirety"; *qurbáni* (printed
  with an acute accent), "the eid sacrifice, a portion is given to the poor and the rest shared among
  kin and friends conceived of as equals"; and *zabah*, "an animal… given as a tribute at a saint's
  lodge and… usually shared out as langar" (p. 115, folio 104).
- **Nazrana is not sacrifice.** "the giving of nazrana… is much more simply an expression of love and
  respect for the saint by his disciplies [text-layer?: disciples]… it is not an act of sacrifice,
  unless we recognise the identification of the saint, as the exemplary person, within the community.
  Sacrifice is necessarily mediated either by the poor or by the community" (p. 118, folio 107). **The
  poor are not scapegoats**: "since they need the meat for their sustenance and survival, it is
  assumed it will do them no harm" (p. 118, folio 107).
- **Motives for giving**, as Werbner reports them: "in fulfilment of a vow (niyat) or to seek merit
  (sawab), but also as an act of identification with an unbounded Muslim community, the umma"
  (p. 118, folio 107).
- **Against "sacred commerce".** "We see the limitations of the suggestion put foward [text-layer?:
  forward] by Eade and Sallnow (1991) according to which much of the activity at saints' lodges is a
  matter of ‘sacred commerce'. Such a conception denies, moreover, the experiential dimensions of
  voluntary labour and sacrifice" (p. 122, folio 111). She grants that the volunteers themselves call
  it "a calculating act of service before God for the purpose of accumulating merit", but holds that
  "these ideological statements tell only a very partial truth" (p. 125, folio 114).
- **The good-faith / bad-faith political economy.** People give because of "the contrast that they and
  the pir draw between the greed and corruption of the ‘world' (dunya) and the purity of the lodge as
  a place of true religion (din)" (p. 119, folio 108); pilgrims see postcolonial Pakistan as "one of
  mendacious politicians, of greed, selfishness and violence" (p. 119, folio 108). Hajji Bashir on
  intercession: "if they stood alone before God they would definitely not go to heaven", "Because this
  is not an Islamic country and it is very hard to be a good Muslim here" (p. 119, folio 108).
- **Transnationalism and labour migration.** "the spread of Zindapir's cult has been associated with
  contemporary global movements of migrant labour from Pakistan to the West" (p. 124, folio 113); the
  hajj langar is "the ultimate objectification of this diasporic transnationalism" and produces "a
  perpetual sacrifice at the global centre of Islam" through "this ritual international division of
  labour" (p. 124, folio 113).

#### Hajj and umra practice, as reported to Werbner by returning pilgrims in Manchester

- Method: "My research on the hajj was conducted in Manchester through discussions with returning
  hajjis" (p. 108, folio 97).
- **Sequence**: pilgrims start "from Mina on the eighth day of the month of hajj", move "on the ninth
  day at the valley of Arafat", and the eid sacrifice "is followed on the tenth day by the encounter
  with the devils" (p. 108, folio 97). **Dress**: "The pilgrim starts this symbolic journey dressed in
  two white sheets, likened to the shrouds of the dead" (p. 108, folio 97); afterwards "his or her head
  is shaved or clipped" (p. 108, folio 97).
- **Pebble counts as printed**: at Muzdaliffah "they collect forty-nine tiny pebbles"; at Mina they
  "cast one lot of seven pebbles onto a single pillar, that of Aqaba" (p. 108, folio 97); in the
  post-Meccan stonings "the pilgrims cast forty-two pebbles, fourteen at each of the pillars (seven
  times three, on each of two days)" (p. 109, folio 98). After the sacrificial meal "they can put on
  their normal clothes and all taboos are lifted, except the prohibition on sexual intercourse"
  (p. 109, folio 98).
- **The three stages of umra**: tawaf, "the circumbulation of the house of God"; the zamzam, where "the
  pilgrim washes and drink the water"; and "sai, the running back and forth between the two hills of
  Saf and Marwa" (p. 109, folio 98).
- **Tokens carried home**: "pilgrims return from hajj bearing with them sacred tokens—bottles of water
  from the holy spring, the zamzam, as well as dates, rosaries and shawls. They sprinkle this water on
  people back home, spreading the blessings they received" (p. 110, folio 99). "One of the key features
  of the eid sacrifice on the hajj is that both the sacrificial slaughter and the prayers accompanying
  it are multiplied a thousand times" (p. 110, folio 99).

#### Small village shrines as an institutional type (Lukas Werth, chapter 4)

- **Trees, groves and shrine land.** "The grove outside Rajpur is left growing only because it belongs
  to the shrine which is inside it, and the people usually emphasize the fact that no wood should be
  taken from there" (p. 95, folio 84); "In the majority of the shrines there is at least one big, old
  tree growing in the compound of a shrine" (p. 95, folio 84). Shrines outside village borders are
  associated with springs or wells (p. 95, folio 84). Graves "are nearly always decorated with
  beautifully adorned shawls" (p. 96, folio 85).
- **Compound dimensions as Werth measured them**: pir Geru's, "about fifteen by thirty metres long";
  the child's, "approximately four by four metres" (p. 95, folio 84).
- **Healing repertoire and visiting pattern.** "These shrines all have the power of healing, often of
  physical pain, and of bringing fertility to barren women" (p. 96, folio 85); "they are regularly
  visited by local people" (p. 96, folio 85); villagers "go there to state their fears, whether
  personal or communal—like the danger of drought. Women—and men—supplicate for the wellbeing of
  relatives or for children" (p. 97, folio 86).
- **Custodianship and caste.** pir Kara's shrine "is managed by his living relatives" (p. 96, folio 85)
  — the only custodianship arrangement Werth specifies. "The Sayyid caste, which is held to be the
  caste of pirs, has served a traditional role within the population of the area for generations"
  (p. 98, folio 87). Deference, one generation back: the Bahadur of Rajpur said "that one generation
  ago they would sit on the floor in the presence of a Sayyid, letting him alone sit on a charpai (a
  South Asian bed)" (p. 98, folio 87).
- **Building date, relative not absolute:** "The green tomb inside Rajpur… is younger than the shrine
  in the grove; it was erected within living memory" (p. 98, folio 87). **No year: (not stated).** The
  pir of the green tomb "never asked for any payment for his services. Everything was given to him
  with the people's free will. He did not care for material possessions" (p. 99, folio 88).
- **Scale is a difference in kind.** At "the famous, large shrines of Pakistan", "the life of whose
  devotees, particularly the most devoted murids, may be considerably influenced by the authority of
  their pir", whereas "Nothing of this holds true for the small shrines" (p. 99, folio 88).

#### A majzub's cult as an institution in the making (Jürgen Wasim Frembgen, chapter 7)

*Mama Ji Sarkar is not an archive row and his two sites are not archive rows; this material attaches
to Rawalpindi at place level (see above). It is the book's closest thing to a founding-in-progress
case study and is worth having for comparison.*

- **Tenure.** "The living-room with anteroom at the ganda nala in which the saint lived is part of the
  waqf (religious endowment) property of the adjacent Jama' Masjid. Mama Ji's followers collect the
  monthly rent for this property among themselves" (p. 157, folio 146). Its acquisition: "In 1978/1979
  his followers rented one of the rooms behind the Jama' Masjid on his behalf, situated in a very
  narrow lane which falls steeply away to a dirty channel (ganda nala), a place now called Mama Ji
  Sarkar Mohalla" (p. 153, folio 142). At the new site, "The plot belongs to the villager Sain Ikram"
  (p. 169, folio 158) — **no waqf, endowment, Auqaf department or state body is mentioned for the new
  shrine anywhere.**
- **Change of name after death.** "The house served as a darbar and baithak for the pir and his
  devotees during the saint's lifetime; after his physical death it is now called an astan" (p. 158,
  folio 147); it is "now the akhir maqam (last place) of the saint, where a framed painting of the
  majzub is placed on his bed. The latter symbolises the saint's ‘empty throne'" (p. 162, folio 151).
- **Spatial hierarchy inside the darbar**: the saint's bed in the right-hand corner "with a space
  reserved for women between the foot of the bed and the door"; "the place of the mutawalli/mujawir on
  duty is between the bedhead and the fireplace"; "privileged seats for high-ranking persons are near
  the end wall, close to the mutawalli", and "the lowest-ranking seats are, of course, near the door"
  (pp. 158–159, folios 147–148).
- **A dated building campaign.** "the mujawirs decided to order a change of the living-room into a
  shish mahal (lit. ‘palace of mirrors')…So in February/March 1990, the local ustad (master), Shabir
  Ahmad, and his shagird (apprentice), Mohammad Amin, carried out extensive mosaic work, using motifs
  like the Kaaba, the tomb of the Holy Prophet in Medina, the shrine of Barri Imam, calligraphies with
  different blessing formulas, and the depiction of flower gardens in the Mughal style"; mirror mosaic
  is said to have become popular "in the time of the emperor Shah Jahan (1628–1658)" (p. 159,
  folio 148).
- **The tomb built year by year.** "In the same month, the mujawirs and prominent murids collected
  enough money to start building the frame of a shrine, which was structurally already complete by
  July 1991" (p. 162, folio 151). "In September 1992, a forged iron door was erected at the entrance
  to the shrine, to protect it from goats" (p. 164, folio 153). "Heavy monsoon rains in summer 1994
  threatened to wash away the tomb. In response, a solidly cemented basement, accessible by four
  stairs, was built and the tomb itself was brought down" (p. 164, folio 153). "During my subsequent
  visits, in 1992 and 1993, the devotees were increasingly discontented with the initial hasty
  planning of the new shrine and decided they wanted a more generous layout" (p. 164, folio 153); two
  Sayyid Ishrat paintings of "the future shrine building" show "a prestigious, cube-shaped, domed
  structure with a veranda surrounding it" (p. 164, folio 153).
- **A financial dispute that stopped construction.** "A period of stagnation followed in the
  construction of the mazar because of a deep disagreement and rift with Mahbub Ilahi, a 42-year-old
  man from Fatehjang, living in ‘Pindi, who had been considered as the fuqra-khadim (leader of the
  followers) of the saint. He embezzled money donated for the shrine and was compelled eventually to
  leave ‘Pindi" (p. 162, folio 151).
- **Formal organisation.** "In Ramadan 1992, the followers of Mama Ji formed an anjuman (‘society'),
  called muridan muntazim kamiti Mama Ji Sarkar (Committee of the Associated Followers of Mama Ji
  Sarkar) or sometimes also intizamia bara-ye ta'mir darbar Mama Ji Sarkar (Organisation for Building
  the Shrine of `Mami Ji Sarkar [text-layer?: Mama Ji Sarkar]`), for the maintenance and organisation
  of the Nurpur-mazar and of the astan in ‘Pindi"; its presidents were "first Babu Mohammad Iqbal and
  then, since 1994, Shaikh Khalid Mahmud", and "as its degree of institutionalisation is still limited,
  arrangements are not yet bureaucratised" (p. 162, folio 151).
- **The first ʿurs, dated and costed.** "The anjuman organised the first ‘urs on 1 April 1992: with a
  kitchen langar consisting only of two huts and one tent, it was still a modest event, with about
  100–130 visitors coming in the evening. After the ‘isha' prayer, the guests were served with rice,
  meat, and tea, under a shamiana tent; a generator provided light for the langar. The tomb under
  construction was illuminated with neon garlands. Later on, a mirasi drummer and a young professional
  dhamal dancer were invited to perform. The visitors at that event were exclusively male and the
  majority belonged to the urban lower classes" (p. 162, folio 151). By the fourth ʿurs it "attracted
  hundreds of pilgrims—if not thousands as proclaimed by his followers" (p. 164, folio 153).
- **The langar's property, dated and counted.** "By April 1994, the langar had already mushroomed, with
  several makeshift huts, small gardens, and fences for the langar's property—now consisting of five
  goats and one sheep!" (p. 164, folio 153). "It is still a casual, unstructured hang-out which, in its
  informality and availability of hashish, attracts free spirits and easy-goers, as well as seekers of
  mystical blessings" (p. 164, folio 153).
- **Langar personnel and provision.** "the mujawirs, cheragh-walas (attendants looking after the oil
  lamps) and kismatgars (servants) offer food and tea as well as the huqqa (water-pipe) to the
  visitors; hashish is also consumed extensively"; chickens or quails are kept in a hutch and "before
  a mela (fair) one or two goats are fattened in advance of being butchered" (p. 159, folio 148).
- **Visitor numbers and social composition.** "From morning until evening on an average day ten to
  fifteen persons—mostly men but usually also a few women—were constantly present in the darbar. The
  total number of visitors per day was around fifty to a hundred" (p. 160, folio 149). Visitors were
  "mostly members of the urban unprivileged classes, including labourers, craftworkers, small
  employees, drug addicts, unemployed and the poor generally", while "acts of patronage mainly came
  from a few persons belonging to the middle classes"; because Mama Ji "was not a Sayyid…non-adherents
  see him in a lower position within the hierarchy of saints" (p. 161, folio 150).
- **Weekly assembly and music.** "each Thursday evening, and often on other weekdays as well, a mehfil
  (assembly) with qawwali was held in front of a congregation of twenty, thirty or even fifty people.
  If musicians were not present then cassettes by the Sabri Brothers were played", and "frequently,
  also, drummers came to perform naubat (ritual drumming) at the saint's baithak" (pp. 161–162, folios
  150–151).
- **Ritual policing by the attendants.** "in both sanctuaries, in ‘Pindi and Nurpur, expressions of
  high ecstasy and excitement by the saint's adherents were disapproved of and seldom seemed to occur.
  The group of mujawirs successfully promoted a serious framework of rituals in order to bring them
  into line with other orthodox Qadiri and Chishti tomb-cults" (p. 162, folio 151).
- **A shrine-side image economy.** "Up to 1989, Ishrat rented a part of a shop adjacent to the astan
  where he sold hand-painted portraits of the saint…as well as individual portraits based upon passport
  photographs"; the portraits are placed in homes and shops and "have a popularising, if not
  advertising, effect", one bus driver displaying his pir's photograph "with a plastic board giving his
  name, honorary titles and address in the front window of his vehicle" (p. 159, folio 148).
- **Women and access.** "In the folk veneration of saints it is usually women who are more involved than
  men", but at the new site "women are conspicuously absent in Nurpur, partly since, during this
  provisional stage of transition, no suitable arrangements have yet been made for their stay and proper
  social contacts" (p. 163, folio 152); "Mama Ji's astan in ‘Pindi is already very confined and, in my
  observation, women do not find it easy to go there" (p. 163, folio 152). In the saint's lifetime "he
  sometimes sent them away from his bed—even if they were not menstruating, as stated by informants—and
  only allowed access to men" (p. 163, folio 152). Frembgen's counterfactual: "A higher participation of
  women in the cult would most probably have lent it another character" (p. 163, folio 152).
- **Fieldwork frame.** "During a seven-year period from March 1988 to September 1995 (in the course of
  twelve trips to Pakistan) I visited these two shrines frequently. Following the death of the majzub in
  1991, the visits were also to the Nurpur mazar" (p. 169, folio 158).

#### A family shrine and a healing economy in rural Punjab (Katherine P. Ewing, chapter 8)

*Ewing's village, family shrine and informants are all pseudonymous and unlocated — "the privacy of the
people who were willing to share their lives with me" (p. 192, folio 181) — so none of this can attach
to any archive row.*

- **Hereditary custodianship.** "Ashiq had been designated the future successor of his paternal uncle,
  who is the current caretaker (gaddi nishin) of the family shrine" (p. 174, folio 163). Succession is
  reassigned after a death: "All say that after Sa'in Sahib, this boy should sit on the gaddi"… "At
  first the nazar [gaze] was on Ashiq, but now, after his death, people's nazar is on Achi" (p. 179,
  folio 168). The qualification for the seat is withdrawal from ordinary work: "he doesn't go to school,
  doesn't work, always sits near Sa'in Sahib. His parents sent him to work, but he also gave that up. He
  returned to the village" (p. 179, folio 168).
- **An annual mela for a dead majzub.** "His mela takes place every year. So Sa'in Sahib repeats all
  these things. Everything happens just as it was on the first day. They cook big pots of food. Qawwal
  [singers] come. There is qawwali [devotional singing] the whole night." (p. 189, folio 178). Ewing
  glosses the mela as "the annual celebration at the family shrine" (p. 188, folio 177) and calls its
  liminal time one "recreated as an annual ritual" (p. 191, folio 180). **No date, month or attendance
  figure is given for the family shrine's ʿurs: (not stated)** (p. 174, folio 163).
- **The healing economy around pirs.** A ta‘wiz (amulet) bought for a dream affliction (p. 176,
  folio 165); a pir's prescription, "Take an almond that has two pieces inside it and bring it to me. I
  will read something over it" (p. 177, folio 166); repeated journeys to "a pir in Kharian" (p. 177,
  folio 166); a faqir consulted at Data Sahib (p. 178, folio 167).
- **Yunani medicine runs alongside shrine remedies.** "kol, tabashir [chalk, which she said was commonly
  used as a Yunani medicine to reduce sores on the tongue or inside body]… and choti ilachi [cardamom]",
  given for five days (p. 176, folio 165); Ewing calls it "a Yunani (Greek) medicine" that "corrected
  the problem of excessive heat in his body" (p. 178, folio 167).
- **Disbelief inside the family is part of the data.** "Her husband [Rabi‘a's daughter's husband]
  doesn't believe this. He says, ‘We will see'" (p. 178, folio 167).

#### The regional hierarchy of shrines and the state, in Gujarat (Helene Basu, chapter 6)

*Basu's material is Indian and no archive row is evidenced by it; it is kept because it is the book's
fullest account of how shrines rank each other through gifts and of a state takeover in another
jurisdiction.*

- **Rank is read off the direction of gifts.** "Shrine cults revolve around exchanges of ritual services
  and gifts between unequals, and the rank of different regional cults, co-existing within a single
  area, may be inferred from the direction of the gifts between cults" (p. 128, folio 117). "The most
  important cult centres are managed by Sayyid pirs who deny taking gifts altogether, but assert their
  supreme status by displaying generosity and giving" (p. 128, folio 117).
- **Categories of gift**: nazrana, "tributary gifts such as those given by a client to a patron",
  "formally the possession of the saint…usually redistributed at the shrine and used for the benefit of
  all"; "jakat (alms)", "sadaqa" and "`qurbáni [text-layer?: qurbani]` (animal sacrifices)" (p. 145,
  folio 134).
- **Langar as a marker of status, and an explicit negative.** "the distribution of langar to devotees is
  a common practice at higher-status shrines, and especially so during the festive days of the ‘urs
  celebration" — but "no langar is provided at the shrine of Bava Gor. Instead, niaz is given by
  individual cult followers in fulfilment of a vow" (p. 145, folio 134).
- **ʿUrs service as a marker of rank**: the asymmetry between Rifa'i Sayyid pirs and Sidi faqirs "is
  revealed particularly at ‘urs celebrations at Rifa'i shrines when Sidi men and women perform ritual
  services for the pir" (p. 145, folio 134).
- **Succession among the Rifa'i**, quoted from van der Veer: "in each generation one member of the family
  which inherits the sajjada nishin (the spiritual succession of Sayyid Ahmad Kabir Rifa'i) becomes a
  mastan", and "a mastan is clearly beyond the Law but…he can never be head of the family, sajjada
  nishin" (van der Veer 1992:560) (p. 146, folio 135).
- **A state takeover in Gujarat, dated.** "Legally, the shrine falls under the laws of the religious
  endowment act, which places it under state control vested in the charity commissioner" (p. 131,
  folio 120); it is "Formally managed by a trust consisting of three male members—two Bohras, one Sidi"
  (p. 131, folio 120). "Conflicts over traditional rights at the shrine originated in the seventies, when
  the charity commissioner's officers charged the customarily installed mujavirs (officiating faqirs)
  with misuse of religious donations and subsequently dismissed them from their offices" (p. 131,
  folio 120). The system before: "the shrine was managed on behalf of the jama'at as a whole. Shrine
  services were carried out by three faqir families taking weekly turns"; "Authority over shrine rituals
  was held by the elders, several of whom happened to be women" (p. 131, folio 120). "At the occasions of
  large shrine festivals, gifts of money and natural products given to the saints were redistributed
  among the other members of the Sidi jama'at" (p. 131, folio 120).
- **Basu's reading of the intervention**: "Whereas the traditional system focused upon the collectivity
  as a whole, the intervention by state agencies into the formal organisation of the cult resulted in the
  centralisation of authority in the family of the Sidi trustee" (p. 131, folio 120).
- **Money thrown at performances**: at Sayyid shrines the comic goma is danced "in a courtyard of Sayyid
  shrines by relatively large groups of male Sidi dancers", and "the audience interacts with the
  performers by throwing money (coins and notes) into the circle" (p. 147, folio 136).

#### The Sufi lodge as an institutional form (Charles Lindholm, chapter 10)

- **Vocabulary and dating.** "Inspired Sufi saints (shaikhs, murshids or pirs) founded tariqas
  (lodges—literally pathways) where they were surrounded by pupils (murids—ones who have made it their5
  [text-layer?: their, with endnote 5 flattened onto it] will to enter the path, also known as saliks,
  wayfarers)", dated "By the twelfth century" (p. 224, folio 213); disciples "gathered together in a
  khanaqa (a Sufi lodge, zawiya in North Africa)" to "learn from their master, practice the discipline of
  the group, undertake collective rituals, and participate in the local community" (p. 225, folio 214).
- **Common property inside the brotherhood**: the novice "shared out all his worldly goods with his
  fellows, living with them in a communist community where distinctions of wealth, knowledge and strength
  ceased and the word ‘my' was anathema" (pp. 224–225, folios 213–214).
- **The tomb as the engine of cult formation**: "After the founder's death, the tomb of a revered saint
  became the centre for the proliferation of new devotional cults around Sufi lodges (Trimingham 1971)"
  (p. 225, folio 214). **Silsila defined**: "Each new cult validated its authenticity by tracing a
  silsila, or spiritual genealogy, to the sacred founder (often Abdul-Qadir al-Jilani), who himself was
  linked back through to Ali or the first caliph, Abu Bakr, and if possible connected back to Muhammad
  himself" (p. 225, folio 214).
- **Dhikr as the revenue-bearing ritual**: "the ecstatic practice of dhikr (remembrance) soon became the
  central and defining ‘liturgical' ritual (Gilsenan 1973) of successful Sufi orders, as dramatic group
  performances of remembrance drew crowds of onlookers, who often became lay devotees of the
  brotherhoods, giving money and patronage to them" (p. 225, folio 214). Itemised for the thirteenth
  century: "successful brotherhoods relied primarily upon emotional public preaching, redistribution of
  wealth in charity, spectacular presentations of dhikr, and the performance of miracles to gain the
  approval of the populace and endowments from lay brothers" (p. 231, folio 220).
- **Saints were not monastics.** "Sufi saints have never been able to withdraw completely into monastic
  isolation, but were often married men who raised children, worked as craftsmen, merchants, soldiers,
  bureaucrats, and so on, while simultaneously serving as the fountains of God's grace" (p. 228,
  folio 217); many "advocated hard work, asceticism, and obedience to authority as disciplines leading to
  enlightenment, instilling in their devotees an almost Calvinist devotion to their daily duties in the
  workplace" (p. 228, folio 217). "The huge financial success of the South Asian Khojas, who are devoted
  to their spiritual leader, the Aga Khan, indicates the potential economic virtues of charismatic
  leadership—when the leader has a high degree of business acumen" (p. 228, folio 217).
- **The anti-shrine counter-movement**: "the eighteenth-century Wahhabi revival in Saudi Arabia, an
  ethically rigorous austerity movement which promulgated anti-Sufi doctrines such as the prohibition of
  all worship at tombs and the cessation of Sufi practices such as dhikr" (p. 228, folio 217).
- **Hierarchy of deputies.** The present-day qutb is surrounded by "an elaborate status order of his
  khalifa (deputies)"; "the resident pir was the khalifa of a greater pir, who was supposedly under the
  authority of a still greater hidden master, and so on" (p. 226, folio 215).
- **The saint's self-presentation as a costume**: "most saints dressed in special clothing, had long
  beards, moved with solemn dignity, showed no overt interest in power, appeared chaste and demure,
  carried no weapons, and spoke with deliberation. Others, taking the role of dervish, had begging bowls
  and wore patched robes; they wandered without a home, transformed in drug-induced trance" (p. 231,
  folio 220).

### Arguments and interpretations, by contributor

**The volume's ten chapters do not argue one case. The editors say so themselves and stage their
contributors against each other; the disagreements are listed at the end of this section.**

#### Pnina Werbner and Helene Basu — chapter 1, the editors' joint introduction

- **The thesis.** "the power of ritual is seen to derive not from belief as a set of abstracted ideas
  but from ritual as a complex set of transformative, embodied, negotiated ethical and aesthetic
  practices and the experiences which their enactment generates" (p. 19, folio 8).
- **The Sufi anthropology of the body, as they state it.** Sufism posits an analogy "between a denial
  of embodied desire (nafs) and the acquisition of true knowledge, gained by the detached, eternal,
  rational ‘soul' (ruh)"; "the death of the desiring soul, the nafs, brings about the eternalisation
  of the body after death, while the soul retains its living agency" (p. 17, folio 6). But "this Sufi
  theory of charisma and the elaborate cosmology it has generated are not necessarily articulated
  explicitly either by saints or by their devotees. The Sufi theory remains an implicit, embodied form
  of charismatic knowledge" (p. 17, folio 6).
- **Three theoretical lineages the volume draws on**: "the processual, grounded in the work of Durkheim
  and of van Gennep on liminality in rites of passage; the aesthetic, inspired by French
  phenomenologists and poststructuralists; and the substantive, building on Mauss's insights into the
  symbolic embodiment of subjects through objects" (p. 18, folio 7).
- **Charisma is not at the centre.** Against Shils, for whom charisma is "located at the centre of
  society (ibid.: 201)": "There is, however, something intrinsically problematic in this siting of
  charisma at the centre and equating of it with state power and its routinised extensions" (p. 26,
  folio 15). What needs theorising is "the nature of charismatic (saintly) dissent and opposition to
  the bureaucratic domination of the state" (p. 26, folio 15).
- **The state as anti-charisma.** "The state and its politicians, by contrast, are seen as menacing,
  corrupt, greedy and unfeeling. They are not truly ‘rational' in the Weberian sense since they bend
  the rules to their selfish interests"; "Theirs is a charisma of unbridled power" (p. 26, folio 15),
  against which the saint's charisma "is the product of his perceived (and projected) self-denial and
  self-mastery, of love and generosity" (pp. 26–27, folios 15–16).
- **"Islamisation of the indigenous", their central inversion.** "In contrast to Ahmad's theory of the
  ‘indigenisation of Islam' we have to recognise the reverse process of an ‘Islamisation of the
  indigenous'" (p. 30, folio 19). They quote Eaton at length to get there: syncretic folk-religion
  rests on "the projection of contemporary religious categories, which gained prominence in the
  nineteenth and twentieth centuries, onto a premodern Indian past" (p. 29, folio 18).
- **Against Imtiaz Ahmad and against Francis Robinson, equally.** For both, "‘Islamisation' refers
  exclusively to Islamic orthodoxy at the cost of plurality, thus overlooking the diversity of views
  represented by Islamic scholars, theologians, Sufis and holy men as well as the historically
  contingent and shifting nature of internal Muslim debates over questions of correct practice"
  (p. 29, folio 18).
- **Against van der Veer, partly.** They accept his caution that "The mere presence of Hindus at Muslim
  shrines… cannot be taken as a sign of a common, syncretic practice of folk-religion" (p. 31,
  folio 20), but conclude "Against van der Veer it may be argued that Hindus and Muslims do, in fact,
  still join together at shrines in amity" (p. 31, folio 20), citing Basu's and Saheb's chapters as
  the counter-reality (p. 31, folio 20).
- **Pilgrimage is counter-structure, not anti-structure.** Against Turner (1974: Ch. 5), "it seems more
  accurate to say that sacred pilgrimage creates not ‘anti'-structure but ‘counter'-structure"
  (p. 23, folio 12). Regional cults "create spatially alternative focal organisations to those centred
  on bounded, territorially based states or administrative units", "inclusive rather than exclusive,
  tending to cross ethnic and national boundaries" (p. 23, folio 12).
- **Two disagreements stated outright.** With Asad: "In this sense one has to disagree with Asad's
  genealogical account of anthropological studies of ritual which, he argues, almost universally have
  tended to divorce symbolic analysis from ‘pragmatics' (Asad 1993: Ch. 4)" (p. 19, folio 8). With
  Csordas and Shilling: their attempt to recover "the pre-or unsocialised dimensions of emotion and
  sensuality" fails because "the moment of the emotionally expressive is also the moment of dialogical
  and social communication, that is, the moment of the cultural"; "We may speculate upon, but cannot
  reach, the biologically ‘unspeakable'" (pp. 19–20, folios 8–9).
- **A named disciplinary target.** "a third scholarly discursive disjunction… best exemplified in
  anthropology by the work of Clifford Geertz and Ernest Gellner (Geertz 1968; Gellner 1981; for a
  critique see Baldick 1989:155–156), severs Sufism from its organisational underpinnings" (p. 15,
  folio 4). Trimingham (1971) is credited with having "spelled out clearly the generative connections
  between saints, cults and tariqa" (p. 15, folio 4).
- **Majzub and mastan.** "those drunk on divine love, the rebels and madmen of medieval Sufism (Eaton
  1978; Digby 1984)"; "In Islamic discourses, majzub represent the Other of orthoprax Sufism
  categorised as ba-shar, outside and beyond the reach of the Law"; their "madness" is "understood as
  a positive sign of proximity to God and absorption in love" (p. 20, folio 9).
- **A question they say is rarely asked**: "One fascinating question rarely addressed in studies of
  Sufism is the sense of loss Sufi devotees experience when their saint dies" (p. 20, folio 9).
- **Inversion as the common mechanism.** "Cults of saints create realms of anti/counter-structure by
  reversals of quotidian logic" — the examples being Werth's absent saint, Werbner's hajj reversal of
  the Ismail myth, Frembgen's pure polluting body and Basu's Sidi hierarchy (p. 25, folio 14).
- **Charisma against modernity.** "Perhaps it is this anti-structural emphasis that accounts for the
  continued vigour of saint cults in Muslim South Asia" (p. 25, folio 14), drawing on Weber via
  Eisenstadt for charisma as "antithetical to routine and rationalised institutional orders" and on
  Foucault (1992) for self-fashioning as "a precondition both of true knowledge and of lasting,
  other-oriented bonds of love and friendship" (p. 25, folio 14).
- **The volume as a challenge to a neighbouring field**: "in highlighting the continued vitality of
  Sufism in South Asia, this book also constitutes a challenge to Middle East scholars to probe more
  deeply into the embodiments of Sufi charismatic authority and organisation in the Middle East and
  North Africa today" (p. 28, folio 17). They also suspect the record: "The impression that Sufism in
  North Africa is no longer viable may stem largely from a dearth of detailed contemporary research on
  living saints" (p. 28, folio 17).

#### Samuel Landell Mills — chapter 2, "The hardware of sanctity" (Bangladesh)

- **His thesis.** "The sanctity of pirs derives not from some imagined essence of Sufism or Islam, nor
  from the mere appropriation of a holy name, but from the structured use by pirs and devotees of a
  whole range of paraphernalia, associated with the saintly body" (p. 42, folio 31). "In practical
  terms, the spiritual power of a dead pir is accessible to devotees through the material objects and
  structures with which he has come to be identified" (p. 46, folio 35).
- **Against Durkheim.** Durkheim attributed the sacred's "‘contagious' quality to the fictive, abstract
  nature of religious entities" (1966:21); Landell Mills counters that "the empowerment of sacred
  objects which from a Durkheimian perspective is seen to stem from the experience of collective
  psychology may in reality be derived from a more intimate experience, that of dyadic
  intercommunication with its moments of mutual incorporation" (p. 43, folio 32).
- **Against Dumont.** Pirs' "uniquely individual identities are assets which are indispensable to their
  aspirations; they are not merely the by-product of challenging a conservative, holistic social order,
  as Dumont has suggested for the Hindu ‘world renouncer' (Dumont 1957)" (p. 62, folio 51).
- **A living pir builds his own tomb-effect before he dies.** "A living pir and his devotees
  progressively sublimate his public persona in the objective structures constructed around him, so
  that they can be taken as extensions of him" (p. 46, folio 35); "In this way, a death-transcending
  personality is constructed during his lifetime" (p. 50, folio 39), so that death "will represent the
  consummation of his sanctity" (p. 50, folio 39).
- **Growth is structurally required.** "there is an implicit imperative to expand embedded within the
  pir identity" (p. 59, folio 48); "When a pir moves from being of local interest to becoming a
  regional, national or international attraction, his putative spiritual power and his accoutrements
  increase in tandem" (p. 48, folio 37). "The acquisition of worldly impedimenta is inevitably taken as
  a sign of hitherto undisclosed spiritual pre-eminence" (p. 60, folio 49).
- **Devotees complete an unfinished image.** The pir "is offering very limited information about himself
  to the disciples in the anticipation that devotees will be able to fill in the gaps themselves"
  (p. 52, folio 41); "Devotees are offered a set of unfinished scripts which they must complete by
  themselves" (p. 53, folio 42); "ritual is allowed to leave intention obscure" (p. 53, folio 42).
- **The tomb is the one symbol that unifies a very varied field.** "The Islamic saints of Bangladesh
  vary enormously in their attributes, and sometimes it is only through the use of the tomb symbol that
  some commonality and shared meaning in the pir category is evoked" (p. 50, folio 39).
- **Pirs against Islamists.** "Whilst Islamists seek to bring the unrealised order of Islam into the
  world, pirs are impresarios of a hidden reality of divine power in the present" (p. 55, folio 44). By
  'Islamist' he means Jama'at-e Islami and Tabliq Jama'at; "Both organisations derive in certain
  respects from Sufism but are antagonistic towards pirs" (p. 64, folio 53).
- **Spiritual authority requires separation from politics** — his explanation for the Zaker Party's
  failure: "in Bangladesh at least, the Prophet's singular fusion of spiritual and temporal authority
  cannot be surpassed by his imitators, because their ‘spiritual' authority demands a materially
  enshrined separation from the hurly-burly of routine politics" (p. 58, folio 47). He offers the
  reading as likelihood, not fact (p. 59, folio 48).
- **Scripturalist and orientalist devaluations are the same move.** "The logocentric bias of the latter
  regards Sufism as degenerate, a view endorsed by some orientalist scholars seeking the ‘essence' of
  the religion in its scriptural past, just as clerics find a perfect Islam in the Qur'an and Hadiths.
  Both types of scholar devalue local practice in favour of the ‘great tradition'" (p. 62, folio 51).
- **Hindu idioms, used self-consciously.** Eck (1985) on "the all-seeing eye" at the centre of Hindu
  temples "can be applied to the structure of the darbar/mazar complex" — but "these idioms are used
  self-consciously by pirs such as Atroshi to convey ‘Islamic' messages", giving "an image of
  indigenous culture and personhood subordinated by the reality of divine power, of Bengal made
  Islamic" (p. 54, folio 43).

#### S. A. A. Saheb — chapter 3, Nagore-e-Sharif (Tamil Nadu)

- **Shrines as inter-religious institutions.** "The liberal attitude of Sufism and its tolerance of
  other religions have made its shrines centres of inter-religious cooperation. The pir-murid
  (master-disciple) relationship is an open one, and hence Sufi lodges in South Asia are places of
  worship for communities of both Muslims and Hindus" (p. 66, folio 55).
- **He declines to call the Hindu elements borrowings.** "It might seem that Hindu devotees' involvement
  and participation in a shrine's activities legitimise ‘Hindu' practices in the dargah's sacred
  performances. Yet the origin and current provenance of various customs is not so clearly
  identifiable" (p. 67, folio 56). Of the flags: "Historically, the flag festival may have been borrowed
  from local Hindu customary celebrations, although the use of flags is common in Islam too" (p. 73,
  folio 62).
- **Two origin stories of the flags, reported and not adjudicated.** Version one, from "the Tanjore
  District Records (Hemingway 1906)", makes the flags a royal Maratha sign "of victory and prosperity";
  version two, "told by the dargah management and descendants of the saint", makes them "the symbolic
  representation of Ali" (p. 73, folio 62). Saheb's own reading: "These multivocal associations explain
  why one can observe a mixture of faiths under the combined shadow of the flags" (p. 73, folio 62).
- **The festival makes locality sacred.** The fusions "are modes of incorporating and sacralising an
  inter-religious community and its spaces, joined in celebrating the anniversary of the saint's death"
  (p. 67, folio 56); "Through the festival, localism is inscribed in the dargah, while the sanctity of
  the dargah is carried to the towns in its immediate vicinity" (p. 81, folio 70).
- **Distance is overcome by flag rituals performed elsewhere.** "devotees who are unable to attend the
  flag festival at Nagore can perform the same festival at their respective native places by way of
  offering fatiha in a manner similar to that at the Nagore dargah", a tradition observed "throughout
  south India and outside the country, in Singapore and Sri Lanka" (pp. 72–73, folios 61–62).
- **A five-element reading of the shrine, borrowed openly from a Hindu temple theory.** Saheb quotes Rao
  (1979:84) — "‘the foundation of the temple is made to represent Earth (prithvi), the walls of the
  sanctum [are] water (ap) and the tower over it fire (tejas)…'" — and maps the dargah onto it term by
  term (p. 81, folio 70).
- **Worship as a transaction.** "matter (flags) moves up and the spirit (blessings of the saint) flows
  down… The emanations that proceed from the tomb of the saint must be picked up by the faith in the
  devotee's heart. Devotion is the transformer" (p. 81, folio 70).
- **A class claim, asserted without evidence in the chunk**: "Undoubtedly, support for Sufi cults comes
  from the lower classes and castes of Muslim society" (p. 84, folio 73) — flagged here because the
  chapter offers no data for it.

#### Lukas Werth — chapter 4, "The saint who disappeared" (Pakistani village shrines)

- **His programme.** "How, then, are all these small shrines used, and why are they there? My hope is to
  find some tentative answers to the question: ‘What, if anything, is a shrine in `Pakistan?'4`
  [text-layer?: Pakistan?', with a flattened endnote marker 4 glued to it]" (p. 90, folio 79). "In this
  chapter I shall concentrate on these small shrines of seemingly only local importance—or not even
  that—like the ones at Rajpur" (p. 90, folio 79).
- **The framing claim about Pakistani Islam.** "Islam in Pakistan, as elsewhere in the Muslim world,
  cannot be explained only by its Five Pillars, but rests also essentially on local traditions. Shrines
  are a part of those traditions in which the universal principles of Islam find the application they
  seem to need" (p. 90, folio 79).
- **The localization argument, the chapter's core.** "the link to the world beyond the grave is in the
  locality itself: in the relics of Christian saints or the graves of Muslim pirs… But since God is
  everywhere, it is the plurality of saints that makes this ubiquitous God concrete. It turns the
  macrocosm into an extension of the microcosm by attaching it to a certain locality" (p. 91, folio 80).
  Localization also determines what is asked for: "People often pray to the pirs at their shrines
  because of concrete problems: disease, childlessness and other afflictions" (p. 91, folio 80).
- **Holiness as a caste.** "The expression pira-di qaumzat, ‘the caste of the pir', is often used as an
  alternative to sayyida-di qaum, ‘the caste of the Sayyid'. The meaning remains the same in both cases"
  (p. 91, folio 80); the brahmin parallel is offered and then limited, because "the high status of the
  Sayyids is due not to their exclusive right to conduct religious services (they are not priests), but
  to their participation in the sacredness of Muhammad" (p. 91, folio 80). Conclusion: "a category of
  Islamic holiness is represented as a caste (qaum, zat), which means a distinct quality or `species.5`
  [text-layer?: species., with a flattened endnote marker 5 glued to it]" (p. 91, folio 80).
- **Why the saints can be anonymous** — the chapter's payoff: "This is one reason why it is not necessary
  in Pakistan to know the name or even the life of the pir of a small shrine: the fact that he is
  supposed to belong to a holy category is sufficient to qualify him to be prayed to, even though the
  personality of the saint has been almost effaced and is of very limited importance" (p. 91, folio 80).
  He immediately limits it: "But still the fact remains that it is human graves which are the point of
  veneration, and by no means the grave of every Sayyid becomes a shrine" (p. 91, folio 80).
- **Small shrines differ from large ones in kind, not scale.** "Large shrines are connected with the
  names of their saints, and with their individual biographies. In the case of the village shrines
  described here, by contrast, the stories of their pirs carry no individuating features" (p. 96,
  folio 85).
- **Marginality is what makes a saint.** "Unknown saints typically come from outside the village and are
  mendicants, and/or otherwise people outside the normal order" (p. 96, folio 85); "It is that
  externality which enables him to perform a miracle, and his final disappearance qualifies him to
  become a saint" (p. 97, folio 86).
- **The wilderness argument.** "there is an association between trees and shrines, and most of all
  shrines which lie outside the borders of villages… These shrines seem to have a clear association with
  the wilderness" (p. 95, folio 84); "Trees, therefore, are associated with the opposite of human
  culture, with the wilderness, and hence with danger" (p. 94, folio 83). He generalises it beyond his
  village: "Many of South Asia's shrines, large or small, seem to rely at least in one of their
  dimensions on the same symbolism (see for example Werbner 1996). They are, or were originally, built
  outside settlements, in areas conceived as jangal" (p. 96, folio 85), jangal covering "not only
  forest, but also wilderness" (p. 101, folio 90).
- **A paradox he records without resolving**: the grove shrine "is the only one of the three shrines
  which lies outside the village boundaries, but it is the most frequented one. If the people of the
  village have troubles, they are most likely to go there" (p. 93, folio 82).
- **Sacred topography.** "Through the shrines a sacred topography linking small village communities
  within an area, across the dangerous spaces between villages, is revealed" (p. 98, folio 87); shrines
  "not only transform the map of South Asia into an Islamic domain, but also localize and concretize
  sacredness" (p. 99, folio 88). "In Pakistan saints are often seen as liminal, dangerous and
  unpredictable figures" (p. 100, folio 89).

#### Pnina Werbner — chapter 5, Ghamkol Sharif

*Her institutional and economic claims are under "Langar, sacrifice and the gift" above; what follows is
the interpretive frame.*

- **The hajj as the template for the lodge.** "In saints' lodges and shrines in South Asia, this moment
  is expanded and magnified to become the central trope of the lodge, binding together the moral ideas
  of mystical Sufism with the organisational agendas of the lodges as centres of far-flung regional
  cults" (p. 110, folio 99). The pilgrim's movement is "backwards from death towards purity like that of
  a baby" (p. 109, folio 98), and "Just as all the pilgrims, both male and female, identify with
  Ibrahim, a male, so too they all identify with Hajara, a female" (p. 110, folio 99) — Werbner calls
  the identification with a woman "significant".
- **Mediation is collective, not individual.** "What is stressed here is mediation with God not by a
  single person but by the community of believers, united in their intentionality" (p. 111, folio 100).
- **The vegetarianism is explained structurally, not devotionally.** "As a perpetual giver he cannot
  take. The unilateral nature of gifting would be compromised if he took of what was given him"
  (p. 118, folio 107).
- **The paradox she names and refuses to resolve.** "just as he denies the importance of the visits and
  honours granted him by state officials and politicians, so too, paradoxically, do the visits prove
  that Zindapir is indeed a great and spiritually powerful friend (wali) of Allah" (p. 121, folio 110);
  "despite the constructed ideological opposition between the lodge and the world, the growth and
  success of the lodge have profited from official goodwill" (p. 121, folio 110).
- **Healing power from gendered ambiguity, marked by her as speculation.** "His persona combines male
  and female qualities—the gentleness, love and tenderness of a woman with the power, authority and
  honour of a man (see also Kurin 1984)"; "he is a maternal father or a paternal mother, protective yet
  authoritative" (p. 123, folio 112). "One can only speculate" (p. 123, folio 112).
- **Two registers of relationship.** "To most women supplicants the saint is a distant, charismatic
  figure, fearful and aweinspiring [text-layer?: awe-inspiring]" (p. 123, folio 112), whereas the women
  disciples who prepare the ʿurs "treat him with the freedom of companions", entertain him with gossip
  "including the ridiculous behaviour of the anthropologist", and are "an invaluable source of
  information to him about what goes on beyond the confines of the room in which he meditates" (p. 123,
  folio 112).
- **Sacred peripherality.** "a key feature of Ghamkol Sharif is its sacred peripherality (Turner 1974)"
  (p. 125, folio 114).

#### Helene Basu — chapter 6, the Sidi cult of Bava Gor (Gujarat)

- **Inheritable charisma and its ranking.** "Inheritable charisma (‘blood') distinguishes Sayyid from
  all other Muslim castes" (p. 130, folio 119); the Sidi, "while acknowledging the ritual superiority of
  the Sayyid… claim a similar though lower-order charisma transmitted by birth from their African
  saintly ancestors" (p. 130, folio 119).
- **Hot and cold as a relational scale, not fixed values.** They "refer metonymically to both physical
  and mental processes, to fertility, death, gender, power, ecstasy, possession, emotion, purity and
  impurity… manifestations of a scale of evaluation that depends on relationships and transformation"
  (p. 132, folio 121). "saints (pirs) are said to be cold because they have overcome the hot nafs
  (desiring soul), that is, the instinctual, ‘animal' nature of humans (Kurin 1984)" (p. 133, folio 122).
- **Possession-healing as cosmic repair, not individual cure.** "the healing of possession is seen more
  as a means of countering a cosmic threat to the hierarchical order than as a cure centred upon the
  individual patient" (p. 133, folio 122); "demons and malign spirits are reifications of social
  disorder" (p. 133, folio 122), a conclusion she reaches by qualifying Kakar (1982) with Kapferer
  (1983).
- **Inversion of value.** The Sidi devalue "‘aql (reason)", call themselves "‘people without reason'
  (‘aql vagar loko)" and "‘mad people' (ganda loko)", and thereby "claim a superior status as dilvale or
  ‘people of the heart'", also "‘Badshah loko' or kingly people" — "Sidi are ‘kings of the heart'"
  (p. 137, folio 126).
- **Against Parry.** Where Benares funeral priests are "negatively charged with the sins of devotees",
  gifts to the Sidi are "loaded with negatively evaluated emotions of hot dukh"; but "unlike Hindu
  funeral priests in `Benaras [text-layer?: Benares]`, the Sidi do not absorb dukh but transform it into
  positive emotions of maja…through a situational inversion of hierarchy" (p. 146, folio 135).
- **The dargah as a site of class control.** "Bava Gor supports the more powerful against the less
  powerful; the dominant—patrons, employers—against the dominated—labourers, clients, and women. Seen in
  this context, the dargah is a place where class-based social control is publicly exercised" (p. 140,
  folio 129). "The lowest position at the shrine is accorded to Hindu and Tribal women afflicted by
  spirit possession" (p. 143, folio 132).
- **One hierarchy is suspended and another enforced.** "while the hierarchy of saints and humans is
  collapsed in trance, the gender hierarchy is maintained and emphasised" (pp. 143–144, folios 132–133).
- **Her conclusion.** The cult "recognises, and is partly resonant with, the hegemonic Muslim status
  hierarchy which places Sayyids at its apex", but "a counter-discourse simultaneously subverts the
  basic premises of this hierarchy", redefining "people normally judged as extremely low (black,
  begging, servile) as morally and cosmically superior" (p. 147, folio 136).

#### Jürgen Wasim Frembgen — chapter 7, the majzub Mama Ji Sarkar (Rawalpindi)

- **His stated aim**: to trace "a cult in transition", "from a cult centred on devotion to a living
  majzub residing in a darbar (court, Sufi lodge) to the establishment, after his death, of a
  dargah/mazar (shrine complex) and the reconstruction of the cult around this new focal place of
  devotion" (p. 151, folio 140).
- **Against the category 'folk' Islam**: by "interrogating the subjective realities of followers we may
  gain new insight into what is normally referred to, somewhat dismissively, as ‘folk' Islam" (p. 151,
  folio 140).
- **Against biomedical diagnosis**: "Advocates of Western orthodox biomedicine might suggest as a
  diagnosis that many majzubs are actually schizophrenics, hysterics, epileptics or autistics", but such
  a diagnosis "misses…the emic perspectives on charismatic ‘possession'"; a majzub is "a person being in
  a permanent state of ecstasy and divine emotion, in most cases enraptured since birth" (p. 156,
  folio 145).
- **Charisma against inheritance** — a direct attack on hereditary custodianship. "Being a real majzub
  gifted with a miraculous charisma makes him superior to any sajjada-nishin or gaddi-nishin,
  descendants of former respected saints, who are nothing more than ‘heirs of prayer carpets'; he is
  therefore considered as having far more personal baraka (blessings) than those embodying a mystical
  hierarchical order" (p. 157, folio 146).
- **The majzub breaks both of Weber's routes, and this is his central finding.** "Max Weber argues that
  with the death of the charismatic, his mystical power either is converted into a ‘charisma of office'
  and thereby routinised, or becomes ‘tradition' in the form of a hereditary charisma" (p. 164,
  folio 153). But: "As it is in the nature of a majzub not to have an heir, the saint's pristine source
  of authority has obviously been lost. Consequently one would expect a decline in the ‘personalised'
  character of his charisma and a growing institutionalisation… Yet the majzub's disciples did not
  become real officials, and the rational organisation remains modest and unstructured" (p. 164,
  folio 153).
- **Death as a break in the devotees' lives.** "the saint's transition to the ‘real life' with God means
  a break in his followers' ordinary lives, a kind of set-back into normalcy" (pp. 164–165, folios
  153–154); "Many of them often think: ‘When Mama Ji was alive, all things went better!'" (p. 165,
  folio 154).
- **Against a purely constructivist charisma.** "this conceptualisation of charisma as ‘imagined' and
  ‘constructed' must contend with an ontological difference: the live saint was experienced not only as
  powerfully sacred but also as human, as vulnerable, as requiring care and loving attention" (p. 166,
  folio 155). "Mama Ji was ‘mother' in two senses (hence the name ma-ma)—as protector and giver but also
  as an object of protection" (p. 166, folio 155).
- **A contradiction he names between doctrine and practice.** Islamic mysticism holds "the saint's power
  being considerably enhanced after his death", yet "my observations of living Sufism show that the
  presence of the majzub amidst his followers is of crucial importance to them"; disciples "attach value
  to the living, physical body of their saint, whereas in the ideology of mysticism the body is seen as
  an obstacle" (p. 166, folio 155).
- **Against Turner on communitas.** The devotees' communitas is "normative" and domestic rather than
  liminal: they "habitually just went around the corner to experience communitas in the presence of
  their saint. Such ‘normative' communitas was part of their ordinary lives" and "did not need to be
  embedded in the liminal situation of a temporary pilgrimage setting" (p. 163, folio 152). **He
  nonetheless applies Turner to the ʿurs** — it "can be interpreted as a liminal situation for the
  pilgrims, who developed among themselves a communitas-type relationship characterised by a sense of
  equality, fraternity and solidarity", citing Turner 1973:207 (p. 162, folio 151). **Both statements
  stand.**

#### Katherine P. Ewing — chapter 8, a majzub and his mother

- **Two explanatory frames held simultaneously, not resolved.** "These two explanatory models—that he was
  a sa'in and that he was ill—are tied to different historical moments in the evolution of her
  relationship with her son. She has not chosen one over the other as ‘reality,' but rather experiences
  both as true" (p. 180, folio 169). "Rabi‘a's concerns as a mother were encompassed by a narrative of
  sainthood. Yet they were not erased. Both voices, both narratives, emerged simultaneously (Bakhtin
  1981)" (p. 181, folio 170). "He was a source of worry and embarrassment, a sign of her failure as a
  mother" (p. 180, folio 169).
- **Interpellation into a discourse of sainthood.** "the process of Ashiq's interpellation vis-à-vis a
  discourse of sainthood can be discerned from her stories" (p. 181, folio 170).
- **Two sources of authority in tension.** Sa'in Sahib's power "stemmed from two sources: he was Rabi‘a's
  husband's elder brother … and he had a special connection to God that was reflected in his status as
  sa'in" (p. 181, folio 170); they "reinforce each other but nevertheless co-exist in uneasy tension"
  (p. 182, folio 171).
- **Sainthood at the edge of respectability.** He "was accused of being corrupt, of indulging in
  debauchery with women and drugs behind the cover of his spiritual claims" (p. 183, folio 172); "his
  identity in many respects operates at the very edge of everyday respectability and even challenges the
  foundations of everyday life" (p. 183, folio 172). Gender transgression is the sharper violation: "he
  is guilty of an even more basic violation of social order, dressing like a woman", and this disruption
  is "precisely the quality that makes sainthood so powerful: it forces people to confront the
  arbitrariness and limited nature of their everyday lives" (p. 184, folio 173).
- **A Lacanian reading, qualified by her own note.** "This is a classic oedipal situation. To use
  Lacanian terminology, the father of these narratives is the representative of the Law, the Symbolic
  Order" (p. 186, folio 175), qualified in note 10 as "the father as signifier", not an actual father
  (p. 193, folio 182). "A saint may be simply an alternative representative of the Symbolic Order, as
  the pir often is … But the true Sufi, as Sa'in Sahib suggests, undercuts this Symbolic Order" (p. 186,
  folio 175). Ashiq "identifies with the abject, that which has been dismissed as impure, dangerous, and
  disgusting (see Kristeva 1982), and evades the discipline of everyday life" (p. 187, folio 176).
- **Death as the transformative event.** "The death reshapes reality and transforms the ambiguities of
  Ashiq's strange actions and peculiar statements into the utterances of a majzub" (p. 189, folio 178).
- **A caution on her own evidence.** Predicting one's own death "is generally recognized as a sign of
  great spirituality" (p. 187, folio 176), but her note 11 warns that "when describing a deceased parent,
  it is not uncommon for a person to attribute their parent with this ability and provide a detailed,
  idealized account of the parent's last day" (p. 193, folio 182).

#### Jamal Malik — chapter 9, the literary critique of popular religion

- **Shrines as microcosms.** Because a variety of activities including "socalled [text-layer?: so-called]
  ‘un-Islamic activities'" take place around them, and because "shrines also often represent the last
  refuge of marginalised social groups, they can be regarded as microcosms of local Islam" (p. 200,
  folio 189).
- **Colonialism inherits and enlarges the reformist critique.** "British colonialism adopted this
  critique and developed it further into a comprehensive attack on the ‘Orient', with the objective of
  legitimising colonial power and pushing through its notion of modernisation" (p. 200, folio 189); it
  "perceived local Islam as a backward, fanatic and obscurantist religion characterised by superstition"
  (p. 200, folio 189).
- **Reformism as a class project.** Eighteenth-century reformism "appealed mostly to restricted urban
  groups", and "was designed mainly to legitimise their new social status as traders, bankers and
  representatives of ‘scribal groups'" (p. 200, folio 189).
- **The reformers' contradiction, the chapter's spine.** "their criticism of their own culture required
  them to perceive an inherent oriental backwardness or non-development", while their self-definition
  "as an avant-garde was based primarily on their having studied in Europe and on their claim to
  privileged agency vis-à-vis the masses" (p. 202, folio 191).
- **His claim about the story, and the dimension he says has been missed.** ‘Bain' "draws a picture of
  the complex hierarchical world of shrines and their milieu as well as of the social stratification and
  stigmatisation of female children and their exploitation and evident rape by male authorities, even in
  a surrounding which is more or less a female domain, the shrine" (p. 207, folio 196). But it also
  "elaborates upon the shrine cult in a literary-aesthetic way and reflects the inherent romantic
  mysticism of the author, who himself hailed from a mystical family tradition. It is this dimension of
  the story which has hitherto been neglected" (p. 204, folio 193).
- **The political allegory, with a dated key.** The story "implies a critique of the political culture
  based on an authoritarian and quasi-military system" (p. 204, folio 193); note 9 offers the key — "The
  girl's age of 16–17 may call to mind the duration of the era between 1962 and 1979", 1962 being the
  lifting of martial law under Ayyub Khan and 1979 the year "the military finally re-established its
  political authority (Jalal 1992:302 ff)" (p. 215, folio 204).
- **Literary institutions, dated.** *Angâre* (Burning Coals) "was published in 1932 in Lucknow by four
  young Urdu writers who had spent some time in England", was banned "some months later", and "since
  then it has not been republished in Urdu" (p. 202, folio 191); it was later "reprinted in Hindi", with
  controversial passages omitted and "several important Persian and Arabic words… replaced by Sanskrit
  idioms" (pp. 214–215, folios 203–204). The Progressive Writers' Association "first came together in
  Aligarh and was soon constituted in London", held its "first all-India meeting in 1936 under the
  chairmanship of Premchand", split "after some years", was declared illegal in 1954, and was "formally
  re-established" only in 1987 (pp. 202–203, folios 191–192). The **halqah-ye arbâb-ye dhauq (Circle of
  Possessors of Good Taste)** "was established in 1939", and it and the movement of **adab islâmî**
  "developed in reaction to this new literature" (pp. 202–203, folios 191–192). The **colonial printing
  press** "introduced in the nineteenth century had a decisive impact on Urdu literature" (p. 201,
  folio 190).

#### Charles Lindholm — chapter 10, "Prophets and pirs"

- **His two questions.** Why "charismatic Sufism first appeared in Islamic society, despite the fact that
  the message of Islam is overtly opposed to all forms of spiritual elitism", and why "Sufism has more or
  less vanished as an active force in the Middle East, yet has remained influential in South Asia"
  (p. 220, folio 209). **He calls his own treatment "admittedly … preliminary and sketchy"** (p. 220,
  folio 209).
- **His thesis for the contrast.** Middle Eastern decline is "an historically conditioned reflection of
  fundamental tensions within the egalitarian Middle Eastern cultural ethos, the rise of modern ideals,
  and the heightened repressive power of the central state", while "the relative success of Sufism in
  South Asia has its roots in a sociocultural context wherein hierarchical distinctions between human
  beings are taken for granted and where the local autonomy and moral authority of powerful Sufi
  brotherhoods have largely been retained" (p. 220, folio 209).
- **The Weberian frame.** Sunni Islam is "the purest case of what Max Weber called emissary religion;1
  [text-layer?: religion, with endnote 1 flattened onto it]", in which "any claim to union with the deity
  is an unforgivable sin" (p. 221, folio 210). Against it Weber sets "exemplary prophecy, wherein the
  prophet is the living receptacle of a static, immanent, and abstract essence"; "Exemplary religion is
  the natural home of charisma" (p. 221, folio 210). Charisma appears anyway because "The Qur'an, being
  finite, does not and cannot explicitly cover all circumstances" (pp. 221–222, folios 210–211).
- **Ambivalence, not veneration, is his account of popular feeling.** The pir's qualities "also aroused
  qualms in the general public, who regarded the ‘friends of God' with a mixture of awe and fear that
  sometimes verged on repulsion" (p. 234, folio 223); the saint was "followed when he could offer
  spiritual influence, ecstatic experience, and a variety of worldly connections and services, but
  abandoned when other sources of leverage and power were available that were less perilous, ambivalent,
  and demanding" (p. 235, folio 224). "In the Middle East, the saint was always a dangerous and ambiguous
  figure" (p. 235, folio 224).
- **He brackets the truth question.** "The truth or falsehood of the beliefs held by the common people in
  the Middle East about the ‘friends of God' is irrelevant for the argument made here; certainly some
  saints were of a peaceful and kindly character" (p. 234, folio 223). **He also rejects the
  false-consciousness reading**: "Black's view that the ideology of equality is merely a form of false
  consciousness is highly debatable (Lindholm 1996)" (p. 233, folio 222).
- **Periodisation.** "the zenith of Sufism in the central Muslim lands was during the Seljuk period, when
  the caliphate had fallen and a purely secular authority had begun to suborn and encapsulate the
  ‘ulama" (p. 235, folio 224). The residue: Sufis "now serve only as mediators in marginal tribal areas,
  or provide ecstatic performances in impoverished urban communities, or serve as guides for cultured
  elites" (p. 235, folio 224).
- **The South Asian outcome.** "South Asian Sufi saints proved that it is possible to have one's cake and
  eat it too" (p. 237, folio 226); "Sufi saints in South Asia therefore were and are the Muslim
  equivalents of sanctified Hindu gurus" (p. 240, folio 229); "while Sufism has almost died in the Middle
  East, it has retained and even improved its position in South Asia" (p. 241, folio 230). **He names and
  rejects the diffusionist explanation** — that South Asia is merely "on the periphery of inexorable
  theological changes emanating from the central" lands (p. 235, folio 224).

#### Where the contributors disagree with each other

- **The editors qualify Lindholm inside their own introduction.** They report his position that "the
  individual egalitarianism and progressive rationalisation of Middle Eastern societies make charismatic
  saints an anachronism" (p. 27, folio 16), note that it "tends to support one kind of interpretation of
  Weber's theory" holding that personal charisma has no place in the modern world (Wilson 1975:ix), and
  then turn his own later work against it: he "argues here and elsewhere that the alienation,
  fragmentation, materialism and solipsism of modernity ‘push individuals toward immersion in a
  charismatic group' (Lindholm 1990:82)" (p. 27, folio 16), and that in Egypt "the lure of charisma has
  resurfaced in the form of Islamist movements" (pp. 27–28, folios 16–17).
- **Frembgen and the editors are on opposite sides of Turner.** The editors keep Turner's key point,
  saying only that pilgrimage makes "counter-structure" rather than "anti-structure" (p. 23, folio 12);
  Frembgen argues the communitas he observed was not liminal at all (p. 163, folio 152) while still using
  Turner for the ʿurs (p. 162, folio 151).
- **Landell Mills and the editors on Durkheim.** The editors list the Durkheimian processual lineage as
  one of the volume's three foundations (p. 18, folio 7); Landell Mills is reported by them as "arguing
  against the Durkheimian view of symbols as collective representations" (p. 19, folio 8) and does so in
  his own chapter (p. 43, folio 32). Frembgen, by contrast, invokes Durkheim approvingly (p. 166,
  folio 155).
- **Werbner against Eade and Sallnow, and the editors against Turner's critics**, are two separate
  quarrels in the same volume (p. 122, folio 111; p. 23, folio 12).
- **Basu cross-references Frembgen and Ewing explicitly**, linking mast to majzub "as Frembgen and Ewing
  show in this volume" (p. 137, folio 126); Basu also builds directly on Werbner's up/down gift formula
  (p. 145, folio 134); and Saheb cites "Werbner 1989" — **which the reference list identifies as Richard
  Werbner, *Ritual Passage, Sacred Journey*, not the volume's co-editor Pnina Werbner, whose 1996 article
  is listed separately** (p. 84, folio 73; p. 87, folio 76).

## Doubts and review points

### 1. The one thing a human must rule on: the Zindapir / Baba Qasim collision

**The archive row `darbar-ghamkol-sharif-zinda-pir` names one principal figure, "Khwaja Muhammad Qasim
(Zinda Pir)", died 1999. This book treats "Zindapir" and "Baba Qasim" as two different men, in the
chapter text and again in the index, and never joins them.**

- **In the chapter.** "The occasion of the sacrificial feast is the ‘urs, the commemoration of the
  death/rebirth of Zindapir's Sufi master, Baba Qasim" (p. 111, folio 100) — master and disciple, two
  people. Zindapir himself is throughout "the living saint of Ghamkol Sharif" (p. 111, folio 100), a
  man Werbner interviewed and visited in 1989 and 1991 and watched limping with bad knees (pp. 123 and
  126, folios 112 and 115).
- **In the index.** Two separate headwords, forty-one index lines and two pages apart, with disjoint
  locator sets: `Qasim, Baba 101` (p. 252, folio 241) and `Zindapir 100, 102, 106; and giving 104–5,
  107, 110–11; and politicians 108–10; relation to supplicants and disciples` (p. 254, folio 243).
  Neither cross-references the other and neither is glossed. **The index is apparatus, not argument,
  and an indexer's choice is not a statement — but it points the same way as the chapter.**
- **The consolidation has assigned the id to the lodge and to Zindapir, and put Baba Qasim under
  "Figures and sites not in the archive" with a cross-reference, because that is what the book states.
  Nothing has been harmonised and nothing should be.**
- **Nothing from the archive row's own fields may be attributed to this book**: not the death year
  1999 (the book's Zindapir is alive), not the silsila "Naqshbandi" (the book never names Zindapir's
  order; the only Naqshbandiyya in the book belongs to an unnamed Rajpur man's personal pir at p. 98,
  folio 87), not a personal name for Zindapir.
- **A second, unrelated "Zinda pir" is in the same chunk.** Werth's note 9: "Khidr, who is often
  called Zinda pir in Punjab, because he also is thought to have disappeared alive (see Schimmel 1975:
  202)" (p. 101, folio 90). **This is not the Ghamkol saint and must never be mapped to that row.**

### 2. Numerals, dates and names a human must check before any of them enters an entry

- **`around AH 1676` cannot stand as printed and has not been corrected.** "Shâh Daulah—whose birth
  name was Qâsim —lived in Mughal times and died at an advanced age around AH 1676 in Gujrat" (p. 205,
  folio 194). An AH year of 1676 does not fall in the Mughal period, while the archive row records
  1675. **Whether the book's error is the era marker, the numeral, or neither is (not stated); a human
  must read the printed page.**
- **The ʿurs crowd figure at Ghamkol Sharif: 300,000 against 20,000, on pages six apart.** "In 1990
  ninety-five goats, thirteen sheep, and seventeen cattle and buffalo were slaughtered over a three-day
  period, the equivalent of over 3,000 kg of meat, distributed to some 20,000 or more pilgrims and
  supplicants" (p. 110, folio 99), against "The ‘urs is said, somewhat hyperbolically, to draw 300,000
