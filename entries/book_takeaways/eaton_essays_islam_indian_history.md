## What this book is

A collection of Eaton's essays, reprinted from their original journals and volumes, organised in
sections by region: the Deccan, then Punjab, then Bengal. Two of them are on a single shrine in the
archive — "The Political and Religious Authority of the Shrine of Baba Farid" (from Metcalf ed.,
*Moral Conduct and Authority*, 1982) and "Court of Man, Court of God", which works from the Lahore High
Court succession suit of 1935 to 1938 — and together they are the most economically substantial
material in this corpus: they document what flowed into a shrine, what flowed out, who controlled it,
and what each successive state bought with its patronage.

The rest is argument rather than site description, and it is argument this archive can use. Eaton's
Bengal essays set out the mechanism by which Mughal frontier land grants, conditioned on building and
endowing a religious institution, produced shrines and mosques as a by-product of agrarian expansion;
his methodological essays reject "conversion" as the wrong frame, in favour of gradual and uneven
acculturation, which he calls "seepage".

Two cautions carried from the chunk notes. Eaton flags some of his own inferences as uncorroborated,
and those flags are preserved below. And several of his load-bearing quantities are cited to his own
other books rather than documented here, so the sections mark what this volume evidences against what
it merely asserts. The text layer is damaged in places — a table with garbled cells, lost map pages,
whole sentences unrecoverable — and every such gap is flagged rather than filled.

### shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar — Shrine of Fariduddin Ganjshakar

Eaton's names for it: "Farid al-Din Ganj-i Shakar", "popularly known as 'Baba Farid'" (p. 16);
"Shaikh Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar (d. A. D. 1265)" (chunk 015); "Shaikh Farid al-Din Ganj-i
Shakar" (chunk 016); "Baba Farid Shakar Ganj", "the gaddi" (chunk 017); "Farid al-Din
Shakarganj (d. 1265) in Pakpattan, Punjab" (p. 265). The site: the shrine of Ajudhan /
Pakpattan. Eaton calls it "perhaps the most famous such tomb-shrine in the Punjab" (p. 16).

**Two whole essays in this book are about this one shrine** — Section 3, Punjab: ch. 9 "The
Political and Religious Authority of the Shrine of Baba Farid" (printed pp. 203ff.) and ch. 10
"Court of Man, Court of God: Local Perceptions of the Shrine of Baba Farid, Pakpattan, Punjab"
(printed pp. 225ff.) (p. 9). Ch. 9 was first published as "'The Political and Religious
Authority of the Shrine of Baba Farid in Pakpattan, Punjab', in Barbara Metcalf, ed., *Moral
Conduct and Authority: The Place of 'Adab' in South Asian Islam* (Berkeley: University of
California Press, 1982), 333–56"; ch. 10 "in Richard C. Martin, ed., *Islam in Local Contexts*,
vol. 17 of *Contributions to Asian Studies* (Leiden: Brill, 1982), 44–61" (p. 11). A reprint
line appears at p. 215 (chunk 015). A map, "Punjab circa 1605", is printed at printed p. 214,
i.e. inside the ch. 9 essay (p. 10).

The two essays' stated questions, in Eaton's own framing:
- Ch. 9 "seeks connections between the processes of Islamization and peasantization in the
  western Punjab. Specifically, I ask why and how it was that Jat tribesmen, as they abandoned
  pastoral nomadism for settled agriculture between the thirteenth and nineteenth centuries,
  gradually took on an Islamic identity" (p. 16).
- Ch. 10 asks how "Islam, a religion par excellence 'of the Book,' [has] been mediated among
  illiterate villagers, and how has the shrine served to promote a conceptual conflation of
  Heaven and Earth among Muslims of rural Punjab?" (pp. 16–17).

**Ch. 10's evidence base is archival and datable.** The essay is "based on legal depositions
given in the context of a **1938 court dispute over the rightful control of the same shrine**"
(p. 16) — the record is **Lahore High Court, Regular First Appeal No. 93 of 1939: *Dewan Ghulam
Rasul vs. Ghulam Qutab-ud-din*, 5 vols (Lahore, 1940)** (p. 239, n. 3), cited throughout ch. 10
by volume and page. See *Custodianship and succession* below for the case's dates and extent,
and Doubts for the p. 16 "1938" against the pp. 238/249 span of 1935–8.

#### Saint

- **Death year 1265** in Eaton's text, four times: "that of Farid al-Din Ganj-i Shakar (d.
  1265)" (p. 16); "In 1265 Baba Farid died" (p. 218); "Baba Farid, who died at the age of ninety
  in A.D. 1265" (p. 241); "Farid al-Din Shakarganj (d. 1265)" (p. 265). **The archive row carries
  1266** (per the chunk 001 and chunk 018 notes). One year apart; see Doubts.
- **Age at death: ninety** (p. 241).
- **He arrived at Ajudhan "sometime in the early thirteenth century"** (p. 217).
- **How long he was actually resident.** *Evidence:* "Baba Farid resided in Ajudhan only sixteen
  or twenty-four years" (p. 228, n. 43, citing *Siyar al-auliya'*, 63). *Eaton's use of it:* this
  is "a very short time span for the many eponymous clan founders or maliks who are claimed to
  have met him actually to have been there" (p. 228).
- **He refused the court.** *Eaton:* he "assiduously avoided contact with the mundane world of
  the court and its ministers" (p. 219). *Evidence:* Barani has him warning a Sufi leaving
  Ajudhan for Delhi, "Have nothing to do with maliks and amirs, and beware of their intimacy as
  dangerous; no darwesh ever kept up such intimacy, but in the end found it disastrous" (p. 219,
  n. 12, via Elliot and Dowson, 3:144).
- **Two constituencies in his own lifetime**: full-time residents of the convent (*jama'at
  khana*, printed "jamd'a.khana") "initiated into the Chishti order, who lived a communal life
  of a strongly ascetic nature"; and the "more popular-oriented" tradition of the masses who
  received amulets (p. 217).
- **The epithet is economic in form.** "Ganj-i Shakar, or 'treasury of sugar,'" was "associated
  with legends concerning the saint's severe austerities" (p. 240).
- **"the Second Adam."** *Evidence:* "It was on account of this vast proliferation of descendants
  that even in the seventeenth century Baba Farid himself was called 'the Second Adam'" (p. 230,
  n. 51, citing G. A. Storey, *Persian Literature, a Bio-bibliographical Survey* (London,
  1927–71), 2:986).
- **Scale of the following, with no number attached.** *Eaton:* "this saint has remained for many
  centuries an immensely popular 'invisible friend' among millions of villagers inhabiting the
  southwestern Punjab" (p. 238); "Like any other of Baba Farid's millions of devotees, Timur
  implored the saint's intercession with God for the attainment of worldly concerns" (p. 228,
  n. 45). "Millions" is Eaton's own characterization; no count and no source for it is given
  (not stated).

#### Shrine / Darbar

- **Location.** It "lies on the right bank of the Punjab's most southeasterly river, the Sutlej,
  roughly halfway between Ferozepore and Bahawalpur" (pp. 216–217). "The town in which the shrine
  is located, known since the sixteenth century as Pakpattan, is the ancient city of Ajudhan"
  (p. 217).
- **The town's economic function before the saint.** "As the principal ferry point on the Sutlej
  river, this town served from ancient times as a major nexus for east-west trade between the
  Delhi region and Multan" (p. 217).
- **The renaming.** "by the sixteenth century the very name of the city containing the shrine,
  Ajudhan, became known as Pakpattan ('the holy ferry'), in honour of Baba Farid's memory"
  (p. 228).
- **The site was already Islamically equipped when he arrived.** *Evidence:* "a Jami mosque had
  already been established there, a resident [q]adi was there administering justice according to
  Islamic law, and the city was politically subordinate to a Muslim governor in neighbouring
  Dipalpur" (p. 217, citing *Siyar al-arifin*, 33–4). The mosque is "now part of the shrine
  complex" and its "large size… attests to the presence of a substantial Muslim population at
  that time" (p. 223, citing Chaghatai, 134–5).
- **The complex begins at the death.** "In 1265 Baba Farid died, and with his death began the
  career of the vast shrine complex based on his tomb, in time encompassing a mosque, a welfare
  kitchen (dangar khana [*langar khana*]), and a number of related buildings" (p. 218). Note the
  printed "dangar khana" for *langar khana*.
- **The grandson's tomb dominates the complex.** "soon after 'Ala' ad-Din's death in 1335, Sultan
  Muhammad commissioned two engineers to construct what proved to be one of the finest examples
  of Tughluq architecture in the subcontinent. It is also the most imposing structure in the
  entire shrine complex, dwarfing by far Baba Farid's own tomb" (p. 221). Its date "comes from an
  inscription on the tomb itself—Safar, 737, which corresponds to Sept./Oct., A.D. 1336" (p. 221,
  n. 19); architectural discussion attributed to Ahmad Nabi Khan, 'The Mausoleum of Shaikh 'Ala
  al-Din at Pakpattan (Punjab)', *East and West* 24, nos 3–4 (1974): 324–5 (cited p. 220, n. 13).
- **A second, unreconciled description of the fabric.** "Both the Diwan and the shrine itself, a
  magnificent marble edifice constructed in the fourteenth century, are perceived to be carriers
  of Baba Farid's baraka" (p. 239) — the chunk worker flagged which building this is; see Doubts.
  Also "the hallowed marble domes of Pakpattan" (p. 257).
- **The Gate.** "Another aspect of the shrine's adab was its carefully defined formula for
  achieving religious transcendence, namely, the practice of passing through its Gate of Paradise
  (bihish[ti] [darwaza — the term is garbled in the text layer, see Doubts]) enabling all who did
  so to ritually enter paradise" (p. 236). The same rite appears at p. 219 as the diwan "opening
  the southern door (shisha darwaza) of Baba Farid's tomb", and at p. 254 as the "Bahishti
  Gate" / "Bihishti Darwaza"; the names are not reconciled (not stated).
- **Satellite shrines, built by the poor and not by the state.** *Eaton:* "small memorial shrines
  to Baba Farid began appearing, scattered throughout the countryside of central Punjab"; "what
  is significant is that they were built not by the Tughluq sultans as were the main structures
  of the Ajudhan complex, but by the common people themselves" (p. 222). *Evidence:* Amir Khurd,
  a contemporary of 'Ala' ad-Din — "in the countryside around Ajudhan, Dipalpur, and in the hills
  toward Kashmir, the people out of love and belief have built structures and tombs in the name
  of his shrine…and they go to these villages for alms and devotions" (p. 222, n. 25, citing
  *Siyar al-auliya'*, 193).
- **Rank among shrines.** *Evidence (depositions):* devotees saw it as "occupying one in a network
  of sacred wilayats, or territories under the spiritual authority of one or another pir (saint),
  which dot all of North India", these shrines being "related to each other in terms of a ranked
  hierarchy, with the shrine of Baba Farid being the supreme shrine of the Punjab, to which
  others were subordinate" (pp. 241–242); "this ranked hierarchy was ritually acted out according
  to an unwritten etiquette governing the attendance of shrine rituals by the sajjada-nishins of
  other shrines" (p. 242). *Eaton:* "the most popular Islamic shrine in the western Punjab"
  (p. 246), "the preeminent shrine of the Punjab" (p. 252).
- **The subordinate-shrine list, as one witness gave it.** "The sajjada-nishin of one of these
  shrines listed the following as among the shrines subordinate to that of Baba Farid in
  Pakpattan: that of Shaikh Jamal al-Din of Hansi, Hissar District, of Muhammad Shah in Basi Nau,
  Hoshiarpur District, of Nizam al-Din Auliya' in Delhi, Piran Kaler in Saharanpur District,
  Golra Sharif in Rawalpindi District, Taunsa Sharif in Dera Ghazi Khan District, Mukhan Sharif
  and Basal Sharif in Campbellpore District, Uch Sharif in Bahawalpur State, and the shrines of
  Kasur and Lakhneke in Lahore District, Kastiwala in Gurdaspur District, and Panipat in Karnal
  District" (pp. 241–242, n. 12, citing Lahore High Court, 1:180). **This is one sajjada-nishin's
  claim, not Eaton's own finding**, and it is a list of *places*, mostly without a saint's name;
  the chunk note refers the archive-row mapping to Doubts.
- **How the "daughter" shrines became subordinate — two distinct mechanisms.** "Although one
  shrine located in the nearby town of Dipalpur had been given land by an earlier Diwan of
  Pakpattan and for this reason saw itself as subordinate to Baba Farid's shrine, most of the
  'daughter' shrines of Baba Farid in the Punjab were founded by family descendants of Baba
  Farid, and hence they looked upon the shrine at Pakpattan as their spiritual superior" (p. 242,
  n. 13, citing 2:51). A land grant in one case, descent in the rest — **here Pakpattan is a
  grantor of land, not only a grantee.**
- **Catchment.** "It is in Pakpattan, a town that lay within easy reach of only several days'
  travel in the Punjab, that God's grace was made concrete and accessible, that Heaven and Earth
  met" (p. 247).
- **Distance to the colonial court.** Montgomery lay "some 25 miles down a dusty road from the
  hallowed marble domes of Pakpattan" (p. 257).

#### Practices & Events

- **Amulet-writing (*ta'widh*), then and later.** *Evidence:* Baba Farid "handed out ta'widh or
  amulets to the common masses, who saw in these ta'widh a protection against evil, a boon for
  good fortune, or an agent for the cure of an illness" (p. 217); "Huge crowds gathered daily at
  Baba Farid's convent to receive ta'widh", normally "a scrap of paper on which were written the
  names of God or some Qur'anic verses", and the work's "tediousness… compelled the saint to
  delegate much of the work to assistants" (p. 218). Continuity: "even now the successors of Baba
  Farid and his assistants, in common with those of other shrines in the Punjab, continue to
  write ta'widh for the masses" (p. 218) — written 1982 (reprint line, p. 215); no date of
  observation is given (not stated).
- **All the major rituals are dated to the first successor.** *Evidence — the Jawahir-i Faridi,
  compiled 1623:* the *dastar bandi* or "tying on of the turban… indicating formal inheritance of
  Baba Farid's spiritual authority"; "the regularizing of ecstatic singing (qawwa[li]) at the
  shrine"; "the establishing of a public kitchen (langar khana) from which the diwan himself
  would, on formal occasions, direct the distribution of food and sweets"; and the diwan "opening
  the southern door (shisha darwaza) of Baba Farid's tomb on the occasion of the shrine's annual
  'urs celebration, allowing the masses of common devotees to pass by the shrine's sanctum
  sanctorum, ritually entering heaven" (p. 219, n. 11, citing *Jawahir-i Faridi*, 298–300). Ch. 10
  restates the same finding from the same source and folios: the *Jawahir-i Faridi*, "an important
  collection of the lives of Baba Farid's spiritual and lineal descendants completed in 1623,
  records that all the major rituals associated with the shrine, including those dealing with Baba
  Farid's turban, had become instituted in the generation following the saint's death, that is, in
  the late thirteenth century" (p. 240, n. 8, citing *Jawahir-i Faridi*, comp. 'Ali Asghar Chishti
  (Lahore, 1883–4), 298–300) — consistent between the two essays.
- **The 1315 'urs.** *Evidence:* Amir Khusrau "mentioned the annual 'urs or death-date celebration
  on the occasion of the fiftieth anniversary of the shaikh's death and described in detail the
  'urs celebration for the year 1315: the pilgrimage of pious persons to Ajudhan, the recitation
  of the saint's wonderful deeds, and the entertainments performed by an ensemble of darweshes"
  (p. 219, n. 10, citing *Raha al-muhibbin*, Urdu ed. Lahore 1957, 63–4, via Chaghatai, *Iqbal
  Review* 9 (1968)). No attendance figure (not stated).
- **The Bihishti Darwaza ceremony, with the largest crowd figure in the corpus.** "that day the
  Diwan personally unlocked and opened the gate, passed through it, and was then followed by
  throngs of murids estimated to number between fifty and sixty thousand" (p. 254); "All through
  the night and in states of intense religious ecstasy, the devotees filed through this 'Gate of
  Heaven' and past Baba Farid's flower-bedecked tomb, thereby both commemorating and ritually
  reenacting the ascension of their patron saint to heaven" (p. 254). The crowd figure is
  footnoted to *Punjab District Gazetteers. Montgomery District* ([La]hore: 1884), 185 (p. 254,
  n. 56) — **the year the figure describes is not stated; see Doubts.**
- **The queue was ranked, and included a pass-holding class.** *Evidence — "a certain Khan
  Muhammad in his deposition of August, 1938":* "Dewan Sahib first opens the Bahishti Gate… he
  enters the gate and thereafter his companions pass through that gate. The companions number
  about 100-125. After that, the pass-holders pass through it, they are about 400-500; after
  that, the darveshes of the Hujra [i.e., resident mystics], who number 1,000—1,200. After them
  the plaintiff, Ghulam Rasul and his party, pass through the Bahishti Gate, after that the
  sajjadanashins of other institutions pass, and so on." (p. 254, n. 58, citing Lahore High
  Court, 2:249). *Eaton:* "The great masses of common devotees, included among the 'and so on' in
  the above quote, were allowed to enter through the gate only after the other dignataries had
  done so" (p. 254; "dignataries" as printed). The existence of "pass-holders" is stated but the
  pass is not described (not stated).
- **The same ritual in 1833, with cross-tradition attendance.** *Evidence:* "numbers of pilgrims,
  both Hindus and Mussalmans, come to visit the shrine, and all who pass through this doorway are
  considered saved from the fires of perdition. The door-way is about two feet wide, and cannot be
  passed without stopping, and the apartment itself is not capable of containing thirty people
  crowded together…. A superlative heaven is allotted to those who are first to enter the tomb on
  the day mentioned. The rush for precedence may, therefore, be better imagined than described.
  The crowd of pilgrims is said to be immense, and as they egress from the sacred door-way, after
  having rubbed their foreheads on the foot of the saint's grave, the air resounds with their
  shouts of Farid! Farid!" (p. 254, n. 57, citing Lieut. F. Mackeson, 'Journal of Captain C. M.
  Wade's Voyage from Lodiana to Mithankot by the River Satlaj on His Mission to Lahor and
  Bahawalpur in 1832-33,' *Journal of the Asiatic Society of Bengal* 6 (1837): 192). Eaton quotes
  it as illustrating "the salvation aspect of the ritual". **Note "both Hindus and Mussalmans" in
  1833.**
- **The "Bihishti mela".** A turban was placed on Qutb al-Din's head by his father "during the
  course of the Bihishti mela of 1933" (p. 255, n. 61, citing 2:181, 193).
- **Turban distribution at the 'urs, by the custodian alone.** *Evidence — a sixty-year-old
  villager:* "pechas [turbans] are distributed at the annual urs only by [the] sajjadanashin and
  nobody else. At that time, [the] sajjadanashin has hundreds of pechas with him. He must
  distribute those pechas personally." (p. 256, n. 67, citing Lahore High Court, 2:104).
- **The Diwan's visit to a subordinate Pakpattan shrine, itemized.** When the Diwan visited "the
  shrine of Baba Farid's son-in-law in Pakpattan, Badr al-Din Ishaq, on the occasion of that
  shrine's 'urs ceremony, the Diwan was carried on a palanquin (pa[l]ki)", a conveyance "from
  ancient times traditionally reserved for persons of privileged status" (p. 255); one villager,
  "it is necessary for the sajjadanashin to go there in a palki" (p. 255, n. 59). See *outflows*
  below for the gifts given on that visit.
- **A devotee dissenting from tomb veneration.** *Evidence:* "one old cultivator who declared,
  'doing sajda [prostration] to the tomb of Baba Farid under Mohammadan Law is not permissible'"
  (p. 257, n. 68, citing Lahore High Court, 2:39).

#### Legends & Miracles (karamat)

- **Ibn Battuta's Alexandria prediction and his 1334 visit.** *Eaton's reading:* these show "that
  Baba Farid, the saint, was now clearly identified with the physical shrine complex, and that his
  (its) fame had spread as far as Egypt" (pp. 221–222).
- **The vow-and-favour transaction.** *Eaton, on the depositions:* "murids regularly made
  religious vows or 'contracts' with Baba Farid, as a mediator between them and God, in hopes of
  receiving any number of concrete favours, such as female fertility, good crops, relief from
  illness, etc." (p. 239).
- **Ritual entry into heaven** through the southern door / Gate of Paradise / Bahishti Gate
  (pp. 219, 236, 254) — see *Practices & Events*.
- **The dream that decided a succession, and became a point of law.** "one of the major
  points of contention[] that emerged was whether or not the former Diwan, Sa'id Muhammad, had
  received a dream in which Baba Farid revealed to him that his son Qutb al-Din should succeed him
  as Diwan" (p. 243). *The judge's ruling, quoted:* "I decide that the defendant has sufficiently
  succeeded in proving that Diwan Said Muhammad had received revelation and that the defendant's
  nomination was made on the strength of that" (p. 243, n. 17).
- **Baraka carried by both custodian and building.** "Both the Diwan and the shrine itself, a
  magnificent marble edifice constructed in the fourteenth century, are perceived to be carriers
  of Baba Farid's baraka"; "Hence both the Diwan and the shrine functioned as intermediaries for
  an intermediary, as on-going vehicles of the saint's mediative power" (p. 239).
- **The turban is charged by contact with the tomb.** *Evidence:* "the headgear used in the
  dastar-bandi ceremony, a special saffron-coloured turban reserved only for the Diwan, was
  physically touched to the saint's tomb before being placed on the candidate's head" (p. 256,
  n. 63, citing Lahore High Court, 2:55, 169, 181). The footnote adds the consequence for the
  rival: "Since the plaintiff was not given access to the shrine for his dastar-bandi ceremony,
  this aspect of the rite had to be omitted in his case" (p. 256, n. 63).
- **Clans' conversion legends.** See *Significance* and the economy sections; the claims are the
  clans', the scepticism is Eaton's.

#### The shrine's economy

*Organised as: inflows (nazrana, futuh) · outflows (langar, gifts) · land grants and revenue
assignments · custodianship and succession · relations with the state under each regime. Every
item marks whether it is a source's evidence or Eaton's inference.*

##### (a) Inflows — *nazrana* and *futuh*

- **The *futuh* gift, in the saint's own lifetime, given in return for an amulet.** *Evidence:*
  the devotee "would frequently offer to the shrine some kind of gift, called futah [*futuh*]…
  typically… in the form of sweets, but could be almost anything, and even in Baba Farid's own day
  the gift swelled to enormous proportions" (p. 218, citing *Fawa'id al-fu'ad* via Nizami).
  "Enormous proportions" is the source's characterization as the chunk carried it; **no quantity is
  attached** (not stated).
- ***Nazrana* — the cash inflow, in the devotees' own words.** *Evidence:* "the murids made
  donations, usually cash contributions, that were called nazra[na]" (p. 239); a cultivator from
  Lyallpur District, "We give nazranas to Dewan Sahib out of love, respect and regard for the Pir
  and to invoke his blessings for the good of the murid" (p. 239, n. 2, citing the appeal record,
  2:239).
- **Donations were earmarked by the donor, and collected at two sites.** *Evidence:* "Though the
  vow was made with the saint, the donations went to one of the saint's on-going representatives,
  namely, either the Diwan or the shrine as a whole… Some murids testified that, having made the
  pious visit to Pakpattan, they would 'earmark' their nazrana to go either in support of the
  shrine's public kitchen, or to the Diwan. Others gave their nazrana to the Diwan when he visited
  them in their villages. The amount of nazr[a]na varied, of course, with the means of the
  devotee" (p. 239, n. 4, citing the appeal record, 1:55; 2:28, 239). **No amounts are given**
  (not stated). Note the two distinct earmarks — kitchen vs. custodian — and the second, itinerant
  collection channel (the Diwan's village tours).
- ***Nazrana* in kind, upward.** "Yet turbans were passed in the opposite direction, too. We have
  seen that oftentimes nazrana given to the Diwan by devotees, or simply by well-wishers, consisted
  of turbans in addition to cash" (p. 256). ("We have seen" refers back beyond the chunk; no
  amounts are given here — not stated.)
- **A parallel inflow to village pirs, outside the shrine.** *Evidence:* "Like the main shrine in
  Pakpattan, such pi[r]s also received cash payments from their murids, who readily identified the
  pirs residing in or near their villages as their links with the shrine of Baba Farid in
  Pakpattan" (p. 242, nn. 15–16, citing 2:164 and 1:193). **No amounts for the village pirs** (not
  stated).
- **Alms at the satellite shrines.** *Evidence:* the people "go to these villages for alms and
  devotions" (p. 222, n. 25, citing *Siyar al-auliya'*, 193).

##### (b) Outflows — *langar* and gifts

- **The *langar*, located and quantified.** *Evidence:* "Located in the shrine's courtyard just
  opposite Baba Farid's tomb, the langar was a vast operation, daily providing physical sustenance
  to the many pilgrims who came to pay respects and devotions at the shrine. One former worker in
  the kitchen estimated that between 240 and 320 [l]bs. (i.e. 3 or 4 mans) of wheat were consumed
  there every day, along with commensurate amounts of da[l], or split pea" (p. 240, n. 5, citing
  the appeal record, 2:256). **This is a witness estimate in a lawsuit, not the shrine's accounts,
  and no figure for pilgrims served is given** (not stated).
- **The kitchen's foundation and the custodian's personal role in distribution.** *Evidence:* the
  first successor established "a public kitchen (langar khana) from which the diwan himself would,
  on formal occasions, direct the distribution of food and sweets" (p. 219, n. 11, citing
  *Jawahir-i Faridi*, 298–300). The complex "in time encompass[ed]… a welfare kitchen (dangar khana
  [*langar khana*])" (p. 218).
- **Sugar and turbans: the symbolic outflow, monopolised by the Diwan.** *Evidence:* "Murids also
  received religious goods of a mor[e] symbolic sort, consisting of sugar or turbans, distributed
  only by the Diwan, and only on the occasion of particularly important shrine rituals at which he
  officiated" (p. 240, n. 6, citing the appeal record, 1:[ ]33 — folio garbled in the text layer).
  *Eaton:* "the distribution of sugar represented the outward flow of Baba Farid's bounty and
  blessings", linking Diwan and murids to "their spiritual ancestor, Baba Farid, whose best-known
  epithet, Ganj-i Shakar, or 'treasury of sugar,' was associated with legends concerning the
  saint's severe austerities" (p. 240); turbans "likewise served to connect murids to the Diwan,
  and both of these parties to Baba Farid himself, whose spiritual authority at a very early
  period in the history of the shrine's cult came to be represented by his turban" (p. 240).
- **Gifts downward to a subordinate shrine, itemized.** *Evidence:* "Once there, moreover, gifts
  presented by the Diwan to Badr al-Din's descendant further suggested the style and magnanimity
  associated with [words lost] nobles— 100 rupees in cash, a horse, and a silver saddle" (p. 255,
  n. 60). **The sentence has a gap in the text layer at "[words lost] nobles"; see Doubts. The
  three gift items are read as printed.**
- **Land granted downward.** An earlier Diwan of Pakpattan "had given land" to a shrine at Dipalpur
  (p. 242, n. 13, citing 2:51).
- **Feeding travellers as a charged head of the Mughal endowment.** Shah Jahan's 1629 *farman*
  names "the feed of Travellers" among the four heads of expenditure (p. 229, n. 47) — see (c).
- **The staff the endowment paid.** "Durveshes and Khadims attached thereto" (p. 229, n. 47);
  in the depositions, "the darveshes of the Hujra [i.e., resident mystics], who number 1,000—1,200"
  pass the gate as a body (p. 254, n. 58).

##### (c) Land grants and revenue assignments

- **A whole city's revenues assigned to the shrine, and the earliest such alienation.** *Evidence:*
  Ibn Battuta "wrote that Sultan Muhammad ibn Tughluq had bestowed the city of Ajudhan [up]on the
  shrine" (p. 220, n. 16, citing *Rehla*, trans. Mahdi Husain, Baroda 1953, 20). *Eaton's
  inference:* "This seems to be the earliest reference to the court's alienation of local revenues
  in favour of the shrine's support" (p. 220, n. 16) — "seems to be" is Eaton's.
- **An endowment specifically for the kitchen.** *Evidence:* in Diwan Mu'izz ad-Din's "brief
  successorship (1335–38), Muhammad ibn Tughluq granted an endowment or muafi [printed "muafin"]
  support of the shrine's public kitchen" (p. 221, n. 22, citing *Jawahir-i Faridi*, 308).
- **Repairs plus confirmation of landholding.** *Evidence:* "Barani recorded that Sultan
  Fir[u]z Tughluq (1351–88) not only repaired the tomb of Baba Farid, but 'granted robes of honour
  to his descendants and confirmed them in possession of their villages and lands.'" (p. 221,
  n. 23, citing Barani, *Tarikh-i F[i]roz Shahi* (Calcutta, 1862), 543, via Rose, *Glossary*,
  1:495).
- **Shah Jahan's 1629 *farman* — the single most quotable endowment document in the corpus.**
  *Eaton's framing:* "in 1629 Shah Jahan issued a farman indicating precisely what his and his
  predecessor's policy was vis-a-vis the shrine" (p. 229). *The document, quoted in full on the
  page; operative terms:* "The sacred town of Pak Pattan with all its dependencies is by old
  agreement held in grant from the preceding emperors for the 'Langar' expenses of the shrine",
  held "by Shekh Mohammad, Sujjadah Nasheen of the shrine, a descendant of the Baba"; "the
  proceeds thereof are applied to his own maintenance and to that of the Durveshes and Khadims
  attached thereto, as well as to the feed of Travellers and the repair and adornment of the
  building"; and the order "Continue the whole 'Muhal' [i.e., pargana] in endowment to the
  shrine." (p. 229, n. 47, citing **West Pakistan Board of Revenue, Lahore. File 131/6/24/24**).
  **Four stated heads of expenditure — custodian's maintenance, staff, travellers' food, fabric
  repair — and the grant is described as pre-existing ("by old agreement… from the preceding
  emperors").**
- **Chishti tax-free land as a general Delhi policy.** *Eaton:* "Since the Tughluq period, this
  order enjoyed a very special status among Delhi's rulers, who lavishly patronized the
  descendants of the great Chishti shaikhs with magnificent tombs and considerable tax-free land"
  (p. 265).
- **Territorial expansion by force, 1757.** *Evidence:* "in 1757 Diwan 'Abd as-Subhan (1752–66)
  gathered an army of his Jat murids, attacked the raja of Bikaner, and thereby expanded the
  shrine's territorial holdings for the first time to the east of the Sutlej" (p. 231, n. 56,
  citing *Montgomery District Gazetteer* (1933), 38).
- **The endowment destroyed in 1810, and what replaced it — a precise figure.** *Eaton:* "In 1810
  the shrine's extensive holdings were seized by Ranjit Singh, and the diwans of Pakpattan, their
  brief period of political independence now at an end, reverted to their former status of
  political intermediaries" (p. 231). *Evidence, quantified:* "Ranjit Singh allowed the diwan and
  his retainers only Rs. 1,000 a year for their maintenance, derived from the town duties of
  Pakpattan, in addition to a fourth share of four small villages nearby" (p. 231, n. 59, citing
  *Journal of the Asiatic Society of Bengal*, 6 (1837): 193). *Eaton's gloss:* "it was under much
  worse terms than under the Mughals" (p. 231, n. 59). **Note the revenue base shifts from land
  and pargana revenue to town duties plus a fractional village share.**
- **Revenue collection as the shrine's service to the state.** *Evidence:* "In return for this
  royal patronage, the diwans of Pakpattan performed several functions for the Mughal government.
  Above all, on certain tracts of land they received the government's share of all crops on which
  revenue was levied in kind, whereas the tax on cash crops such as cotton, indigo, or tobacco had
  to be paid in cash and went straight to revenue officials without passing the diwan" (p. 229,
  n. 48, citing *Punjab District Gazetteers*, vol. 18-A, Montgomery District, 1933 (Lahore, 1935),
  38).
- **The incentive that follows — stated as a mechanism, and flagged by Eaton as uncorroborated.**
  *Eaton:* "It was therefore in the diwan's interest as a de facto chaudh[ri], first, that
  agriculture be expanded at the expense of pastoralism, for the diwan derived no cattle tax from
  the barr clans, and secondly, that food crops be sown instead of cash crops" (p. 229).
  **Eaton flags his own inference: "Although there is no corroborating evidence to this effect,
  these circumstances suggest that the diwans might have been promoters of peasant agriculture,
  and might explain the Jawahir-i Farid['s] seeming approval of peasant agriculture as a way of
  life for the shrine's dependent clans" (p. 229).** *The evidence he points at:* "so these
  clans—Adhank, Valank, and Sipan—are all farmers in Pakpattan… The Baritis are originally Jats.…
  They live in Pakpattan and practice agriculture" (p. 229, n. 49, citing *Jawahir-i Faridi*, 397).
- **The descendants became a landholding caste with tenants.** *Eaton:* "The darweshes and khadims
  (i.e., 'servants') mentioned in Shah Jahan's farman as receiving royal support comprised the many
  hundreds of lineal descendants of Baba Farid living in the Pakpattan region. So numerous were
  these descendants that they literally formed a separate zamindar caste in the area, the 'Chishti'
  caste, possessing both economic privileges and ritual status vis-a-vis the local clans" (p. 230).
  *Evidence — a colonial revenue source:* the Chishtis were "a semi-religious Mussalman tribe"
  having "considerable local influence," who "are not working agriculturalists, but depend for
  cultivation entirely on tenants" (p. 230, n. 52, citing Patrick J. Fagan, *Assessment Report for
  Pakpattan Tahsil* (Lahore, 1896), 50 — **the running text calls it "An 1897 British Assessment
  Report"; see Doubts**). *Eaton extends it backwards as a probability:* "It is probable that the
  Chishtis enjoyed the same sort of proprietary rights in relation to their tenant clients from a
  very early date" (p. 230) — "It is probable" is Eaton's.

##### (d) Custodianship and succession

- **Hereditary succession established immediately, with one irregularity in the founding case.**
  *Evidence:* "the position of prime successor to Baba Farid, later called the 'diwan,' fell to his
  son, Badr ad-Din Sulaiman (1265–81)"; his accession "immediately set a pattern of hereditary
  religious leadership at the shrine" (p. 219). Footnote: "although every diwan of the shrine down
  to the present has been the eldest son of his predecessor, Baba Farid's own first and second sons
  were passed over in favour of Badr ad-Din, who was the third son", and he alone did not receive
  the office from his predecessor but "directly by the Chishti elders in Chisht, western
  Afghanistan" (p. 219, n. 9, citing *Siyar al-auliya'*, 188).
- **The custodian's title is a revenue title.** *Eaton:* "the special title of Baba Farid's chief
  successor, a personage who at other shrines was designated simply sajada nishin ('one who sits
  on the prayer carpet'), at Pakpattan was and is 'diwan,' a term taken directly from the lexicon
  of Indo-Islamic royal courts, and possibly alluding to the man's revenue-collecting function
  mentioned above" (p. 230) — **"possibly" is Eaton's.** Also: "the very word for 'shrine' used in
  the subcontinent, 'dargah,' is also the word for a royal court" (p. 230).
- **The titles as the depositions have them.** "Called the sajada-nishin, ga[dd]-nishin, or most
  commonly the 'Diwan,' the shrine's religious leader was a direct lineal descendant of the saint
  and was believed to be Baba Farid's living representative" (p. 239).
- **The shrine's internal hierarchy, ranked.** *Eaton:* "the adab of the shrine, like that of the
  court of Delhi, also established and sustained a hierarchic principle: in descending rank there
  was the diw[a]n, the diw[a]n's family, the khalifas and shrine functionaries, the Chishti caste,
  the clan leaders, and the common Jat agriculturalists" (p. 236); the code "sorted, arranged, and
  held such peoples in a graded hierarchy that has persisted for centuries" (p. 236) — no end date
  is given for "has persisted" (not stated).
- **The same structure as reconstructed from the depositions.** "At the bottom of the shrine's
  social hierarchy, then, were the peasant murids, 'we smaller people,' belonging for the most part
  to Jat agricultural clans in southwestern Punjab. At the top, of course, was the Diwan, the
  supreme patron of all ritual functions at the shrine and the chief dispenser of Baba Farid's
  blessings. In between these was an endogamous clan of family descendants of Baba Farid, the
  Chishti clan (named after the Sufi brotherhood to which the saint had belonged), which possessed
  both economic privileges and ritual status" (p. 248).
- **The turban as the instrument of succession.** *Eaton:* "the tying of the turban (dast[a]r
  ba[n]d[i]) possessed a great symbolic repertoire: it defined relations of kinship between the
  shrine and subordinate clans, it symbolically conferred legitimacy on actual rulers in Delhi, and
  it conferred spiritual discipleship at the shrine itself" (p. 236); the ceremony "has obvious
  parallels with a coronation ceremony" (p. 230). *Evidence:* "the depositions are full of evidence
  indicating that a proper successor to Baba Farid's shrine had to be 'crowned' with a turban
  representing the saint's own turban, i.e., the dastar-bandi ceremony. Both disputants in this
  case accordingly went through such 'turban-tying' ceremonies by way of laying claim to legitimate
  succession as Diwan" (p. 255).
- **Two words, two social effects.** *Eaton:* the *pecha* "got passed to and from persons otherwise
  occupying different positions in the social hierarchy" and so "promoted egalitarian feelings of a
  shared, common tradition", while "the word dastar referred to the turban that served to separate
  Baba Farid's legitimate successor from the common crowd, thereby having the opposite effect of
  maintaining hierarchy" (p. 257).
- **The 1935–38 succession litigation — a custodianship dispute that generated a mass documentary
  record.** *Evidence:* "In December 1934, Sa'id Muhammad, who was the religious head of the shrine
  of Baba Farid, died, leaving behind him his eleven-year-old son, Qutb al-Din, as his chosen
  successor. Only four months later, however, a local group of devotees at the shrine, who like
  Sa'id Muhammad were genealogical descendants of Baba Farid, filed a legal plaint in the nearby
  District Court in which they not only disputed Qutb al-Din's claim to the shrine's leadership,
  but substituted a rival claimant, Ghulam Ras[u]l, for this post" (p. 238). **The record's
  extent:** "Between 6 April 1935, when Ghulam Rasul filed his plaint, and 2 December 1938, when a
  District Court judge decided the case in favour of the defendant, Qutb al-Din, no less than 159
  oral depositions were filed on behalf of the plaintiff and 172 on behalf of the defendant,
  covering in all over 836 typed pages. In addition, both sides filed nearly three hundred
  documents with the Court" (p. 238). The deponents were "for the most part… local peasant-devotees
  from the district of Montgomery (now Sahiwal), Bahawalpur and Lyallpur" (p. 238). Source:
  **Lahore High Court, Regular First Appeal No. 93 of 1939: *Dewan Ghulam Rasul vs. Ghulam
  Qutab-ud-din*, 5 vols (Lahore, 1940)** (p. 239, n. 3).
- **The dates, as chunk 017 assembled them.** Sa'id Muhammad died 1934 (p. 250, n. 42); Qutb
  al-Din, aged eleven, was turbaned on "1 February 1935" (p. 252, n. 50, citing 3:215; 2:163); the
  trial ran 1935–8 (p. 249); Judge **Guru Datta** delivered final judgment on "2 December 1938"
  (p. 243, n. 17, citing 3:290). The plaintiff lost (p. 249).
- **What the case was actually about.** "the essential purpose of the trial of 1935–8 was to judge
  the plaintiff's claim that the prerogative to select a new Diwan belonged to the Chishti clan and
  not to the previous Diwan" (p. 249).
- **The two rival theories of succession, as the parties stated them.** The defendant "claimed that
  only a former Diwan could choose his successor", and his father "Diwan Sa'id Muhammad, had placed
  a turban on the boy's head during the course of the Bihishti mela of 1933"; "on the occasion of
  the fortieth day after his father's death, the boy had a second dastar-bandi ceremony during
  which the sajjada-nishin of the Ajmer shrine placed the turban on the boy's head" (p. 255, n. 61,
  citing 2:181, 193). The plaintiff "claimed that a new Diwan must be elected by the Chishti clan",
  and "a special dastar-bandi ceremony was held at which representatives of the whole clan placed
  a turban on his head" (p. 255, n. 62, citing 1:47).
- **Physical control of the tomb was decisive leverage.** "Since the plaintiff was not given access
  to the shrine for his dastar-bandi ceremony, this aspect of the rite had to be omitted in his
  case" (p. 256, n. 63).
- **The murids were excluded from the decision.** *Eaton:* "Neither side in this case argued that
  the murids should have had any say in the matter, and in fact the evidence shows that the murids
  were clearly outside the decision-making process" (p. 249). *Evidence — a villager:* "the
  [Chishti] biradri was at first consulted about the appointment of the plaintiff. The murids were
  never consulted, they only said Amin to the biradri's election" (p. 249, n. 40, citing 2, 18–19).
  *A fifty-five-year-old Jat peasant, asked why he had not signed the installation petition:*
  "Prominent Pemres [text-layer garble] were made to sign, and we smaller people were not asked to
  sign" (p. 248, n. 34, citing 1:148).
- **Indifference to the outcome, from below.** *Evidence:* "'I am a follower of the Gaddi Nashin
  whosoever may be occupying it,' stated one of Baba Farid's murids" (p. 258, n. 70, citing Lahore
  High Court, 1:138).
- **Marriage alliances as the patronage instrument, counted from a dated source.** *Evidence:*
  "The Jawahir-i Farid[i] not only lists the clans that, as of 1623, had entered into a
  bride-giving relationship with the diwan and his family, but even names the groom and the bride's
  father in such alliances" (p. 232). The counts **as printed**: of thirteen Khokhar–shrine
  alliances, "seven of the Khokhar brides were daughters of clan m[a]liks, or chiefs", and "three
  brides went to the diwans themselves and six went to sons of various diwans"; the earliest was
  "a Khokhar bride given to a son of Diwan Ahmad Shah, who was diwan of the shrine from 1452 to
  1474" (p. 232, n. 60, citing *Jawahir-i Farid[i]*, 323–4). "Of five exchanges involving the
  Bhattis, two brides were daughters of Bhatti chiefs and three grooms were sons of diwans, the
  earliest alliance dating back to the late 1400s" (p. 232, n. 61). "Of four alliances mentioned
  between the diwan's family and the Hans and Dhudhi tribes, one involved a chief's daughter and
  two others involved granddaughters, and on the other side one alliance involved a diwan and the
  other three, sons of diwans" (p. 232, n. 62). **The Khokhar sub-counts do not exhaust the row:
  3 + 6 = 9 of 13; see Doubts.**
- **The direction of bride-giving is the patronage relation.** *Evidence:* "the latter groups gave
  their daughters to the diwans and their immediate family, whereas Chishti daughters were
  evidently kept within the caste". *Eaton's interpretive premise:* "client-patron relations among
  castes are often structured by the direction of bride-giving, the bride-giving groups normally
  being clients of the bride-receiving group" (p. 232). *Eaton's comparison:* "the kinship
  relations of the diwan and the clans immediately call to mind those of the Mughal court and its
  subordinate Rajput clans, except that in the case of the diwans religious as well as political
  patronage was involved" (p. 232).
- **Allegiance was sworn to the family, not the saint.** *Eaton:* "the clans swore allegiance not
  to Baba Farid but to his family—i.e., the Chishti caste of Pakpattan" (p. 230). *Evidence:* "The
  Khokhars, Bhattis, Dhudhis, and Hans are found in the environs of Pakpattan, and all the clans
  take bai'at with this family and have become murids" (p. 230, n. 53, citing *Jawahir-i Faridi*,
  323); elsewhere "these clans serve the progeny of Baba Farid" (p. 231, n. 54, citing ibid., 396,
  with "Emphasis added" by Eaton).
- **What *bai'at* meant in practice, including a military obligation.** *Eaton:* "bai'at means a
  compact of allegiance, which in early Sufism meant spiritual allegiance only, but which among
  the unlettered Jat clans carried political as well as ethical obligations" (p. 231). *Evidence:*
  the clans who became Muslim from Baba Farid's time "are the possessors of dignity in the
  environs of Pakpattan. They can place ten thousand cavalry and foot soldiers in his [Baba
  Farid's] service, and have complete faith in Baba Farid and his descendants, and are their
  murids" (p. 231, n. 55, citing *Jawahir-i Faridi*, 397–8). *Eaton dates the account to
  Jahangir's reign and records that the levy was not called on then:* "we do not hear that the
  diwan's Jat murids were actually called upon to do battle in service of the shrine" (p. 231).
  **The ten thousand is a claim of capacity in a 1623 source, not a muster.**
- **Living pirs as a distributed franchise.** *Eaton:* "the saint's baraka was seen as continuing
  to flow not only through the medium of such shrines, but also through living pirs who likewise
  traced their spiritual, if not familial, descent from Baba Farid" (p. 242). *Evidence — a
  fifty-four-year-old cultivator-and-pir of Dipalpur:* "I am a ghulam ['slave'] ef [of] Baba
  Farid's sélsila ['spiritual order']. I am Watuzai Pathan and my ancestor of the 16th degree
  higher up was a disciple of Baba Farid. I am personally a pir of my tribe" (p. 242, n. 14, citing
  2:13). *Eaton:* such a pir's authority "stemmed from his descent not from Baba Farid's family,
  but from one of the saint's disciples", by which the following "spread out over many ethnically
  distinct groups, yet was sustained within such groups by living pirs" serving only their own clan
  (p. 242).

##### (e) The shrine and the state, regime by regime

- **The saint's own refusal (thirteenth century).** He "assiduously avoided contact with the
  mundane world of the court and its ministers" (p. 219); Barani's warning quoted above (p. 219,
  n. 12).
- **Khalaji [Khalji] sultans, 1288–1321 — nothing.** *Eaton:* they "do not seem to have been
  interested in the shrine" (p. 220).
- **Tughluqs, 1321–98 — the turn.** *Eaton:* "the whole picture changed with the advent of the
  Tughluq period (1321–98)" (p. 220). Under Muhammad ibn Tughluq: the city of Ajudhan bestowed on
  the shrine (p. 220, n. 16); a *muafi* for the kitchen in Mu'izz ad-Din's successorship, 1335–38
  (p. 221, n. 22); and 'Ala' ad-Din's tomb commissioned, inscribed Safar 737 / Sept.–Oct. 1336
  (p. 221, n. 19). Under Firuz Tughluq, 1351–88: the tomb repaired, robes of honour, and villages
  and lands confirmed (p. 221, n. 23).
- **The custodians absorbed into the state — and Eaton's strongest statement of dependence.**
  *Evidence:* Mu'izz ad-Din "was even called to Delhi by Muhammad ibn Tughluq, placed in government
  service, and sent to Gujarat as deputy governor", and "The diwan's brother, meanwhile, was
  appointed to the office of Shaikh al-Islam of India" (p. 221, nn. 20–21). *Eaton:* "Never were
  the affairs of the shrine more firmly welded to court interests. The shrine's leaders were now
  under Delhi's control, and the shrine became even more dependent economically upon court
  patronage" (p. 221).
- **Royal visits, dated.** *Evidence:* "in October 1398 Tim[u]r, amidst his plundering of northern
  India, took the time to visit the shrine" (p. 228, n. 45, citing *Malfuzat-i Timuri*, abridged
  trans. in Elliot and Dowson, 3:421); "Akbar, in March 1571, opened his sixteenth regnal year in
  Pakpattan, where he implored strength at Baba Farid's shrine" (p. 229, n. 46, citing Abu al-Fadl
  'Allami, *Akbarnama*, trans. H. Beveridge (Delhi, n.d.), 2:525–6). *Eaton generalizes:* "No major
  ruler passed by the area without showing deference to its spiritual power" (p. 228).
- **Mughal settlement, 1629 onward.** Shah Jahan's *farman* continuing the whole *muhal* in
  endowment, described as pre-existing (p. 229, n. 47); the diwans collecting the state's in-kind
  crop share in return (p. 229, n. 48); the diwan as *de facto* chaudhri (p. 229).
- **Mughal decline, early 1700s — the shrine as an armed territorial power.** *Eaton:* "Formerly,
  in the history of medieval Punjab, the Diwans of Pakpattan had frequently played important
  political roles, enjoying close relations with the sultans and Mughal emperors of Delhi. And
  during the decline of Mughal authority, the Diwans' political roles even outweighed their
  religious roles" (p. 243). *Evidence — a shrine functionary's deposition:* from the early 1700s
  the Diwans "were inclined to make conquests, to maintain armies, and to build forts. They
  themselves stopped giving spiritual education and employed Maulvis, Imams, Khatibs and Mubilighs
  for the purpose" (pp. 243–244, n. 18, citing 2:278). **Note the substitution of salaried
  religious staff for the custodian's own teaching.**
- **1757: offensive war and territorial gain.** Diwan 'Abd as-Subhan (1752–66) and his Jat murids
  against the raja of Bikaner, expanding holdings east of the Sutlej for the first time (p. 231,
  n. 56).
- **c. 1776: successful defence against the Sikhs.** *Evidence:* "Supported mainly by his Wattu
  murids, the diwan successfully defended a Sikh attack on Pakpattan around 1776 in which Hira
  Singh was killed, and then pursued the retreating Sikhs with four thousand cavalry, killing a
  great number of them" (p. 231, nn. 57–58, citing ibid., 35, and Syad Muhammad Latif, *History of
  the Punjab* (Lahore, n.d.), 312). The attacker was "the expanding Sikh power to the north, in
  particular the Nakkai mi[s]l headed by Hira Singh" (p. 231).
- **1810: Ranjit Singh seizes the holdings.** Rs. 1,000 a year from town duties plus a fourth share
  of four small villages replace the endowment (p. 231 and n. 59).
- **Colonial period: a revenue-report description of the caste, and a colonial court as the forum.**
  Fagan's assessment report (Lahore, 1896 / "1897" in the running text), 50, on the Chishtis as
  non-cultivating landholders (p. 230, n. 52); the *Punjab District Gazetteers* Montgomery volumes
  of 1884 and 1933 as the source of the crowd figure and of the eighteenth-century military and
  revenue narrative (p. 254, n. 56; p. 229, n. 48; p. 231, nn. 56–57); and the 1935–8 District
  Court trial with appeal to the Lahore High Court (pp. 238–239, 249). **The state's archive is
  where this shrine's economy becomes legible: Mughal *farman* held by the West Pakistan Board of
  Revenue, colonial gazetteers and assessment reports, and a five-volume appeal record.**

#### Significance — Eaton's arguments, kept separate from his evidence

- **Conversion claimed by clans, doubted by Eaton.** *The claim:* "of those Jat and Rajput groups
  that became Muslim in the medieval period, the vast majority of them claim to have been converted
  either by Baba Farid or by his contemporary, Baha' al-Haqq Zakariya (d. 1263)" (p. 227); **Table
  9.1 at p. 227 lists the clans and their sources** — see Doubts, the table's cells are not carried
  into this CSV. *Eaton's position is against the claim* (chunk 015 refers it to the notes'
  Arguments section, which is not in this CSV — see Doubts). His two stated grounds here: the
  sixteen-or-twenty-four-year residence is too short for the many claimed meetings (p. 228, n. 43,
  p. 228); and **the shrine, not the saint, is the agent** — "The shrine, on the other hand, has
  been there all along, sustaining the powerful baraka of the saint through its line of diwans"
  (p. 228). The renaming of Ajudhan to Pakpattan is his explanation of why clans remember the saint
  rather than a particular diwan (p. 228).
- **Clans claiming conversion by Baba Farid, from the depositions.** *Evidence:* "the Wattu, the
  Siyal, the Dogar, the Kathia, the Bhatti, and the Tiwana" (p. 241, n. 9, citing 1:124, 138, 188,
  225; 2:80, 87) — "many of the major endogamous clans that are spread throughout the southwestern
  Punjab, mainly as peasant agriculturalists" (p. 241). Quoted: a fifty-four-year-old cultivator of
  Lyallpur District, "Our Tiwana tribe was converted by Baba Farid. Since then our relation of
  discipleship has been continued with the gadd[i]" (p. 241, n. 10, citing 2:87); another Lyallpur
  farmer, "Baba Farid converted us Bhattis into Islam. Since that time we are the followers of the
  gaddi and respect the gaddinashin [i.e., sajjada-nishin, or Diwan] in office" (p. 241, n. 11,
  citing 2:80); a Wattu villager, "Khewa, one of my ancestors, was converted into Islam by Baba
  Farid, and since that time we have been following the gaddi in dispute" (p. 248, n. 35, citing
  2:64). A universalizing claim by one devotee: "All the M[uh]ammadans are the Murids of Baba
  Farid… and so is my family" (p. 248, n. 37, citing 1:205).
- **"The shrine was Baba Farid."** *Eaton's reading of the depositions:* "many murids referred to
  the saint and his shrine in the same terms, sometimes defining themselves as murids of Baba
  Farid, and sometimes referring to themselves as murids of the gaddi, or shrine… The shrine was
  Baba Farid" (p. 241). This is the premise of his conversion argument.
- **The Diwan as living representative.** *Evidence — a forty-year-old landholder of Ferozepore
  District:* "I believe in the sanctity of the sayings, commands, or revelations of Baba Farid…
  Baba Farid's command can never be incorrect. As the Sajjada Nashin in office is a representative
  of Baba Farid, the same sanctity and reverence is shown to the Gaddi Nashin as is shown to Baba
  Farid" (p. 247, n. 32, citing 1:220). One witness called the first four successors to the Prophet
  "gaddi-nishins" rather than caliphs (p. 247, n. 33, citing 1:150).
- **Two-stage intercession, with the custodian holding the first stage.** *Eaton:* "because only the
  Diwan opened the gate, it was only through his agency that devotees gained access to Baba Farid,
  and only through the saint's agency that they gained access to Heaven" (p. 254). **This is the
  clearest statement in the corpus of the shrine's access-control mechanism.**
- **Its place among Indian Muslim holy places.** *Eaton:* the tombs "of Mu'in ad-Din Chishti at
  Ajmer, Nizam ad-Din Auliya' at Delhi, and Baba Farid at Pakpattan—became the first Muslim holy
  places within India", which meant "that South Asian Muslims were no longer compelled to look
  exclusively to the Middle East for spiritual inspiration" (p. 236). *Footnote:* "early
  hagiographic manuals declar[e] that a certain number of pilgrimages to certain Sufi shrines in
  India would be equivalent in moral value to a single pilgrimage to Mecca" (p. 236, n. 68) — **no
  manual is named and no number is given** (not stated).
- **The shrine inside the Chishti patronage settlement.** Pakpattan is named as one of the three
  "enormous cults" on which the order's "wealth and power centred", beside Ajmer and Delhi (p. 265).
- **The cult travelled with Punjabi migrants.** *Evidence:* Mirza Nathan, a Mughal officer in
  Bengal, "was himself a 'faithful disciple' (mu[ri]d-i bandag[i]) of Farid al-Din Shakarganj"
  (p. 265, n. 10). *Eaton's inference, flagged by him:* "probably because the writer's ancestors had
  come from the Punjab where Baba Farid's cult was especially prominent" (p. 265, n. 10) —
  "probably" is Eaton's, not the source's statement.

## Cross-cutting material

Organised by mechanism, not by essay. Under each, Eaton's own conclusions are separated from his
sources' evidence, and his hedges are kept where he uses them. Two closing blocks collect the

### 1. Land grants, revenue assignments and tax-free tenure

**The Mughal frontier grant in Bengal — the most completely documented instrument in the book.**

- The instrument: the Mughals "granted favourable or even tax-free tenures of land to industrious
  individuals who were expected to clear and bring into cultivation undeveloped forest tracts.
  The policy was intended to promote the emergence of local communities that would be both
  economically productive and politically loyal. Every recipient of such grants, Hindu or
  Muslim, was required to support his dependent clients and to pray for the long life of the
  Mughal state" (p. 275).
- The condition: "one of the conditions for obtaining a grant was to build on the land a mosque
  or temple, **to be supported in perpetuity out of the wealth produced on site**" (p. 276).
- The categories, sorted by the universe they built: grants to Hindu institutions "(e.g.,
  brahmottar, devottar, vishnottar, sivottar)" "tended to integrate local communities into a
  Hindu-ordered cultural universe, whereas grants authorizing the establishment of mosques or
  shrines tended to integrate such communities into an Islamic-ordered cultural universe.
  Subsequent demographic patterns evolved from these earlier processes" (p. 276). Because "most
  pioneers were Muslims… mosques comprised the majority of institutions established, with the
  result that the dominant mode of piety that evolved on East Bengal's economic frontier was
  Islamic" (p. 276).
- Personnel carried on the endowment, outliving the founder: "long after the founding pioneer
  died, the mosque he had built would continue to diffuse Islamic religious ideals amongst local
  communities, since Qur'an readers, callers to prayer, and preachers were also supported in
  perpetuity according to terms specified in the foundational grants" (p. 276). Corroborated
  from documents: "Contemporary government documents confirm that Qur'an readers were attached
  to rural mosques and shrines as part of their endowments", the office titled
  *Quran-khwani* or *tilawat* (p. 277, n. 41, citing three Chittagong "Kanun Daimer Nathi" cases).
- Who took the grants, and why: "petty mullas, pilgrims returned from Mecca, preachers,
  charismatic pirs, and local chieftains **seeking tax-free land**. These men oversaw, or
  undertook to oversee, the clearing of forests and the construction of mosques or shrines,
  which in turn became the nuclei for the diffusion of Islamic ideals along the agrarian
  frontier" (p. 286). Tax-free land is named as the recipients' motive.
- The two frontiers fused: "in the Mughal period, Bengal's agrarian and political frontiers had
  collapsed into one. From Sylhet through Chittagong, the government fused the political goal of
  deepening its authority among dependent clients rooted on the land with the economic goal of
  expanding the state's arable land area" (p. 286).
- The one individual grant given in this book: **2 September 1666, of 166.4 acres** (p. 275,
  n. 37). The "hundreds of Mughal records" behind the claim are cited to Eaton's own *Rise of
  Islam* — see the block on quantities documented elsewhere.
- Built form of what the grant produced: "the mosques themselves were not architecturally
  comparable with the great stone or brick religious monuments that the Mughals built in the
  cities. They were, rather, humble structures built of thatching and bamboo" (p. 276). No
  dimensions or building dates (not stated).
- How tomb cults began from it, in Eaton's account: "many pioneers who had obtained the land
  grants, mobilized labour, and founded these institutions passed into subsequent memory as
  powerful pirs. In not a few cases, tomb cults grew up on their gravesites" (pp. 276–277); and
  "the hundreds of tiny mosques and shrines that sprang up along the eastern frontier" (p. 277),
  with no count given (not stated).
- The literary model of the same arrangement, and his limit on it: the *Sekasubhodaya* "presents
  us with a model of patronage—a mosque linked economically with the hinterland and politically
  with the state—that was fundamental to the historical expansion of Muslim agrarian
  civilization throughout the delta"; the shaikh "issued formal documents of settlement to these
  men, who now cultivated the fields whose income would be used to support the mosque" (p. 281).
  **This is a text, and Eaton says so:** the texts are "metaphors for changes experienced by
  people all over the delta" (p. 281), evidence of a pattern and not of their protagonists'
  existence (p. 280).
- Village foundation practice as recalled in 1898 Sylhet, where "the colonization of some of the
  Sylhet forest was still within living memory": a Kali temple if the founding landlord were
  Sakta, a Vishnu temple if Vaishnava, an *akhra* to Radha and Krishna if the village majority
  were Vaishnava, Manasa if the area were snake-infested, "and if the village were founded by
  Muslims, a shrine to some Muslim pir would be established" (p. 276, n. 39, citing
  P. N. Bhattacharjee, *Man in India* 10/1, 1930, 133). This is the informant's report, not
  Eaton's finding.

**Punjab: Pakpattan's endowment, traced through four regimes.**

- The general pattern, stated without a named grant: "the Delhi sultans and Mughal emperors
  granted huge tracts of rich land for the support of the ritual ceremonies performed at the
  shrines" (p. 48), with descendants of the Sufis establishing "marriage alliances with the
  leaders of the pastoralist clans" — three institutional facts in one passage: state land
  grants tied specifically to ritual, affinal alliance, and the shrine as broker between clan,
  state and cosmology (p. 48).
- Ajudhan: the revenues of the city bestowed on the shrine (p. 220). **Eaton's method here is
  explicit and worth keeping:** against shrine traditions of 'Ala' ad-Din's "refusal to consort
  with royalty" he sets Ibn Battuta, "certainly an impartial authority in this matter" — the
  disinterested outsider outweighs the shrine's own literature where the shrine has an interest
  (p. 220).
- A *muafi* endowment for the *langar khana* from Muhammad ibn Tughluq, 1335–38 (p. 221, n. 22).
- Under Firuz Tughluq: tomb repairs, robes of honour, and descendants "confirmed…in possession of
  their villages and lands" (p. 221).
- **1629, the fullest endowment document in the book:** the whole *pargana* ("Muhal") of Pak
  Pattan "with all its dependencies", held in grant from previous emperors, designated for
  *langar* expenses and applied to four heads — the *sajjada nashin*'s maintenance, the
  *darweshes* and *khadims*, "the feed of Travellers", and "the repair and adornment of the
  building" (p. 229).
- Revenue farming as the custodian's function: the government's in-kind crop share on certain
  tracts was collected by the diwan, while cash crops bypassed him entirely; the diwan is
  described as "a de facto chaudhri" (p. 229). And a fiscal gap: "the diwan derived no cattle
  tax from the barr clans" (p. 229) — which is why, **on Eaton's reading**, the shrine's fiscal
  interest lay with settled agriculture rather than pastoralism.
- Landholding by the saint's descendants: the "Chishti" *zamindar* caste, hundreds strong,
  cultivating through tenants rather than themselves, with "both economic privileges and ritual
  status vis-à-vis the local clans" (p. 230, and p. 248 where the clause is partly lost in the
  text layer). The evidence is a colonial settlement document: P. J. Fagan, *Assessment Report
  for Pakpattan Tahsil, Montgomery District, 1897* (Lahore, 1898), 50, calling the Chishtis "a
  'semi-religious Mussalman tribe'" with "'considerable local influence'" who "'are not working
  agriculturalists, but depend for cultivation entirely on tenants'" (p. 248, n. 38).
- The shrine as grantor, not only grantee: land granted **by** an earlier Diwan to a Dipalpur
  shrine, producing acknowledged subordination (p. 242, n. 13).
- Territorial expansion by the shrine itself: holdings extended east of the Sutlej for the first
  time in 1757 (p. 231).
- Expropriation, 1810: Ranjit Singh seized the holdings; the substitute settlement was Rs. 1,000
  a year from Pakpattan's town duties plus a fourth share of four small villages (p. 231, n. 59).
- The Chishti order's endowments generally: "magnificent tombs and considerable tax-free land" to
  the descendants of the great shaikhs, "Since the Tughluq period" (p. 265). No acreage or
  revenue figure (not stated).

**Deccan and elsewhere.**

- A whole city's revenues assigned to a shrine, **with the source's own doubt attached**:
  Muhammad Suleman Siddiqi reports a document of Firuz's reign recording "the donation of the
  whole of Firuzabad's revenues to Khalifat al-Rahman. The document also gives an account of a
  miracle that the Sufi purportedly performed in the sultan's presence, **which leads Suleman
  Siddiqi to question the document's authenticity**" (p. 195, n. 14, citing Siddiqi, *The
  Bahmani Sufis*, Delhi, 1989, 76).
- A coronation gift, weighed: on his accession at Gulbarga, Hasan Bahman Shah "ordered a gift of
  five *man* (200 lbs.) of gold and ten *man* (400 lbs.) of silver to be given to the shrine of
  Burhan al-Din Gharib" at Khuldabad (p. 182, citing Firishta, *Tarikh-i Firishta*, Lucknow edn,
  1:27). The pound equivalents are the text's own gloss on *man*, i.e. 40 lbs to the *man*.
  **Eaton's reading is strategic, not pious:** the gift "was politically astute, since it
  acknowledged not only Burhan al-Din Gharib, but that shaikh's own master, Nizam al-Din Auliya"
  (p. 182).
- Bengal, a dated cash figure: "while in Bengal in 1624, the future emperor Shah Jahan
  distributed Rs 4,000 at Nur Qutb-i 'Alam's shrine, his largest cash contribution in all of
  Bengal" (p. 267, n. 17).
- The military land assignment as the surrounding fiscal system: *iqta'*, introduced to northern
  India in the eleventh century, and its Kakatiya analogue *nayamkara*, both "referred to a
  distinctive mode of land tenure that linked a state's revenue system to its armed forces.
  Specifically, nobles were given land assignments commensurate in area and productive capacity
  with the size of cavalry or other military units that they were expected to maintain"
  (pp. 174–175); Wagoner calls the Vijayanagara version "'an Indic adaptation of the *iqta'*
  system'" (p. 175). Tabataba's *Burhan-i ma'athir* (completed 1595–6) on the 1347 distribution:
  "Each of the kingdom's amirs, vazirs, and nameworthy cavalrymen, having been distinguished
  with a *jagir* and *muqassa* according to his own rank, received an *iqta'* in towns and
  *parganas*, and he maintained troops, attendants, and equipage that were proportionate to
  those [lands]" (p. 175, n. 11, Eaton's own translation, so noted).
- The deep background he supplies: the land-revenue system as the inherited institution "on which
  the political economies of all great empires of the Fertile Crescent had rested" (pp. 34–35);
  and "systems of land tenure and taxation that encouraged land reclamation and a more
  intensified use of older fields, contributed to a significant increase in food production in
  the eighth to eleventh centuries" (p. 40).

**Temple endowments under the same instrument — the exact shape of a shrine endowment applied to
a temple.** Aurangzeb's order of 28 February 1659 protects "a group of Brahmans who are in charge
of ancient temples", forbids removing them "from their charge of temple-keeping", and requires
"that nobody unlawfully disturbs the Brahmans or other Hindus of that region, **so that they might
remain in their traditional place and pray for the continuance of the Empire**"; the justification
is "'According to the Holy Law (*shar'a*)… ancient temples should not be torn down'" — followed
immediately by "'nor should new temples be built'", which Eaton says "broke decisively from
Akbar's policy" (p. 129, Persian at n. 51). Note the structure: a named grantee class, a
protection order, a date, and a reciprocal obligation to pray for the dynasty. Compare the 1326
Kalyana inscription: Muhammad bin Tughluq "appointed Muslim officials to repair a Siva temple
in Kalyana (in Bidar District), thereby facilitating the resumption of normal worship"; on that
sultan's interpretation of the law, "anybody who paid the poll-tax (jizya) could build temples in
territories ruled by Muslims" (pp. 126–127, citing P. B. Desai, *Epigraphia Indica* 32).

**And the fiscal instrument working against religious institutions:** the jizya, "never previously
been imposed or collected in Bengal", collected from Dhaka's Hindus "ever so strictly" from early
1681; at Qasimbazar officials "forcibly demanded the jizya from Hindu silk workers, which
disrupted the local textile production business and drove the city's 'little people' into the
interior" (p. 268, n. 20).

### 2. The shrine as a redistributive institution: what flows in, what flows out, who is fed

**The founding description.** "it was Baba Farid's practice to distribute among his khalifas and
the common devotees the presents that other devotees had brought. The shaikh's convent, and
later his shrine, thus served as a nexus for the circulation and redistribution of a great deal
of material wealth in the region" (p. 218, citing Mir Khurd Kirmani, *Siyar al-auliya'*, Delhi,
1885, 131).

**Eaton derives the institution from the flow, not from doctrine:** "In a social and institutional
sense, moreover, the ta'widh-futah system required the shrine to adopt a certain degree of
rational organization with respect to the distribution of material wealth" (p. 218). The same
system in its religious register: it "defined and sustained Baba Farid's intermediary status
between the devotee and God, as a conduit through whose intercession with God one's wishes may be
fulfilled" (p. 218). He treats the two registers as one mechanism: the *ta'widh* given and the
*futuh* received "provided the structural framework upon which the subsequent devotionalism of
the shrine rested" (p. 218).

**He names the model himself.** The section heading is "THE SHRINE AND THE EXCHANGE OF RELIGIOUS
GOODS" (p. 239), and the structure is stated in patron–client terms: "an exchange of 'religious
goods' between the shrine's patrons and its clients—the chief patron being the Diwan, and the
clients being the masses of murids" (p. 239).

**Inward flow.**

- *Nazrana*, "usually cash contributions" (p. 239), with donor earmarking — devotees could direct
  it to the public kitchen or to the Diwan personally, and the Diwan also collected on village
  tours (p. 239).
- Turbans "in addition to cash" from devotees and well-wishers (p. 256).
- At initiation: a murid's own account — "my hand was placed in the hand of the Gaddi Nashin. I
  offered Nazrana, and I was given spiritual instructions… I was also given a 'Wazifa' for my
  recitation" (p. 251, n. 47, citing 1:109). **No amount is given for an ordinary murid's
  nazrana** (not stated).
- At the *dastar-bandi*, with figures, and from Hindus: Ganpat Rai testified "on the dastarbandi
  of the defendant nazranas were paid [by] us on behalf of the Mandi [market]" (p. 253, n. 51);
  Devi Bakhsh, a Khatri, offered "a turban and twenty-one rupees cash" (p. 253, n. 52); Ram
  Rakha Mal "a turban and eleven rupees" (p. 253, n. 53). The amounts are printed as words, not
  digits — a text-layer note worth keeping.
- Cash payments to village pirs replicate the income stream at village level (p. 242, n. 15).

**Outward flow.** "the opposite flow, from the shrine outward to the devotees, took place in
several ways" — the *langar*, sugar, and turbans (pp. 239–240).

- *Langar khana*: instituted under the first diwan, with distribution of food and sweets directed
  by the diwan in person on formal occasions (p. 219); endowed by the 1335–38 *muafi* (p. 221,
  n. 22) and by the 1629 *pargana* grant (p. 229). The complex's kitchen is also called a
  "welfare kitchen" (p. 218).
- Operations in the 1930s: sited in the courtyard opposite Baba Farid's tomb, "between 240 and
  320 lbs. (i.e. 3 or 4 mans) of wheat… every day, along with commensurate amounts of dal"
  (p. 240). **This is the only measured daily throughput anywhere in the book.**
- Distributions in kind, monopolised by the custodian: sugar and turbans "distributed only by the
  Diwan, and only on the occasion of particularly important shrine rituals at which he
  officiated" (p. 240); "hundreds of pechas" at the annual 'urs, distributed personally and only
  by the *sajjadanashin* (p. 256).
- Transfers between custodianships: at Badr al-Din Ishaq's 'urs, a satellite shrine in the same
  town, the Diwan arrived in a *palki* and gave the descendant-custodian "100 rupees in cash, a
  horse, and a silver saddle" (p. 255).
- Salaried religious employees paid out of the establishment: from the early 1700s the Diwans
  "employed Maulvis, Imams, Khatibs and Mubilighs" to give the spiritual education they had
  stopped giving themselves (pp. 243–244, n. 18).

**Who is fed.** The 1629 grant's four heads name the beneficiaries: the *sajjada nashin*, the
*darweshes* and *khadims*, travellers, and the fabric of the building (p. 229). To these the
depositions add a resident dervish body — "*darveshes of the Hujra*, 1,000–1,200" — which is
itself an institution the shrine supported (p. 254).

**The living-pir version of the same flow, from a Bengali ballad:** "Hundreds of men and women
came every day to pay him their respects… Presents of rice, fruits, and other delicious food,
goats, chickens, and fowls came in large quantities to his doors. **Of these offerings the pir
did not touch a bit but freely distributed all among the poor**" (p. 44, quoting D. C. Sen,
*Eastern Bengal Ballads*, 3 vols, Calcutta 1923, 1: 219–20). Eaton's reading: "the Sufi's
widespread popularity rested on his perceived supernatural, healing, and psychic powers, and the
simple generosity he displayed to the poor" (p. 44). **This is a ballad, and Eaton presents it as
one.**

**Two qualifications that must travel with any welfare reading.**

1. Eaton puts the religious function first, and says so in terms that qualify every economic fact
   he then supplies: "Although such shrines possessed important economic, political, and social
   ties with the masses of villagers who frequented them, their fundamental raison d'être was
   religious. For it was through its rituals that a shrine made Islam accessible to nonlettered
   masses… integrating them into its ritualized drama both as participants **and as sponsors**"
   (p. 216). The financing is inside his description of the ritual, not separated from it.
2. **The shrine is not described as a leveller.** "Even while integrating diverse peoples into a
   common religious culture, the shrine's highly elaborated code of conduct sorted, arranged,
   and held such peoples in a graded hierarchy that has persisted for centuries" (p. 236). And
   ritual is assigned a distributive *and* stratifying function: "A series of elaborate
   initiatory and commemorative rituals had the effect of reinforcing existing hierarchal
   arrangements, while at the same time providing expressive meaning and cognitive order in the
   religious world of the shrine's devotees" (p. 250).

### 3. Patronage by successive states, and what the state bought with it

**The doctrine that makes a tomb worth money to a state.** "as the spiritual power of a
charismatic Sufi was believed to adhere after his lifetime to his tomb-site, shrines at such
tombs were patronized by Indo-Muslim rulers just as they were frequented by Muslim devotees"
(p. 113). And the theory of office behind it: temporal rulers "had only been entrusted with a
sort of temporary lease of power through the grace of Muslim shaikhs considered to have been
especially well-endowed with *baraka*" (p. 195) — the *wilayat*/*hukumat* dual sovereignty in
which shaikhs "leased out political sovereignty (*hukumat*) to kings, who were thus charged with
the messy and very worldly business of administration, taxation, warfare, and so forth"
(pp. 180–181), a variation on the Iranian sultan/caliph arrangement, with shaikhs now in the
caliph's place "since no functioning caliph had existed since 1258" (p. 181).

**Separate the evidence from the use.** The doctrine is evidenced from 'Isami — "'Although there
might be a monarch in every country, yet it is actually under the protection of a fakir [Sufi
shaikh]'" (p. 112, Persian at n. 13; *Futuh us-salatin*, 1350) — and for 'Isami "history itself
was but the working out of divine will as mediated by spiritually powerful shaikhs" (p. 181).
**That is 'Isami's theology; its use as evidence of an assumption current at the Bahmani court is
Eaton's.** Note too the direction of his sentence: the shaikhs are "the preeminent patrons of
Indo-Muslim kingship" (p. 180) — patrons, not clients.

**The policy, stated as a thesis.** With the Ghurids and the Delhi Sultanate, "we can identify
two principal components to this project: (a) state patronage of an India-based Sufi order, and
(b) a policy of selective temple desecration that aimed not, as earlier, to finance distant
military operations on the Iranian Plateau, but to delegitimize and extirpate defeated Indian
ruling houses" (p. 112). The patronage "contributed positively to the process of Indo-Muslim
state-building" by "effectively injecting a legitimizing 'substance' into a new body politic at
the moment of its birth" (p. 116).

**Why the Chishtis, and not another order.** "since the tomb-shrines of the greatest shaikhs of
this order were located within South Asia, and not in distant Central Asia or West Asia as was
the case with those of other orders, a ruling dynasty's patronage of Chishti shrines could
bolster its claims to being both legitimately Islamic and authentically Indian" (p. 113, restated
p. 180). Plus the publicists: Amir Hasan, Amir Khusrau and Zia al-Din Barani were disciples of
Delhi's principal Chishti shaikh and patronized by the Tughluq court, so "the public and the
ruling classes alike gradually came to associate dynastic fortune with that of the Chishti order"
(pp. 112–113). And the mobility: "Sufi shaikhs, like robes or mantles, were highly mobile, which
is another reason they played such critical roles in launching new states" (p. 185); the
founder's problem "always came to this: how to sink down permanent roots and rule with security
and credibility in a land that only a generation earlier had been plundered with impunity? **This
was where immigrant Sufi shaikhs, as portable vessels of spiritual sovereignty, played their
crucial role**" (pp. 185–186).

**The pattern, dated.** "within the space of just five years, between 1342 and 1347, founders of
independent Indo-Muslim dynasties in both Bengal and the Deccan patronized local Chishti shaikhs
whose own spiritual masters had migrated from Delhi" (p. 114); repeated for Gujarat (1396) and
Malwa (1404); and "the entire Mughal dynasty, believing that the blessings of Chishti shaikhs
underpinned their worldly success, vigorously patronized the order" (p. 115).

**What victory bought, and what patronage followed.** "Two of Akbar's fourteen pilgrimages to the
shrine of Mu'in al-Din Chishti at Ajmer, those of 1568 and 1574, were made immediately after
conquering Chitor and Bengal respectively" (p. 115, citing P. M. Currie, *The Shrine and Cult of
Mu'in al-Din Chishti of Ajmer*, 1992, p. 100), several of them on foot (p. 116). Akbar to
Badauni: "'All this (success) has been brought through the Pir'" (p. 115, citing Badauni,
*Muntakhab al-tawarikh*, trans. Lowe, 2:243). **The count of fourteen is Currie's, not documented
in this book.**

**Mughal building at Chishti shrines, itemised** (p. 115): Babur's brother-in-law rebuilt Nizam
al-Din's tomb (1526); Akbar built Humayun's tomb near it and began Fatehpur Sikri at Salim
Chishti's hospice, both in 1571; Jahangir built gates and other buildings at or near Ajmer, "as
did Shah Jahan as part of his victory celebrations after defeating the raja of Mewar"; Aurangzeb
made "sizeable contributions" at Gulbarga, Khuldabad, Ajmer and Delhi; 'Alamgir II repaired Nizam
al-Din's tomb; Bahadur Shah II built beside Bakhtiyar Kaki's shrine. The habit is stated
generally too: monarchs "typically sought their favour and patronized the construction of great
edifices for them" (p. 195).

**A complete shrine-building programme in a single epigraph** — the Dhar inscription, on a grave
that "although a renowned place of pilgrimage, had suffered from neglect": "'Graves from their
oldness became leveled (to the ground)… There was also (no place) for retirement, wherein the
distressed darvish could take rest. Thereupon the king of the world gave the order that this top
of Tur [Mount Sinai] be built anew'". Eaton: "His great act was to patronize the cult of 'Abdullah
Shah by (re)building his shrine which… **included a strong vault, a mosque, and a caravan-sarai**.
The inscription closes by offering a prayer that the soul of the benevolent sultan may last until
Judgment Day and that his empire may last in perpetuity" (p. 109). The site's own pre-history is
in the same text: "'Now this (burial place and) grave of martyrs remained a trace of those holy
and pious people'" (p. 108) — martyrs' graves before the saint — and "'Now this tomb since those
old days has been the famous pilgrimage-place of a world'" (pp. 108–109). **But this inscription
is also Eaton's worked example of why such a text is not a newspaper: see §7.**

**What the state bought at Pakpattan, and what the shrine did with it.**

- The two-way relation: "As an intermediary institution in both a religious and a political
  sense, the shrine of Baba Farid was itself patronized by Delhi" (p. 228), and "As the Delhi
  court patronized the shrine, so also the shrine patronized the agricultural clans, in some
  ways even mimicking the symbols of the larger court" (pp. 229–230).
- Patronage bought pliability: "Once begun, the Tughluq court's patronage of the shrine continued
  to grow as 'Ala' ad-Din's successors proved more pliable to the court's will" (p. 221), with
  government service for the diwan and the office of Shaikh al-Islam for his brother as the
  evidence of state capture (p. 221).
- What the clans bought: "The shrine thus gave clan leaders and their followers not only access
  to Islam, but **the honour of participating in the reflected splendour of the Sultanate or
  Mughal courts without actually being directly subservient to the authorities in Delhi**"
  (p. 228) — prestige without subordination, which is Eaton's account of why chiefs bought in.
- Military service as a component of discipleship: *bai'at* carrying "political as well as
  ethical obligations" and, in one passage, a claimed levy of "ten thousand cavalry and foot
  soldiers" (p. 231) — the claim is the source's; actually mobilised in 1757 and c. 1776, the
  latter with "four thousand cavalry" in pursuit (p. 231).
- **Contradiction, kept:** Eaton summarises at p. 228 that "since the third diwan, the Delhi court
  lavished the revenues of towns and villages in support of the shrine and its attendants",
  which the chunk 016 worker flagged against chunk 015's Ibn Battuta evidence for the second
  diwan 'Ala' ad-Din at p. 220. Both readings stand until a human checks the printed page.

**Mosques and Sufi shrines as a protected class — the finding that most constrains a
state-capture story.** "No evidence… suggests that ruling authorities attacked public monuments
like mosques or Sufi shrines that had been patronized by disloyal or rebellious officers. Nor
were such monuments desecrated when one Indo-Muslim kingdom conquered another and annexed its
territories. To the contrary, **new rulers were quick to honour and support the shrines of those
Chishti shaikhs that had been patronized by those they had defeated**" — Babur at Delhi in 1526,
the Mughals at Pandua in 1574, Aurangzeb at Khuldabad and Gulbarga, "notwithstanding his
reputation for eschewing the culture of saint-cults" (p. 134). The reason he gives: "the deity
worshipped in mosques or shrines had no personal connection with a Muslim monarch. Nor were such
monuments thought of as underpinning, far less actually constituting, the authority of an
Indo-Muslim king" (p. 134), so mosques were "detached from both sovereign terrain and dynastic
authority, and hence politically inactive" (p. 135). The symmetry holds in reverse: Shivaji's
Marathas and Vijayanagara did not desecrate mosques or shrines when they took Muslim territory
(p. 135).

**Temples, by contrast, were state property inside the state — and that cuts both ways.** "from
Akbar's time (r. 1556–1605) onward, Mughal rulers treated temples lying within their sovereign
domain as state property; accordingly, they undertook to protect both the physical structures and
their Brahman functionaries" (p. 128); "pragmatism as well as time-honoured traditions of both
Islamic and Indian statecraft dictated that temples lying within such states be left unmolested"
(p. 126). But a disloyal patron's temple was liable, "treating that temple as an extension of the
officer, and hence liable for punishment" (pp. 129–130). The hinge sentence: "That same
significance, in turn, **rendered temples just as deserving of peace-time protection as it
rendered them vulnerable in times of conflict**" (p. 136). And the consequence Eaton draws, which
is directly useful to a welfare argument: "by appropriating Hindu religious institutions to serve
imperial ends… the Mughals became deeply implicated in institutionalized Indian religions, **in
dramatic contrast to their British successors, who professed a hands-off policy in this
respect**" (p. 128); "For the Mughals, politics within their sovereign domains never meant
annihilating prior authority, but appropriating it within a hierarchy of power that flowed from
the Peacock Throne to the mass of commoners below" (pp. 128–129).

**Patronage and sanctity compound each other** — stated in one clause, at a non-Muslim shrine:
"Royal patrons gravitated toward a shrine to a river goddess that was already endowed with
sanctity, **and whose sanctity increased with increasing royal patronage**" (p. 185). Patronage
escalated with political ambition: "from the thirteenth century politically ambitious or
already-dominant rulers in the area began cultivating closer ties with the shrine and its
deities, in contrast to the earlier phase, when the shrine was patronized by none but passing
chieftains" (p. 184); and the Kampilas' attempt to build a state on the site "thereby to reap the
ritual and political benefits of an expanding religious cult, established an important precedent"
(p. 184). Its growth is "measured" only in households: "inscriptions that enumerate the many
hundreds of households then located there" (p. 184) — no figure is given (not stated).

**Bengal's state patronage, and its deliberate absence.**

- Pandua's tombs were the shrines "most lavishly patronized by the Mughals" (p. 266); Nur
  Qutb-i 'Alam's shrine had been under state patronage since Sultan Ahmad (r. 1432-33) became
  the shaikh's disciple, and was "the focus of annual pilgrimages performed by Sultan 'Ala
  al-Din Husain Shah (r. 1493-1532)" by the end of the fifteenth century (p. 266, n. 14).
- Functional specialisation of cities: Dhaka "primarily devoted to revenue collection,
  administration, politics, and military reviews… In short, Dhaka was a secular city"
  (pp. 265–266); Gaur and Pandua, "denied any political significance under the Mughals, emerged
  under their rule as Islamic sacred centres" (p. 266). Dhaka's mosques "bear the stuccoed stamp
  of their north Indian patrons, and seem intended more to display imperial power than to
  inspire piety" (p. 266).
- **Non-intervention as stated policy**, with the Ottoman comparison as control: "Unlike the
  contemporary Ottoman Empire where non-Muslim military recruits were converted to Islam as part
  of their assimilation into the ruling class, in India non-Muslims were given full admission
  into the Mughal officer corps as non-Muslims" (p. 267); the binding agent "was not a common
  religion, then, but the ideology of 'salt'" — ritual eating binding "people of unequal
  sociopolitical rank to mutual obligations: the higher-ranked person swore to protect the
  lower, in return for which the latter swore loyalty to the higher", across religious
  communities and "over several generations" (p. 267, n. 18). "Mughal officials did not patronize
  Islam as a state religion" (p. 267); Bengal's rulers "maintained a strictly non-interventionist
  position in religious matters, despite pressure from local religious functionaries (mullas) and
  Sufis to support Islam over other religions" (p. 268). The 1609 Tuqmaq Khan case: "the Governor
  did not view government service as a reward for conversion to Islam; to the contrary, in this
  instance the man responsible for causing the conversion was censured and transferred" (p. 269),
  and it was not Islam Khan alone but "'the other officers of the State'", "suggesting that the
  hands-off policy was a general one" (p. 269). Later: "some Mughal officers even opposed the
  Islamization of native Bengalis" (p. 285).
- **The contradiction is Eaton's own point, not an inconsistency to resolve:** "although the
  Mughal government does not appear to have intended to Islamize the East Bengali countryside,
  such an outcome nonetheless resulted from its land policies" (pp. 277–278).

**What the state's patronage left behind in shrine practice — the clearest description of shrine
office-holding in the book.** The Ajmer 'urs protocols "betray the impact of Mughal court
etiquette. **The diwan, dressed Mughal fashion, represents in fact the Mughal king rather than a
religious dignitary**, and comes escorted by the torch-bearers and mace-bearers wearing Mughal
costumes. He takes his seat on the cushion (*gadela*) under a special tent (*dalbadal*)… On his
arrival in the shrine the diwan kisses the tomb and offers flowers, and then one of the khadims,
who happens to be his wakil, like the other pilgrims, ties a *dastar* (turban) over his head…
and then gives him *tabarruk*, consisting of flowers, sandal and sweets… Then he sits down and
**the fatiha khwans, who are permanently and hereditarily employed, recite the fatiha, as well as
prayers for the sovereign (*badshah-i Islam*), the diwan, the *mutawalli* and other officials,
and for the general public**" (p. 116, n. 25, quoting Syed Liyaqat Hussain Moini, "Rituals and
Customary Practices at the Dargah of Ajmer", in Troll, ed., *Muslim Shrines in India*, 1989,
pp. 72, 74). Offices named: diwan, khadim, wakil, mutawalli, fatiha khwan. Objects: gadela,
dalbadal, dastar, tabarruk. **Eaton's conclusion from it:** "the Mughal–Chishti partnership even
survived the collapse of the Mughal state. In a sense it persists to this day. The ceremonies, the
terminology, and the protocol still found at Chishti shrines generally, and at the Ajmer shrine
particularly, all reflect the extraordinary intrusion of the Mughals' courtly culture into that of
the Chishti order" (p. 116). Directly usable for reading court idiom in a present-day Chishti
shrine's practice — noting that the protocol description is Moini's 1989 observation, not Eaton's.

**The colonial and post-colonial state.**

- 1810: expropriation (above).
- 1935–38: the succession suit before the senior subjudge of Montgomery District (p. 257).
  Superhuman evidence "such as a revelation from Baba Farid was inadmissible" (p. 257). The
  general rule Eaton states: "In British India, the effect of such laws was to render state
  sanction to whatever local customs the courts judged were already being practiced by a
  majority of the people" (p. 257); the District Court "merely ratified what it deemed to be the
  customary practice with regard to succession at the shrine, and then declared its judgment to
  be in conformity with Islamic Law" (p. 258).
- **His political reading of the verdict, and he hedges it:** "this formula **would seem** to have
  provided the Court with a practical means of settling the dispute… In 1938, with the forces of
  Islamic reform and Muslim separatism rising on all sides in British India, the government could
  hardly have taken the politically dangerous step of declaring that the most popular Islamic
  shrine in the western Punjab was not, after all, Islamic" (p. 246).
- The effect on the institution: litigation "seemed to promote the popular perception of the
  shrine as a religious institution only, and to diminish perception of it as a political
  institution" (p. 243). **Evidence from both directions, kept side by side:** older murids
  "tended to see the Diwan's traditional function as both religious and political", one calling
  him a "pishwa… 'who leads us on the material and spiritual lines'" (p. 244, n. 19); while
  others "sensed a sharp separation" — a Jat murid of Bahawalpur: "On the political side, there
  is now no Khalifa in British India, only the British Government governs the country. The
  spiritual system was taken up by Baba Farid Shakar Ganj" (p. 244, n. 20). Eaton's inference:
  "The fact that 'only the British Government governs the country' had as its natural corollary,
  then, **the enhancement of the shrine's religious status in the eyes of its devotees**" (p. 244).
- The contradiction of imperial jurisprudence, which he states as such: "Here was an
  Anglo-Indian civil court deciding, according to British legal procedures, a dispute between
  two Muslims over which one of them, according to Islamic Law as well as local custom, should
  legitimately succeed" and being "forced to make decisions that were strictly religious in
  nature: for example, that the former Diwan did in fact have a revelation from Baba Farid"
  (p. 243).
- **Pointers, not findings:** David Gilmartin, "Shrines, Succession, and Sources of Moral
  Authority", in Barbara Metcalf, *Moral Conduct and Authority* (1984), 221–40, on "the intrusion
  of British power in Punjabi shrines" (p. 244, n. 21); Katherine Ewing, "The Politics of Sufism:
  Redefining the Saints of Pakistan", *JAS* 42/2 (Feb. 1983), 251–68, on "how the Government of
  Pakistan has dealt with the culture of shrines and saints since 1947" (p. 246, n. 30). **Eaton
  supplies no post-1947 material of his own.**

### 4. Custodianship, succession and lineage as economic structures

**Doctrine to institution.** "By the thirteenth century, it had become commonly believed in the
Muslim world that a great shaikh's baraka adhered, after his death, **to his familial descendants
as well as to the place of his burial**" (p. 218) — which is how Eaton derives hereditary
custodianship from doctrine rather than from mere family interest. And the structural statement:
"both the Diwan and the shrine functioned as intermediaries for an intermediary, as on-going
vehicles of the saint's mediative power" (p. 239) — a hereditary custodian monetising a dead
saint's *baraka*, put in structural rather than polemical terms.

**The chain as an institution:** "establishing and maintaining a graded, spiritual hierarchy
descending from God through the Prophet Muhammad and Baba Farid to the Diwan and from him to his
khalifas (spiritual adepts) and murids (spiritual affiliates)", with "a perceived flow of grace,
or baraka, from God to the murid in which process the shrine is critical since it represents the
visible, tangible point at which this grace is localized" (p. 247).

**The office's vocabulary read as evidence of function.** *sajada nishin* elsewhere, *diwan* at
Pakpattan (p. 230); in the 1930s depositions "sajada-nishin, gadd-nishin, or most commonly the
'Diwan'" (p. 239). *dargah* as royal court, *dastar bandi* as coronation, and *diwan* as a revenue
office — "possibly alluding to the man's revenue-collecting function" (p. 230), which is the link
between Eaton's symbolic argument and his fiscal one. The royal symbolism marks "the Diwan's
supreme religious **and administrative** position" (p. 255), the *palki* being "from ancient times
traditionally reserved for persons of privileged status" (p. 255).

**Succession: the archive must keep both accounts.**

- Chunk 015 records the office as hereditary and by primogeniture "down to the present", with two
  documented exceptions (p. 219, n. 9). All major rituals, including the turban rites, were
  instituted "in the generation following the saint's death, that is, in the late thirteenth
  century" (p. 240, n. 8).
- But in the 1935–38 suit the rule was contested in open court. **Defendant:** succession by "de
  facto primogeniture… accorded with the shrine's traditional custom", and since "the shari'a is
  not generally interpreted as sanctioning primogeniture", "'this Gaddi follows custom and not
  Mohammadan Law'" (p. 245, n. 22, citing 1:12); plus "a wealth of historical evidence showing
  that previous Diwans had succeeded to the shrine's leadership as minors" (p. 245).
  **Plaintiff:** he "claimed to have been 'elected' Diwan by the Chishti biradari", arguing
  "'The gaddi in dispute is an Islamic gaddi… It is governed by Mohammadan Law and not by
  custom'", that the fittest man is selected by assembly "'as was in vogue during the time of
  Khulafa-i Rashedin'", and "'We, the descendants of Baba Farid, claim to be the descendants
  directly from Hazrat Umar, the second Caliph'" (p. 245, n. 23, citing 2:95).
- The Court's resolution: it "saw the [shrine] basically as a local institution governing its
  affairs by its own traditional custom, but that, conveniently, this traditional custom happened
  to coincide with the shari'a since 'Muhammadan Law itself reserves [customary] usage as the
  primary rule for governing succession to a religious institution'" (pp. 245–246, n. 27), and
  cited jurisprudence "to show that the shari'a even allows a shrine's leader to name his own
  successor" (p. 246, n. 28).
- A devotee's own version of the compromise, from a fifty-two-year-old villager: "The gaddi in
  dispute is [an] Islamic gaddi, but is governed by custom. The gaddinashin [Diwan] must be of
  his father's caste, the mother's caste is immaterial. **A Dewan can appoint whether he receives
  a revelation or not. But a revelation is generally received**" (p. 246, n. 29, citing 2:138).
- **And chunk 018 records that primogeniture is not asserted there at all** (not stated): the
  rules in dispute are nomination by the incumbent versus election by "the Chishti clan"
  (p. 255), with one witness's *bradari* deciding by ordeal (p. 256).

**The installation, described.** *Dastar-bandi* held on "the fortieth day after the death of the
previous Diwan", coinciding with the *chehlum*, "thereby balancing the grief over the loss of a
departed Diwan with the joy over the installation of a new one" (p. 252). Purpose, in the court
record: Q. "What is the aim and object of the Dastar bandi performed on the fortieth day?"
A. "So that all may come to know that such and such person has succeeded" (p. 250, n. 43). The
candidate's "whole head was shaved" (p. 252, n. 49). On stage: "the family of the Diwan, the most
respected members of the Chishti clan, resident mystics living at the shrine, and sajjada-nishins
of all of Baba Farid's 'daughter' shrines in the Punjab" (p. 252), with murids as an "off-stage
audience… in the shrine's spacious courtyard in front of Baba Farid's tomb" (pp. 252–253).

**The rank rule, which is the clearest statement of inter-shrine hierarchy in the book.** "Only
somebody of equal or higher spiritual rank than the new sajjada-nishin could perform this
ceremony… since there was no one within the shrine's own hierarchy possessing a higher rank than
that of the Diwan-elect, this ceremony was normally performed by the head of an outside shrine
having at least equal if not more spiritual authority" — heads of subordinate shrines attended as
guests, and only a superior shrine's head performed it (p. 252). **In 1935 that was the
sajjada-nishin of Ajmer** (p. 252). Chunk 018 adds variants: performed "variously by a
predecessor, by an outside *sajjada-nishin* (Ajmer), or by clan representatives" (p. 255), and
"the fortieth day after his father's death" as a succession occasion (p. 255); the saffron turban
was reserved to the Diwan, touched to the tomb, then tied (p. 256). Physical control of the shrine
was itself a resource in the litigation: the plaintiff "was not given access to the shrine for his
dastar-bandi ceremony" (p. 256, n. 63); the Bihishti mela of 1933 was used as the occasion for a
*dastar-bandi* (p. 255).

**Eaton's reading of the turban's double economics** — the sharpest interpretive move in this
group: it "served as a vehicle both for religious legitimacy and for the distribution of Baba
Farid's grace" (p. 255), with "ambiguous associations with the crown and thereby with royalty"
(p. 255). The vocabulary is his evidence: *pecha* circulating up and down the hierarchy "promoted
egalitarian feelings of a shared, common tradition", while *dastar* "separate[d] Baba Farid's
legitimate successor from the common crowd" (p. 257), so that "it is in the turban's rich symbolic
repertoire that we see its capacity to express and sustain values, so central to the shrine, that
might otherwise appear mutually antagonistic, i.e., **a hierarchy of religious status and a
commonality of cultural inheritance**" (p. 257). On the competing origin stories for the turban
rite, he reads for the shared premise rather than adjudicating the events: "In all of these
versions we find the common assumption that the turban, used in the proper ritual context, was
uniquely empowered to endow the wearer with Baba Farid's own spiritual authority" (p. 256).

**Marriage as patronage infrastructure, counted.** As of 1623: 13 alliances with Khokhar, 5 with
Bhatti, 4 between Hans and Dhudhi — **asymmetric in direction, clans give brides and Chishtis do
not** (p. 232).

**Endogamy as a control instrument, with a dated document.** On 16 February 1921 a faction of the
Chishti clan issued a notice that the descendants of "Hazrat Shah Ala-ud-Din Mauj Darya … grandson
of Hazrat Baba Sahib" are "the most honourable and distinguished of all the descendants of Farid",
have intermarried among themselves, and "though they took girls of other families in marriage,
yet they did not give away their daughters in marriage in other families for the last five or six
centuries… Whoever of the descendants of Mauj Darya gives his daughter in marriage in [another]
family, should be excommunicated from the brotherhood, and it is also improper to maintain
connections of any sort with him" (p. 249, n. 41, citing 4:27), justified from "'traditions from
the holy Prophet… in regard to prohibition from abandonment of one's own family in matters of
matrimonial relations'" (pp. 249–250, n. 42). The judge observed that the former Diwan's having
"given his own daughter in marriage outside the lineage constituted one of the ultimate causes of
the entire dispute, since that act had touched off the profound discord within the Chishti baradari
that erupted only after the former Diwan died in 1934" (p. 250, n. 42, citing 3:298).

**Eaton's inference from it, marked as his.** "It is well known that a rigid control over marital
contracts, especially with respect to bride-giving, is a key mechanism by which the Hindu social
order maintains its system of endogamous castes ranked in a graded hierarchy. The influence of
Hindu social concepts on the functioning of the Chishti clan's social relations is therefore
obvious" (p. 249), and he finds it "even more remarkable" that the 1921 statement "appealed to
Islamic Law for its justification" (p. 249). **No source asserts the borrowing.** The same move is
made for forelock-clipping at initiation: it "follows a basic Indian paradigm of religious
initiation", and since murids took no ascetic vows it "must either be a remnant from earlier
times… or, as is more likely the case, it was borrowed from Hindu-Buddhist initiatory practices"
(p. 251) — his inference, and his hedge.

**The two-tier lineage system — the load-bearing economic structure.** The Chishti clan "possessed
both economic privileges and ritual status vis-à-vis the local [text lost here]" (p. 248), on
Fagan's 1897 Assessment Report evidence (quoted in §1). Eaton's conclusion: "As these tenants were
drawn from among Jat clans whose members were murids of the shrine, what we see here is **a system
embracing two tiers of lineages: an upper tier comprising the Chishti clan, and a lower tier
comprising a number of subordinate lineages linked religiously to the shrine as murids, and
economically to the Chishti clan as tenant farmers**" (p. 248). **He limits the claim
geographically himself:** "Away from the immediate area around Pakpattan, this connection was
**probably** religious only, and not economic, since the dominant Chishti clan, many of whose
members were landholders, were traditionally clustered around the town of Pakpattan"
(pp. 248–249, n. 39). The "probably" is his.

**And the hierarchy is social, not only spiritual** — the claim most load-bearing for a welfare
argument, and it points the other way: "it is evident from the documents of the Court's
proceedings that the shrine had the objective function of establishing and maintaining not only a
spiritual hierarchy, but also a social hierarchy as between the Diwan and his innermost family,
the Chishti clan, and the many agricultural clans who as murids constituted the shrine's lay
affiliates and traditional supporters. But these hierarchies were maintained not just by the fact
of birth-ascribed kin groups or the marital policies of elite groups at the shrine. **A series of
elaborate initiatory and commemorative rituals had the effect of reinforcing existing hierarchal
arrangements**, while at the same time providing expressive meaning and cognitive order in the
religious world of the shrine's devotees" (p. 250).

**A shrine's rank is measured by the shrines it outranks.** "The spiritual potency, or baraka, of
Baba Farid's shrine thus flowed out over the Punjab, infusing a series of subordinate shrines with
sanctity" (p. 242), reproduced by descent-founded daughter shrines and, in one case, by a land
grant (p. 242). **This is the list that carries the Golra, Taunsa and Uch Sharif names — see
Minor archive mentions for the collision warnings attached to it.**

**Two comparative structures the chunk workers flagged, which are not Eaton's own comparisons.**

- Jurisdiction transferred by discipleship rather than descent: *wilayat* over Daulatabad "and by
  implication all Deccan territories under the city's jurisdiction" passed from Burhan al-Din to
  Zain al-Din on the former's death in 1337 (p. 181).
- A succession rule forcing constant fission, and the mechanism by which it collapsed: Sema
  chiefs had "constantly to transfer retainers to his kin, who would then move away and colonize
  new villages or conquer old ones, themselves becoming chiefs in the process"; Hutton — "'**The
  authority of a Sema chief is quickly sapped when he can no longer shed off his brothers and
  sons to found new villages** with retainers of their own. In such circumstances he ceases in a
  generation to be socially distinct from others of his family, and a decline in his political
  authority follows as a matter of course'" (p. 79), the outlet closed by the British ban on
  warfare (pp. 79–80). The chunk worker offered this as a structural parallel for the fission of
  *sajjada-nashin* lineages; **Eaton makes no such comparison.**

### 5. Conversion and acculturation: Eaton's rejection of "conversion", and "seepage"

**He refuses both nouns, and gives the reason.** "this process should not be construed as
'conversion' to Islam, nor should the Sufis themselves be considered as Muslim 'missionaries,'
though both terms have frequently been used" — because both "carry connotations of a nineteenth-
and twentieth-century Christian movement in India, a context in which 'missionary' denoted a
self-conscious propagator of the Christian faith and 'conversion' a self-conscious turning around
in religious conviction. **But the evidence concerning Bijapur's Sufis would not permit calling
any of them missionaries in this sense. They made no conscious effort to gain non-Muslim
followers**" (p. 211).

**Gradual and uneven, with the evidence.** "'conversion' in the sense of a self-conscious, sudden,
and total change of belief is an inadequate term"; against the claims of several Bijapur District
Muslim groups to have been converted by a medieval Sufi, "ethnographic evidence indicates that
these same groups, far from having suddenly 'converted' to Islam at any single point in time, have
been and still are undergoing **a gradual process of Islamic acculturation—reflected in dress,
food, speech, etc.—which is not only gradual but uneven from one group to another**" (p. 211). The
evidence is the 1884 *Bombay Gazetteer*'s twenty-one Muslim "castes" of Hindu origin, graded by
"purity of Urdu speech, practice of circumcision, diet, attachment to Hindu deities and festivals"
(p. 211, n. 34). His final formulation there: "what they left behind them, namely, their folk
literature and their tombs, have deepened and continued **an on-going process of Islamic
acculturation** among nonelite groups in Deccan society" (p. 211).

**"Seepage" — the formulation, in full.** Islam was not perceived "as alien, or as a closed,
exclusive system to be accepted or rejected as a whole"; the frontier was "a fluid context in
which Islamic superhuman agencies, typically identified with local superhuman agencies, **gradually
seeped into local cosmologies that were themselves dynamic. This 'seepage' occurred over such a
long period of time that one can at no point identify a specific moment of 'conversion,' or any
single moment when peoples saw themselves as having made a dramatic break with the past**"
(p. 286). He flags the anachronism explicitly: "Although today one habitually thinks of world
religions as self-contained and complete systems with well-defined borders, such a static or fixed
understanding does not apply to Bengal's premodern frontier" (p. 286).

**What replaces "conversion" in Bengal.** "In the context of premodern Bengal, then, it would seem
inappropriate to speak of the 'conversion' of 'Hindus' to Islam. What one finds, rather, is an
expanding agrarian civilization, whose cultural counterpart was the growth of the cult of Allah"
(p. 287), composed of three named processes: "(a) the eastward movement and settlement of
colonizers from points west, (b) the incorporation of frontier tribal peoples into the expanding
agrarian civilization, and (c) the natural population growth that accompanied the diffusion or the
intensification of wet rice agriculture" (p. 287). And the model of the religion: not "a monolithic
essence that simply 'expanded' across space, time, and social class" (p. 285) but a succession of
"dominant carriers, spokesmen, or representatives" — thirteenth-century Turkish conquerors'
ruling ethos sustained in cities "especially by Sufi shaikhs of the Chishti order"; then the
Mughal-era *ashraf*, "administrators, soldiers, mystics, scholars, or long-distance merchants",
for whom Persian art and literature mediated a piety "which most of them subordinated to the
secular ethos of Mughal imperialism"; then, by the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, "the
dominant carriers of Islamic civilization in the delta were no longer the urban ashraf, but
peasant cultivators of the eastern frontier" (p. 285).

**The vocabulary rejected as analysis** — a general methodological position, not a Bengal-specific
one, and the most transferable line in the book for an archive's labelling practice: "it becomes
unproductive, or simply wrong, to speak of one group's understanding of Islam as 'orthodox' and
another's as 'unorthodox,' or of one variant as 'fundamentalist' and another as 'syncretic,' or
whatever. Such rhetorical labels may help in identifying and sorting out competing social classes
in a given historical situation, or in determining who is on whose side in a particular debate.
**But as analytical tools they are quite useless**" (p. 285, and in nearly the same words at
p. 14). Likewise "spread" and "expansion" — "as though South Asia were an inert soil that only
passively received the active seed of a religion imported from elsewhere" (p. 14); and the molasses
image: not "a substance, like molasses or lava, flowing outward from some central point, engulfing
and incorporating all that it passes over while itself remaining unchanged" (p. 58). **But note
the limit he puts on the anti-syncretism point:** of the *Nabi-Bamsa*, "it would be wrong to
characterize the work as merely 'syncretic'; on fundamental points of theology, the poet clearly
drew on Judeo-Islamic and not on Indic thought" (p. 282) — the rejection of "syncretic" cuts
against the label, not in favour of blending.

**"Creative adaptation", and whose phrase it is.** "we should adopt the perspective of the society
actually undergoing change and see conversion not as passive acceptance of a monolithic, outside
essence, but as '**creative adaptation**' of the unfamiliar to what is already familiar, a process
in which the former may change to suit the latter" (p. 58). **The phrase is credited to John
R. W. Smail** (p. 58, quoting *Journal of Southeast Asian History* 2/2, July 1961, p. 91), and the
question Eaton draws from it is his own: "who, in the meeting of two cultures, is actually changing
whom" (p. 58). The standpoint shift, stated as method: "instead of adopting the perspective of one
standing in Mecca… **one adopts the perspective of someone standing in a remote and dusty
village**" (p. 47); and the restatement — "at the folk level millions of Indians were converting to
Islam, **or more precisely, assimilating Islamic rituals, cosmologies, and literatures into their
local religious systems**" (p. 48).

**What it correlated with: the plough, not the army.** "the growth of sedentary agriculture in
lightly Hinduized regions of India will tell us more about conversion than will the movement of
medieval armies" (p. 48), with the negative control stated too: "in India's heartland, where both
agriculture and a sedentary Hindu society were well established, conversion to Islam was far less
significant" (p. 49). The regional division of agency is insisted on: "in Punjab/Sind the agents
are shrines; in Bengal, mosque-building agrarian pioneers; in interior Java, roaming saints; in
Malabar and the Malay world, trade diasporas" (p. 53).

**The Punjab mechanism: the agent was the institution, not the man.** "If one were to hypothesize
that the agent of the clans' conversion, instead of Baba Farid himself, was the shrine of Baba
Farid as a highly complex religious and social institution, a number of problems fade" (p. 228) —
offered as a **hypothesis**, and with the clans' own claims salvaged rather than discarded: "we
need not, because of these hagiographic and chronological problems, dismiss outright the claims of
the clans" (p. 228). Why the clans nonetheless name the saint: because shrine and saint were fully
identified, "it would hardly be surprising that the clans, in reconstructing the story of their own
conversion to Islam, should recall the name of the saint himself and not that of any particular
diwan" (p. 228). Resolved in chunk 017: "as is clear from these depositions, saint and shrine are
conceptually fused in terms of religious function, **no real contradiction exists. As the tribes
were converted at some point in medieval history by the agency of the shrine, so also were they
converted by Baba Farid**" (p. 241).

**The order of causation is explicit, and it is the economics reader's sentence:** "throughout the
period when Jat groups moved up the riverine region and on to the barr country, there were several
**nonreligious** ways in which the shrine patronized the clans and thus integrated them into its
wide orbit of social and political influence, **paving the way for** the Jats' gradual integration
into its ritual and religious structure" (p. 228). The four ties are documented in sequence:
economic (pp. 229–230), political and military (p. 231), kinship (p. 232), and then ritual
(pp. 232–235) — "Paralleling these economic, political, and kinship ties between the shrine and
its neighboring clans, the latter gradually became integrated into the shrine's ritual
functionings" (p. 232).

**Quantified, and hedged.** Conversion "was remarkably slow" and never "en bloc" (pp. 232–233),
measured by Bulliet's index — "the frequency with which Muslim given names were bestowed on
males", justified by Bulliet's own reasoning that naming "is an act that reflects, usually
unconsciously, their view of the society around them at that particular point in time" (p. 233,
n. 63, citing Bulliet in Levtzion, ed., *Conversion to Islam*, New York, 1979, 43). The method:
"the fourteen genealogical charts of prominent Siyal families given in the *Tarikh-i Jhang Siyal*.
These charts record twenty generations of leading Siyals from Ray Siyal to the time of the book's
composition in 1862", with dates estimated "by using the rule-of-thumb of three generations per
century", anchored on two known chiefs' dates (p. 233). The result: Muslim names appear only "in
the early fifteenth century", rise "from 10.24 per cent of the total to 39.21 per cent" by the
early seventeenth century, reach parity "about the middle of that century", become "clearly
preponderant (81.81 per cent)" in the early eighteenth, and Punjabi secular names disappear
entirely by the early nineteenth (pp. 233–234). Span: "from the sixth to the nineteenth
generation, or from the late fourteenth to the early nineteenth centuries" (p. 234).

**His own conditional on it:** "**If** these data on the Siyals are at all indicative of the
conversion pattern for the other clans that had taken bai'at with Baba Farid's shrine, or for that
matter with any other shrine, then we may conclude that religious conversion among Punjabi clans
was very slow indeed—not only slow, but **probably** unconscious as well" (p. 234). Why
unconsciousness is plausible is an argument about categories, not belief: "This was, after all, a
period long before either British census officials or zealous reformers began urging Indians to
place themselves into sharply differentiated religious categories" (p. 234). **Table 9.2 is badly
damaged in the text layer — see §8 before citing any cell.**

**Not conversion from Hinduism, but conversion instead of Sanskritization.** "This process,
however, did not involve for newly incorporated groups a change from a Hindu to a Muslim identity,
for at the time of the clans' first contact with Baba Farid's shrine these groups had not yet
become integrated into the Hindu ritual or social structure" (p. 235). The positive mechanism is
put in status terms, with his hedges intact: "Although the precise nature of the Jats' pre-Muslim
religion is **as yet unclear**, they **seem to** have had a deep-rooted tradition of social
egalitarianism. Hence their rise in status from low Sudras to agrarian zamindars could more easily
find ideological/ritual expression in Islam than within the highly stratified Hindu social system
via the process of 'Sanskritization'" (p. 235). **The Sikh parallel is attributed to Irfan Habib,
not claimed by Eaton** (p. 235, n. 67).

**The clans' claims against the sources — keep both.** Table 9.1, "Some Clans Claiming to Have
Been Converted by Baba Farid" (p. 227), lists fourteen clans each with the book's own source:
Bhatti, Dhudhi, Hans, Khokhar (*Jawahir-i Faridi*, 323); Chhina, Gondal ×2, Jo'iya, Siyal, Watt
(Rose, *Glossary*); Dogar (*Rauaij-i 'Amm*, 1860s); Tiwana (Sir 'Umar Hayat Khan); Kharral and
Ara'in (oral tradition). **It is a claim list, not Eaton's attribution.** Against the claims he
sets two arguments: "as Mohammad Habib notes, converting non-Muslims was not a function of the
early shaikhs of the Chishti order, Baba Farid included; and **the earliest primary sources on Baba
Farid make no mention of his having converted anyone**" (p. 227, n. 42, citing *Islamic Culture*,
April 1946, 140); and a chronological objection, "it is **probable** that many if not most of
these clans, in the course of their northward migrations up the rivers of the Punjab, had not yet
reached the Ajudhan area during Baba Farid's lifetime" (p. 227). Separately, Baba Farid and
Bahauddin Zakariya are named as the two saints to whom "the vast majority" of medieval Jat and
Rajput Muslim converts credit their conversion (p. 227).

**The material substrate of the Punjab story.** The *barr* country "though possessing excellent
natural soils for agriculture, was but little cultivated owing to the very scanty rainfall",
supporting "a type of pastoral nomadism based primarily on the herding of goats and camels"
(p. 223, n. 29, citing "chapter 1 of Emily Hodges' dissertation, in progress"), with short-range
movement of "less than one hundred miles" (p. 223, n. 30). **The hinge of the conversion
argument** is a dependency: pastoralists "needed access to the rivers for their herds… but they
were nevertheless dependent upon the agrarian-based urban centres for trade. Moreover… **they were
also dependent upon the riverine peoples for providing the rituals and belief structures that made
up their religious system**" (p. 223). The Jat sequence is argued from dated sources — Jats moving
from Sind into the Multan area between the seventh and eleventh centuries (p. 224), Hsuan Tsang's
Sindi cattle-keepers who "'have no masters'" (p. 224, n. 31), the eighth-century *Chach-nama* on
"an absence among them of social hierarchy" (p. 224, n. 32), al-Biruni's "'cattle-owners, low Sudra
people'" (p. 224, n. 34), the 1260s first mention in the Punjab proper, Babur's 1519 and 1525
descriptions (p. 225) — from which **Eaton concludes** "these Jat pastoralists, before their entry
into the Punjab, had not yet been integrated into Hindu society" (p. 224) and that by Akbar's time
"the Jats had clearly risen in social position, having become the dominant agrarian caste in nearly
half of the Punjab" (p. 225). The measurement: of 186 Punjabi parganas in the *Ain-i Akbari*'s
Lahore and Multan *subas* "whose dominant zamindars are known, fully 82, or nearly half, were
controlled by Jat groups" (p. 225, nn. 38–39, using Jat-equivalent castes added from Ibbetson and
Rose). The technology, credited to Irfan Habib: the Persian wheel, extensively used by Babur's
time "thereby making much of the arid western Punjab's naturally rich soil capable of supporting
the cultivation of food crops" (p. 225, n. 41), with Babur naming the Bet Jullundur and Bari Doabs
and Captain Wade noting the remains of the system down the Sutlej valley in 1832 (pp. 225–226,
n. 41). The parallel general statement: irrigation "permitted a rapid growth in wheat production"
(pp. 48–49).

**Bengal: religion arriving with the plough.** Because rural eastern Bengalis "had not yet been
integrated into a rigidly-structured caste society informed by Brahmanical notions of hierarchy
and order… they were not yet 'Hindu,'" and so "in much of the eastern delta rice agriculture and
Islam were introduced simultaneously and grew together, **both of them focused on these humble
mosques**" (p. 276). The occupational shift the grants produced: "local communities formerly
engaged mainly in hunting, fishing, or shifting agriculture began devoting more time to full-time
wet-rice peasant agriculture" (pp. 275–276). Dated first appearances of the community: Muslim
cultivators "first reported in the Dhaka region in 1599", "in the Noakhali region in the 1630s, and
in the Rangpur region in the 1660s" (p. 269, nn. 24–25) — all in the eastern half, which is
Eaton's clue to the cause (p. 269). The obstacles that made the west different: "Two major
obstacles inhibited the advance of Brahmanical society into the eastern delta: heavy forestation,
and an absence of direct riverine contact with upper India" (p. 270), with archaeology as the
inference base — "Greater urbanization suggests greater occupational specialization and social
stratification" (p. 270), on Morrison's comparative palace areas (p. 270, n. 26) — and
Mukundaram's c. 1590 caste lists as the western terminus: "**No such evidence exists for the
east**" (p. 270). Ecology as prime mover: the Ganges's eastward migration "carried with it the
epicentre of Bengali civilization" (p. 274); and the conjuncture, "the merger of the Ganges with
the Padma occurred at the very moment that the whole of Bengal was absorbed into… the Mughal
Empire under Akbar" (p. 274), whose provincial capital was planted "in the heart of the eastern
delta, Dhaka" (p. 274). The rainfall and river-course evidence is dated and sourced (pp. 270–271,
nn. 28, 30–32), and he keeps the qualifier "Assuming this climatic pattern held in ancient times"
(p. 270).

**Literacy as the durability mechanism, in both wings.** In Bengal: papermaking, "[t]raceable to
the fifteenth century and unmistakably identified with Islamic civilization", "fostered attitudes
that endowed the written word with an authority qualitatively different from oral authority",
spreading a "'culture of literacy'" beyond the bureaucracy and the cities, so that it "endowed the
cult of Allah with a kind of authority—that of the unchangeable written word—that the delta's
preliterate forest cults had theretofore lacked"; hence "In the east, then, Islam came to be
understood as a religion, not only of the axe and the plough, but also of the book" (p. 277), with
the loan words *kagaj* and *kalam* as the linguistic evidence (p. 277). **The same argument is made
at length on Naga evidence, and is transferable:** "Tsungrem, the one deity preached by the
Christians, was seen as possessing a fixed and unalterable status in the cosmos. **His commands and
promises, frozen in time by the power and the technology of the written word, could not easily be
ignored or forgotten**" (pp. 77–78), with J. D. Y. Peel on the Yoruba — in non-literate societies
"'legitimate belief is as a particular priest or elder expounds it. But where the essence of
religion is the Word of God… religion acquires a rigid basis'" (p. 77) — and Clark's 1876 inference
about "the corresponding imperviousness of Hindus, Muslims, and others already exposed to
scriptural authority" (p. 77 and n. 58).

**What was new was the attribution, not the clearing.** "deltaic peoples had been transforming
forested lands to rice fields long before the Mughal age. What was new from at least the sixteenth
century on, however, was that this process had become particularly associated with Muslim holy
men, or perhaps more accurately, **with industrious and capable forest pioneers subsequently
identified as holymen**" (p. 278). And his theory of why those cults endured: "In popular memory,
some of these men swelled into vivid mythico-historical figures, saints whose lives served as
metaphors for the expansion of both religion and agriculture. **They have endured precisely
because, in the collective folk memory, their careers captured and telescoped a complex historical
socioreligious process** whereby a land originally forested and non-Muslim became arable and
predominantly Muslim" (p. 278).

**Negative controls Eaton insists on — all from the Nagaland essay, but stated as method and
directly applicable to any adherent count.**

- Proselytization eliminated as a variable: "there is **no positive correlation between the degree
  of proselytization and the rate of conversion**… What mattered, in the last analysis, was the
  nature of Naga cosmologies and the things that were happening within those cosmologies at the
  time of contact with outsiders" (p. 86); the negative correlation in time and space at p. 70.
- Material inducement disposed of: not "'rice Christians'", and the specific version — the 1905
  tax exemption for converts — fails because potential converts weighed nonpayment "against the
  severe disapprobation of the other villagers on whom they depended for economic, political, and
  social support", and "**the truly large conversion movement did not begin until four decades
  after that exemption was put in place**" (p. 70 and n. 33).
- Relative deprivation: "one finds no correlation between conversion and the sort of severe decline
  in economic standards" (p. 70).
- **The register/census divergence, which is the sharpest caution for an archive that counts
  adherents from an institution's own books:** "Owing to their strict criteria of what constituted
  a convert, **the missionaries' baptismal tabulations always lagged behind the much larger
  Christian 'community'**, which comprised those who, regardless of what the missionaries thought,
  considered themselves Christian and told the Census officials so" (p. 70). Table 2.2 measures it
  for 1971: **Ao 44.07 vs 98.1 per cent; Sema 43.4 vs 77.4; Angami 12.0 vs 40** (p. 61, source
  Puthuvail T. Philip, *The Growth of Baptist Churches in Nagaland*, 2nd ed., 1983, 78, 92, 110)
  — a more than twofold divergence between formal affiliation and self-ascription, in a case Eaton
  calls one of the best documented.
- Reversion is possible: converted West African merchant groups that "returned to a peasant life…
  **reverted to their former pagan cults**" (pp. 52–53), and Mali's rulers reverting in the
  fifteenth century when the Muslim merchants left (p. 53).
- **And "colonialism" refused as an explanatory variable:** Nagas "converted most dramatically
  only after the dismantling of the colonial state and the expulsion of foreign missionaries…
  Therefore, blanket explanations focusing on monolithic abstractions like 'colonialism' or on
  activities of the missionaries alone—as though these were independent, explanatory
  variables—clearly will not do" (p. 60).

**The theories he borrows, and marks as borrowed.** Horton's intellectualist theory of a two-tier
cosmology whose high god gains attention as social contacts widen, so that conversion follows when
Christian or Islamic influences are "'in the air'" and the high god is "accommodated within the
indigenous cosmology" (pp. 61–62, citing Horton 1971, 1975, 1993) — Eaton's gloss is that it
"removes from centre stage the role of religious proselytizers" (p. 62). Spiro's definition of
religion as "an institution consisting of culturally patterned interaction with culturally
postulated superhuman beings", from which he derives the measurement rule: "**changes in the
naming patterns of both humans and superhumans, in addition to self-ascription as reflected in
census data, would appear to be our most reliable indices of religious change**" (p. 58) —
directly transferable to the naming of saints, sites and deities in a shrine archive. Bulliet's
onomastic method for dating conversion from biographical dictionaries (p. 36). Morony's
prior-affinity argument, against conversion by conquest (pp. 36–37).

**And the naming argument turned on the sources themselves:** "contemporary inscriptions never
identified Indo-Muslim invaders in terms of their religion, as Muslims, but most generally in terms
of their linguistic affiliation (most typically as Turk, '*turushka*')" (p. 139, citing Cynthia
Talbot); symmetrically the *Rayavacakamu*'s author "never refers to the rulers of these states as
Muslims, which they were, but as 'Turks'" (p. 165); and, in a footnote easily missed, of the 1354
Harihara/Bukka inscriptions — "these are the earliest known instances in which Indians referred
to themselves as 'Hindu'. At this time, the term seems to have had a geographical and ethnic
meaning—that is, 'a native of India'—and not the religious meaning that it would acquire later"
(p. 176, n. 15). His conclusion from the pair of sixteenth-century texts: "the stereotypes that
emerge in both texts were not of religious communities, but of regional or linguistic groups"
(p. 165).

**Who called Islam foreign, and why that is a historiographical fact.** "the cultivating classes
never seem to have regarded it as 'foreign'—even though some Muslim and Hindu literati and foreign
observers did, and still do" (p. 286), the persistence attributed to Manrique's 1629 categories and
then to "British imperialists and both Hindu and Muslim reformers, each for their own reasons,
stress[ing] Islam's 'foreignness'" (p. 286, n. 58). Compare the Khulna gazetteer on the Muslim
masses: they "'do not, however, know or admit that they are the descendants of converts to Islam;
according to them they are the tillers of the soil, while the Ashraf do not cultivate the land with
their own hands'" (p. 284) — occupational identity in place of convert identity.

**The end point he claims:** "by the eighteenth century Islam in this part of the world had in many
respects become **as thoroughly Indian as any other religion of South Asia**" (p. 17). And the
volume's last sentence of running prose, which is an evaluative judgement and should be cited as
one: "It is testimony to the vitality of Islam—and one of the clues to its success as a world
religion—that its adherents in Bengal were so creative in accommodating local socio-cultural
realities with the norms of the religion" (p. 287).

### 6. The outer and inner circles, vows, and what devotees came for

**The two-tier membership structure, named.** "All available evidence indicates that in the
seventeenth century a sizable nonelite constituency clustered around famous pirs, believing in
their miraculous powers (karamat) and their ability to intercede with God, taking blessings from
them, **lighting candles at the dargahs, or tombs, of departed pirs, and participating in festivals
at the dargahs. This was the outer circle of a pir's following, as distinguished from his inner
circle of murids, or initiates**, and it was to this outer circle that the folk literature seems
especially to have appealed, serving as the litany of what may be called Indian folk Islam"
(p. 209). [The chunk records the text layer printing "karimat" for *karamat*.]

**The offering, itemised, and what it buys.** Devotees' motivation "seems to have been primarily
votive in nature. That is, **flowers, coins, or prayers would be offered up to the spirit of the pir
buried at a particular dargah in the belief that the latter would redress some specific grievance
or provide some specific fortune that had become associated with that dargah**" (p. 209).

**Vows are site-specific — but the formulation is Bharati's, not Eaton's.** Eaton endorses this as
"very much in keeping with indigenous traditions with respect to shrines and pilgrimages" and
quotes Agehananda Bharati: "*vrata*, 'vows' are often highly specific and they usually require a
visit to one place only. **It is the place where a deity specializes, as it were, in repairing
damage, or balancing some need of the pilgrim who seeks remedy.**" The vow's form: "'If I gain x or
overcome y or accomplish z, I shall make a pilgrimage to A'; or, 'Because I have not gained x,
etc., I shall make a pilgrimage to A in order to gain it, for A is known to specialize in granting
x'" (pp. 209–210, n. 32, citing Bharati, 'Pilgrimage Sites and Indian Civilization', in *Chapters
in Indian Civilization*, ed. Elder, 1970, 1:94). **This is Bharati on Hindu, Buddhist and Jain
pilgrimage, which Eaton imports; the "specializes" language is his source's.**

**What the specialisation converged on, and who came.** "But whatever the special vows that became
associated with individual dargahs, they were all generally associated with fertility. Indeed, the
belief that visits to dargahs would in some measure enhance a woman's fertility is an obvious
reason for their continuing popularity among rural women of the Deccan today" (p. 210). And: "as
the Sufis became replaced by their tombs as objects of popular veneration, **women came to comprise
the great majority of devotees at any given dargah in the Deccan**" (p. 209). **Eaton marks the
next step as speculation and it should be quoted with the marking:** "One could speculate that
nonelite women living on the fringes of Hindu society would have gravitated towards Sufis and their
tombs as **places of religious refuge**… These women would certainly have included **widows of most
castes, for whom organized Hindu society has little room**… barren women of various castes"
(p. 210). Women in the inner circle rest on one manuscript: a seventeenth-century *malfuzat* "noted
that women were allowed to enter even the inner circle of a Sufi's followers. This **would mean**
that at one time women, along with men, were instructed in the religious exercises of living
Sufis" (p. 209, n. 31, citing *Maqsud al-murad*, MS, Asafiyah Library, Tasawwuf no. 335, 129) —
one manuscript, and the inference is Eaton's.

**No numbers here.** "No visitor numbers or receipts are given anywhere in the chunk" for the
Deccan dargahs (not stated).

**What devotees came to Pakpattan for.** The burial place "frequently evolved into a great centre
of pilgrimage for persons seeking divine aid in their **personal, matrimonial, or business
affairs**" (p. 216). In a colonial witness's words, with Eaton's own discount attached: Miles
Irving, 1911 — "To the ordinary Montgomery cattle-thief who comes once a year to Pakpattan to
obtain remission for the enormities of the past twelve months, Baba Farid is the mediator by whose
merits he obtains forgiveness, assurance of which he obtains through the presence in the flesh of
the descendant of the saint" (p. 235); Eaton: "It was, of course, Irving's imperial-administrative
viewpoint that caused him to judge most clans of the barr country as 'cattle-thieves' and their
actions as 'enormities'" (p. 235, n. 66). **The substantive data inside the quotation are an annual
visit and forgiveness mediated by a living descendant.** Another colonial source used the same way:
Major Aubrey O'Brien, 1911 — "The general idea of our riverain folk seems to be that the Deity is a
busy person, and that his hall of audience is of limited capacity… Thus, all human beings require
an intervener between them and God" (p. 216, n., *JRAI* 41 (1911):511). **Eaton's assertion that
"[t]he remarks of a British officer concerning the mediating role of Punjabi saints in 1911 are
equally true of medieval shrines" is his, not the officer's.**

**What they took themselves to be.** "murids of Baba Farid's shrine **probably** saw themselves
less in terms of adherents of the Book and more in terms of **clients and sponsors** of a
theater-shrine that displayed the wondrous baraka of its saint through its pageantry, festivals,
and ceremonies" (p. 235) — the answer to the question the essay opens with at p. 215. And they
described themselves collectively: "the murids almost without exception identified themselves not
as individuals but in terms of a collective group, be that their kin group (baradari) or the
collective mass of murids" (p. 248), the varieties running from "we Jat people" to "All the
Muhammadans are the Murids of Baba Farid" (p. 248, nn. 35–37).

**Entry to the inner circle: *bai'at*.** "Belonging to a kin group that at some distant point in
time had been converted to Islam by Baba Farid did not automatically make one a murid of the
shrine. One still had to participate in a formal initiation ceremony during which one made a solemn
oath, known as bai'at, swearing spiritual allegiance to Baba Farid and his spiritual descendants,
including of course the incumbent Diwan" (p. 251), once in a lifetime per one devotee (p. 251,
n. 45). Recorded ages at initiation: "one was age twenty-eight, one eighteen, one fourteen or
fifteen, one ten or twelve, and one said simply that he was a young child" (p. 251, n. 46).
Elements: hand placed in the Diwan's hand, *nazrana*, spiritual instruction, and a *wazifa* — "a
prescribed liturgical formula, often a verse from the Qur'an" (p. 251). Three murids "reported
having their forelocks (jat) clipped by the Diwan as part of their bai'at ceremony" (p. 251,
n. 48); the Hindu-Buddhist derivation Eaton proposes for the clipping is his own inference (p. 251,
and §4 above).

**The calendar, and the one rite the whole institution turns on.** The shrine "displayed throughout
the ceremonies and celebrations that marked its liturgical calendar the pageantry of both the court
of God and the court of Delhi" (p. 228) — **no individual festival dates are given in that chunk**
(not stated). The 'urs was annual on the death date, recorded from the fiftieth anniversary, the
1315 celebration described with pilgrimage, recitation of the saint's deeds and dervish
entertainments (p. 219); *qawwali* — "the regularizing of ecstatic singing" — dated to Badr
ad-Din's day (p. 219). Eaton glosses 'urs as "'marriage with God'", so that it "commemorates not
the saint's death so much as his everlasting union with God, and hence his spiritual survival for
purpose of passing on his grace, or baraka, to his devotees" (p. 253). The date: "**Called the
bihishti darwaza, or 'Gate of heaven,' the south door of Baba Farid's tomb remained closed on all
days of the year until the date of Baba Farid's 'urs, the fifth of Muharram**" (p. 253); by 1938
the anniversary "was celebrated in a vast pageant known… as the Bihishti mela" (p. 253). The rite:
opened by the Diwan in person, once; devotees file past the tomb all night; it "reenact[s] the
ascension of their patron saint to heaven" (p. 254). Physical constraint, from an 1833 European
account: the doorway "about two feet wide", the chamber "not capable of containing thirty people
crowded together", passage single-file (pp. 254–255).

**The only crowd figures in the book.** "between fifty and sixty thousand" murids following the
Diwan through the gate (p. 254, n. 56, **from the 1884 Montgomery Gazetteer**, not Eaton's count);
and "The crowd of pilgrims is said to be immense" (1833, p. 254). **No gate receipts, offering
totals, mela revenue or attendance figures are given anywhere** (not stated).

**The four-tier access hierarchy at the gate, with counts** (p. 254): Diwan → companions 100–125 →
pass-holders 400–500 → *darveshes of the Hujra* 1,000–1,200 → the plaintiff's party →
*sajjadanashins* of other institutions → the masses. **Eaton's inferences from the ritual's form,
marked as such:** "because only the Diwan opened the gate, **it was only through his agency that
devotees gained access to Baba Farid, and only through the saint's agency that they gained access
to Heaven**" (p. 254); "it **seems** that in the minds of the murids, a fixed order of precedence
existed with respect to entering the 'Gate of Heaven,' **despite the rush mentioned by the European
visitor in 1833**" (p. 254) — his evidence is the 1938 deposition, not any observation of the rite,
and the 1833 account contradicts him on the point. Keep both. His reading of the whole: "the
Bihishti ceremony served to act out and confirm the sense of spiritual hierarchy that pervades the
whole ethos of the shrine", with the physical constraint folded in — "The Gate of Heaven, after
all, is a narrow one, and can be entered only one by one, in single file" (pp. 254–255).

**Hindu participation, doubly hedged.** Hindu civic leaders and wealthy merchants attended the
*dastar-bandi* and offered *nazrana* (p. 253, figures in §2). Eaton: "As Hindus, these gentlemen
were **probably** not offering nazrana out of expectations consonant with the Muslim-oriented
conceptual structure of the shrine, but out of respect for a local institution of great repute, and
perhaps also out of a sense of civic responsibility… Nonetheless, the presence of Hindus
participating in this event **suggests** how, in the past, generations of people of various castes
and creeds had become integrated into the public aspects of the shrine's activities" (p. 253),
which he calls "one of its oldest and historically most significant roles" (p. 253, n. 55). Both
hedges are his.

**Vow-driven pilgrimage elsewhere, and the relic economy.** Mirza Nathan "made a three-day
pilgrimage to the shrine, **having vowed to do so should his father recover from an illness**"
(pp. 266–267, n. 15), and another on the occasion of his marriage (p. 267, n. 16). The Qadam Rasul
footprint "had been purchased and brought from Arabia by one of the sultans 'so that the people of
Bengal and everybody else, who were destined to come there, might attain eternal blessing by
kissing the holy footprint'" (p. 266, n. 13). At Pakpattan, the 1884 gazetteer reports of the
*chapati* legend that "The truth of the story is vouched for by the preservation of the identical
cakes and dates to this very day. They are kept at his shrine…" (p. 240, n. 7, citing *Punjab
District Gazetteers. Montgomery District*, Lahore, 1884, 184) — **the gazetteer's report of 1884
practice, not Eaton's observation.**

**The producer's incentive — the closest thing to an explicit supply-side claim in the book.** "One
important reason that Sufis wrote the literature, apart from their general desire to expand their
teachings among a constituency of commoners, was **the object of securing for themselves the role
of mediator between God and the people who used the literature**" (p. 209), the textual proof being
the poem's own closing line binding the reciter to a dervish's household (p. 209, n. 30). Restated:
"The folk literature examined in this essay **aimed primarily at committing its readers to a pir**;
the diffusion of Islamic precepts seems in the final analysis to have been a by-product of this
effort" (p. 211). The devotional labour is even made countable and payable: "The threads of breath
should be counted one by one, oh sister. / Up to twenty-four thousand. / Do this day and night, /
**And offer this to your pir as a gift**" (p. 208, n. 29). And the household transmission channel,
hedged twice: "it **seems likely** that the women who had come into contact with the culture of the
dargahs transmitted this tradition to the children living in their households by constantly
repeating the poetry… **the mother—or indeed any household woman in the proximity of children—has
doubtless been instrumental in the transmission of religious practices and attitudes at rural
levels**" (p. 210). The eclecticism that made the boundary crossable is also his speculation: "What
all such women **probably** shared in common was an eclectic religious attitude on account of
which they would have perceived no great theological or social wall existing between Islam and
Hinduism. For them the village dargah formed only one more facet of an already diffuse and eclectic
religious life" (p. 210), supported by Trimingham's "'holy-man Islam'" formulation, which is
Trimingham's (p. 210, n. 33).

**The saint as advocate, and the limit Eaton sets.** "**Not that Baba Farid himself was confused
with God. This would of course have been blasphemous idolatry, as was fully understood by one old
cultivator**" (p. 257) — the devotee's own orthodoxy is his evidence. The saint is instead "a
'friend in court,' as it were, who represented his interests there. This 'friend in court,' this
special pleader, was Baba Farid" (p. 257).

**The mediation thesis that frames all of it.** Moral authority in Islam is textual — "in Islam God
revealed Himself not in any historical personage as in Christianity, but in a Book" (p. 215) — and
the problem he sets is "But what could nonliterate, non-Arabic-speaking villagers or pastoralists
make of such a religion?" (pp. 215–216). Gellner's Berber answer ("'Islam is what they do. They are
Islam'") is quoted and **not adopted wholesale**: "Another way in which the Book was conveyed to
such peoples was through the vast shrines built over the tombs of saints" (pp. 215–216). Hence the
governing image: "In India these shrines displayed, theatre-style and in microcosm, the moral order
of the Islamic macrocosm" (p. 216), and of the reformists, "**Theatre, in a word, was to be
replaced by Scripture**" (p. 216). The Geertz borrowing: "the shrine provided the tribes with a
tiny 'theatre-state' of their own" (p. 228, and p. 250, n. 44, citing *Islam Observed*, 1971, 38,
and *Negara*, 1980, "especially chapter four") — and note the phrase "**client clans**": the
relation is named as clientage (p. 250). The closing thesis: "the shrine of Baba Farid in Pakpattan
provides a striking example of how Islam, the religion par excellence 'of the Book,' has been
mediated among common villagers most of whom were illiterate. For them it was the shrine, and less
so the Book, which manifested the juncture 'where the contrasted poles of Heaven and Earth met'"
(p. 257 — the quoted phrase is Peter Brown's, from p. 237, and carries no visible source at
p. 257).

**The function, as a formula.** "In both the Sultanate and Mughal periods a long tradition of
economic, political, and social patronage by the diwans had absorbed into the shrine's orbit of
ritual influence groups which, as former pastoralists who had only recently achieved a settled way
of life, had not formerly been integrated into anything approaching urban culture. **It was the
shrine's historical function to incorporate local systems of culture into a larger cultural system,
to connect rustic clans politically with Delhi and religiously with Islam**" (p. 235); and "Shrines
like that of Baba Farid made a universal culture system available to local groups, enabling such
groups to transcend their local microcosms" (p. 236). With the qualification he attaches himself:
"Although I have argued that the shrine of Baba Farid integrated local systems of culture into a
larger one, the shrine nonetheless remained a local manifestation of that larger culture"
(pp. 235–236). The *adab* of the shrine is set beside the *adab* of the court (p. 236).

**Territory: *wilayat* mapped onto administration.** The memorial shrines show "that the baraka and
authority of Baba Farid became physically established over the land in much the same way that
political/administrative authority was", and that a tract of the Punjab "had become identified with
Baba Farid's wilayat, or spiritual kingdom, which to his devotees was perceived as having specific
geographic boundaries that bordered the wilayats of other saints" (p. 222) — the Zakariya frontier
passage being the demonstration (see Minor archive mentions). Who built what matters to the
argument: **the state built the Ajudhan monuments, the people built the village shrines** (p. 222);
devotee-built "structures and tombs in the name of his shrine" stood in the countryside around
Ajudhan, Dipalpur and "the hills toward Kashmir", visited "for alms and devotions" (p. 222) — no
count is given (not stated). The Deccan version of the same idea is the sharpest statement of it as
an economic mechanism: inclusion in a *wilayat* "meant that such territory could not be viewed—as
the Deccan formerly had been—as **land ready for plunder, like a ripe plum**… Sufi shaikhs… could
transform yesterday's Abode of War, *Dar al-Harb*, into today's Abode of Peace, or *Dar al-Islam*"
(p. 183). **The chunk worker's flag on this belongs with the claim: it is an institutional claim
about extractive incentives — a saint's jurisdiction reclassifying land from plunderable to
governable — and it is argued from chronicle and hagiography, not from revenue data.** Eaton also
states the practical limit: "In practice, however, the domain of a Sufi's jurisdiction, or
*wilayat*, was just as limited as the territorial reach of his royal patron's worldly domain
(*hukumat*), if not more so" (p. 183, and p. 187).

### 7. Source criticism Rauf can apply to the archive's own material

Not a mechanism, but the book's most directly transferable methodological content, and it bears on
every shrine *tazkira* and survey deposition in the archive.

- **A shrine inscription is not a newspaper.** Against Sita Ram Goel, who "accepts the inscription's
  reference to temple destruction more or less at face value, as though it were a contemporary
  newspaper account reporting an objective fact": "the text is concerned not with documenting an
  instance of temple destruction, but with narrating and celebrating the fabulous career of
  'Abdullah Shah Changal, the saint who lies buried at the site of the tomb" (p. 108). The dating
  argument: "Although Indo-Muslim epigraphs are typically recorded near in time to the events they
  describe, **the present one is hardly contemporary, as it was composed some four hundred years
  after the events to which it refers**" (p. 109). The decisive move is a symmetry argument:
  "Whether or not any temple was actually destroyed four hundred years before this narrative was
  committed to writing, we cannot know with certainty. However, **it would seem no more likely that
  such a desecration had actually occurred than that the renowned Raja Bhoja had been converted to
  Islam, which the text also claims**" (p. 110). The conclusion is double: caution in interpreting
  literary claims, and the recognition that such a claim is **evidence about memory, not about
  masonry** (p. 110).
- **Both directions of error in a record.** "Undoubtedly some temples were desecrated but the facts
  in the matter were never recorded, or the facts were recorded but the records themselves no
  longer survive. Conversely, later Indo-Muslim chroniclers, seeking to glorify the religious zeal
  of earlier Muslim rulers, sometimes attributed acts of temple desecration to those rulers even
  when no contemporary evidence supports the claims" (p. 119). And the sharper case: "**Even
  contemporary sources could make false claims**" — a mosque inscription at Bidar dated 1670 claims
  a temple was destroyed and the mosque built on its site, but "'as a matter of fact,' noted the
  epigraphist who published the inscription, '**the mosque is a new construction, and the rock does
  not seem to have been disturbed, for it still survives**'" (pp. 119–120, n. 34).
- **Why he distrusts chronicles, in words that apply to shrine *tazkiras*:** such texts "were
  frequently formal works written by Muslim chroniclers—many of them **in the pay of political
  leaders**—who were self-consciously writing about their own present or recent past with a view to
  posterity. Hence the texts such authors produced were deliberate constructions or
  reconstructions of people or events, carrying with them the same risks of bias, judgment,
  perspective, or interest" (p. 24). Worked on the Deccan chroniclers: Tabataba, Shirazi and
  Firishta "were all high-born Iranian immigrants who tended to adopt a colonialist view towards
  non-Muslim Indian society", "routinely stigmatized the people of Vijayanagara as 'infidels'", and
  "**were hired to chronicle their patrons' grand deeds**" (pp. 186–187). And worked out loud on a
  single chronicler: Firishta's account of Firuzabad's purpose is rejected against the city's own
  plan, walls and mosque size, because "We know that Firishta was often given to exaggeration"
  (pp. 189–190, n. 3).
- **Rhetoric versus practice, stated as the methodological conclusion:** "Much misunderstanding…
  results from **a failure to distinguish the rhetoric from the practice** of Indo-Muslim
  state-formation. Whereas the former tends to be normative, conservative, and rigidly ideological,
  the latter tends to be pragmatic, eclectic, and non-ideological" (p. 136). His stated method: "to
  identify the different meanings that contemporary actors invested in the public monuments they
  patronized or desecrated, and **to reconstruct those meanings on the basis of the practice, and
  not just the rhetoric, of those actors**" (p. 140).
- **What internal religious sources systematically omit.** "No Indo-Muslim chronicle or contemporary
  inscription associates the use of Ganges water with the establishment or maintenance of
  Indo-Muslim states. **We hear this only from foreign visitors**: an Arab traveller in the case of
  Muhammad bin Tughluq, a Portuguese friar in the case of the sultans of Bengal", and the Jagannath
  car-festival image "comes to us not from Mughal chronicles but from an English traveller who
  happened to be in Puri in 1633" (pp. 137–138). The same holds on the Hindu side at Vijayanagara:
  "**As with Indo-Muslim states, we hear of such practices mainly from outsiders—merchants,
  diplomats, travellers—and not from Brahman chroniclers and ideologues**" (p. 138). **A general
  warning squarely applicable to shrine tazkiras and survey testimony.**
- **The provenance warning on Elliot and Dowson**, which any citation of a temple-desecration report
  through that channel should carry: Elliot, "keen to contrast what he understood as the justice and
  efficiency of British rule with the cruelty and despotism of the Muslim rulers who had preceded
  that rule, was anything but sympathetic to the 'Muhammadan' period" (pp. 106–107), and hoped the
  translations "'will make our native subjects more sensible of the immense advantages accruing to
  them under the mildness and the equity of our rule'" (p. 107). With Mohammad Habib on the long
  effect (p. 107).
- **Selective reading of a colonial record, caught in the act.** C. A. Bayly, quoted in a footnote:
  "'in a paragraph from the much-quoted CID report on the Kisan Sabha which does not find its way
  into the analysis in *Subaltern Studies* the writer remarks that it is "**only a very foolish
  landlord who cannot build up a party to support him in his own villages**"'" (p. 150, n. 15) —
  the source itself recorded peasant collaboration and the selection dropped it. Directly
  applicable to gazetteer and settlement-report material.
- **Litigation as an accidental oral-history corpus — a model source type for this archive.** "Just
  as the immediate beneficiaries of legal disputes are lawyers, historians are perhaps the ultimate
  beneficiaries" (p. 238): the succession suit forced hundreds of peasant devotees to testify about
  shrine practice, producing "a remarkable sampling of the shrine's local 'constituency',
  documenting how the latter viewed the shrine's relationship to God, to Islamic Law, to the British
  government that ultimately ruled them, and to their own daily lives" (p. 238).
- **The prosopographical programme he endorses**, which the archive's *silsila* and *khalifa* data
  could serve: "**By painstakingly identifying who studied with whom, where, when, and under whose
  (if anybody's) patronage**, the hope is that we may one day be able to make more meaningful
  statements about the process of Islamization" (p. 51, credited to Anthony Johns).
- **The source classes the field opened up**, which reads as a list an archive of shrine provenance
  can use: "commercial documents, **tax registers, official land grants**, administrative seals,
  census records, coins, **gravestones**, magical incantations written on bowls, **memoirs of
  pilgrims**, archaeological and architectural data, biographical dictionaries, inscriptional
  evidence, and most recently, **oral history**" (p. 25).
- **And the defence of fieldwork**, with its sting for anyone theorising from published material:
  "to do Indian history one has to visit the Indian hinterland… developing a 'nose' for new
  materials and imaginatively reconstructing the past through critical engagement with those
  materials", where discourse analysis instead made it "feasible not to go to India or to undertake
  original field research, while providing unlimited opportunities for armchair theorizing"
  (p. 162). The measured symptom: "Of the 118 proposals submitted in the fall of 1996 to the
  American Institute of Indian Studies… all but a handful were crowded into the nineteenth or
  twentieth century" (p. 161, n. 57).
- **One historiographical point that bears on how a Pakistan-based archive frames its own periods:**
  the continuities he finds "call into serious question the sort of civilizational divide between
  India's 'Hindu' and 'Muslim' periods first postulated in British colonial historiography and
  subsequently replicated in both **Pakistani** and Hindu nationalist schools" (pp. 139–140).

### 10. Flags Eaton puts on his own inferences

Where an inference is his and he says so, the marking must survive into any entry. Collected:

- The Pakpattan account rests on 1935–38 data, and the extension forward is asserted, not evidenced:
  "The past tense is used here… because the data on which the discussion is based belong to the
  period 1935–8. **It is fair to assume, however**, that the most general aspects of the shrine as
  described here hold as true for the present day as for the 1930s" (p. 238, unnumbered footnote).
  **And "the present day" means the essay's 1982 original** (p. 237), not the 2000/2003 volume.
- The 1911 officer's remarks on mediation are "equally true of medieval shrines" — Eaton's assertion,
  not the officer's (p. 216, n.).
- The two-tier tenancy system is limited away from Pakpattan "**probably** religious only, and not
  economic" (pp. 248–249, n. 39).
- The Siyal generalisation is conditional — "**If** these data… are at all indicative" and
  "**probably** unconscious" (p. 234).
- The Jats' pre-Muslim religion is "**as yet unclear**" and they "**seem to** have had" an
  egalitarian tradition (p. 235).
- The clans' chronology is "**probable**", not established (p. 227).
- The Hindu derivation of Chishti bride-giving rules and of forelock-clipping is his own inference,
  the second explicitly "**as is more likely the case**", and no source asserts either (pp. 249, 251).
- Hindu *nazrana* at the *dastar-bandi* is read with two hedges, "**probably** not… " and
  "**suggests** how" (p. 253).
- Women in a living Sufi's inner circle rests on one manuscript, and "This **would mean**" is his
  (p. 209, n. 31); the account of who the women devotees were is introduced "One **could
  speculate**" (p. 210); the household transmission channel is "it **seems likely**" (p. 210); the
  devotees' eclecticism is "**probably** shared" (p. 210).
- The Court's 1938 motive is "this formula **would seem** to have provided" (p. 246), and the
  devotees' indifference rests on one murid's sentence, marked "this sentiment **seems to suggest**"
  (p. 258).
- Firuz's turn to new Sufi patrons after 1415 is "It therefore **seems likely**", "This is
  **probably** what happened", "it **seems very likely**" (pp. 195–197).
- The 1461 evacuation yields a negative fact by inference: "This **suggests** that as of 1461
  Firuzabad no longer functioned as a permanent residence" (p. 198).
- The measurability of the folk literature's effect is disclaimed: "impossible to measure with
  precision" (p. 208).
- The moral-economy argument for a shared Islamicate political ideology rests on **an argument from
  silence in Sanskrit** plus a dating claim — "Since no comparable statement can be found in
  classical Sanskrit texts on governance, it would appear that such a conception… became current in
  the subcontinent only after the rise of Indo-Turkish rule" (p. 173) — which the chunk worker
  flagged.
- On the Malay world he concedes the residual gap outright: "Although historians agree on a clear
  correlation between Islamization and expanding trade networks… **the precise links between the two
  still seem to elude their grasp**" (p. 50).
- On Adam as first cultivator: "**may** therefore be a uniquely Bengali variant" — "may", not is
  (p. 283, n. 52).
- The Peter Brown comparison for the Islamic cult of saints is **Eaton's**; Brown writes only about
  Christianity (p. 237).
