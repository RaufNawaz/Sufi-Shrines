# waris_shah_hir_ranjha — chunk 002 — pp. 11–19

Continuous narrative plus the translator's/editor's numbered notes, covering the tail of
**Chapter 7** (pp. 11, folio 10, the Five Pirs' gifts and the buffalo herd) and then
**Chapter 8** "Hir and Ranjha meet in the forest" (p. 11, folio 10), **Chapter 9** "Hir's
mother is angry with her and Kaidu finds her in the forest with Ranjha" (p. 13, folio 12),
**Chapter 10** "Scandal spreads in the village and Chuchak dismisses Ranjha and then recalls
him" (p. 15, folio 14), **Chapter 11** "The Qazi admonishes Hir but she refuses to give up
Ranjha" (p. 16, folio 15) and **Chapter 12** "Ranjha has audience of the Five Pirs and Mithi
discourses on love" (p. 18, folio 17). The chunk ends mid-sentence inside note 7 of Chapter 12.

**Folio, as the pages actually print it.** Every page in this chunk carries a running header of
the form `Waris Shah: The Adventures of Hir and Ranjha 10` (p. 11), and the number in that header
is the printed folio. Measured across all nine pages: p. 11 = 10, p. 12 = 11, p. 13 = 12,
p. 14 = 13, p. 15 = 14, p. 16 = 15, p. 17 = 16, p. 18 = 17, p. 19 = 18. **`folio = PDF − 1`
holds exactly, with no disagreement anywhere in this chunk.**

**Where the archive material is.** The verse is narrative and yields almost nothing; the five
archive mappings below rest on the Five Pirs passage (p. 11, folio 10), on the editor's note 2
to Chapter 7 (p. 11, folio 10), on the editor's note 10 to Chapter 10 (p. 16, folio 15) and on
the editor's note 1 to Chapter 12 (p. 19, folio 18). The last three are the editor's own
identifications, not Waris Shah's.

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **What a Pir is, and what makes one important — the single most archive-usable sentence in the
  chunk, and it is explicitly economic.** Note 1 to Chapter 7: "'Pir' is literally an old man. It
  has come to mean a saint, dead or alive. The importance of a Pir' depends on the number of his
  disciples, or the visitors and offerings to his tomb, as the case may he [text-layer?] [be]. See
  also Invocation, note 2." (p. 11, folio 10). **The editor's own general theory of shrine
  standing: living pirs are ranked by disciples, dead ones by footfall and offerings.**
- **Faqir, darvesh and malang, each defined by the editor as a degraded or mendicant status.**
  Note 5 to Chapter 7: "A 'faqir' is a poor man, a mendicant. It also means a saint. See also
  Introduction, note 24." (p. 11, folio 10). Note 6 to Chapter 9: "'Darvesh originally meant a
  wandering mystic. Later, when the institution deteriorated, the word came to mean a mendicant
  living on charity." (p. 14, folio 13). Note 4 to Chapter 12: "Malang — a free-living mendicant."
  (p. 19, folio 18).
- **Initiation into an order, requested and granted inside the narrative.** "Ranjha replied,
  Admit me to your holy order; make me a Malang and give me Hir as my Malangan and Mate." (p. 18,
  folio 17). The Pirs' answer attaches a condition: "Hir is yours but use her not as a wife, as
  men use women.5 Do not desert her nor take her away from her parents house for she is no
  penniless girl nor a girl of mean birth, arid [text-layer?] [and] remember to cast your eyes on
  no other woman than her." (pp. 18–19, folios 17–18). The editor's note 5: "This is salutary
  teaching for a young man in love, and the first expression of a moral sentiment on the part of
  the Five Pirs. Whether Ranjha honoured this injunction is far from certain." (p. 19, folio 18).
- **Samāʿ licensed by a named silsila.** Note 1 to Chapter 12 on music as "a permissible means of
  experiencing spiritual exaltation" within "The Chishtiyya sect of Sufis, which counts Fariduddin
  Shakarganj among its leaders" (p. 19, folio 18) — attached to Ranjha's flute performance before
  the Five Pirs.
- **The performance itself, as a catalogue of modes, tunes and song repertoires.** "He played the
  seven modes, even the modes of Kharj,2 Raldiab [text-layer?], Sanehar, Panchant [text-layer?]
  Maddham, Durat [text-layer?], and Nikanli [text-layer?], and with much skill he kept time to the
  beats of the drum, and he also played many variations on these tunes, even from Drab unto Gaddi
  Dun. And he sang all the songs that men sing, even the song of Bishenpatti and the songs that
  women of Manjha [text-layer?] sing, the song of Sohni and Mahiwal and the songs of the Gujars2
  and the songs of the women of he [text-layer?] [the] east." (p. 18, folio 17). Note 2: "These are
  the names of the seven notes in the classical music of the Indo-Pakistan subcontinent." (p. 19,
  folio 18). **Readings for the individual mode names are not recoverable from this file — see
  Doubts.**
- **Alms-giving in the name of God, and the props of a faqir, used as a disguise.** "Kaidu, in the
  guise of a mendicant faqir, came to him and begged for alms in the name God. And Ranjha, thinking
  he was truly a holy man gave him half of his pastry. Kaidu gave him a faqir's blessing and
  retired towards the village." (p. 13, folio 12). The props are then stripped off him: "She tore
  off his faqir's cap and ropes of beads and threw tern [text-layer?] [them] on the ground."
  (p. 13, folio 12). And Chuchak's judgement on outward marks of sanctity: "lie [text-layer?] [he]
  thinks he becomes a perfect faqir by wearing a rosary. He thinks the girdle makes the darvesh.8"
  (pp. 13–14, folios 12–13).
- **Bhang, and the professions and purposes attached to it — the chunk's fullest institutional
  note.** Note 4 to Chapter 8: "Bhang is the Indian hemp which i5 [text-layer?] [is] used as an
  intoxicant and a narcotic. It is a favourite with the mystically inclined, particularly among
  Muslims and Sikhs. It is still smoked, chewed, eaten or drunk in the old Punjab area by wandering
  mendicants and many of those seeking spiritual exaltation or sexual pleasure, to both of which it
  seems to minister with equal facility. It is often euphemistically referred to as 'Thandyai',
  that is, cool drink." (p. 12, folio 11).
- **The Qazi as a village institution, summoned by the parents and sitting in judgement.** "her
  parents sent for the Qazi and the Qazi sat between Chuchak and Milki, and hr [text-layer?] [Hir]
  was made to sit in front of the Qazi." (p. 16, folio 15). He prescribes marriage and threatens
  death: "The Qazi said, 'Those who do not obey their parents will be burnt alive… If we were to
  condemn you, you will be done to death at once.4 If evil doers are killed, God does not avenge
  their death.'" (p. 16, folio 15). Earlier he is quoted at second hand: "The Qazi always says,
  Marry a naughty girl as soon as you can." (p. 14, folio 13). The editor's note 4: "All this is
  more in the nature of psychological warfare than any considered legal observation." (p. 17, folio
  16).
- **The council / assembly of village elders as the forum for reputational complaint.** "Then
  Kaidu came before the council of the village elders" (p. 13, folio 12); "they came and told
  Chuchak what Kaidu had been saying in the assembly of the elders" (p. 13, folio 12); "Ghuchak
  [text-layer?] [Chuchak] and the assembly of elders will thrash you" (p. 19, folio 18).
- **Wage labour, non-payment and a twelve-year term of service.** "'For twelve years2 I have been
  grazing your buffaloes and now you turn me away without wages.3 You are looting me like a Bania'
  whose ledger stays quietly in his shop while the interest swells into a mountain. So your
  daughter stayed in her house and you got my services for nothing.'" (p. 15, folio 14). Public
  opinion enforces payment: "All the people curse us for having turned the cowherd out without
  paying him his wages. Had he asked for his wages you would have had to pay him a whole hag-full
  [text-layer?] [bag-full] of money." (p. 15, folio 14). The editor's note 3: "Incidentally, the
  poet has never told us what wages were fixed by Chuchak with Ranjha. Indeed, there has been no
  mention of wages at all." (p. 16, folio 15). Note 4: "Bania is a Hindu shopkeeper." (p. 16, folio
  15).
- **Cash as an instrument of secrecy.** "And Hir slipped five gold coins into Mithi's hands and
  made her promise not to tell the secret to the other girls. And Mithi's heart grew warm when she
  saw the money. Verily without money there is no wit nor wisdom. With the help of money the fool
  is the equal of the wise man." (p. 18, folio 17). Mithi's house then functions as the meeting
  place: "Mithi's house was near the watering place of the cattle, a little aside in a quiet
  corner. It was full of quilts and beds and soft coverlets, and Mithi used to scatter flowers for
  Ranjha and Hir to walk on." (p. 18, folio 17).
- **The spinning wheel as the organising institution of women's rural economy.** Note 1 to Chapter
  11: "A spinning-wheel (charkha) used to be the inseparable companion of every woman, old or
  young, in the rural Punjab. Young girls and brides had red spinning-wheel, [text-layer?]
  [spinning-wheels] of smaller size. The spinning-wheel was an important link in rural society.
  Women spun cotton yarn, and the village weaver made it into cloth." (p. 17, folio 16). In the
  verse the Qazi prescribes it as the correct female occupation: "You should sit in the assemblies
  of women in their spinning parties. Turn your red spinning wheel1 and sing the merry songs of the
  Chenab." (p. 16, folio 15); and Hir's abandonment of it marks her fall: "She gave up her spinning
  and no longer sat with her girl friends." (p. 12, folio 11).
- **Purdah, and the editor's claim that it was an urban not a rural institution.** Note 8 to
  Chapter 9: "The 'purdah' has never been an established institution in rural areas as it has been
  for a Long time among Muslims in towns and cities, and it is quite usual for girls to move freely
  in the villages without a veil," (p. 14, folio 13). Sultan nevertheless demands it: "If she does
  not obey you and sit in purdah,7 I will kill her." (p. 17, folio 16).
- **The mosque as a woman's licensed pretext for leaving the house.** "She goes to the forest under
  the pretence of going to the mosque, but she is getting another lesson from a different chapter."
  (p. 14, folio 13). Bathing serves the same function: "Under the pretence of bathing, Hir and her
  friends used to meet him in the forest on the banks of the Chenab." (p. 19, folio 18).
- **Ritual prostration, and its Mughal-court provenance.** "I salaam before you seven times.3"
  (p. 11, folio 10), glossed at note 3: "Seven low curtseys, a milder version of the Chinese
  kow-tow, were part of the ritual of the Mughal Court." (p. 11, folio 10). Ranjha prostrates to
  the Pirs: "Ranjha bowed himself to the ground" (p. 15, folio 14); Hir to Ranjha: "and she bowed
  herself before him" (p. 15, folio 14).
- **"Darbar" defined as a court, not a shrine.** Note 4 to Chapter 7: "Darhar [text-layer?]
  [Darbar] is the court of a king or notable." (p. 11, folio 10), glossing "Hir has been bestowed
  on you by the Darbar of God." (p. 11, folio 10). **The book uses the word for God's court, not
  for a shrine complex.**
- **Amir and faqir as social categories, and the drift of "Amir".** Note 6 to Chapter 7: "'Amir' in
  Arabic means a ruler, from 'Am? [text-layer?] [reading not recoverable] meaning rule, government,
  order. In the Urdu and Punjabi languages it has now come to mean a rich man." (p. 11, folio 10),
  glossing the poet's aside: "(Quoth the poet: 'When the days of good fortune come, all the Pirs,
  Faqirs,5 and Amirs,6 are ready to help.')" (p. 11, folio 10).
- **The turban tuft, dated and attributed to a hereditary caste of bards.** Note 7 to Chapter 7:
  "'Tuft is the well-known 'turrah', beloved of the rural aristocracy of the Punjab… The tufted
  turban is a comparatively recent development, and i5 [text-layer?] [is] said to have been
  invented by the 'mirasis' or hereditary bards of the Punjab in the late nineteenth century."
  (p. 11, folio 10).
- **The Gujars as a pastoral and agricultural tribe, with towns named after them.** Note 3 to
  Chapter 12: "The Gujars are a famous tribe of the old Punjab who keep animals and sell their
  milk. They also engage in agriculture on a large scale. A number of Punjab towns, such as
  Gujranwala, Gujrat and Gujar Khan are named after the Gujar tribe." (p. 19, folio 18).
- **Occupational caste names used as social markers in the village.** Mithi "the barber woman"
  (pp. 14, 18, folios 13, 17); "Sonia the goldsmith girl", "The daughter of Sadoo, the weaver",

  the advent of the steam laundry i5 [text-layer?] [is] interfering increasingly to the regret of
  many." (p. 14, folio 13).
- **Joint-family housing, described by the editor as built form.** Note 7 to Chapter 10: "Old
  houses in the Punjab countryside had a big courtyard with a number of living-rooms and bedrooms
  round it. A number of brothers living with their parents as a joint family was quite common."
  (p. 16, folio 15).
- **State archaeology named as an active institution.** Bhambore is "an old city, the ruins of
  which are now being excavated by the Pakistan Archaeological Department." (p. 12, folio 11).
- **The Persian wheel and agricultural technology.** Note 3 to Chapter 9: "Well-gear refers to the
  gear of the Persian wheel." (p. 14, folio 13), glossing "He will put in motion the well-gear5 of
  destruction" (p. 13, folio 12). Note 7 to Chapter 9: "Cornbins arc [text-layer?] [are] plastered
  with mud and straw." (p. 14, folio 13). Note 4 to Chapter 9: "Ak is a wild plant which gives out a
  white milk-like substance believed to be poisonous." (p. 14, folio 13).
- **Marriage as an inter-tribal transaction, with the Kheras as the receiving party.** "The Kheras
  will bring a marriage procession in a few days to take you to the house of your husband." (p. 16,
  folio 15); "I will not accept a Khera in marriage even though I am bound with iron chains or
  ropes." (p. 17, folio 16). Ornaments are removed as a sanction: "Come, Mithi, take off her
  ornaments. What is the good of giving jewellery to a girl like this?" (p. 14, folio 13).
- **Food as the medium of the relationship, and "pudding" glossed.** "She brought him boiled rice,

- **Buffalo herding as the economic substrate of the whole episode.** The herd is described at
  length (p. 11, folio 10: "There were black buffaloes, grey buffaloes and brown…"); its failure is

  graze any longer. Some were lost, some were drowned; others were devoured by tiger5 [text-layer?]
  [tigers] or got lost on the further bank of the river." (p. 15, folio 14).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The Five Pirs bestow Hir on Ranjha — the chunk's central act of saintly intervention, and the
  poet states it as accomplished fact.** "The holy Pirs answered and said, 'Child, all your wishes
  will be fulfilled; your arrow will hit the target, and your boat will reach the shore. Hir has
  been bestowed on you by the Darbar of God. My child, remember the Five Pirs in the time of your
  distress.' Thus by the grace of God and the kindness of the Five Pin [text-layer?] [Pirs], Hir,
  the Jat girl, was bestowed on Ranjha." (p. 11, folio 10).
- **The five gifts, one from each Pir, with a guarantee of protection and title to the herd.** "The
  Five Pirs were gracious unto Ranjha, Khizr gave him a turban tuft,' Sayyid Jalal a dagger,
  Zalkaria [text-layer?] [Zakaria] a thick blanket, Lal Shahbaz a ring, and Shakarganj a kerchief.
  And they said, 'No one will do you harm. God has made you the owner of these cattle.'" (p. 11,
  folio 10).
- **A first counsel of prosperity, given before the gifts.** "The Pir replied, Child, eat your fill
  and drink grey buffaloes' milk and live on the fat of the land. Dismiss all sadness from your
  mind. God Himself will set your affairs a-right.'" (p. 11, folio 10).
- **A second apparition, blessing the couple and predicting trouble.** "One day the Five Pirs
  appeared before him, and Ranjha bowed himself to the ground, and Hir was with him. And the Pirs
  said, 'Children, we salute you. Remember God. Do not tarnish the world of love. Ranjha, you are
  Hir's and Hir is yours. A pearl and ruby have come together.10 Your love will cause trouble and
  strife in the world. The world will taunt you, hut [text-layer?] [but] be brave and steadfast. Do
  not abandon love, and remember God day and night.'" (p. 15, folio 14).
- **A third apparition, in which the Pirs claim knowledge of his dream and ask to be sung to.**
  "And the Five Pir at once came to his help, saying 'We have seen in your dream that your mind has
  been perplexed and your soul sore troubled within you.' And the Pirs said, 'Sing to us two or
  three songs as our heart is yearning for song.'" (p. 18, folio 17). The performance moves them:
  "And with the singing and the music he became as one in a trance. He swayed like a cobra. And the
  hearts of the Five Pirs were movedhearing [text-layer?] [moved hearing] Ranjha sing, and they
  said, 'Ask any favour of us and we will give it.'" (p. 18, folio 17). **The Pirs are summoned by
  bathing and flute-playing**: "he bathed in the river and then took his flute and began to play.
  And he stood before the Five Pirs with folded hands and weeping eyes" (p. 18, folio 17).
- **Grass growing green on Laila's tomb — the one standing marvel at a grave in this chunk.** "Is
  not the love of Laila5 known throughout the world and does not the grass row [text-layer?] [grow]
  green on her tomb to this day?" (p. 12, folio 11). Attributed by the book to Hir, in argument
  with Ranjha.
- **Zakaria sawn asunder in a tree; Solomon cast from his throne** — both given by Mithi as proofs
  that love afflicts the holy: "Zakaria who got caught in a tree and was sawn asunder… Was not God
  displeased with King Solomon and did not He cast him down from his throne in his displeasure, in
  the twinkling of an eye?" (p. 18, folio 17). **See Doubts on this "Zakaria".**
- **Majnun on one leg for twelve years, given by the editor as a topos and not as fact.** Note 2 to
  Chapter 10: "Twelve years is, by tradition, a perfect period of time for legendary lovers to spend
  in the service of their sweethearts. Majuun [text-layer?] [Majnun] is traditionally supposed to
  have stood on one leg for twelve years to please Laila or earn her compassion as may have been the
  case. As compared with this impossible performance, Mahiwal only tended buffaloes for twelve
  years." (pp. 15–16, folios 14–15).
- **The Pen and Tablet of Destiny.** "My fate was written by the Pen of Destiny on the First Day of
  Days. The Pen and the Tablet of Destiny prostrated before love." (p. 17, folio 16); "What the Pen
  of Destiny has mitten [text-layer?] [written] has come to pass." (p. 14, folio 13).
- **A promised divine reproof at the Resurrection for those who kill daughters.** "Those who kill
  their daughters will be accounted sinners on the Day of Resurrection. God will say, 'Eat them, as
  you have killed them with your own hands.'" (p. 17, folio 16) — **flatly denied by the editor's
  note 5, see Arguments.**

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The editor's general theory of shrine importance is stated as a definition, not argued.** A
  pir's importance "depends on the number of his disciples, or the visitors and offerings to his
  tomb, as the case may he [text-layer?] [be]." (p. 11, folio 10, note 1).
- **The editor repeatedly overrides the poem on points of Islamic law and doctrine.** On women's
  contractual capacity: "This is incorrect. Under Muslim (to call it Muhamnmadan [text-layer?]
  [Muhammadan] is again incorrect) law, women are entitled to enter into contracts, including that
  of marriage." (p. 12, folio 11, note 2). On female infanticide: "Female infanticide was not
  unknown in the subcontinent until the nineteenth century. Whatever Hir and the Qazi say about
  father, [text-layer?] [fathers] killing their daughters has no religious foundation in Islam. In
  fact, the Prophet condemned and abolished the custom, which was well known in pre-Islamic Arabia.
  Nor i5 [text-layer?] [is] there any basis for the supposition that those who killed their
  daughters would have to eat them on the Day of Judgement." (p. 17, folio 16, note 5). On
  eschatology he does report a belief without contradicting it: "It is believed by Muslims that on
  the Day of Judgement like-minded people will stay or move together." (p. 17, folio 16, note 8).
- **The editor consistently discounts the poem's hyperbole, sometimes while conceding the sentiment
  is real.** "This is surely an exaggerated way of putting it, so far as Hir is concerned" (p. 14,
  folio 13, note 1); "A grossly exaggerated way of asserting parental authority. The expression is
  by no means unfamiliar in the Punjab villages even now." (p. 14, folio 13, note 2); "This again is
  an exaggerated way of putting it, although there may be a genuine sentiment at the bottom of it."
  (p. 14, folio 13, note 8); "This would seem to be the limit of poetic exaggeration." (p. 11, folio
  10, note 8); "This is merely a way of saying nice things and naturally does not imply any solemn
  promise or undertaking, least of all one involving a possible transfer of worldly possessions."
  (p. 16, folio 15, note 8).
- **The editor treats the poem's internal chronology as conventional rather than evidential.** "The
  reference to twelve years here is merely a conventional expression (unless it is a slip of the pen
  on the part of the poet) and is hardly meant to be taken literally. In any car [text-layer?]
  [case], it could not have taken twelve years for a scandal like this to develop, particularly when
  it all started with love at first sight!" (p. 16, folio 15, note 2). Likewise on the tigers: "This
  is the first time tigers are mentioned as being active in this neighbourhood. The reference need
  not however, be taken seriously. In Punjabi poetry tigers have a way of being at hand when the
  poet wants them to do a job of this kind, and are prompt in doing the needful." (p. 16, folio 15,
  note 5).
- **The editor defends the Jats against the poem.** Note 6 to Chapter 10, on "We Jats are known to
  be sharp customers.6": "This is uncalled for self-praise, if one could call it so. Actnally
  [text-layer?] [Actually], Jats have a reputation for being simple and honest folk." (p. 16, folio
  15).
- **The editor criticises Usborne's translation directly, once.** Note 9 to Chapter 8: "That is, she
  revealed her wantonness for all to see. This is a !somewhat [text-layer?] [somewhat] circuitous
  translation by Usborne." (p. 13, folio 12). **This is the only place in the chunk where the
  translator and the annotator are visibly distinguished.**
- **The editorial apparatus dates itself.** Note 2 to Chapter 7 describes Uch as "in Bahawalpur, now
  part of West Pakistan" (p. 11, folio 10) — a present-tense political description in the editor's
  own voice, not the translator's or the poet's.
- **The editor is willing to joke at the saints' expense.** Note 10 to Chapter 10 on the Five Pirs
  being "concerned more with the success of an illicit love affair than with any idea of social
  morality!" (p. 16, folio 15); note 11's aside that "It is easier for five brothers to live
  together than for five Pirs to do so!" (p. 16, folio 15); note 7 to Chapter 8, in full: "Quite! He
  seems to have had good reason to know." (p. 12, folio 11).
