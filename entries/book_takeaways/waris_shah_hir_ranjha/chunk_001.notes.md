## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Pir and murid defined, and the literal sense of "Pir" given — the single most archive-usable
  note in the chunk.** "'Pir' is a person who is regarded as a spiritual leader. The disciples and
  followers of a Pir are known as Murids'. Literally the word 'Pir' means an old man. 'Mohiuddin'
  means one who revives religion, and is the popular title of Sayyid Abdul Qadir Gilani, the chief
  saint of the Qadiriyya Sect of the Sufis. The Shakarganj of Pakpattan mentioned here belonged to
  The Chishtiyya Sect. It is not unusual for a person to owe allegiance to more than one sect of the
  Sufis. This is the case with Waris Shah." (p. 2, folio 1, note 2). **Two orders named — Qadiriyya
  and Chishtiyya — and multiple simultaneous allegiance stated as normal.**
- **Intercession by holy men, asserted as necessary.** "Let us also praise the holiness of Pirs;2
  without the help of holy men our boat cannot reach the shore. Praise to score out even the writing
  of the Pen of Destiny.8 [text-layer?] [Destiny.³]" (p. 2, folio 1). And "it is meet and proper to
  praise God and invoke the help of Saints and Prophets before essaying this story of Love." (p. 2,
  folio 1).
- **Faqirs and the ferry toll — a small, concrete economy of exclusion.** "He who is for yonder
  shore, let him pay his pence.2 Him who gives his pence we will take across; even though he be a
  dacoit or a thief we will not repeat his name, but we chase away all beggars, faqirs5
  [text-layer?] [faqirs³] and those who eat unlawful meat like dogs. Those who attempt to enter our
  boat forcibly we throw into the river. Even the son of a Pir like Waris,' we will not take into our
  boat for nothing." (pp. 5–6, folios 4–5). The editor glosses the coin — "Pice would be more
  appropriate here. Usborne has used the English equivalent as it came more naturally to him." (p. 6,
  folio 5, note 2) — and the status: "Faqir is a mendicant. See also Introduction, note 24." (p. 6,
  folio 5, note 3).
- **The mosque as an institution, and who may sleep in it.** "Mosques are God's houses and evil liven
  [text-layer?] [lives / livers?] are not admitted therein. You have abandoned prayer and keep long
  hair and scented moustaches. Such men we beat out of mosques." (p. 4, folio 3). The Mullah's
  concession is conditional and timed: "I give you leave to pass the night in the mosque, but see to
  it, foolish Jat, that you leave it with covered head at early dawn, or I will summon four lusty
  scoundrels who will belabour you with cudgels and thrust you out of the assembly." (p. 5, folio 4).
- **Music at a mosque as a communal flashpoint — the editor's note, dated to the colonial period.**
  "Playing the flute in a mosque is regarded as a sacrilege. It is strange that throughout the
  somewhat vigorous exchanges that follow between Ranjha and the Mullah, the latter makes no
  objection to this. Music before mosques was a serious point of dispute between Hindus and Muslims
  before the Independence and led to frequent inter-communal rioting." (p. 5, folio 4, note 1).
- **The mullahs' funeral economy, as the poem attacks it.** "Mullahs run after women in mosques and
  cultivated land like laymen. They are like curses clinging to the house of God. They are blind men,
  lepers and cripples, always waiting greedily for a death in the house so that they may take the
  dead inn's [text-layer?] [man's] raiment. They arise at midnight their fat bellies are smitten with
  hunger and they cry for something to eat. Under the shelter 0f [text-layer?] [of] Holy Writ they
  curse the living and when poor wayfarers and strangers come to beg for succour they cry, begone,
  begone!'" (p. 5, folio 4). Ranjha's opening charge is about the pulpit: "You sit in the pulpit with
  the Quran in front of you, yet your mind is bent on iniquity. You lead the village women astray"
  (p. 4, folio 3).
- **A catechism of ritual, put as a challenge to the Mullah.** "0 [text-layer?] [O] deputy of Allah,
  may your sins be forgiven… Tell me, 0 [text-layer?] [O] learned in wisdom, what is clean and what
  is unclean? What is right and what is wrong? What is prayer made of and what is it built? How many
  cam [text-layer?] [ears?] and noses has prayer? What is its length and size, and with what is it
  caparisoned? To whom was prayer ordained in the beginning?" (pp. 4–5, folios 3–4). The Mullah's
  claim in reply is a claim of office: he "knew all the doctrines of the faith and all the prayers
  ordained for believers, and could lead the pious across the bridge of salvation." (p. 5, folio 4).
- **The pul sirat, and the editor's flat denial of it.** "Bridge of salvation is the pul sirat, a
  mythological bridge no broader than a sword's edge over which saint and sinner have to pass before
  reaching their final destination, The saint will cross to heaven, while the sinner will be cut
  through and fall to hell. Needless to add, the bridge is an invention of the pious imagination. The
  Quran does not mention it. 'Sirat', in Arabic means 'path'." (p. 5, folio 4, note 3).
- **Land measurement, the bribed Qazi, and the patwari who replaced him.** "So they sent for the
  Qazi7 and the assembly of the elders to measure the family land. They gave bribes to the Qazi and
  thus the good land was given to the brothers and the barren and inhospitable land was given to
  Ranjha" (p. 2, folio 1), with note 7: "The job of measuring land today is one for the village
  'patwari, a subordinate revenue official who wields great power and local influence." (p. 4, folio
  3). **This is the chunk's only passage on land tenure and it is a dispossession narrative.**
- **The Panchayat, and its formal recognition by the colonial state.** "The Panchayat, as it is
  called, is the Council of the elders of the village which hears disputes and settles them without
  recourse to the ordinary law. It was formally recognised as an institution by the British régime."
  (p. 10, folio 9, note 5).
- **Chaudhri and Mihr as titles of local authority.** "Chaudhri means primarily the headman of a
  village. It is also an honorific appellation used for addressing members of certain tribes, such as
  Jats and Rajputs. The word is common to Urdu, Punjabi and Bengali." (p. 4, folio 3, note 4). "Mihr
  is an honorific title used by some castes of ]ats [text-layer?] [Jats] and Arains." (p. 10, folio
  9, note 1). "Mauju3 Chaudhri4 was chief land owner in the village. He had eight sons and two
  daughters." (p. 2, folio 1).
- **The Ranjha and Sial names as castes, and the hero named by caste.** "Ranjha is still a well-known
  caste of the Jats in the old Punjab. The hem [text-layer?] [hero] of Hir-Ranjha is known by his
  caste. His real name is given as Dhidhu, which with the plebeian ring about it, would be somewhat
  unusual for a man of aristocratic origin like Ranjha." (p. 3, folio 2, note 2). "Neither the Chinese
  nor those of the North can rival the features of the Sials." (p. 7, folio 6); "She is the pride of
  the Sial assembly." (p. 6, folio 5).
- **Spinning parties (tarinjan / bhora) as a village institution.** "Spinning parties — the Punjabi
  name is 'tarinjan' or 'bhora' — were a famous institution of the Punjab village. Young girls,
  generally unmarried ones, sat together wit [text-layer?] [with] their spinning-wheels and competed
  with each other in spinning skill and scandalous conversation." (p. 4, folio 3, note 14). In the

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The notes in this chunk are the edition editor's, not Usborne's, and they say so by criticizing
  him in the third person.** "Usborne has left out some of the coarser gibes of Ranjha here, which
  are full of anatomical detail typical of the more vigorous variety of Punjabi conversation." (p. 5,
  folio 4, note 2). "This is a mistake of Usborne's. 'Chuselra' is a misreading for 'joshelra', which
  means a snake which is excited and on the offensive. This can be true of any snake and certainly
  does not refer to any particular variety of snake." (p. 9, folio 8, note 7). "Pice would be more
  appropriate here. Usborne has used the English equivalent as it came more naturally to him." (p. 6,
  folio 5, note 2). **The editor is nowhere named in this chunk** — (not stated). See Doubts.
- **The editor systematically corrects Waris Shah on fact, in a debunking register.** Four instances:
  on Shakarganj and the Punjab's troubles (p. 2, folio 1, note 4); on Destiny — "This is not what
  Omar Khayyam has to say about the Moving Finger of Destiny! See also Chapter 19, note 7." (p. 2,
  folio 1, note 3); on the Quran and women — "Ranjha is certainly no scholar of the Quran, which does
  not lay down any general proposition about the deoeitfulne~ [text-layer?] [deceitfulness] of women
  anywhere… This is only an individual remark which carries no authority with it." (p. 4, folio 3,
  note 10); on the pul sirat — "the bridge is an invention of the pious imagination. The Quran does
  not mention it." (p. 5, folio 4, note 3).
- **An explicit political correction, with a charge of "local patriotism".** "Historically speaking,
  this is an imaginary situation, as in the past the armies of the Punjab are known to have defended
  Hindustan on more than one occasion rather than to have fallen upon it at any time. Even the Sikh
  power which rose long after Waris Shah's poem was written, was contained on the banks of the Sutlej
  river by the Anglo-Sikh treaty of 1809, , and never became a menace to Hindustan. The poet's flight
  of fancy may have been inspired by a mistaken sense of local patriotism." (p. 7, folio 6, note 2).
- **The editor also moralizes on the narrative, repeatedly.** "Is not Hir committing herself too
  early and too far? Sitting in a stranger's lap is certainly unusual, even in a case of alleged love
  at first sight." (p. 9, folio 8, note 1). "'Queen', lady', girl' and 'fir [text-layer?] ['Hir']
  used by Ranjha in such quick succession and I-hr's [text-layer?] [Hir's] tender response to each
  loving epithet shiny [text-layer?] [show] her precipitous descent into familiarity with the
  stranger whom she originally wanted to belabour. No wonder this was followed before long by
  physical intimacy." (p. 9, folio 8, note 5). "Rather a large retinue for a girl who wants to keep
  her affair secret!" (p. 10, folio 9, note 7). "This is obviously a coquettish exaggeration – Hir
  could not have waited so long before venting her feelings on Ranjha!" (p. 8, folio 7, note 12).
- **The edition treats the Hir story as one recension among several, with Persian antecedents.**
  "Mauju is a corruption of Muizud Din, which is the name given in some of the Persian Hir-Ranjha
  Mathnavis." (p. 4, folio 3, note 3). Waris Shah's own framing is of a re-telling, not an invention:
  "'Write for us afresh the forgotten story of the Love of Hir.'" (p. 2, folio 1).
- **Two cross-references point to apparatus outside this chunk.** "See also Introduction, note 24."
  (p. 6, folio 5, note 3) and "See also Chapter 19, note 7." (p. 2, folio 1, note 3). **The contents
  list on p. 1 shows neither an Introduction nor the Invocation** — see Doubts.
