# waris_shah_hir_ranjha — chunk 003 — pp. 20–28

Narrative and notes for **six chapters**: the tail of Chapter 12's notes (notes 8–13, p. 20, folio
19), then **Ch. 13 "Hir thrashes Kaidu and Kaidu complains to the village elders"** (p. 20, folio
19), **Ch. 14 "Chuchak finds Ranjha and Hir in the forest"** (p. 21, folio 20), **Ch. 15 "Chuchak
proposes to get Hir married"** (p. 23, folio 22), **Ch. 16 "Hir is married to Saida against her
will"** (p. 24, folio 23), **Ch. 17 "Hir is taken to Rangpur"** (p. 27, folio 26) and **Ch. 18 "Hir
is unhappy in her new home"** (p. 28, folio 27). This is continuous *qissa* narrative in English
prose; the archive-usable material is concentrated in the **numbered notes** (Ch. 16 alone carries
22, pp. 26–27, folios 25–26) and in one three-line passage on shrine fairs (p. 24, folio 23).

**Folio, as the pages actually print it.** The running header prints the folio at its end on **all
nine pages of this chunk**, and `folio = PDF − 1` holds exactly across every one: "Waris Shah: The
Adventures of Hir and Ranjha 19" (p. 20) through "… 27" (p. 28), with 20/21/22/23/24/25/26 on
pp. 21–27 in order. **No page in this chunk prints a competing number**, so the run sheet's rule and

**Chapter openings sit in column 2, as warned.** Ch. 14 opens in `[col 2 of 2]` of p. 21 below that

**Two annotating hands, and the chunk proves it.** Note 8 of Ch. 12 reads "Shroff woman — **Usborne
means** a woman belonging to a goldsmith family." (p. 20, folio 19), and note 7 of Ch. 17 is the
single word-pair "**Usborne's note.**" attached to a bracketed editorial excision (p. 28, folio 27).

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **`mausoleum-of-waris-shah`** (the book's running header spells him **"Waris Shah"** throughout,
  and so do the notes): **the poet intervenes inside his own poem, and is cursed by his own
  character.** Ranjha's sisters-in-law end their letter to Hir: "May the curse of the Poet Waris
  Shah fall upon you, Hir, for you have robbed us of our dear brother-in-law." (p. 22, folio 21).
  The editor's note 5 to that chapter judges the intervention: "Here the poet has jumped quite
  unnecessarily into the fray. It may be presumed that he is tired of being a mere spectator of
  these exciting events and feels called upon to participate, **even though on the wrong side**"
  (p. 22, folio 21). **No tomb, no place, no date, no order is attached to him anywhere in this
  chunk** — Jandiala Sher Khan, 1798 and Chishti are all (not stated). See Doubts.
- **`mausoleum-of-waris-shah`**: **a second authorial intrusion, this one anti-Qazi, and the editor
  defends it.** The verse prints, in parentheses, "(Quoth the poet: Qazis have no fear of God and
  eat the bread of iniquity.')22" (p. 26, folio 25). Note 22: "This is more an expression of
  sympathy with Hir in her distress on the part of Waris Shah than a personal opinion, although he
  certainly seems to be angry with this particular Qazi." (p. 27, folio 26). **This is the editor
  reading the poet's stance toward the religious establishment**, and it is the only place in the
  chunk where Waris Shah's own opinions are discussed.
- **`mausoleum-of-waris-shah`**: **the translator cut a passage, and says so.** At the end of Ch. 17:
  "(There follows in the text a tirade against Jats generally. As bringing out the weak points of
  the Jats it is of some ethnological and historical interest, but it has nothing much to do with
  the story so it is omitted here.)'" — with note 7 identifying it as "Usborne's note." (pp. 27–28,
  folios 26–27). **A named excision from Waris Shah's text, with the reason given**; the passage
  itself is not in this book.
- **`shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar`** (the chunk names the place, **"Pakpattan"**, and "the tomb of
  the saint", but **never names the saint** — see Doubts before using this): **a shrine fair used as
  the poem's unit of measurement for a crowd, with the practice attached.** "The crowd and the noise
  was as great as at the Fairs of Pakpattan or Nigah or Rattan or Thamman, where women flock to kiss
  the tomb of the saint and attain the achievement of their desires." (p. 24, folio 23). Three facts
  the archive can use, all in that one sentence: **Pakpattan holds a "Fair"**; **women are the actors
  named**; **the act is kissing the tomb**, and its purpose is "to attain the achievement of their
  desires". **No date, month, duration, custodian, offering or fee is given** — all (not stated).
  Mapped because a tomb and a fair are attached to the toponym, not the toponym alone; the mapping
  itself is a judgement and is flagged under Doubts.
- **`gurdwara-dash-mesh-pita`** (the chunk's name for him is **"Guru Gobind Singh"**): **named once,
  in the editor's note, as the militarizer of the Sikhs.** "The Sikhs became a militant body under
  Guru Gobind Singh, the tenth successor of Guru Nanak. They have a religious prohibition against
  shaving and smoking." (p. 20, folio 19). Mapped under the person-ruling of 18 September 2026 —
  he is the sole principal figure of that row. **Nothing else attaches**: no gurdwara, no Sukkur, no
  Sindh, no date, no tomb — all (not stated). See Doubts.

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Shrine fairs and tomb-kissing by women — the single most archive-usable sentence in the chunk.**
  "The crowd and the noise was as great as at the Fairs of Pakpattan or Nigah or Rattan or Thamman,
  where women flock to kiss the tomb of the saint and attain the achievement of their desires."
  (p. 24, folio 23). Four named fair-sites; women as the named pilgrims; the physical act (kissing
  the tomb); and the stated transaction (desires attained). **The comparison itself is economic and
  demographic — a shrine fair is the largest crowd the poet can name.**
- **Vows and offerings promised in exchange for a return — a whole cluster in five lines.** Ranjha's
  sisters-in-law write: "Come and set your font [text-layer?] [foot] in our courtyard. **We will
  present offerings to Clod [text-layer?] [God]** if you come back to Hazara. **We will dedicate a
  saucepan3 to the name of Mi [text-layer?].** **We will hold a wrestling match and offer garlands
  to Ghazi Pir.** Have we not promised to **light the lamps in honour of Khawaja Khizr** if you
  return to us?" (p. 28, folio 27). Four distinct votive practices — general offerings, a dedicated
  vessel, a wrestling match with garlands, and lamp-lighting — each tied to a named or damaged
  recipient and each **conditional on an outcome**. Note 3 of that chapter does not gloss the
  saucepan dedication.
- **Opium and bhang as the equipment of a religious mendicant, and the editor says so plainly.**
  Note 3 to Ch. 13: "Post i5 [text-layer?] [is] opium. An opium eater is called 'posti'. **Kaidu, as
  an aspirant to spiritual promotion, presumably required opium as an aid to contemplation.**"
  (p. 21, folio 20). In the verse the village elders compensate the beaten fakir with exactly this:
  "promising to build him a new hut and to give him more post3 and 'bhang' and all the things the
  girls destroyed." (p. 21, folio 20). Kaidu's stock is itemized when it is looted: "Dogs and cocks
  and hens have looted my opium and bhang. They have broken my pots and pans and **the bowl my
  master gave me**." (p. 20, folio 19) — the begging bowl is a master's gift. And the simile
  "Chuchak stood bewildered like a saint that has drunk deeply of bhang,'" (p. 22, folio 21) points
  by note 1 to "Chapter 8, note 4", outside this chunk.
- **The village assembly of elders as a court, and its partiality.** Kaidu appeals to it ("I come
  for justice. Administer justice, O Assembly of Elders"), it hears both parties, then "the elders
  advised him to be patient and quiet, saying, 'The girls have behaved exceedingly foolishly. The
  fakir has been very harshly dealt with.' Then they scolded the girls and consoled the cripple"
  (pp. 20–21, folios 19–20). Kaidu's verdict on it: "These elders have lost faith and have abandoned
  justice. **They show partiality to their daughters.** It is a poor consolation they have given me.
  Verily it is a case of '"a blind king and oppressing officials"'." (p. 21, folio 20) — note 4 to
  that chapter records "This is a famous Punjabi proverb." as a note, though it is attached in the
  text at "Toss the Red handkerchief" (p. 21, folio 20; and note 4 at p. 22, folio 21). Publicity is
  by drum: "He has noised the whole affair in the bazaar by beat of drum." (p. 20, folio 19).
- **The nikah as procedure, itemized — and then voided by force.** The Qazi is "given a seat on the
  floor. **They appointed two witnesses and an attorney** and prepared to offer prayers. They told
  her the definition of Faith and made her repeat, 'There is only one God and Muhammad is his
  Prophet.' **They made her read the six Kalmas' and taught her the Five Times of Prayer.**" (p. 25,
  folio 24). The Qazi's own statement of the law: "**Without witnesses a marriage is invalid.** These
  are the clear directions of the law of Muhammad." (p. 25, folio 24). His remedy when Hir refuses:
  "You can only gain your object by deceit. The powerful and mighty have a way of their own. **It is
  only Pirs, Fakirs and Saints who are afraid of using violence.** Tell the bride's attorney that
  consent to the marriage must be wrung from Hir, even against her will. **Let us gag her and read
  the marriage service.**" (p. 26, folio 25). And the outcome: "So the Qazi, by guile, against hr's
  [text-layer?] [Hir's] will, solemnised the marriage. The witnesses and the attorney ran away
  afterwards as a camel flees on seeing a lion." (p. 26, folio 25).
- **The Qazi's price, and the accusation of bribe-taking.** Chuchak: "There is no man whom I respect
  and trust as I trust you. **I will give you anything you ask if you will bring the affair to a
  successful conclusion.**" (pp. 25–26, folios 24–25). Hir's closing curse: "If you are so anxious
  to give a bride to the Kheras, why not give your own daughter to them? **God's curse on all Qazis
  and bribe takers.**" (p. 26, folio 25). Her earlier attack on the office: "What have Qazis and the
  '"Shara"'15 got to do with True religion? **There is a big well in Hell into which Qazis will be
  thrown by God.**" (p. 25, folio 24).
- **The Shara defined, and the Kalmas counted.** Note 15: "Shara or Shariat' is the system of Muslim
  Law." (p. 26, folio 25). Note 14: "Kalma is the Muslim declaration of Faith. 'There is no God but
  one and Muhammad is his messenger', i5 [text-layer?] [is] the first Kalma. **There are five ve
  [text-layer?] others.**" (p. 26, folio 25) — against the verse's "the six Kalmas'" (p. 25, folio
  24); see Doubts.
- **Hindu Brahmans fix the date of a Muslim wedding, and the editor flags the fact as normal.**
  "Meanwhile the Kheras asked the Brahmans1 to consult the Stars and to fix the marriage. **The
  Brahmans fixed Virwati (Thursday) in the month of Sawan2 for the wedding**" (p. 24, folio 23).
  Note 1: "Brahmans. the 'twice-born', are the highest of the Hindu castes, the intellectual group
  in ancient hindu [text-layer?] society. **It may appear strange that a Muslim family should depend
  on Hindu Brahmans for fixing a marriage but this was by no means unusual in the old Punjab.**"
  (p. 26, folio 25). Note 2 places the month: "Sawan and Bhadon are the two rainy months of the year
  in the old Punjab. Two of the fountains in the Red Fort of Delhi are called Sawan and Bhadon.
  Poets are in the habit of comparing the profusion of their own tears for the beloved with the rain
  of Sawan and Bhadon." (p. 26, folio 25). **This is the only calendar date in the chunk, and it is
  a weekday-plus-month, not a year.**
- **Barbers as marriage go-betweens and as caterers.** "They said that the Kheras had already sent
  their barbers' to propose the betrothal." (p. 23, folio 22), with note 1 "Barber — See
  Introduction, note 1." (p. 23, folio 22) — the gloss is outside this chunk. Again: "the barbers
  have delivered their message all over the country" (p. 23, folio 22); "There were multitudes of
  barbers cooking the food." (p. 24, folio 23); "the barbers presented dishes of sweets for the
  bridegroom's party" (p. 24, folio 23).
- **Caste endogamy as the reason for the marriage, stated as a rule.** Chuchak's brethren "urged
  that **the Sials had never given theft [text-layer?] [their] daughters to the Ranjha tribe** and
  that they would be disgraced if they gave their daughters to such lowly and needy folk. **The
  brotherhood recommended an alliance with the house of the Kheras as being Jats of good lineage**
  whom Chuchak would be proud to own as relations." (p. 23, folio 22). The decision is the
  brotherhood's, not the father's alone: "So Chuchak took the advice of the brotherhood and announced
  the betrothal".
- **The Sial tribe's customary law on women's property — the editor's note, and it is a hard
  economic claim.** Note 13 to Ch. 12: "Hir was obviously a favourite child and a spoilt one.
  Besides, **the Sial tribe has always been more than usually considerate towards its women. It is
  one of the few tribes in the Punjab which allows women to inherit property under customary law.**"
  (p. 20, folio 19).
- **The village as one social unit across caste and creed — the editor's most extended sociological
  note.** Note 12 to Ch. 12: "Judging by their names, some of Hirs [text-layer?] [Hir's] girl friends
  are Hindus. **This symbolises the atmosphere of the village in the old Punjab, where the village
  community as a whole was regarded as one social unit irrespective of caste or creed.** A young man
  belonging to a village would be referred to as the son' of that particular village; similarly, a
  young girl marr~1g [text-layer?] [marrying] into that village would be its daughter-in-law'. **Even
  a sweeper woman would be addressed as daughter', sister' or 'mother'** (depending on her age and
  the age of the speaker) **in the most aristocratic household in the village. If she was elderly, it
  would be rude to call her by her name.**" (p. 20, folio 19).
- **Caste and occupation glosses by the editor.** "10. Kanjars are people who live on prostitution. A
  prostitute is called Kanjari in Punjabi." (p. 20, folio 19); "11. Khatri — the second of the four
  principal Hindu castes. They are the merchant class of Northern India. See also, Chapter 3, note
  7." (p. 20, folio 19); "8. Shroff woman — Usborne means a woman belonging to a goldsmith family."
  (p. 20, folio 19); "10 A Nwab [text-layer?] [Nawab] is a Muslim ruling chief or a big landlord."
  (p. 26, folio 25).
- **The dowry and the wedding economy, itemized at length.** "there was no end to the ornaments,
  amulets, anklets, necklaces, earrings and nose-rings which were prepared as a dowry for the bride"
  (p. 24, folio 23); the kitchen inventory "There were all sorts of Pilao3 and soups and all kinds of
  varieties of rice, even Mushki and Basmutti4 and Musagir and Begami and Sonputti" (p. 24, folio
  23); the labour "The potter women brought earthen pots and bakers brought fuel from the forest The
  water carriers rushed about drawing water from the wells." (p. 24, folio 23); and the display's
  competitive function, "**The guests turned green with jealousy when they saw the abundance of good
  things.**" (p. 24, folio 23). Notes gloss the food: "3. Pilao is a meat and rice dish, very popular
  among the Muslims of the Indo-Pakistan subcontinent Pilao has a number of varieties." and "4
  Basmutti, as its name implies, is a sweet-smelling variety of rice. **It is grown In the central
  districts of the old Panjab. By all accounts it is the best in the subcontinent and is now a
  valuable export from Pakistan.**" (p. 26, folio 25).
- **Hospitality converted into standing — stated as credit.** "A large host of people came to enjoy
  Chuchak's hospitality… **Chuchak has gained credit in the world and the people are praying for his
  long life and prosperity.**" (p. 24, folio 23).
- **Cash sums, land and grain — every quantity in the chunk.** "we do not intend to restore him even
  for hundreds of thousands of rupees" (p. 22, folio 21); "Then five rupees and a shawl were given to
  the Kheras" (p. 24, folio 23); "they drew aside her veil and placed a Quran and five gold mohurs
  in front of her" (p. 27, folio 26); "Did not his brothers expel him for a few moods [text-layer?]
  of land?" (p. 22, folio 21); "All his fields have been made ready for cultivation; **we have
  bushels of grain ready for him when he returns, the produce of all these years that he has been
  away.**" (p. 22, folio 21); "the I4heras [text-layer?] [Kheras] distributed money to the minstrels
  with lavish hands" (p. 24, folio 23); "the singing women sang songs and were given money" (p. 25,
  folio 24); "**They gave presents to the midwife and other menials.**" (p. 27, folio 26).
- **Performers, and a note that their institution is dying.** "Then came the musicians, the dancing
  girls and the jesters" and the minstrels with trumpets and cymbals even from Kashmir and the
  Dekkan" (p. 24, folio 23); "The women of his household beat drums and gave presents to the
  minstrels and gave them bowls of sugar." (p. 23, folio 22). Note 8: "**The jesters, namely the
  'bhands' and the 'Mirasis', who used to be the life of such occasions are vanishing fast along with
  the old feudal order which they adorned.**" (p. 26, folio 25).
- **Wedding rites and objects, each glossed by the editor.** The *surma* exchange, "The bride and
  bridegroom were made to sit facing each other and each one put 'surma' in the other's eyes" (p. 25,

  exchanged ribald6 songs and pleasantries with the women of the bridegroom's party" (p. 24, folio
  23) with note 6 "Ribald longs [text-layer?] [songs] are a well-known institution. **Most of the

  25); the *arsi*, note 7: "Thumb looking-glasses — this refers to the 'Arsi', an outsize silver ring
  with a mirror fitted into it, worn on the right thumb. It was one of the favourite ornaments of a
  bride in the old Punjab, and **continued to be worn for a couple of months or so after marriage,
  during which period the newly-married girl was not expected to do household work. This is still the
  case in the less sophisticated parts of the Punjab countryside.**" (p. 26, folio 25); the *sarbala*,
  note 11: "'Best man' is usually a little boy, the Sarbala or Shahbala, who rides in front of and
  sometimes behind the bridegroom on the same horse and **receives his allotted quota of abuse from
  girls of the bride's party.**" (p. 26, folio 25); the *huqqa*, note 9 (p. 26, folio 25), which "**ants
  [text-layer?] [acts] as a medium of peaceful social intercourse**"; and the arrival rites, "the women
  came out to greet the bride and sang songs of welcome… **poured oil over the threshold. hr's
  [text-layer?] [Hir's] mother-in-law swung water round the bride's head and drank it and gave thanks
  to God.**" (p. 27, folio 26).

  marriage still retains the symbolism of 'marriage by capture'.** The bridal party, although it
  usually lacks the ferocity of an invading army, is led by the bridegroom who, complete with horse
  and dagger, is made to look like a general. although a somewhat slender one. **The 'invaders'
  celebrate their victory by carrying off the bride in the midst of fireworks and fanfare.**
  Resistance from the brides [text-layer?] [bride's] side now takes the form of abusive language
  towards the bridegroom and his family, par-ticularly the ladies." (p. 28, folio 27). The verse bears
  it out: "**The Kheras took her off as thieves drive off stolen cattle.**" (p. 27, folio 26).

  midwife6 sit by her side" (p. 27, folio 26), with note 6: "**It is still usual for a dai to
  accompany the bride in her in-laws' house for the first two or three occasions to help her settle
  down in her new surroundings.** 'Dai' means both a midwife and a nurse. In the present context it
  is the latter." (p. 28, folio 27).
- **Widowhood and the colour code of clothing.** "Other brides have clothes of green, red and yellow
  but I wear only mournful white"" (p. 27, folio 26), with note 4: "Brides in the old Punjab wore
  coloured (and colourful) garments. **White clothes, by tradition, were worn by widows, particularly
  among the Hindus, No ornaments were worn. Muslim widows, if they were childless, wore black or
  white. If they had children, particularly boys, they wore normal clothes, and ornaments too. The

  usual on such occasions, which are marked by youthful frivolity." (p. 26, folio 25), on the run of

  balloons, and coloured rain rockets, and set pieces of elephants, stags, peacocks, coloured
  circles, and moving thrones and revolving moons. All the neighbourhood flocked to see." (p. 25,

  to you that you should come and see me **in the disguise of a fakir. Abandon all your caste and

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The buffaloes mourn their herdsman, and the villagers must placate them by kissing his feet —
  the one quasi-miraculous episode in the chunk, told flatly by the poet.** "Meanwhile the buffaloes
  were ill at ease without their master Ranjha. They gathered together on the bank of the river and
  lifted their mouths in protest. They pushed folk hither and thither with their horns and broke the
  pots and pans of the village. Then the people bade them conciliate Ranjha **by kissing his feet**."
  (p. 27, folio 26). No saint, shrine or *karamat* label is attached — the book presents it as
  narrative.
- **Hir's counter-marriage, witnessed by the Prophet's soul, the polar star and four angels** — her
  answer to the Qazi's demand for witnesses: "**The soul of the Prophet solemnised our marriage. By
  the order of God, the polar20 star was my attorney.** 'The angels Gabriel, Michael, Israel
  [text-layer?] and Israfil were the four witnesses.'" (p. 25, folio 24). Attributed to Hir as speech,
  not asserted by the poet.
- **The Five Pirs as enforcers of a vow.** "Surely the Five Pirs will punish him if he tries to come
  near me." (p. 27, folio 26) — Hir's claim, not the poet's.
- **The throne of God trembling at injustice** — the girls to Hir: "Remember that **the throne of God
  trembles when a man is deprived of his right.**" (p. 23, folio 22).
- **ISHQ as a colour that enters the soul.** "The arguments and threats of the Qazi were of no avail
  for **the colour of Am [text-layer?] [Ain], Shin and Kaf21 had entered into her soul.**" (p. 25,
  folio 24), with note 21: "Ain, Shin and Kaf: these three Arabic letters spell ISHQ which means
  Love." (p. 27, folio 26).
- **Cosmological legends carried by the notes, attributed to Hindu mythology and to Persian legend
  respectively:** the cobra/Bashak Nag supporting the world (p. 26, folio 25; p. 28, folio 27), and
  Jamshid "said to have invented the art of music and medicine" and dethroned by Zahhak "after a
  rule of 700 years" (p. 26, folio 25).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The editor corrects the poet on chronology.** "There seems to be some mistake about the figure
  37, because Muhammad Shah, known as 'Rangila' or the dissolute, did not reign for more than 28
  years." (p. 20, folio 19) — an explicit rejection of a number in the text, on regnal grounds.
- **The editor corrects the poet on history.** "**The military expedition referred to by the poet
  seems to have been an imaginary one.**" (p. 21, folio 20), on the Mughal armies "subduing Muttra".
- **The editor judges the poet's craft, negatively.** "Here the poet has jumped quite unnecessarily
  into the fray. It may be presumed that he is tired of being a mere spectator of these exciting
  events and feels called upon to participate, even though on the wrong side" (p. 22, folio 21).
- **The editor defends the poet's anticlericalism as pathos rather than polemic.** "This is more an
  expression of sympathy with Hir in her distress on the part of Waris Shah than a personal opinion,
  although he certainly seems to be angry with this particular Qazi." (p. 27, folio 26).
- **The editor reads the poem as evidence for a vanished social order, repeatedly.** The recurring
  formula is "**in the old Punjab**" (pp. 20, 21, 22, 23, 26, 28 — folios 19, 20, 21, 22, 25, 27),
  with a present-tense contrast each time: the arsi "is still the case in the less sophisticated
  parts of the Punjab countryside" (p. 26, folio 25); the ceremonial dish "**The institution is alive
  in the countryside of the old Punjab**" (p. 23, folio 22); the bhands and Mirasis "are vanishing
  fast along with **the old feudal order which they adorned**" (p. 26, folio 25); widows' clothing
  "The social pattern is now changed." (p. 28, folio 27). **This is the edition's governing
  interpretive frame in this chunk.**
- **The editor reads religious boundary-crossing as unremarkable.** On Brahmans fixing a Muslim
  marriage: "It may appear strange that a Muslim family should depend on Hindu Brahmans for fixing a
  marriage **but this was by no means unusual in the old Punjab.**" (p. 26, folio 25); on Hindu names
  among Hir's friends, the village "was regarded as one social unit **irrespective of caste or
  creed**" (p. 20, folio 19).
- **Usborne's editorial excision, and its stated grounds.** "As bringing out the weak points of the
  Jats it is of some ethnological and historical interest, **but it has nothing much to do with the
  story so it is omitted here.**" — marked "Usborne's note." (pp. 27–28, folios 26–27). **The
  translator is on record removing material he judges ethnographic rather than narrative**, which
  matters to anyone treating this translation as a source.
- **Ranjha's stated ethic against elopement — the poem's own moral argument.** "Love does not taste
  well if it is composed of theft and stealth and abduction. You are asking me to run away like a
  thief that has been found out'" (p. 23, folio 22); and his patience doctrine, "The uttering of many
  words is folly; all ills must be borne with patience… **The patience of the heart is victorious
  over the world.** You chattering women know nothing of Love. Verily Love is a bitter herb and sour
  to eat. **Patience alone can mend the cloven sky.** Those who keep silent always succeed." (p. 23,
  folio 22).
- **Hir's argument that consent, not ceremony, constitutes marriage.** Against the Qazi's "Without
  witnesses a marriage is invalid" (p. 25, folio 24) she sets prior betrothal ("my mother betrothed
  me to Ranjha and has broken her promise", p. 25, folio 24) and an irrevocability doctrine stated in
  four images: "**The once plucked Belflower cannot be reset on the branch. Broken glass cannot be
  united.** The bones that have once been thrown in the Ganges3 cannot return. Past times can never
  come back. **The contract of love once entered on cannot be broken.**" (p. 22, folio 21).
