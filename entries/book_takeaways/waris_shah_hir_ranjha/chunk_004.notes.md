# waris_shah_hir_ranjha — chunk 004 — pp. 29–37

Nine PDF pages of a verse romance rendered as continuous English prose, with a numbered "Notes"
block closing each chapter. The chunk opens mid-chapter (the chapter whose notes close on p. 29;
the next is **CHAPTER 19**, so that is Chapter 18), then carries **CHAPTER 19 "Hir sends a message
to Ranjha"** (p. 30, folio 29), **CHAPTER 20 "Ranjha decides to become a Jogi"** (p. 31, folio 30),
**CHAPTER 21 "Ranjha leaves Tilla for Rangpur disguised as a Jogi"** (p. 34, folio 33) and
**CHAPTER 22 "Ranjha arrives at Rangpur"** (p. 35, folio 34), which runs past the chunk boundary
unfinished at the foot of p. 37. **The archive-usable material is almost entirely in the notes,
not the verse**, and the notes are the annotator's, not Waris Shah's — see the attribution problem
in Doubts before quoting any of them as "Usborne".

**Folio, as the pages actually print it — the rule holds, measured on all nine.** The running
header sets the folio at the end of the line: "Waris Shah: The Adventures of Hir and Ranjha 28"
(p. 29), "… 29" (p. 30), "… 30" (p. 31), "… 31" (p. 32), "… 32" (p. 33), "… 33" (p. 34), "… 34"
(p. 35), "… 35" (p. 36), "… 36" (p. 37). `folio = PDF page − 1` is confirmed on every page of this
chunk with **no disagreement anywhere**; nothing to record against the rule.

**Layout note, against the run sheet's expectation.** On every chapter-opening page in this chunk
the chapter heading stands at the head of **column 1**, not column 2 (pp. 30, 31, 34, 35). The
"Notes" block sits in column 2 on p. 29, opens in column 1 on p. 30 and continues into column 1 of
p. 31, and on p. 33 runs across **both** columns (notes 1–4 in col 1, notes 5–15 in col 2) before
finishing in col 1 of p. 34. Reading order within each column block is sound.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **`mausoleum-of-waris-shah`** (the book spells him **"Waris Shah"** in the running header of
  every page, and once as `Wan, Shah` [text-layer?: Waris Shah] in a note): **the date he wrote
  the poem, given by the annotator.** "To Waris Shah, who wrote his poem in `i 768`
  [text-layer?: 1768], the invasion was a recent memory (see also Chapter 8, note 4)" — the
  invasion being Nadir Shah's, "whose invasion, `In 1739` [text-layer?: in 1739], shook the Mughal
  Empire to its foundations" (p. 33, folio 32, Chapter 20 note 2). **This is the only date attached
  to Waris Shah anywhere in the chunk**; no birth year, no death year, no place, no order, no tomb
  — all (not stated). The damaged form of the composition year is the single most consequential
  flag in this chunk: see Doubts.
- **`mausoleum-of-waris-shah`**: **his own invocation is reported to name Mohiuddin Abdul Qadir
  Gilani.** "`Wan, Shah` [text-layer?: Waris Shah] himself, in speaking of Mohiuddin Abdul Qadir
  Gilani in the 'Invocation' says that he can score out even the writing of the Pen of Destiny.
  Omar Khayyam, of course, would not support such an expectation. See also Invocation, note 3."
  (p. 31, folio 30, Chapter 19 note 7). **The book does not call Waris Shah a Qadiri, or attach any
  silsila to him** — (not stated). The Invocation itself falls outside this chunk.
- **`mausoleum-of-waris-shah`**: **the book's own title, as the header prints it on all nine
  pages** — "Waris Shah: The Adventures of Hir and Ranjha" (pp. 29–37, folios 28–36). No other
  authorial statement, preface or self-reference by Waris Shah appears in the chunk.
- **`tilla-jogian`** (the book's forms are **"'Tilla'"**, **"Tilla"**, **"your hill of Tilla"** and,
  in the annotator's note, **"'Tilla Guruan' (the mound of the Gurus)"**): **the site, its resident
  guru and its lineage position.** "So Ranjha set off for 'Tilla', the hill where `Balnath'`
  [text-layer?: Balnath¹] the Jogi dwelt, for he said to himself, 'Balnath surely knows the way of
  salvation.'" (p. 31, folio 30). "At last, after many days' journeying, Ranjha reached Tilla the
  hill on which Balnath lived" (p. 31, folio 30). The note: "According to tradition, Balnath was
  the fourth Guru of 'Tilla Guruan' (the mound of the Gurus) on the banks of the river Jhelum."
  (p. 33, folio 32, Chapter 20 note 1). **This is not a bare toponym** — a named resident guru, a
  numbered position in a guru lineage, a river location and a living cult are all attached to it,
  so it takes the id. See Doubts on the principal-figure mismatch.
- **`tilla-jogian`**: **the site is reported as surviving, and the cult as still practised.** "The
  place is still preserved, and the 'Jog Mat' or the cult of the wandering mystics, with its
  emphasis on pantheism, is still practised there." (p. 33, folio 32, Chapter 20 note 1). **The
  note gives no building, structure, custodian, ʿurs, date or district** — all (not stated). It is
  the annotator's present tense, not Waris Shah's.
- **`tilla-jogian`**: **the community resident there, as the narrative describes it.** A body of
  **Chelas** under Balnath, with a "house and kitchen" they can withdraw from and a fixed term of
  service: "'For eighteen years we have been serving him. We have given up all and live by begging.
  All day and night we remember God. Yet he does not give us Jog.'" and "They left the Jogi's house
  and kitchen. They pierced the Guru with their shameless taunts." (p. 32, folio 31). They are also
  described at prayer and study on Ranjha's arrival: "They were all engaged in religious
  contemplation and prayers. They were reading Gayan Gita Bhagvat and Bharat" (p. 31, folio 30).
- **`tilla-jogian`**: **it is where the initiation happens, and the initiation is described in
  full** — see Practices below (p. 32, folio 31). Also the point Ranjha leaves from: "So Ranjha
  made haste to leave Tilla and he collected strange herbs and potent roots from the woods"
  (p. 34, folio 33), and the place he swears he would never have come to: "Had I known that you
  would try to keep me from my Love, I would never have set foot on your hill of Tilla." (p. 32,
  folio 31).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **An annual week-long congregation at a shrine, with its catchment named by district — the single
  most archive-usable sentence in the chunk.** "The people of Montgomery (now renamed Sahiwal),
  Jhang, Sheikhupura, `Lyahlpur` [text-layer?: Lyallpur] and Sargodha Districts even now congregate
  annually at this shrine and the celebrations last for more than a week." (p. 29, folio 28,
  note 4). **The note gives no month, no ʿurs name, no attendance figure and no custodian** — all
  (not stated).
- **Offerings made at tombs to secure a granted prayer.** "I long to meet him and have given
  offerings to the tomb of Hasan and Husain,' to Shudaa Ghazi and Bhola Pir that my `player`
  [text-layer?: prayer] may be fulfilled." (p. 30, folio 29). The offering is not itemized —
  (not stated).
- **Gur (raw sugar) as the offering placed before a guru, and as a social instrument.** In the
  narrative: "Ranjha bowed his head and placed a piece of gur3 before Balnath as an offering, and
  clasped the feet of all the Jogis." (p. 31, folio 30). In the note: "Gur is raw sugar. It is well
  known as a ceremonial offering in the Punjab countryside. An invitation to a wedding or other
  social occasion accompanied by `gun` [text-layer?: gur] cannot be trifled with." (p. 33, folio
  32, note 3).
- **The initiation into Jog, itemized rite by rite.** "They obeyed the orders of their Guru and
  brought Balnath the earrings as he had told them, and the razor wherewith to shave Ranjha. And
  the Guru took off Ranjha's clothes and having rubbed him in ashes and embraced him, made him sit
  by his side. Then he took the razor of separation and shaved him completely. Then he bored his
  ears and put earrings on him. He gave him the beggar's bowl, the rosary, the horn and the shell
  in his hands, and made him learn the word `Allah.1` [text-layer?: Allah¹⁴]." (p. 32, folio 31).
  The moral rule taught with it: "'An old woman should be treated as your mother and a young woman
  as your sister.'" (p. 32, folio 31).
- **The conditions of Jog as Balnath states them — a full list of renunciations.** "You will have to
  dress as a Jogi, to wear dirty clothes, long hair, cropped skull and to beg your way through
  life. You will have to give up the pleasures of birth, to cease to rejoice when friends come or
  to grieve when they die. You will have to abstain from casting eyes on women. You will have to
  become divinely intoxicated by taking kand, mul, post, opium and other narcotic drugs.'"
  (p. 31, folio 30).
- **Guru/Chela and Murshid/Murid set out as parallel institutions.** "A Murshid is a spiritual
  teacher." (p. 33, folio 32, note 4). "Guru — the Hindu holy man, the teacher, the Hindu
  counterpart of the Muslim Murshid. Gurus have Chelas' and Murshids have Murids', although the
  bonds of spiritual subjugation are similar in both cases." (p. 33, folio 32, note 5). In the
  verse: "'Make me a fakir. Let me be your chela and be my Pir. The straight Path to God is
  inaccessible without the intervention of Murshids4 even as rice cannot be cooked without milk.'"
  (p. 31, folio 30).
- **Cross-confessional discipleship, stated as normal for its time.** "Here Ranjha, a Muslim,
  becomes a spiritual disciple of Balnath, a Hindu yogi. This may seem strange today, but was by no
  means unusual at the time." (p. 33, folio 32, note 4).
- **The mendicant economy, from the recruiting pitch.** "'Brothers come with me and be a fakir. You
  have nothing to do but beg and eat. You get your ears bored and put some ashes on your body and
  all the world reveres you as a saint. Without toil or labour you can be as eminent as `Nadir
  Shah?` [text-layer?: Nadir Shah²] The mysteries of birth and the sadness of death have no concern
  for `the fair` [text-layer?: the fakir]. He sleeps in the mosque free from the cares of this
  impure world and begs and eats. He can scold people and incur no blame. If you call yourself a
  Pir or a fakir everybody is your servant.'" (p. 31, folio 30). The annotator's comment on the same
  passage: "The desire to achieve social or spiritual eminence by living on other people is an
  eternal temptation for those who suffer from ergophobia." (p. 33, folio 32, note 2).
- **Alms in kind, and the fakir's reputation staked on what he will accept.** "So Ranjha took up his
  beggar's bowl and went from door to door, playing his shell and crying, 'You mistress of the
  courtyard, give alms, give alms.' Some gave him flour, others bread, others dishes of food. They
  asked for his blessings and he invoked blessings upon them. Some said, 'We shall acquire holiness
  through the power of his intercession.'" (p. 37, folio 36). Against it, the sceptics: "Said
  another, See he takes wheat `flower` [text-layer?: flour] and butter, but will not touch millet or
  bread crusts. He is chasing the women and is no real fakir.'" (p. 37, folio 36).
- **Amulets, talismans and divination as the Jogi's trade.** "Ranjha made all the girls sit close to
  him and told them to fetch freshly broken potsherds from the kiln. On them he drew mysterious
  lines and designs. Some he told to wear them round their necks, others to bind them round their
  loins. Others he told to put them in pitchers of water and make all the family drink them."
  (p. 36, folio 35). And "He set up as a conjurer and gave some of them charmed threads7 and lucky
  knots." (p. 37, folio 36). The clientele and their complaints are itemized: fathers- and
  mothers-in-law, husbands who "beat `then` [text-layer?: them]", unkind neighbours, sons gone "to a
  distant country", and the pain of love (p. 36, folio 35).
- **The Jogi's kit and public appearance, as villagers describe it.** "'Mother,' said they, 'a `Jog1`
  [text-layer?: Jogi] has come to our village with rings in his ears. He has a beggar's bowl in his
  hand and a necklace of beads round his neck. He has long hair like a Juggler. His eyes are red and
  shine with the brilliance of fire; sometimes he plays on the "King"2 and weeps. At other times he
  plays on the Nad and laughs. He calls on God day and night. He is the chela of Balnath'" (p. 36,
  folio 35). **The note glossing "King" is outside this chunk** — (not stated).
- **Narcotics in fakiri practice, with the annotator's qualification.** "Narcotic drugs are not an
  essential part of the theory or practice of Jog, but certainly common practice both among Hindus
  and Muslims who profess to be faqirs. See also Introduction. note 24." (p. 33, folio 32, note 7).
  And on the verse's bhang-and-opium simile: "The sole reason for this strange comparison is
  Balnath's familiarity with opium and bhang (hemp) as supposed `aid,` [text-layer?: aids] to
  spiritual contemplation." (p. 33, folio 32, note 11). In the verse: "'He is as it were the spirit
  of bhang and `opium?'` [text-layer?: opium¹¹]" (p. 31, folio 30); and Ranjha later, "'I eat kand
  and mul,3'" (p. 36, folio 35).
- **Village literacy and the Mullah as scribe.** "he called the Mullah to him and said 'Write6 for
  me to Hir'" and "'Write it down, Sir Mullah, every bit. Omit no part of my complaint.'" (p. 30,
  folio 29). The note: "The village Mullah was probably the only person in the village who could
  read and write. Ranjha is obviously illiterate like his sweetheart." (p. 31, folio 30, note 6).
- **"Darbar" and "Court" used of God, and glossed.** "He closed his eyes in the Darbar18 of God and
  uttered this prayer" (p. 33, folio 32), with note 18 reading simply "Court." (p. 34, folio 33);
  "The Five Pirs also prayed in the Court of God19" and "there came a reply from the Darbar of God"
  (p. 33, folio 32).
- **State power reaching into the shrine relation — a single striking line.** Ranjha threatens the
  Guru: "'Set my ears right or I will bring the sepoy of the Sirkar15 here.'" (p. 32, folio 31),
  glossed "Government. The word 'Sirkar' is also an old form of address for kings and nobles. It is
  still applied by sycophants to lesser luminaries.." (pp. 33–34, folios 32–33, note 15).
- **Wedding and henna custom, in the chunk's longest note.** "Brides and bridegrooms still have the
  palms of their hands and the soles of their feet tinted with henna, which retains its importance.
  The 'Suhag Pura' which is a paper packet tied with a ceremonial red-and-white tape, and is sent
  from the bridegroom's house to that of the bride, contains henna as a symbol of matrimonial
  happiness which is used to tint the bride's hands and feet." (p. 33, folio 32, note 12).
- **A charitable distribution named and described.** "Saucepan is probably a reference to 'koondah',
  an outsize dish which `i5` [text-layer?: is] filled with sweet preparations and distributed to the
  poor `hi` [text-layer?: in] the name of members of the Prophets family and his descendants."
  (p. 29, folio 28, note 3).
- **Village games recorded as vanishing, twice.** "Some of these games were still known at the
  beginning of the twentieth century. They are now dying out and the fun and frolic of the old days
  `i5` [text-layer?: is] giving place to more modern and less innocent amusements." (p. 29, folio 28,
  note 2). The games are named in the verse: "No one plays Kilbari8 or Samsni and makes the earth
  dance. No one hunts for needles or plays "Walan"9 No one plays Maya or makes crows or peacocks
  fly. No one sings Choratori or plays Garidda in the street." (p. 37, folio 36), with "Not a girl
  sings at her spinning wheel" as the mark of "a ruined village" — **the notes glossing these games
  fall outside this chunk** — (not stated). Spinning parties as a social institution: "'We will show
  you the place where the girls sit in their spinning parties.'" (p. 37, folio 36).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **A Pir summoned by prayer, appearing at once and inflicting bodily punishment.** "Hir in her
  distress prayed to her Pir6 The Pir at once appeared and Hir said, 'I am the betrothed of Ranjha.
  My love is pledged to him.' So the Pir chastised Saida, broke his bones and tied up his hands and
  feet.7" (p. 29, folio 28). The annotator's two comments on it are sceptical: "Many people still
  pray to the Pirs more readily than to God." (note 6) and "What an effective protector the Pir must
  be!" (note 7) (p. 29, folio 28).
- **The Five Pirs appear to Hir and produce an apparition of Ranjha.** "When the Five Pirs saw Hir
  sitting in devout meditation they at once appeared by the order of God.8 They awakened her by
  placing their hand on her shoulder" (p. 29, folio 28); "The `Pin` [text-layer?: Pirs] were
  overcome with compassion, and using their spiritual power of ecstasy, they presented the image
  -of Ranjha before her, saying, 'Child, spread out your skirt and receive the object of your
  wish.'" (p. 29, folio 28). The annotator: "God `i5` [text-layer?: is] dragged in here quite
  unnecessarily." (p. 29, folio 28, note 8).
- **A prayer answered audibly from the Darbar of God.** Balnath "prayed and poured ashes over his
  body and plunged his soul into the deep waters of meditation"; "Then there came a reply from the
  Darbar of God Hir has been bestowed on Ranjha and his boat has been taken ashore.'" (p. 33, folio
  32). The annotator: "These may appear to be strange proceedings, but God here has obviously been
  modelled on the absolute and arbitrary monarchs of the time!" (p. 34, folio 33, note 19).
- **The Guru's enchantment subdues the rebellious Chelas.** "Whereupon the Guru rebuked them and his
  anger blazed from his eyes. The Chelas instantly obeyed him, so powerful was the enchantment that
  the Guru laid upon them. All ill feeling vanished from their minds." (p. 32, folio 31).
- **The killing of the wolf, taken by its witness as proof of sainthood.** "The Jogi called upon the
  Five `Pin` [text-layer?: Pirs] who supported him in the encounter. He smote the wolf with his
  beggar's bowl and the wolf fell to the ground like a log. Then the Jogi pierced him with his
  `fakirs tongs` [text-layer?: fakir's tongs] and brought the body to the shepherd who was amazed at
  the sight. So the shepherd was convinced that the Jogi was a perfect saint and endowed with
  miraculous power and he fell at his feet in supplication." (p. 35, folio 34).
- **Power over destiny attributed to Pirs, Fakirs and Jogis, and reported as a current belief.**
  "According to popular superstition in the Punjab countryside `Pin` [text-layer?: Pirs], Fakirs and
  Jogis can, with, the force of their piety, make the Moving Finger of Destiny cancel what it has

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The annotator's running scepticism about pir-devotion is a stance, not an aside.** Four notes in
  this chunk deflate the narrative's supernatural machinery: "Many people still pray to the Pirs
  more readily than to God." (note 6), "What an effective protector the Pir must be!" (note 7),
  "God `i5` [text-layer?: is] dragged in here quite unnecessarily." (note 8) — all p. 29, folio 28 —
  and "God here has obviously been modelled on the absolute and arbitrary monarchs of the time!"
  (p. 34, folio 33, note 19). **Any fact drawn from these notes carries that stance with it.**
- **Hindu–Muslim interpenetration is the annotator's repeated thesis, stated four times.** (i) "Here
  Ranjha, a Muslim, becomes a spiritual disciple of Balnath, a Hindu yogi. This may seem strange
  today, but was by no means unusual at the time." (note 4). (ii) "The Vedantists say the world
  `i5` [text-layer?: is] 'Maya' or illusion. This is essentially a Hindu doctrine, but it has `bad`
  [text-layer?: had] a profound influence on the Muslim mystics of India." (note 8). (iii) "Song of
  Nonentity — this is the doctrine of annihilation in God (fana fillah) as practised by the Muslim
  Sufis, which has strong spiritual affiliations with the 'Nirvana' of the Hindus and Buddhists."
  (note 10). (iv) "The Hindu Balnath teaching Ranjha the name of Allah is a reminder of the Bhakti
  Movement which was the result of the impact of Islam on Hinduism" (note 14). All p. 33, folio 32.
- **The annotator corrects Usborne by name — the clearest evidence that the notes are not
  Usborne's.** "Mehndhi — `Ulsborne` [text-layer?: Usborne] thought it was indigo, which is
  incorrect. Mehndhi is the Henna plant or Blood Egyptian Privet, `Lawsonia. Alka`
  [text-layer?: Lawsonia Alba], or Lawsonia Inermis, or Lawsonia Spinosa" (p. 33, folio 32, note
  12). See Doubts: this bears directly on how every note in the book must be attributed.
- **The annotator dates himself after Partition.** Note 4 places the Ghazi Pir shrine "in West
  Pakistan" and glosses "Montgomery (now renamed Sahiwal)" (p. 29, folio 28); note 2 says the games
  "were still known at the beginning of the twentieth century" and "are now dying out" (p. 29, folio
  28). **The chunk gives no date of publication or of the annotation** — (not stated).
- **Literary-critical judgements on the poem's consistency, four of them.** "This unfilial sentiment
  is hardly in harmony with the 'folded hands' and the 'garments of humility' with which the message
  is prefaced, but this apparent inconsistency merely reflects the conflict between `fir's`
  [text-layer?: Hir's] regard for her parents and the painful way she feels they have let her down."
  (p. 30, folio 29, note 1). "Ranjha's speech is full of inconsistencies. `lie` [text-layer?: he]
  begins by accusing Balnath of captivating young men and ensnaring them, and ends up by calling him
  the only true Guru in the world. These are obviously good tactics, as Ranjha does succeed in
  having his way with the Guru." (p. 34, folio 33, note 17). Also notes 6 and 16 (p. 33–34, folios
  32–33), above.
- **The poem's own argument about fakiri, put in Ranjha's mouth and then punctured.** Ranjha admits
  the vocation was instrumental: "'Had I been only a lover of God, I should have sought only Him…
  Hir has captivated my heart. That is why I have become a Jogi. I have become a fakir only that I
  might keep my Love in remembrance.'" (p. 32, folio 31). Balnath's verdict: "'Verily I repent and
  am sorry for having given Jog to this youth. He has got the treasures of jog without spending a
  single farthing.'" (p. 32, folio 31).
- **Jog defined in the poem's own terms.** "'Jog means to be dead while alive. One has to sing the
  song of nonentity10 using ones meagre body as a guitar. One's self has to be entirely absorbed. It
  is no child's play.'" and "'God has made his abode in this body of dust He is in every thing as a
  thread runs through the beads.'" (p. 31, folio 30). The three planes: "'I have not reached the
  degree of Jog called Chit Akas after passing through the stages of Bhut Kas and Juda Kas.13'"
  (p. 32, folio 31), glossed only as "The three different planes of Jog Philosophy." (p. 33, folio
  32, note 13).
- **Caste and vocation asserted against the fakir's role.** "'What can a Jat do with a beggars bowl
  or horn, whose heart is set only on ploughing? What is the good of teaching him to tell his beads
  when all he can do is to tell the tale of his cattle?'" (p. 32, folio 31); Balnath earlier: "'You
  Jats cannot attain Jog.'" (p. 31, folio 30); and "'We Jats are cunning strategists and we use all
  means to compass our hearts' desire.'" (p. 32, folio 31).

## Doubts

**Damage-flag convention used here:** printed form first, then `[text-layer?: my reading]`. Every
damaged form is both flagged on the word inline and inventoried below, per the protocol's interim
convention (b) for a `text_layer` route. **62 distinct damaged forms are flagged.**

- **The note attribution is unresolved and it affects every note-derived fact above.** Two notes in
  this chunk consist of nothing but an attribution: "9. Usborne's note." (p. 29, folio 28) and
  "5 Usborne's note." (p. 31, folio 30). I cannot tell from the chunk whether these are the texts of
  notes 9 and 5 (i.e. the marker in the verse points to a note that says only "this is Usborne's")
  or a compositor's label attached to the adjacent note. What is certain is that **the bulk of the
  notes are NOT Usborne's**: note 12 corrects him by name — "`Ulsborne` [text-layer?: Usborne]
  thought it was indigo, which is incorrect" (p. 33, folio 32) — and notes 2 and 4 write from after
  Partition ("in West Pakistan", "now renamed Sahiwal", p. 29, folio 28). **I have therefore
  attributed notes to "the annotator" throughout rather than to Usborne. A human must settle who the
  annotator is, from the front matter, before any note-derived fact enters an entry.**
- **`tilla-jogian` is mapped on the site, not on the row's principal figure, and the figures do not
  match.** The archive row's principal figure is "Guru Gorakhnath (Nath yogis)" (Salt Range, near
  Dina/Jhelum). **Gorakhnath is never named anywhere in this chunk.** The book's guru of Tilla is
  **Balnath**, "the fourth Guru of 'Tilla Guruan' (the mound of the Gurus) on the banks of the river
  Jhelum" (p. 33, folio 32). What supports the mapping: the name Tilla, the river Jhelum, the "mound
  of the Gurus", the Nath/"Jog Mat" cult, and a hilltop guru's seat with resident chelas. What does
  not: the book never writes "Tilla Jogian", never says Salt Range or Dina, and never names
  Gorakhnath. The competing Nath row is `gorakhnath-goraknath-temple` (Gor Khatri, Peshawar), which
  the Jhelum location excludes. **A human should confirm that "Tilla"/"Tilla Guruan" is the
  `tilla-jogian` row before its facts are used; if it is reversed, six bullets move.**
- **"Mian Lal Hosain Shahbeg" is deliberately NOT mapped, and this is the live trap in this chunk.**
  The name could be read toward `madho-lal-hussain` (row name "Shrine of Hazrat Madho Lal Hussain
  (Shah Hussain Darbar)", principal figure "Shah Hussain (Madho Lal Hussain)", Baghbanpura, Lahore,
  d. 1599, Malamati) on the strength of "Lal Hosain"/"Lal Hussain" and the bangle-wearing. **But
  every particular the book gives points away from it:** "Shahbeg" is not in the row's name; the
  book calls him "the traditional guru of sweepers", "popularly known among the sweeping fraternity
  as Gugga" (p. 35, folio 34, note 1); and the row's own distinguishing marks — Madho, Baghbanpura,
  1599, Malamati — are all (not stated) here. **Do not resolve this from the chunk.** If Rauf rules
  it is the same figure, it takes `madho-lal-hussain`; if not, it is a candidate new row.
