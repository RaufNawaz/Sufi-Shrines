## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **`mausoleum-of-waris-shah`** (the book's form is **"Waris Shah"** in the running header on all
  ten pages, and **"Waris"** in the poem's own signature line): **the date and place of
  composition, in the poet's own voice, in the epilogue.** "When love came to me I felt a desire to
  write this story in verse. I wrote it in the year 1180 Hijri in the southern country. 2" (p. 56,
  folio 55). The date is printed **only as "1180 Hijri"**, in Latin script — no Hijri numerals, no
  CE equivalent, and the note marker `2` that would gloss "the southern country" falls outside this
  chunk. **No birth or death year for the poet appears anywhere in the range** — (not stated).
- **`mausoleum-of-waris-shah`**: **a named place of composition, and it is not the archive row's
  place.** "When I produced the tale among learned men it became known to the world. Waris, those
  who have recited the Holy Kalima have attained salvation. **Kharal Hans is a well known place.
  Here I composed this story.**" (p. 56, folio 55). **Jandiala Sher Khan, the archive row's
  location, is never named in this chunk** — (not stated). See Doubts on "Kharal Hans".
- **`mausoleum-of-waris-shah`**: **Kasur, named as the one place he is anxious for.** "Of all the
  wretched Punjab I am most concerned for Kasur. I am concerned for my faith and conscience even as
  Moses was frightened on Holy Sinai." (p. 56, folio 55). The sentence breaks off into "Ghazis" at
  the end of the chunk. **No shrine, tomb, teacher or patron is attached to Kasur here** — the
  patrons and the teacher the briefing expects are further into the epilogue, past p. 56.
- **`mausoleum-of-waris-shah`**: **his own claim for the poem, made twice and immodestly.** "Poets,
  you should determine the worth of any poem. I have let my horse loose in the arena. Other poets
  have wasted their efforts in writing on petty themes.3 I have composed a grand poem.'" (p. 56,
  folio 55); and, closing Chapter 32, "Only the poet's poetry remains m [text-layer?] [in]
  everlasting remembrance, for no one has written such a beautiful Hir." (p. 55, folio 54).
- **`mausoleum-of-waris-shah`**: **the poem declared to have an inner sense, by the poet.** "Oh
  wise man, you should note that there is a secret under the guise of my words. I have written this
  Hir with care and meditation. Young people read it with pleasure. I have planted a flower to give
  a sweet savour. Thank God my purpose is achieved. I have worked at it anxiously day and night."
  (p. 56, folio 55). He follows it with a self-abasement: "I have no capital of good works. Of what
  can I be proud? I have no hope without Thy grace. I am only a poor sinner. Without the favour of
  the Prophet I am helpless." (p. 56, folio 55).
- **`mausoleum-of-waris-shah`**: **"Bhag Bhari" named in the editor's note, in connection with the
  poet's own life.** The editor's note 2 to Chapter 29 reads: "Waris Shah is so intent upon bringing
  about a union of true lovers that he does not hesitate even to invoke the Almighty's own
  interference in their favour. Inwardly, he must have wished for some such miracle for himself and
  **Bhag Bhari** as well, but it does not seem to have happened." (p. 51, folio 50). This is the
  **editor's** inference, explicitly hedged ("must have wished", "does not seem"), and the chunk
  gives **no** account of who Bhag Bhari was — (not stated).
- **`shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar`** (the book's form here is the bare epithet **"Shakarganj"**,
  never "Farid", never "Ganjshakar", never "Pakpattan"): **named as one of the Five Pirs, by a

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Relics of named saints, carried and used as instruments of power — the chunk's densest
  archive-usable passage.** "at midnight he remembered the Five Pirs. He kissed the handkerchief of
  Shakarganj and took the ring of Lal Shahbaz; he smelt the sweet savour that came from the cudgel
  of Sayyad Jalal of Bokhara, -and he grasped the dagger given him by Makhdum Jabnian
  [text-layer?] [Jahanian]." (p. 50, folio 49). Two of the four are then used as weapons: "Ranjha
  took the cudgel of Jahanian and thrust it into the side of the lion and he drove the dagger of
  Sayyid Jalal Bokhari into his belly." (p. 52, folio 51). **Four relic types named — handkerchief,
  ring, cudgel, dagger — each tied to a different named saint.**
- **The fakir's hut as a sanctified space, with a shoe rule and a physical expulsion.** "'You have
  come into my holy hut with your shoes on. You have profaned this holy place.' And he thrust him
  out and beat him even as cattle are thrust out of the cattle-pen. He dealt so severely with Saida
  that he was covered with blood'2" (p. 49, folio 48). The editor comments on the conduct rather
  than the rule: "Strange proceedings on the part of a faqir even though a fake one, although quite
  understandable from a jealous rival, faqir or no faqir." (p. 50, folio 49).
- **The healing economy around a possessed or stricken woman, itemized and costed.** "We have
  consulted Mullahs and physicians and hakims and they cost much money." (p. 48, folio 47); "the
  Kheras brought hundreds of fakirs and hakims and enchanters and they gave her cunning drugs. They
  brought Tiriak10 for snakes from Hazara and amulets and incense. They supplied milk of AK to the
  wound, powdered metals and curds of milk which no woman or man had ever cast eyes upon. **They
  spent bags of money tying [text-layer?] [trying] to cure the bride.**" (p. 49, folio 48). The
  editor glosses only the drug: "Tiriak — Tiryaq or antidote." (p. 50, folio 49).
- **The holy man's terms of service, set out as a list of ritual requirements.** "Meanwhile, Sehti
  took charge of the Jogi and lodged him in the hut belonging to the village minstrel. And he gave
  his orders that bread must be cooked for the holy man. 'No man or woman must come near or cast
  their shadow on it. A separate place must be prepared and Hir's couch placed on it. I will burn
  incense at night. I will read enchantments over her. None must be allowed to come near her as the
  snake is a powerful1 one and his bite is difficult to cure. Only Sehti may come; only a virgin
  girl must be allowed to cross the threshold.' And the Kheras did as the Jogi bade them" (p. 50,
  folio 49). **Lodging, cooked bread, purity of the food from shadow, a separated enclosure,
  incense, recitation, and a virginity condition on access — all in one passage.**
- **Approaching a holy man: the posture of petition.** "Go to him at once as a submissive subject
  with a present in your hand, for a new governor has been appointed to rule over us." (p. 47, folio
  46); "'Son, brides are precious things. Go to the fakir and salaam him with folded hands.'"
  (p. 49, folio 48); "So Ajju went and stood before the Jogi with folded hands and besought him"
  (p. 50, folio 49); "And Sehti said, 'Father, you should go yourself to the Jogi. Perhaps Saida
  stood with pride before him and not with proper humility.'" (p. 50, folio 49). **Rank matters to
  the outcome: the son is beaten, the father is granted.**
- **Oath-taking, with a knife and a drawn square.** "Whereupon the Jogi drew a square on the ground
  and thrust a knife therein and said, 'Sit down Jat, and swear on the Quran that you have never
  touched Hir.' He put the knife to his throat and made him swear and Saida swore saying, 'May I be
  a leper if I ever touched Hir.'" (p. 49, folio 48).
- **Marriage: rites, witnesses, and the legal test the Qazi applies.** "'We took a marriage
  procession and brought back our bride and spent munch [text-layer?] [much] money. Thousands of
  people, Hindus and Mohammadans, were present at the marriage ceremony. The proper rites were
  performed. The Mullah read the Quran and witnesses were present. The whole countryside knows she
  was given to us in marriage.'" (pp. 52–53, folios 51–52). The Qazi's test: "'Fakir, have you got
  any witnesses? **Without witnesses to the marriage she can be no wife.**'" (p. 53, folio 52).
  Ranjha's counter-claim is a marriage in another register: "'On the day our souls said yes, I was
  betrothed to Hir. In the Tablet of Destiny, God has written the union of our souls8'" (p. 53,
  folio 52), which the editor glosses "A marriage made in heaven. It i5 [text-layer?] [is] a pity
  such unions are not always recognised on earth!" (p. 54, folio 53). Also claimed: "The Five Pir
  gave us in marriage." (p. 52, folio 51).
- **Bride price, stated and then denied by the editor.** "But this bride whom we purchased5
  [text-layer?] [marker for note 3] with so much difficulty is the beginning of our misfortune."
  (p. 48, folio 47) — against which the editor's note 3 argues: "'Purchase' here is only a strong
  expression used by Sehti to lend emphasis to her point, and is not to be taken literally."
  (p. 50, folio 49). **Recorded side by side as the edition prints them.**
- **Betrothal custom, as the editor describes it.** "While boys and girls in the rural Punjab move
  about freely to do their daily work and can see or talk to each other, it is not usual for them to
  do so if they are betrothed to be married. After the 'mangni' or betrothal, a girl does not see her
  fiancé until marriage" (p. 48, folio 47).
- **The barber as envoy, and compensation in kind.** "Now at the same time a barber came up on an
  embassy from the Kheras to demand back Hir" (p. 54, folio 53); the Sials' reply, ending "'You have
  ruined our daughter. **We will take in exchange a girl from you by way of satisfaction.**"' And
  they sent back the barber with taunts saying, 'Do not come again on an embassy to us." (p. 54,
  folio 53).
- **Courts, bribes and the price of justice.** "'I am a poor fakir and have no money to give to the
  officers in whose hands the decision lies. He has Hir and l [text-layer?] [I] have the pain'"

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Pir Bahauddin shakes the earth and a voice opens the way** — "And Pit [text-layer?] [Pir]
  Bahauddin shook the earth, and the way was opened unto Ranjha, and a voice spoke, 'Jat, arise, go
  on your way.'" (p. 50, folio 49). Told flat by the narrator, with no hedge.
- **The Five Pirs come "in the twinkling of an eye"** and advise Ranjha against the lion: "'Go to,
  my son, arid [text-layer?] [and] you will be victorious. Abandon all pride and beseech the lion
  with entreaty. And if he will not listen to your entreaty, slay him.'" (p. 51, folio 50).
- **The Five Pirs' prayer is answered and Murad is transported to Sehti** — "And the Five Pirs
  prayed, 'Oh God unite the girl to her lover.' So God2 showed his kindness and Murad, her lover
  stood before her." (p. 51, folio 50). Murad's own account is of an enchantment: "'Some spell or
  enchantment fell on me, someone caught the nose string of my camel and brought me to your door…
  Then a voice from heaven came into my ear; my camel heard it and grunted. She sped as quick as an
  arrow or a storm wind. My string of camels has been lost. You have exercised some sorcery over
  me.'" (p. 51, folio 50). **The editor treats this as the poet's own doing, not as a miracle** —
  see the Bhag Bhari note (p. 51, folio 50), and "An Arab poet seems to have arranged it
  differently" (p. 51, folio 50).
- **The speaking lion, and its killing with the relics of two named saints** — "And the lion
  replied, 'Ranjha listen to me. For the last seven days, I have not had food… Now God has sent me a
  victim.'" (p. 51, folio 50); killed with the cudgel of Jahanian and the dagger of Sayyid Jalal
  Bokhari (p. 52, folio 51); "And ten [text-layer?] [then] Ranjha skinned the lion and put his nails
  and flesh in his wallet." (p. 52, folio 51).
- **The city burns at the lovers' curse — the chunk's largest karamat, and it is attributed to God,
  not to a saint.** Hir: "'0 [text-layer?] [O] God, consume this city with fire. Let your wrath fall
  on this city even as it fell on Pharaoh whom you drowned'" (p. 53, folio 52); Ranjha: "'Put the
  city to fire. Burn the whole city, save only the herds and the cattle.'" (p. 53, folio 52); the
  event: "**See the power of God. Owing to the sighs of the lovers, the city caught lire
  [text-layer?] [fire].** Fire broke out in all four quarters of the city." (p. 53, folio 52). The
  diagnosis is professional: "So the astrologers cast their lots and said to the Raja, The pens of
  your officials are free from sin. But God has listened to the sighs of the lovers… Fire has
  descended from Heaven and it has burnt the palaces, forts and ditches of the city" (p. 53, folio
  52).
- **A miracle reported at second hand, before the chunk opens** — "obtained the help of the Five
  Pirs and he has shown Hir his power by a miracle. **I have seen each miracle of his more wonderful
  than the last.** It is as if Christ had corn [text-layer?] [come] down from Heaven to earth.'l"
  (p. 47, folio 46), with the editor's note: "The Second Coming of Christ on earth to reform a
  sinful world is part of Muslim legend." (p. 48, folio 47).
- **Omen** — "as he went to the house of Ajju a partridge sang on the right for good luck." (p. 50,
  folio 49). Immediately undercut by the narrator: "Thus the Kheras themselves worked their own
  destruction and shaved their own beards. The wolf had been called in to guard the flock." (p. 50,
  folio 49).
- **The fake snakebite, and the editor's two attempts to explain the snake away** — the trick: "one
  of them tonic [text-layer?] [took] a sharp thorn from an acacia bush and pricked Hir's foot Sehti
  bit it with her teeth and caused blood to flow, and they pretended Hir had been bitten by a snake"
  (p. 49, folio 48); "Never was such a crafty swindle found in any book. They shaved the very beard
  -of Plato.9 Satan came and salaamed and said, 'I have been outmatched by these girls.'" (p. 49,
  folio 48). The editor: "It must be presumed to have ken [text-layer?] [been] an imaginary snake
  with very slow poison." (p. 50, folio 49); and "The snake could hardly have been a powerful one; a
  powerful snake would have killed her long before." (p. 51, folio 50).
- **The Jogi's flute and command over snakes, as Sehti advertises it** — "'There is a very cunning
  Jogi in the Kalabagh garden in whose flute there are thousands of spells. Cobras and Krites
  [text-layer?] [Kraits] bow down before him and hooded snakes and crested snakes stand in awe of
  him. All evil spirits and Jinns" fly away at his word.'" (p. 49, folio 48), the editor glossing
  only "Jinns — Genie." (p. 50, folio 49).
- **Destiny as the governing power, stated by the Jogi and by the narrator** — "'Who can avoid
  destiny? Snakes bite according to the decree of destiny.'" (p. 49, folio 48); "Destiny
  overwhelmed both the lovers." (p. 52, folio 51); "'The hour of destiny has arrived. We had hoped
  otherwise but no one can escape the destiny of death. Even as it is written in the Holy Quran,
  "Everything is mortal save only God."" (p. 55, folio 54). The editor objects: "This is a highly
  deterministic view of human action" (p. 56, folio 55).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The edition has two layers of annotation and says so.** Note 4 to Chapter 28 breaks off mid-note
  with "**(Usborne's note)**" and then continues in a second voice (p. 50, folio 49), and note 2 to
  Chapter 30 writes "'The Lord of Pirs' **notes Usborne**" before adding the Gilani identification
  (p. 52, folio 51). **So in this chunk the identifications that matter to the archive — Pir Dastgir
  = Abdul Qadir Gilani, the Qadiriyya, the Baghdad shrine — sit in the later editor's layer, not in
  Usborne's**, and neither layer is Waris Shah's. Attribute accordingly.
- **The editor repeatedly rationalizes the marvellous instead of reporting it.** The snake is
  "presumed to have ken [text-layer?] [been] an imaginary snake with very slow poison" (p. 50, folio
  49) and "could hardly have been a powerful one" (p. 51, folio 50); the books of curses are "only
  metaphors. There are no such books." (p. 50, folio 49); the bride's "purchase" is "not to be taken
  literally" (p. 50, folio 49); Raja Adali "has probably been invented by the poet" (p. 53, folio
  52); "If Satan ever had a grandmother!" (p. 50, folio 49).
- **The editor reads one of the poem's references as courtly tactics** — the Ravan–Sita comparison is
  "intended to curry favour with the Raja, who is a Hindu." (p. 54, folio 53).
- **The editor's own historical argument, volunteered in a note about elephants** — "Indeed, a
  number of decisive battles In [text-layer?] [in] Indian history are battles between the horse and
  the elephant, a fast-moving, quick manoeuvring mobile force defeating a slow and stationary
  opponent" (p. 50, folio 49).
- **The editor sets tribal honour against religion, quoting Shakespeare to do it** — note 7 to
  Chapter 32 gives "As flies to wanton boys are we to the gods / They kill us for their sport." and
  continues: "**Tribal pride was apparently more powerful than the laws of God. This is a frequent
  phenomenon.**" (p. 56, folio 55).
- **The editor defends the poet against a modern objection** — "The word property' is used here in
  the sense of something held dear. **Ranjha is not trying to speak of women as chattels.**" (p. 56,
  folio 55), on "'Is my property safe?"'" (p. 55, folio 54).
- **Waris Shah's own closing judgement on the world and on poetry** — "Poets and sinners offer
  counsel in the world. The counsel of the wise is held of no account. No one speaks the truth.
  Lying has become the custom of the world." (p. 56, folio 55); and "The world is but a play and
  fields and forests all will melt away in the final day of dissolution. Only the poet's poetry
  remains m [text-layer?] [in] everlasting remembrance" (p. 55, folio 54).
