# waris_shah_hir_ranjha — chunk 005 — pp. 38–46

Nine pages of Usborne's English prose rendering of the *qissa*, covering the close of the chapter
in which the disguised Ranjha quarrels with Sehti, then **CHAPTER 23 "Ranjha meets Hir"** (opens
p. 40), **CHAPTER 24 "Sehti quarrels with the Jogi and turns him out of the house"** (opens p. 42),
**CHAPTER 25 "Ranjha retires to Kalabagh"** (opens p. 44), **CHAPTER 26 "Sehti and Hir make
friends"** (opens p. 45) and **CHAPTER 27 "Sehti takes Ranjha's message to Hir and Hir meets
Ranjha in the garden"** (opens p. 46, running into the next chunk). This is narrative verse in
prose translation; the archive material is almost entirely in the numbered notes, which print in
blocks at each chapter's end (pp. 39–40, 41, 43–44, 45, 46).

**Folio, as the pages actually print it.** The running header is `Waris Shah: The Adventures of Hir
and Ranjha <n>` on all nine pages, and **`folio = PDF − 1` holds nine times out of nine**: p. 38
prints 37, p. 39 prints 38, p. 40 prints 39, p. 41 prints 40, p. 42 prints 41, p. 43 prints 42,
p. 44 prints 43, p. 45 prints 44, p. 46 prints 45. No page in this chunk disagrees with the rule.

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Tilla** — "He went to Tilla and got his ears bored and became a Jogi. He has today entered your
  village." (p. 41, folio 40). This is the Jogi initiation place of the whole romance, but **no
  shrine, tomb, saint, ʿurs or custodian is named there**, and the book never writes "Tilla
  Jogian". Held here under the interim convention; see Doubts for the row it would take on a
  reversal. It is the closest call in the chunk.
- **Kasur** — "he smote his assailants even as the Pathans of Kasur looted the camp of Bakshi.11"
  (p. 43, folio 42); the editor glosses only "This is presumably a reference to some contemporary
  event." (p. 44, folio 43). A military simile with no saint or shrine attached. See Doubts.
- **Lahore** — three bare mentions, all secular. "lie [text-layer?] [he] was as handsome as the
  subadar12 of Lahore" (p. 38, folio 37), glossed "Governor of Lahore. In the old days, Governors
  and other high dignitaries were expected to be handsome, and were in any case supposed to be so
  by those who wanted to flatter them and obtain favours." (p. 39, folio 38). And "Look at her
  showing off! her airs and graces like a prostitute of Lahore5" (p. 42, folio 41), glossed
  "Lalhore [text-layer?] [Lahore] has always been a great centre of civilisation and culture,
  Lahore courtesan, were famous for their beauty and cultivated manner.." (p. 43, folio 42).
- **Kalabagh** — the garden of Ranjha's retirement and austerities, and the site of the chunk's one
  divine voice. Chapter title "Ranjha retires to Kalabagh" (p. 44, folio 43); "and lie
  [text-layer?] [he] collected ashes from the hearth and sat down on a hillock in the garden of

  the garden in Kalabagh" (p. 44, folio 43); Hir's boast "I will subdue the garden of Kalabagh and
  levy tribute on Jog." (p. 45, folio 44). **No archive row carries this name.**
- **Ghunian / Chunian, Abu Samand, Abdus Saman, Nawab Hosain Khan** — "they broke his head with
  milk pots and crushed him even as Abu Samand fell on Nawab Hosain Khan at Ghunian."" (p. 43,

- **The Five Pirs** — a collective invoked three times, with no place, tomb or names given. "The
  Five Pirs had married you8 to him and this second marriage was not lawful." (p. 41, folio 40),
  glossed "This is the equivalent of marriage made in heaven, and equally difficult to prove."
  (p. 41, folio 40). "And he swore to take vengeance on Sehti, if the Five Pir would help him."

  ability of die [text-layer?] [the] Five Pirs to help him in his love is a matter of private
  belief." (p. 44, folio 43).
- **Solomon** — "Solomon is the Pir of Jinns and evil spirits. Self-interest is the Pir of all Jats
  and Love is the Pir of all lovers." (p. 39, folio 38).
- **Mansur** — "Did not Mansur get crucified for telling his secret?" (p. 44, folio 43).
- **Harut and Marut, Zohra, Babylon** — note 5: "Harut and Marut art [text-layer?] [are] two angels

- **Tan Sen** — "He sings as sweetly as Tan Sen, 15 and he knows songs by sixties and hundreds"

- **Alexander and the fort of Daz; Timurlane** — "Remember Alexander touched the feet of a fakir and

- **Ahmad Shah Abdali and Nadir Shah** — in the verse as "a sudden invasion of Ahmad Shahs" (p. 43,

- **The Feringhees** — "She entered the garden of the Ferringhees and set the well machinery going."

- **Adam and Eve, Pharaoh, Joseph, Israfil** — "Fate expelled Adam and Eve from Paradise and drove

- **Bare toponyms with nothing attached**: Kashmir ("Surely there is no country like Kashmir",

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Ear-boring is the act that makes a Jogi, and the book says so four times.** "He went to Tilla
  and got his ears bored and became a Jogi." (p. 41, folio 40); Hir's hope "Perhaps he has obeyed
  my word and got his ears bored." (p. 40, folio 39); Sehti's taunt "Fate has shaved your beard and
  bored your ears." (p. 43, folio 42); Hir's account of Ranjha's renunciation "He has renounced home
  and fortunes and has tended buffaloes. He has bored his ears and has become a Jogi for my sake."
  (p. 45, folio 44).
- **Alms as the transaction between householder and fakir, graded by grain.** The Jogi at the door:
  "Give me alms, give me alms." (p. 38, folio 37). Sehti's dismissal: "Let us give this fakir alms
  and turn him out Give him a handful of millet and tell him to go away." (p. 42, folio 41). His
  complaint: "You ate shaving my beard in giving me mere bird's food.'" (p. 42, folio 41). The
  servant's defence: "Why do you find fault with millet? All Jats eat it. It is the food of the
  hungry and povertystricken [text-layer?] [poverty-stricken]. It is the father and mother of the
  poor." (p. 42, folio 41). The editor's gloss: "Birds food — a proud beggar indeed! Ranjha expected
  wheat and rice, instead of the millet grain given to him, which is even now considered inferior
  food in the Punjab countryside." (p. 44, folio 43).
- **The fakir's kit is property, and breaking it is an injury with a named remedy.** The inventory:
  "his beggar's bowl and his beaded necklaces" (p. 38, folio 37); "They broke his beggar's bowl and
  rosary." (p. 43, folio 42); "They put out his fire, threw away his beggar's bowl and wallet and
  scattered his bhang.2 They broke his pestle and mortar. They threw away his turban, his chain and
  his tongs, his cup and his horn." (p. 44, folio 43). The cup's provenance and value: "My Pir gave
  it to me and it was very precious." (p. 42, folio 41); "A great tyranny has been committed. You
  have ruined the fakir by breaking his cup." (p. 42, folio 41); and the threat of redress through
  the pir: "If I tell my Pir he will ruin your family." (p. 43, folio 42).
- **An offering brought to a holy man, itemized with cash.** "So Sehti filled a big dish with sugar
  and cream and covered it with a cloth and put five rupees therein. Then she went to the garden of
  Kalabagh and stood with her offering near the Jogi." (p. 45, folio 44). **The contents are given
  differently one page later — see Doubts.**
- **The currency is the translator's, not the poem's.** Note 6: "This is Usborne's expression. A
  farthing is an English coin quite unknown to Ranjha or Sehti. The reference in the text is to a
  small local coin." (p. 46, folio 45).
- **A vow of lamp-lighting and sweet cakes, and the editor's statement that the practice is
  current.** In the verse: "If anybody can remove the pain in my heart he may make shoes of my
  flesh. Oh poet Waris Shah! If I hear that my lover is returning, I will offer sweet cakes and
  light my lamps with rich butter for oil.'5" (p. 41, folio 40). Note 5: "A proverb common to
  Punjabi and Urdu. 'Rich butter' here is intended for ghee or melted butter. Lighting lamps in
  mosque even with ordinary oil is still a well-known way of expression of gratitude to God for His
  favours. Punjabi mothers still promise lamp-lighting in mosques when they pray for their
  children," (p. 41, folio 40).
- **The fear of a holy man's displeasure stated as the exact counterpart of hope in his blessing —
  the single most transferable statement in the chunk.** Note 21: "This may seem strange in the
  mouth of a faqir who is dedicated to the service of God. In actual fact, the displeasure of a
  faqir is as much feared and avoided as his blessing is sought after." (p. 40, folio 39). In the
  verse: "Beware, the fakir is dangerous. Do not tease him or he will cause trouble. He is simply
  and quietly worshipping his Guru,. Take care that he does not invoke his aid." (p. 43, folio 42).
- **Falling at the feet as the prescribed act of submission and the prescribed remedy for having
  offended.** "You should catch hold of the feet of the fakir in humility and with supplication
  instead of quarrelling with him." (p. 42, folio 41); "Go and fall at the feet of the fakir or his
  curse will fall on you." (p. 43, folio 42); "Remember Alexander touched the feet of a fakir and
  then he conquered the fort of Daz." (p. 43, folio 42); and at the reconciliation, "she besought
  him with folded hands saying, 'I have teen your slave from the beginning with all my heart and
  soul. I will follow your footsteps and serve you with devotion as your maid-servant.'" (p. 46,
  folio 45).
- **A forty-day austerity (chilla) undertaken as leverage.** "I will fast forty days and forty
  nights and I will recite powerful enchantment which will overcome all difficulties and will unite
  me to my beloved." (p. 43, folio 42).
- **Breath control named and glossed as a Hindu discipline.** "We become deaf and dumb by holding
  our breath in the tenth position.20" (p. 39, folio 38), glossed "This is a reference to Vogic
  [text-layer?] [Yogic] practices. 'Prana Yam', as it is called, involves the holding of breath for
  long periods and simultaneously concentrating on the idea of the Deity." (p. 40, folio 39).
- **Bhang as the devotee's stock-in-trade, flagged as a first occurrence.** Note 2: "Bhang — this is
  the first time Ranjha is specifically mentioned as being in possession of bhang, Indian hemp, like
  many other devotees of the contemplative life." (p. 45, folio 44).
- **Amulets and charmed threads, itemized and priced to a holy man's prayer.** Note 7: "'Taweez',
  'dhaga' and 'gat' are all charmed devices for warding off evil. 'Dhaga' is a twisted cotton
  thread, over which a holy man says a prayer. The 'dhaga' may be tied into knots in which case it
  becomes a gat'. A 'gat' is sometimes made of a leather string. 'Taweez' is an amulet." (p. 39,
  folio 38). In the verse the Jogi "spends all his time singing songs and wearing charms." (p. 38,
  folio 37).
- **Divination by the Quran and by horoscope, with the editor's disapproval attached.** "If you will
  sit near me I will open the Holy Book and by casting magic' [text-layer?] [magic⁷] lots I will
  tell your horoscope." (p. 41, folio 40), glossed "An unauthorised but popular use of the Holy
  Quran." (p. 41, folio 40). Hir's verdict on the reading: "This Jogi has read the signs of the
  stars correctly. He is a true pandit and Jotshi9 [text-layer?] [Jotshi⁹]" (p. 41, folio 40),
  glossed "Jotshi is an astrologer. 'Pandit' is a learned Brahmin, but is now used by both Hindus
  and Muslims for a wise and erudite person." (p. 41, folio 40).
- **The fakir as physician, midwife and undertaker — a long service catalogue in his own voice**
  (p. 40, folio 39): "Through the blessings of try [text-layer?] [my] Pir and teacher, I can tell
  the names of all diseases. I can whisper the call to prayer in the ears of a newly born babe,2 I
  can weave spells and put children to sleep with lullabies. I can dry up the womb of women and slay
  liars, adulterers and infidels. With cunning oils and potent herbs I can cure pains and paralysis
  and the eighteen kinds of leprosy… I can make a perfect cure of a barren woman by letting out
  blood from her ankle vein." And the last offices: "At his last hour, when the expiring life sticks
  fast in the gullet of the dying man, I recite the Holy Quran and his soul passes away in peace."
  The editor's comment: "Ranjha is speaking here more like a learned physician than a faqir. That,
  of course, is intended to impress his ignorant hearers." (p. 41, folio 40).
- **The azan whispered to the newborn, named as a current custom.** Note 2: "This is a regular and
  well-known custom among Muslims everywhere. The azan or call to prayer is whispered in the ear of
  the new-born baby either by a priest or a relative." (p. 41, folio 40).
- **Abortifacient practice, with the editor hesitating over a faqir's part in it.** "With boiled
  Ghaghar3 herbs I can bring about miscarriage." (p. 40, folio 39), glossed "Ghaghar is a creeper,
  the leaves of which are boiled and used for fomentation. It may also possibly be used for inducing
  abortion, although it would be unusual for a faqir to participate is [text-layer?] [in] bringing
  this about." (p. 41, folio 40). Also "Dhannia is coriander seed. It is believed to have properties
  similar to bromide." (p. 41, folio 40).
- **The vocabulary of religious status, defined by the edition.** "A Bairagi is a holy mendicant, a
  homeless wanderer, who has severed all human ties. H1e [text-layer?] [He] is virtually a Jogi, but
  possibly a more hard-boiled one." (p. 39, folio 38); "Shinas and Bairag-Sanyas, or renunciation of
  the world. Bairag means the same thing. This is the last state of human life according to the
  Hindus." (p. 40, folio 39); "A mahant is a saintly devotee, a Guru." (p. 45, folio 44). The Jogi's
  own account of the discipline: "We get up at midnight and pore over sacred books by the banks of
  untrodden streams, we expel all impurities from our speech by using the toothbrush of repentance
  and we sit on the carpet of true belief." (p. 39, folio 38).
- **Shared Hindu–Muslim practice asserted three times by the editor.** The joint family "though
  originally a Hindu institution, later on became more less [text-layer?] [more or less] common to
  Hindus and Muslim, alike." (p. 39, folio 38); the snake and the bull supporting the earth "(Usborne's
  note). This is Hindu tradition, which became common popular belief both among Hindus and the more
  ignorant among Muslims. See also Chapter 16, note 16." (p. 41, folio 40); the salaam with folded
  hands — "This is the Hindu way of greeting others." (p. 46, folio 45).
- **Marriage argued as a religious obligation from the Quran, and the editor's correction.** "How
  goes the well known saying, "To have a wife is equivalent to being in possession of Half
  Religion." Only he who is married can have prayers lawfully said over him when he dies.4 God has
  said in the Quran, MARRY."5" (p. 46, folio 45), with note 4: "This is an exaggeration. Funeral
  prayers are by no means restricted to married men." (p. 46, folio 45). And "Is it not said in the
  Quran, 'We have created every living thing in pairs."" (p. 46, folio 45).
- **Mullahs named as the resort of hypocrites.** "They sit at the feet of Mullahs and listen to the
  doctrines of Hypocrisy." (p. 46, folio 45).
- **Access to a court priced in bribes and nazrana — and note that this "darbar" is a ruler's court,
  not a shrine.** "I have no money to give to the officers and no tribute to enable me to reach to
  the darbar32 [text-layer?] [darbar¹²]" (p. 43, folio 42), glossed "I 2 [text-layer?] [12] This
  refer, [text-layer?] [refers] to bribery and corruption in the administration, never entirely
  absent from India or any other country at any time. Tribute' refers to the Nazrana, a gift,
  usually presented to a ruler in olden times in the form of cash or other valuables. A number of
  kings and emperors were, however, accessible to their poor subjects easily and without Nazrana'."
  (p. 44, folio 43).
- **Music, dance and games of "the old Punjab", named in the notes.** "'King' [text-layer?]
  [reading uncertain] and Nad' are both musical instruments, popular in the old Punjab." (p. 39,
  folio 38); "Wa!an [text-layer?] [reading uncertain], Garidda, Choratori and Maya are dances
  accompanied by music and singing and are popular among girls." (p. 39, folio 38); "Kilbari — a
  game something like Here we go round the mulberry bush'. Sammi is a similar game. (Usbome's
  [text-layer?] [Usborne's] note.)" (p. 39, folio 38); "Kand and Mul — narcotic preparations of
  opium. (Usborne's note.)" (p. 39, folio 38).
- **Virginity marked by a hair fringe, with a named ceremony at the bride's house.** Note 2:
  "Fringes is a reference to the 'Mehndhi, a fringe of delicately intertwined strands of hair all
  round the forehead of a virgin girl in the old Punjab. The 'Mehndhi', which is a symbol of
  virginity, disappears at the wedding and is not resumed after marriage. There is a touching
  ceremony at the bride's house when her Mehndhi is untied as a token of her goodbye to her
  unmarried past." (p. 43, folio 42).
- **Itinerant trades named as still current.** "Jugglers and their bears are still a familiar sight
  in the old Punjab. Some have monkeys which are equally popular." (p. 40, folio 39). The Jogi is
  abused as "a wandering minstrel, or a leather worker, or a sweeper of some sent" (p. 38, folio 37;
  see Doubts on the last word).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The one miracle in the chunk, and the book itself calls it a miracle: the fakir names the
  contents of a covered dish unseen.** "And Ranjha replied, 'The dish is filled with sugar andrice
  [text-layer?] [and rice] and you have put five farthings5 on the top of it. Co [text-layer?] [Go]
  and see, if you have any doubt in your mind.' So Sehti uncovered the dish and looked at it, and
  behold, it was full of sugar and rice. And when Sehti beheld the miracle which the fakir had
  performed, she besought him with folded hands" (p. 46, folio 45). **See Doubts: the dish was
  filled with "sugar and cream" a page earlier.**
- **The voice of the Five Pirs, after ashes, fire, breath-holding and spells at Kalabagh.** "And he
  kindled fire and meditated on God, and sparks came from his body. He stopped his breath and
  meditated like a holy mahant,' [text-layer?] [mahant¹] and under the shadow of the tree he was
  absorbed in deep meditation. Then he recited spells and incantations and a voice came from the
  Five Pirs saying, 'Go to, my child, your grief is gone. You will meet your beloved in the
  morning.'" (p. 44, folio 43).
- **The powers claimed for fakirs, in the Jogi's own voice.** "We fakirs are like black snakes and we
  acquire power and virtue by reading spells… We can ward off deceit and burn evil spirits. We can
  cast spells and destroy those whom we want to destroy.21 We can make absent lovers smell the
  fragrance of their beloved's presence. Women who revile us we can make ride in penance on an ass
  with a shaved head. Let virgins beware who oppose our power or it will fare ill with their
  virginity." (p. 39, folio 38). And "I can ruin you utterly, as I have saintly power in each finger
  tip." (p. 42, folio 41); "We are the perfect fakirs of God. Ask anything from us, fair beauty, and
  we can bring it about. If a lover parts from his beloved one, with spells of magic numbers we can
  unite them." (p. 40, folio 39).
- **Blessings that gave kingdoms** — Alexander at the fakir's feet before taking the fort of Daz,
  and the fakir's blessing on Timurlane under which "sovereignty remained in his family for several
  generations" (p. 43, folio 42). The editor's verdict on both: "All this, of course, is poetic
  invention rather than history." (p. 44, folio 43).
- **Harut and Marut imprisoned in a well at Babylon over a dancing girl, Zohra, who became the
  morning star** (p. 39, folio 38) — the editor calls the well "still supposed to exist and… pointed
  out to credulous tourists by local guides".
- **The apocalypse catalogue, with seven things surviving.** "When Israfil blows his trumpet all the
  habitations of men will fall down… The snake and the bull6 will be flied [text-layer?] [filled]
  with fear on that day… All that will remain will be the seven last things; the Chair and the Throne
  of God, the Tablet of Destiny, and the Pen, Paradise, the Soul and Hell. Everything will vanish in
  an instant. Only lover and fakir will remain constant." (p. 41, folio 40).
- **Proverbs of the price of disclosure, in Hir's mouth.** "Did not Mansur get crucified for telling
  his secret? Did not Joseph get put In the well for telling his dream? Have not parrots been put in

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The edition carries two layers of notes, and marks only one of them.** Where a note is the
  translator's it says so: "(Usborne's note.)" on Kand and Mul (p. 39, folio 38); "(Usbome's
  [text-layer?] [Usborne's] note.)" on Kilbari (p. 39, folio 38); "(Usbornes [text-layer?]
  [Usborne's] note)" on Tan Sen (p. 39, folio 38); "(Usborne's note)" on the snake and the bull
  (p. 41, folio 40); "Ujsborne's [text-layer?] [Usborne's] note.)" on Bhag Bhari (p. 45, folio 44);
  "This is Usborne's expression" on the farthing (p. 46, folio 45). **Every other note in this chunk
  is unattributed and reads as the edition editor's, not the translator's and not Waris Shah's** —
  attribute accordingly, and see Doubts.
- **The editor insists throughout that Ranjha's holiness is a performance.** Note 11 — "Ranjha does
  not appear to be much of a Jogi in the whole of this episode." (p. 39, folio 38). Note 1 — "Ranjha
  is speaking here more like a learned physician than a faqir. That, of course, is intended to
  impress his ignorant hearers." (p. 41, folio 40). Note 13 — "Ranjha is, of cease, [text-layer?]
  [of course] only masquerading as a jogi and his faith in the willingness and ability of die
  [text-layer?] [the] Five Pirs to help him in his love is a matter of private belief." (p. 44,
  folio 43).
- **The editor separates legend from history explicitly, three times.** "This again is mere legend.
  History does not relate how, if at all, Plato and Aesop were outwitted by women." (p. 39,
  folio 38); "All this, of course, is poetic invention rather than history." (p. 44, folio 43);
  "This is the equivalent of marriage made in heaven, and equally difficult to prove." (p. 41,
  folio 40).
- **The editor corrects the poem's theology against the Quran.** Note 2 — "This is the Christian
  legend. The Quran says, on the other hand, that it was Satan who led both Adam and Eve into
  trouble. Nor does Islam encourage or approve of celibacy. On the contrary, the Prophet has
  dissociated himself from those who do not want to marry. Celibacy and monasticism both represent
  Christian, not Muslin,, [text-layer?] [Muslim] tradition." (p. 46, folio 45). He also calls one
  practice in the poem "An unauthorised but popular use of the Holy Quran." (p. 41, folio 40).
- **The editor admits the limits of his own apparatus.** "This is presumably a reference to some
  contemporary event." (p. 44, folio 43); "The garden of the Feringhees is an obscure phrase."
  (p. 44, folio 43).
- **A running claim that the poem's Punjab survives into the editor's present.** "In similar
  circumstances one would expect to hear similar compliments in the Punjab countryside even today!"
  (p. 39, folio 38); "Jugglers and their bears are still a familiar sight in the old Punjab."
  (p. 40, folio 39); "Arabic verbs, alas, are s [text-layer?] [as] mysterious today as they were in
  Waris Shah's days." (p. 40, folio 39); millet "is even now considered inferior food in the Punjab
  countryside" (p. 44, folio 43); "Punjabi mothers still promise lamp-lighting in mosques when they
  pray for their children," (p. 41, folio 40).
- **The notes are one apparatus across the whole book, and they cross-refer.** "See also Chapter 16,
  note 16." (p. 41, folio 40); "See Chapter 1, note 10." (p. 46, folio 45); "See Chapter 16, note
  17." (p. 46, folio 45); and note 1 to the Sehti chapter refers back outside this chunk — "an
  oblique reference to Murad, Sehti's Baluch lover, the camel-driver about whom the shepherd had
  already told Ranjha." (p. 43, folio 42).

## Doubts

- **The folio rule is confirmed, not contradicted, on all nine pages.** Measured from the running
  header: 38→37, 39→38, 40→39, 41→40, 42→41, 43→42, 44→43, 45→44, 46→45. `folio = PDF − 1` holds
  nine for nine. Nothing to report against it from this chunk.
- **Only one archive row is mapped, and the negatives are explicit.** In pp. 38–46 there is **no
  mention of** Bulleh Shah, Baba Fariduddin Ganjshakar, Shah Hussain / Madho Lal Hussain, Hujwiri /
  Data Ganj Bakhsh, Shah Inayat, Sachal Sarmast, Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai, Sultan Bahu, Gorakhnath,
  Guru Nanak or any other Guru, or any temple, gurdwara, Udasi darbar or memorial row. **Pakpattan,
  Jhang Sial, Takht Hazara and Rangpur are not named anywhere in this chunk** either. This is a
  narrative stretch of the *qissa*; one row is the honest yield.
- **`mausoleum-of-waris-shah` is mapped on the poet's own signature lines plus the notes about
  him, and on nothing else.** The book gives **no tomb, no death year, no birth year, no silsila and
  no custodian** for him in this chunk — all (not stated). "Jandiala (the birthplace of the poet)"
  (p. 43, folio 42) is a **birthplace**, not a burial place, and the book prints "Jandiala" without
  "Sher Khan"; the archive row's Sheikhupura District and 1798 and Chishti are not in the book.
  Additionally, **the poet's name is itself damaged at the one place a biographical fact attaches to
  it**: "the poet Wails Shah" [text-layer?] [Waris Shah] in the Bhag Bhari note (p. 45, folio 44). A
  human should confirm that reading before the Bhag Bhari note is cited to this row.
- **Tilla is the hardest call in the chunk under still-unruled question (a), and I have held it
  out.** It is not an idle place name — the verse attaches a Jogi initiation to it, "He went to Tilla
  and got his ears bored and became a Jogi" (p. 41, folio 40) — but **no shrine, tomb, saint, ʿurs or
  custodian is named there**, so under the interim convention it takes no id. **If the ruling
  reverses, or if an initiation site counts as an attachment, it takes `tilla-jogian`** (Tilla
  Jogian, Salt Range near Dina/Jhelum, principal figure "Guru Gorakhnath (Nath yogis)"). The book
  never writes "Tilla Jogian", never names Gorakhnath, and never calls Tilla a temple or a shrine.
  **Rauf to decide.**
- **Kasur held under the same convention; on a reversal it would take `mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**
  (Mazar of Bulleh Shah, Kasur). But the chunk's Kasur is purely military — "the Pathans of Kasur
  looted the camp of Bakshi" (p. 43, folio 42) — and names no saint, so the reversal would be a weak
  placement. Note that the edition elsewhere in this book does gloss a "Saint of Kasur"; **no such
  note falls in this chunk.**
- **Lahore held under the same convention and would not resolve even on a reversal:** the archive
  holds more than twenty Lahore rows, and this chunk's Lahore is a subadar, a courtesans' reputation
  and a proverb of culture (pp. 38, 39, 42, 43; folios 37, 38, 41, 42), with no shrine, tomb or
  saint attached. **Kalabagh, Ghunian/Chunian, Daz, Babylon, Aleppo, Kabul, Panipat, Kashmir and
  "the Punjab" take no row on any ruling.**
- **`Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir` does not appear in this chunk,** so the archive's empty
  id cell for that row is not exercised here. Recorded only so the consolidator knows it was
  checked. No id has been invented anywhere in these notes; the single id used is literally in the
  TSV.
- **The offering's contents contradict themselves across one page, and the edition reconciles only
  the coin.** "a big dish with sugar and cream and covered it with a cloth and put five rupees
  therein" (p. 45, folio 44) against "The dish is filled with sugar andrice [text-layer?] [and rice]
  … you have put five farthings5 on the top of it" and "behold, it was full of sugar and rice"
  (p. 46, folio 45). The editor addresses **only** rupees/farthings (note 6, p. 46, folio 45), not
  cream/rice. **This matters to the karamat bullet, because the miracle consists precisely in the
  fakir naming the contents correctly.** Kept side by side, not resolved.
- **A place name spelled two ways within two pages:** the verse has "at Ghunian" (p. 43, folio 42),
  the note has "Chunian" twice (p. 44, folio 43). The edition does not flag the discrepancy.
- **Note-block numbering is a trap for the consolidator.** Notes **1–15** print on p. 39 (folio 38)
  and notes **16–21** on p. 40 (folio 39), all belonging to the chapter that ends on p. 40 — so
  **notes 1–9 on p. 39 gloss text that falls before this chunk** and cannot be matched to any marker
  in pp. 38–46. Every later chapter restarts its notes at 1, so "note 5" alone is ambiguous
  (p. 39 note 5 is Harut and Marut; p. 41 note 5 is lamp-lighting; p. 43 note 5 is Lahore
  courtesans; p. 45 note 5 is Bhag Bhari; p. 46 note 5 is a cross-reference). **Cite notes by page,
  never by number alone.**
- **Column behaviour, confirmed and irregular in the way warned.** On pp. 39 and 46 the Notes block
  sits in **column 2** while narrative runs in column 1; on pp. 40, 44 and 45 the Notes block sits in
  **column 1** with a new chapter opening below it in the same column. Chapter openings at pp. 40,
  42, 44, 45 and 46 all begin in **column 1**, not column 2. Reading order within each de-interleaved
  block was coherent throughout; I found no spliced sentence.
- **No non-Latin script appears anywhere in this chunk.** Every vernacular term is in Latin
  transliteration only — Jog, Bairag, Sanyas, Taweez, dhaga, gat, Prana Yam, Mehndhi, Ghaghar,
  Dhannia, Kilbari, Sammi, Wa!an, Garidda, Choratori, Maya, Nazrana, Doli, bhang, kebab. **The
  protocol's "quote the original script where it is printed" rule cannot be exercised here**, and
  the chunk prints **only one date in nine pages**: "i1761" [1761] in note 8 (p. 44, folio 43).
