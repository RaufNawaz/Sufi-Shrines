## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The Hindu population of Pakistan, with the book's own hedge.** "Today, Pakistan's Hindu community
  comprises between 6 and 10 million people—an estimate that varies widely according to the source.1"
  (p. 13, folio not stated) "the vast majority—over 90 percent—belong to the underprivileged Hindu
  tribes or sub-castes, such as the Meghvar, the Bhil, and the Kohli, who frequently work as manual
  laborers for the powerful landowning elite in Sindh." (p. 13, folio not stated) Pakistan hosts
  "the world's fifth largest Hindu population" (p. 13, folio not stated). **Note marker `1` is a call
  only; the Notes are outside this chunk.**
- **Who speaks for the community, and the economy behind it.** "Only a small minority have made a
  comfortable living in Pakistan for themselves through trade and other professions. This upper-class
  Hindu elite significantly influences the rest of the community as it provides authors, politicians
  (such as members of the Pakistan National Assembly), or, in the case of the temple of Hinglaj in
  Balochistan, temple committee members who claim the right to speak to and for the rest of the
  community." (p. 13, folio not stated) Against that: "Recent decades, nevertheless, have witnessed
  the increasing emancipation of the unprivileged sub-castes, who have begun to voice their own
  interests nationally and internationally." (p. 13, folio not stated)
- **The sects the book distinguishes**: local Sufi pīrs; the fourteenth-century saint Ramdevji; "the
  westernized and proselytizing ISKCON society" among "urban Hindu youths"; communities who worship
  "manifold Mother Goddesses as their clan or family patrons, who must be regularly appeased with
  blood sacrifices"; and "A different branch follows the teachings of the Guru Granth Sahib, better
  known as the holy book of the Sikhs." (pp. 13–14, folios not stated) **Blood sacrifice is stated as
  a practice of clan-Goddess worship generally, not of Hinglaj**: the chunk attaches it to no named
  site.
- **New pan-Hindu institutions, with founding dates.** "Organizations like the Pakistan Hindu Council
  (established in 2006) or the Pakistan Hindu Seva Trust (established in 2010) appeal to the
  community as a whole." (p. 14, folio not stated) They "share an explicit pan-Hindu orientation and
  thus, differ from the customary paṃcāyat (Hin. council) systems, which mainly deal with issues
  relating to their own village, caste, or sect." (p. 14, folio not stated) They "distribute
  information, organize cultural and religious events, and stage protests concerning human rights
  issues"; they "not only help to represent and describe Hindu life to a larger Pakistani public but
  also prescribe a certain Hindu identity to their own community.2" (p. 14, folio not stated) "most
  organizations adopting it have been established within the past decade" (p. 14, folio not stated).
- **Press and broadcast liberalisation, with a counted series.** Censorship "was first lifted in 1988";
  in 2002 the television landscape "was further liberalized, and many of the newly established
  channels started reporting on minority issues" (pp. 14–15, folios not stated). "English newspapers
  such as the News (established in 1991) and the Express Tribune (established in 2010) regularly cover
  Hindu festivals and cultural activities." (p. 15, folio not stated) **The count**: "while reporting
  on Hindu activity in Pakistan averaged twenty-eight articles per year between 1987 and 2002 (with a
  steep increase around the year 1992…), the years 2003 to 2011 saw an average of a hundred articles
  annually about issues relating to Hindu communities. The year 2010 witnessed an especially large
  increase in reporting on minorities following the establishment of the Express Tribune.3" (p. 15,
  folio not stated) **Both figures are printed as words, not numerals** — "twenty-eight" and "a
  hundred". The catch: "Liberal news coverage often only appears in English and so frequently preaches
  to an already sympathetic English-speaking upper-middle class.4" (p. 15, folio not stated)
- **Electorates, a dated sequence.** "The abolition of separate electorates in 2002"; "The British Raj
  originally introduced separate electorates in 1909 to ensure Muslims greater representation in
  majority Hindu areas of British India. Governments in Pakistan maintained separate electorates in
  the years 1956 and 1973, and Zia-ul-Haq rejuvenated the system in 1985." (p. 15, folio not stated)
  "The separate electorates, together with the implementation of the blasphemy laws, created an
  environment in Pakistan in which political representation became directly linked to religious
  affiliation (Rais 2007)." (p. 15, folio not stated) "Under this system, Hindus were only able to
  vote for a Hindu candidate, which broadly marginalized them in their political activities." (p. 15,
  folio not stated) The verdict on the reform: "the joint system did not practically increase their
  political representation." (p. 15, folio not stated)
- **Reserved seats, counted.** "The 1973 constitution provided the first legal basis for minority
  political representation in Pakistan: under its terms, 10 seats out of 342 are reserved for
  non-Muslims in the national assembly, and a variable ratio is retained in the provincial assembly.5"
  (p. 15, folio not stated) "The Eighteenth Amendment, adopted in 2008, improved non-Muslim
  representation by adding four more seats for minorities in the Senate." (pp. 15–16, folios not
  stated) "Currently, the Pakistan Muslim League occupies six of the minority seats, while the
  Pakistan People's Party, the Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf, the Muttahida Qaumi Movement, and the Jamiat
  Ulema-e-Islam (F) occupy one seat each." (p. 16, folio not stated) **See Doubts: the Eighteenth
  Amendment's date is given as 2008.**
- **State money for temples — the economic mechanism the book names.** "The most important effect of

  stated)

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The book's opening question and its negative finding about the field.** "WHAT IS PAKISTANI
  Hinduism? Or, even more generally, is there anything that can be called Pakistani Hinduism at all?
  Not much has been published to date about the numerous Hindu communities that exist in today's
  Islamic Republic of Pakistan." (p. 13, folio not stated) The reasons he offers: "The country's
  instability, the feudal environment in Sindh—home to the majority of Pakistan's Hindus—or perhaps a
  simple lack of interest have all effectively kept scholars from inquiring" (p. 13, folio not
  stated).
- **The coherence problem, stated as a problem and then refused.** "one may wonder about the
  coherence of a group that ultimately seems connected merely by a common designation as 'Hindu.' The
  longer one's gaze lingers on this diversity, the more any general statement about Hinduism in
  Pakistan appears to be sheer presumption. In short, Hinduism as an identity marker seems an elusive
  term that has no beginning and no end, made up only of thriving and ongoing discussions about what
  it means to be a Hindu in Pakistan." (p. 14, folio not stated) His resolution: "the difficulty of
  producing general descriptions about the Pakistani-Hindu community as a whole, I argue, must be
  juxtaposed with certain unifying particularities of Hindu (and non-Muslim) life in Pakistan."
  (p. 19, folio not stated)
- **Method declared**: "this challenge will be taken up using approaches from current political and
  anthropological theory applied to the data from my intensive fieldwork and textual research between
  2009 and 2015." (p. 14, folio not stated)
- **Two predicaments, historical and everyday** — the Introduction's two named sections. The
  historical: "The complex link between Pakistani citizenship and Hindu identity has its roots in the
  two-nation theory (Urd. do qaumī naz̤ariyah), later also accentuated in the Pakistan ideology (Urd.
  naz̤ariyah-i pākistān). This founding myth, central to the Islamic Republic, claims that Muslims and
  Hindus are two distinct nations that can only thrive through their separation, and ultimately led to
  the partition of the subcontinent in 1947." (p. 19, folio not stated) **He then disowns the
  narrative**: "Today, however, we know that the movement for Pakistan (Urd. taḥrīk-i pākistān) was
  not a straightforward development or the manifestation of a clear demand for a separate Muslim
  homeland (cf. Shaikh 2009; Jalal 1995; Talbot 2012)." (p. 19, folio not stated) And, citing Farzana
  Shaikh: "the very existence of minorities inside the country constantly contests the two-nation
  theory and the understanding of who is a 'real' Pakistani (Shaikh 2009, 69)." (p. 19, folio not
  stated) The epigraph to the section is Herzfeld: "Nationstate ideologies tend to divide the world
  into Manichaean pairs and to coerce or seduce their citizens into adopting the same rhetoric for the
  moral organization of their own everyday social relations. (Herzfeld 2004, 15)" (p. 19, folio not
  stated).
- **Althusser applied, and explicitly amended.** "Louis Althusser, a French Marxist, termed
  institutions that help to disseminate such ideologies 'ideological state apparatuses' (ISA)
  (Althusser 1971)… Here, the educational ISA do not primarily reproduce relations of production as it
  is found in Althusser, but rather aim to sediment differences between Muslims and Hindus, which
  serves as a legitimization of the very existence of the Islamic Republic of Pakistan." (p. 20, folio
  not stated) **The amendment is the author's own and should be cited as such.**
- **Schoolbooks as the evidence.** "National and international scholars working on education in South
  Asia have found that in Pakistani schoolbooks, India and 'the Hindu' are often portrayed as threats
  to the Pakistani nation state (cf. Aziz 1993; Lall 2008; Rahman 2011)." (p. 20, folio not stated)
  Quoted from the textbooks: "'The Hindu' therefore is not only a danger to national security but
  'has always been an enemy of Islam.'7"; "references to India such as 'Bharat is the country of
  non-Muslims'8 intentionally omit the millions of Muslims living in India." (p. 20, folio not stated)
  "Such omissions enable children to draw a seemingly clear line of identity between 'us' and 'them'
  consistent with the two-nation theory (Saigol 2005, 1010)." (p. 20, folio not stated) His
  recommendation: "one of these changes would certainly be revising Pakistan's schoolbooks." (p. 20,
  folio not stated) **Note markers 6, 7 and 8 are calls; the quoted textbook sources are not
  identified in this chunk**: **(not stated)**.
- **Synecdoche as the mechanism of prejudice**: "schoolbooks suggest, through synecdoche, that the
  habits of some Hindus are the habits of all Hindus." (p. 20, folio not stated) The parallel term for
  the violence: "alleged desecration conducted by non-Muslim communities often leads to pars pro toto
  actions in which religious identity temporarily becomes a sufficient reason for mob violence to
  occur." (p. 17, folio not stated) **He immediately broadens it**: "Such pars pro toto actions are
  triggered not only by religious identities, as the manifold and complex cases in the city of Karachi
  exemplify. Similar incidents are also connected to multiple other identity signifiers including
  ethnicity, language, class, and so forth." (p. 18, folio not stated)
- **He refuses a minorities-only framing of insecurity**, and lets an informant make the case: "At
  times, it appears as if everybody in Pakistan belongs to some sort of minority" (p. 16, folio not
  stated); the Hindu lawyer, quoted from 2012, lists "Kidnapping, looting, robbery, killing, stealing,
  life threats, a lack of security… Everyone in Pakistan is insecure—the Sunni, the Shia, the Sindhi,
  the Mohajir, the Punjabi, the Balochi, the Hindu, the Christian, the rich, the poor—every Pakistani
  faces these problems. We Hindus are simply no exception." (p. 16, folio not stated) **The author
  then qualifies**: "While I generally agree with this assessment, there are, however, characteristic
  predicaments that many non-Muslims share, even if they do not appear as hard and fast rules but
  rather in the form of recurring patterns." (pp. 16–17, folios not stated)
- **The Urdu horror genre as a second archive of demonisation.** "Duśmanī is a characteristic example
  for how Hindus feature in such and similar Urdu horror tales in Pakistan (Schaflechner 2016a). The
  story uses a wide set of genre-typical tropes including power-hungry Hindu villains, black magic,
  and pious Muslim saviors." (p. 23, folio not stated) "In Duśmanī, this also extends to India as a
  nation as it is the silent supporter of Dr. Shankar's insidious plan… India, thus, emerges as a
  cunning nation, which is conniving with an evil scientist and a tantric to gain world domination."
  (pp. 22–23, folios not stated) "These structures abound within the genre, and—with few
  exceptions—can be seen as the main characteristic of stories that feature Hindu characters
  (Schaflechner 2016a)." (p. 23, folio not stated)
- **The book's summary verdict on Pakistani-Hindu identity.** "Such examples show that being a Hindu
  in Pakistan today means to be split between one's religious affiliation and one's loyalty to the
  nation state." (p. 23, folio not stated) "Due to a host of factors, thus, including the absence of
  patron-client relationships, the structural exclusion of Hindus within the governmental education
  system, their demonization in parts of popular culture, and the very foundation of the two-nation
  theory itself, the Pakistani-Hindu identity exists in a kind of purgatory, caught betwixt and
  between." (p. 23, folio not stated) **And the mirror finding on the Indian side**: "Hindu migrants
  to India also face extreme difficulties after their migration. This time it is not their religious
  identity, but their Pakistani heritage that causes them to be eyed with suspicion by their
  environment (Schaflechner 2014). This marginalizes many Pakistani Hindus on both sides of the
  border." (p. 23, folio not stated)
- **Why a single remote site can carry the whole argument.** "notwithstanding that this study is
  centered on the rituals and narratives of one particular site located somewhere in the far-off
  desert of Balochistan, its recent developments reveal much about the Hindu communities of Pakistan."
  (p. 24, folio not stated)
- **The book's own theoretical term, named here for the first time.** "Chapter 1 will elaborate on the
  theoretical foundations of this work through a concept I have called 'the solidification of
  tradition.' Utilizing newer anthropological theories of ontology and supplementing them with the
  political philosophy of post-foundationalism will help me to produce my own engagement with the
  various truth-claims encountered during my research." (p. 24, folio not stated) The problem it
  answers: "Doing fieldwork at the site together with engaging with a variety of texts and other media
  confronted me with the question of how to organize all of these voices that uniformly claimed to
  speak the truth about the shrine and its annexed practices." (p. 24, folio not stated)
- **Invention of tradition, stated as a finding about the pilgrimage.** The Narayanpur case study
  "shows how 'traditional' pilgrimage practices are reinterpreted—or even retrospectively invented—to
  adapt to modern circumstances." (p. 26, folio not stated) And the gap it opens: "the recent
  developments around Hinglaj have exposed a gap between the traditional discursive representations of
  the pilgrimage (Chapter 3) and the current practices at the shrine (Chapter 4)." (p. 26, folio not
  stated)
- **Indian devotional publishing is politicised, Pakistani sources are not, in his reading**: "newer
  publications by devotees from India display a pronounced political inclination, distinguishing them
  from the more dominant sense of spirituality and self-abandonment found in the older texts."
  (p. 25, folio not stated) And: "stereotypes linked to Pakistan play a significant role in the
  'mystification' of the pilgrimage across the border, in India, and… such visions of the Islamic
  Republic serve to bolster the shrine's symbolic capital." (p. 26, folio not stated) **"Symbolic
  capital" is the author's term, used without attribution here.**
- **Lefebvre announced for the spatial argument**: "Applying the theoretical work of Henri Lefebvre,
  this chapter goes on to show how spatial practices," (p. 26, folio not stated) — **the sentence is
  cut off by the chunk boundary.**
