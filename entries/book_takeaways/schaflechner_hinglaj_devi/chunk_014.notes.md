## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The purification bath at Sharan Kund, and its lapse.** Three submersions with a chosen
  `śaraṇ'bhāi/bahan` before entering the garbha (p. 251, folio not stated); the bath "is frequently
  mentioned in literature (Samaur 1999, 506; Joshi 2010, 22; Jethamalani 1994, 8)" (p. 252, folio not
  stated). "The gradual drying up of the pond and the subsequent ceasing of the purification practice
  in the pool first triggered efforts by the HSM to prevent its desiccation." (p. 252, folio not
  stated). By 2011 the site was concreted over (p. 251, folio not stated).
- **The sesame rite at Til Kund, with its arithmetic of purity.** "The main ritual practice at the Til
  Kund consists of the washing of til (Hin.), black sesame seeds that pilgrims either bring themselves
  or receive from the pujārī. After the pūjā at Hinglaj and the subsequent spiritual rebirth out of the
  Goddess's womb, the yātrīs travel on to Til Kund." (p. 254, folio not stated). "According to the
  percentage of seeds that have turned white, the pilgrim can determine the extent to which his or her
  soul has been purified and how much still remains stained. So, for example, if a quarter of the seeds
  stay black, the pilgrim's soul has now become 75 percent pure." (p. 254, folio not stated). The rite
  is repeatable, including a second rebirth: "The pilgrims may also return to the Mother's garbha to be
  reborn yet again." (p. 254, folio not stated).
- **The eye-cure at Kir Kund.** "the devotee seeking healing is supposed to stand beneath the ledge and
  allow the drops to fall directly into his or her eyes" (p. 254, folio not stated).
- **The ascent to Anil Kund as required labour.** "In the local Sindhi (Lohana) tradition, pilgrims
  must ascend to Anil Kund after they have emerged from the Goddess's lap in order to successfully
  complete their yātrā (Jethamalani 1994, 8)." (pp. 255–256, folio not stated). In the Gujarati
  tradition the order is reversed: "one must bathe in its waters to purify the body before proceeding
  to enter the Goddess's lap" (p. 256, folio not stated). **A direct contradiction, kept side by
  side.**
- **A substitute rite for those who cannot climb.** Seven circuits of the boulder: "if they were unable
  to reach Chaurasi, then they should instead make seven circuits (Hin. cakkar lagānā) around the stone
  and would thereby gain the same merit as if they had taken a bath there" (p. 259, folio not stated).
  "Nowadays, elderly and handicapped people who are unable to make the arduous climb to Chaurasi use
  the stone as a proxy to acquire a similar benefit as from a bath in the mountain pond." (p. 259,
  folio not stated).
- **Animal sacrifice at Astola, in Hart's account.** "According to his informants, the place is
  dedicated to the Goddess Kali, and goats are sacrificed at a particular spot on the island." (p. 260,
  folio not stated). Also "it is not the custom to stay at the island and that no pilgrim dares to
  remain there at night (Hart 1839, 105)" (p. 260, folio not stated).
- **Transport to Astola, then and now.** "To reach the place, the yātrīs take boats from nearby ports,
  or even set out from Karachi." (p. 260, folio not stated). "Today, because of the military presence
  in the Arabian Sea, pilgrims rarely visit Astola Island. Some members of the HSM tried to organize a
  trip to the island with the help of the military in 2012 but did not succeed, and I do not know if
  such a tour has been successfully organized since then." (p. 260, folio not stated).
- **The stops on the route, in decline.** "Even though an array of stops (paṛāv) along the route from
  Karachi to Hinglaj have been documented over the past two hundred years, a conspicuous decline in the
  quantity and complexity of these ritual stops has occurred over the past few decades. Yet over this
  same time period, certain localities have gained more elaborate ritual practices and have become
  increasingly central to the pilgrimage." (p. 260, folio not stated).
- **Rule-making by the temple committee.** "for some years now, the HSM has perpetuated a set of rules
  and regulations during the annual festivals with the goal of fashioning a certain behavior among
  visitors. According to the organization's spokespersons, these regulatory norms serve merely as
  suggestions and are never strictly enforced." (p. 267, folio not stated). "Within this process,
  certain ritual practices and narratives are singled out and either condemned as improper Hindu
  practices or emphasized as proper ones." (p. 267, folio not stated).
- **Contestation of the committee's authority, with a named community.** "Many communities claim
  Hinglaj as a ritual space in their own tradition, refusing to follow the particular guidelines
  perpetuated by the HSM and the Lasi Lohana community. Smaller groups and individuals, however, lack
  the Mandali's influence and thus the ability to spread their own perception of the histories and
  practices at the shrine." (p. 267, folio not stated).
- **Physical works by the organisation, listed.** "the temple organization constructed new routes to
  the shrine and altered the topography by filling up or digging new ponds" (p. 261, folio not stated);
  a ladder to `rām jharoku`, since destroyed (p. 257, folio not stated); a new road and a cemented
  front courtyard in 2012 (p. 265, folio not stated — note 30); the tunnel extension after the late
  1980s (p. 251, folio not stated).
- **New images brought in by communities.** "certain Hindu communities have brought new statues and
  related myths to the valley" (p. 261, folio not stated). **Neither the communities nor the statues
  are named here**: **(not stated)**.

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The Goddess closes the pond at a bereaved mother's demand**, told by Gopal Maharaj, the local
  priest, as his own eyewitness account on both sides: "After only fifteen days there was so much rain
  that a huge landslide filled the pond... it was only since a mother cried in front of the Goddess,
  because she had lost her child, that the pond disappeared." (p. 253, folio not stated). He explicitly
  rejects rival accounts: "People talk a lot about what has happened, but none of their stories are
  true" (p. 253, folio not stated).
- **Anil Kund carries the water of the Ganges through Shiva's hair.** "Once Shiva saved the earth from
  destruction by taming the waters of the River Ganges with the help of his long locks of hair (Wilkins
  2003, 467). He also used to come to the Hinglaj mountains for the solitude necessary for his
  meditation, and so some of the water from the Ganges also found its way from his hair into the hollow
  at Anil Kund." (p. 255, folio not stated) — attributed to "the local tradition" (p. 255, folio not
  stated).
- **Nine hundred, or eighty-four thousand, rivers meet at Anil Kund.** "all the holy rivers, including
  the Ganges, the Sarasvati, the Kaveri, and others, assembled together to meet and worship her.
  Altogether, nine hundred rivers came together here to form Anil Kund... In other accounts, the number
  of rivers convening at Anil Kund is eighty-four thousand—thus the alternate name, Chaurasi (Hin.
  `<caurāsī>`[text-layer?] [caurāsī] eighty-four)." (p. 256, folio not stated) — attributed to
  "Pilgrims from Gujarat" (p. 256, folio not stated).
- **The Goddess has retreated up the mountain.** "visitors at Hinglaj often claim that Anil Kund has
  now become the "real" tīrtha, the present-day abode of the Goddess who retreated high up into the
  mountains to escape the tiresome noise and masses of people now overrunning her temple in the valley"
  (p. 255, folio not stated).
- **The Goddess divides her day between the valley and the island.** "the Goddess moves between the
  Hinglaj Valley and the island, where she resides for the largest part of the day, for seven
  "pruhurs" (also from Hin. pahar, a three-hour period) (Hart 1839, 98)" (p. 260, folio not stated).
  A living version: the few who know of Astola "portray it as the "actual" seat of the Goddess who
  nowadays only rarely resides at Hinglaj" (p. 260, folio not stated).
- **Which body part fell where.** "One pilgrim mentioned that the island is the place where the
  Goddess's brahmarandhra is found, while at Hinglaj it is actually her womb (Hin. kokh) that fell to
  earth. He also added that this is why pilgrims can be reborn at Hinglaj and that he never saw
  anybody being born from a woman's head." (p. 260, folio not stated).
- **Rama's sun and moon.** "the imprint of a sun and a moon on the side of the mountain, supposedly
  etched there by Rama after he completed his journey to Hinglaj to receive purification for the sin of
  killing a Brahmin (see Chapter 3)" (p. 257, folio not stated). "The place is said to be unreachable
  by mortals and indeed looks quite inaccessible from the bottom of the valley, though it is possible
  to climb up there." (p. 257, folio not stated).
- **The Goddess drops the boulder as a concession.** "In ancient times, munis and r̥ṣis (Hin. sages,
  wise men) would meditate in the northern mountain range. For this reason, common people could no
  longer visit Chaurasi since they did not dare disturb the sages. As a result, the pilgrims asked the
  Goddess to arrange some other way for them to receive the merit" (p. 259, folio not stated) —
  attributed to Gopal Maharaj. **A rival origin for the same stone**: "Another story says that Guru
  Gorakhnath himself took the stone from somewhere around Anil Kund and brought it to this place
  (Jethamalani 1994, 8)." (p. 259, folio not stated).
- **The Nereid of Astola.** "Once a Nereid lived on Astola, who tricked sailors into coming to the
  island and on their arrival turned them into fish. The sun got irritated with the nymph and so told
  her to leave... which also freed the sailors and they all turned back into humans, thus creating the
  people later called the fish-eaters (Eggermont 1975, 81–82)." (p. 259, folio not stated) — attributed
  to Arrianus (AD 96–180) via Eggermont.
- **Pilgrims vanishing in the tunnel, and the author's disbelief.** "There are many legends about
  pilgrims disappearing in the tunnel due to such cavities, which allegedly lead deeper into the
  mountain. I strongly doubt that these holes were once big enough for a human being to fit through. No
  one I spoke with had actually witnessed such an incident, but the idea seems to persist among
  pilgrims at the shrine" (p. 265, folio not stated — note 30).
- **Guru Gorakhnath's and Guru Nanak's journeys to Hinglaj** — recorded here as claims the book reports
  and, in Nanak's case, evaluates; see "Arguments and interpretations" below.

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The author's central claim for Chapter 5: Hinglaj is a space of religious spatialization, not
  simply a religious space.** "inspired by the work of Lefebvre, I have suggested approaching Hinglaj
  not merely as a religious space but also as a space of religious spatialization, where practices,
  concepts, and imaginaries of space constantly work together. These three modes are mutually
  interdependent and influence each other via, in the words of Shields, a "three-way-dialectic"
  (Shields 1998, 120)." (p. 261, folio not stated).
- **The imaginary dominates the other two modes at religious sites.** "religious sites, in particular,
  tend to prioritize the moment of religious imaginary, where images of continuity and stability are
  considered as defining spatial practices or the representations of space." (p. 261, folio not
  stated). "At Hinglaj, for example, this process is seen in the notion of svayaṃbhū, which is also
  largely responsible for the site's cultural capital and which gains validity through the obfuscation
  of the site's history and course of development. The svayaṃbhū character of the religious imaginary
  distinguishes Hinglaj from other religious places in the Pakistani-Hindu landscape and ignores the
  involvement of human actors in this production of space." (p. 261, folio not stated).
- **The author's reading of the Sharan Kund story as narrative repair.** "The events surrounding Sharan
  Kund show the close connection between practice, conception, and the imaginary of a religious space."
  (p. 252, folio not stated). "an organizational explanation incorporated the event into the official
  corpus of mytho-historic narratives inside the valley" (p. 252, folio not stated). "Gopal Maharaj
  rearticulates contesting elements into a seamless, logical explanation... The mytho-historical
  explanation by one of the main religious authorities at the shrine helped to once more create a
  harmonious narrative in which the ceasing of the purification practice is the work of the Goddess and
  hence part of the divine will." (p. 253, folio not stated).
- **Tropes migrate between locations in the valley — an explicitly first-person argument.** "In my
  opinion, this shows how certain tropes migrate around the valley of Hinglaj over time, becoming
  associated with different locations during different periods." (p. 256, folio not stated). The
  evidence is the eye-cure, which Abadhut placed at `ākāś gaṃgā` / Chaurasi (Abadhut 1964) but which
  today attaches to Kir Kund: "I have not heard any particular mention recently of the restorative
  properties of Anil Kund's waters with respect to such diseases." (p. 256, folio not stated).
- **Power, after Fairclough, applied to the HSM.** "Fairclough offers a description of power as
  "controlling and constraining the contributions of non-powerful participants" (Fairclough 2001b,
  38f.), which is illustrative in this case." (p. 261, folio not stated).
- **A general "Hinduization" of the shrine, dated loosely.** "This development goes hand in hand with a
  general "Hinduization" of the shrine that began just a few decades ago and has gradually claimed the
  valley and the shrine as Hindu religious space. Since Hindu organizations—foremost the HSM—firmly
  control the representations of space in the area, their interpretations of what is seen and should be
  seen around the image currently prevail." (p. 262, folio not stated).
- **Stability of the three modes explains why the produced space is hard to oppose.** "The more
  coherent the interaction between these three modes, the more solidified and unopposed the produced
  spatiality will eventually be." (p. 261, folio not stated). "If the practice, conceptualization, and
  imaginary all articulate one coherent narrative, then opposition against such modes of spatialization
  is difficult." (p. 263, folio not stated).
- **"Duplication" or "multiplication" of sacred landscape, after Eck.** "Such associations of
  localities with supralocal mytho-historic narratives are quite common and create a kind of
  "duplication" or "multiplication" within the sacred landscapes of South Asia (Eck 1998)." (p. 256,
  folio not stated). "In general, these three locations in the northern mountain range and their focus
  on Gorakhnath, Guru Nanak, and Rama connect the sacred geography of Hinglaj to the broader religious
  traditions of South Asia." (p. 258, folio not stated).
- **The author's scepticism about Lakhavat's proof of Nanak's visit, stated by attribution not by
  refutation.** "It is again Lakhavat who tries to provide evidence for the guru's journey to Hinglaj.
  He publishes a map from the Atlas Travels of Guru Nanak showing a route allegedly traveled by Nanak
  on his way to Mecca on which he passes by the shrine in Balochistan. For Lakhavat, this provides
  sufficient proof of Nanak's presence in Hinglaj, and he concludes that the footprints found in the
  valley are proof of this visit (Lakhavat 2011, 76)." (p. 257, folio not stated). The words "tries to
  provide", "allegedly" and "For Lakhavat" carry the author's distance; **he does not state a verdict
  of his own**: **(not stated)**.
- **The Ctesias identification, offered and then withheld.** "The most intriguing aspect of these
  symbols is the mention of such a place by Ctesias in his work Indica... Charles King judges these
  mountains to be fifteen days' walking distance from the desert between Cutch and Multan (1865, 297).
  This certainly could be a reference to the valley of Hinglaj/Nani. To conclusively establish this
  connection, however, an archeological survey of the signs and their age would be necessary." (p. 258,
  folio not stated).
- **The Nath link is broken, offered as negative evidence.** "It is yet another sign of the breaking of
  the connection between the Nath saṃpradāy and Hinglaj after Partition that no one at the shrine
  mentioned to me a link between Chaurasi and the eighty-four Siddhas who are so significant within the
  Nath tradition." (p. 265, folio not stated — note 35).
- **Chapter 6's programme, stated in advance.** Hinglaj "has increasingly become a unifying
  socioreligious public space for myriad diverse Hindu communities" (p. 266, folio not stated); the
  author will examine the HSM's "guiding" of visitors, "shaping what I will call an "ideal Hindu
  body."" (p. 268, folio not stated), drawing on "Foucault's work on governmentality (Foucault 1982)"
  and "Gramsci's concept of hegemony (later adopted by Laclau and Mouffe [2001])" (p. 268, folio not
  stated). He also insists solidification is "not necessarily a one-way street" (p. 268, folio not
  stated).
- **Hinglaj as a condensation of national argument.** "Competing views found at Hinglaj relate to
  discourses among Hindu communities all over Pakistan (and in fact all over the world) and serve as a
  localized condensation of national and international discussions of what it actually means to be a
  (Pakistani) Hindu." (p. 267, folio not stated).
- **On his own taxonomy, a disclaimer.** "But it is not my aim to present a clear-cut taxonomy,
  explaining the function of religious sites. I want to show the intrinsic dynamics involved in the
  production of Hinglaj as a religious site." (p. 263, folio not stated — note 1).
