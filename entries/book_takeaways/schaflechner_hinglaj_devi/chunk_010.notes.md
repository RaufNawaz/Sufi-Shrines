## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The pilgrimage market, priced in three figures.** "a market dedicated to the organization of such
  cross-border journeys has developed" and "members of such communities are willing to pay huge
  amounts of money to complete it" (p. 183, folio not stated). Aguā 1 "charges around 60,000 Indian
  rupees (at that time approximately €700) for the organization of the journey, including the cost of
  the flight from Bombay to Karachi" (p. 183, folio not stated). "the major tour organizers, who
  usually charge 40,000 to 60,000 Indian rupees, depending on the mode of transportation. A journey
  with the help of aguā 2 costs between 30,000 and 45,000 Indian rupees." (p. 185, folio not stated)
  **"at that time" is not converted to a year in this chunk**: **(not stated)**.
- **What pilgrims sell to pay.** "Some of the members of his pilgrimage group had been saving up money
  for years or had even sold land or cattle to be able to afford the journey." (p. 183, folio not
  stated) A prospective Ahir pilgrim's family "was apparently well off and had offered to pay for the
  trip with money earned through renting a tractor to other farmers" (p. 182, folio not stated).
- **The aguā as an office, old and new.** "In representations of the traditional pilgrimage introduced
  above, the aguā served as the group's leader who provided important ritual and geographical know-how
  that guaranteed the journey's success. Similarly, the modern aguās are responsible for the
  well-being of the pilgrims, but today they must also provide bureaucratic connections and the
  political sophistication necessary for leading groups over the border." (p. 183, folio not stated)
- **Bribery as an established cost of the pilgrimage.** "Usually the Pakistani tour organizers were
  able to ensure a smooth process due to their even distribution of money among clerks and the police
  staff." (p. 184, folio not stated) "This logic of bribery and networking has become an important
  part of the pilgrimage in the era of modern-day nation states and is a lucrative business for Indian
  and Pakistani tour organizers." (p. 185, folio not stated) Rajendra Singh "was also very critical of
  certain wealthy Charan businessmen who, in his opinion, simply bribed some embassy clerks in order
  to obtain their visas" (p. 177, folio not stated).
- **Aguā 2's counter-business, and what it cannot do.** He "lives in a major Pakistani city", is "in
  contact with Indian devotees mainly from Gujarat", "organizes tours for up to seven people", houses
  pilgrims "in his private house", rents "a small bus" for the Hinglaj trip, and helps "with the police
  registration and the dealings with the army on the road" through connections said to follow from a
  business "important for the military" (p. 185, folio not stated). But "since this guide operates only
  from Pakistan, he has limited connections in India and thus cannot be of much help in the visa
  application process" (p. 185, folio not stated). **His city is deliberately unnamed**: **(not
  stated)**.
- **The tour operators' dependence on bad relations.** "the political volatility between the countries
  is a double-edged sword; the livelihood of such tour operators depends on the political atmosphere
  between the two countries remaining tense as well as upon the appointing of certain known
  individuals into influential offices" (p. 186, folio not stated).
- **The itinerary of the 2010 Gujarati group, stop by stop, with the author's film timecodes**:
  Swaminarayan temple, Karachi → Mata Bhawani temple, Thatta, offerings (11:00) → back to Karachi for
  the night → Liari, bhag'vā distributed (17:00) → hut on the Makran Coastal Highway, transfer to
  off-road vehicle → Chandrakup, coconut into the crater (23:00) → the shrine of Hinglaj, pūjā "until

  mountain was the climax of the group's journey."** (p. 181, folio not stated)
- **The bhag'vā.** "the pilgrims received the bhag'vā there, the saffron-colored piece of cloth that
  serves as a sign of the Goddess's protection on the way" (p. 181, folio not stated).
- **Taking stones home as temple-founding matter.** "one of the most important parts of the journey was
  that he had the chance to take several stones (Hin. piṃḍ, Skt. piṇḍa) from the River Aghor with him"
  (p. 173, folio not stated). The reported rule: "you can't establish a Hinglaj temple without having
  gone on the pilgrimage to the original [mūl] Hinglaj in Pakistan. You have to go there and you have

  Jaipur, and two he presented to his family." (p. 176, folio not stated) **The rule is contested
  inside the community: "Other Charans were puzzled by his interpretation and did not support the idea
  that it is absolutely necessary to have been at Hinglaj to establish one of her temples."** (p. 176,
  folio not stated)
- **Tokens from Balochistan as heirlooms.** "Tokens from Balochistan are held in high esteem, and some

- **Burial instead of cremation for Hinglajis of Kutch.** "Hinglajis in Kutch are not—as usual—burned

  `worshipped by their ancestors`[text-layer?]" (p. 178, folio not stated — see Doubts on this

  womb of the Goddess and thus gains the ātmā of a newborn child. They also may assume the name Kapri,

  not stated) **No festival is named**: **(not stated)**.
- **Dayaram's set formula for the practice, which the author heard repeated verbatim in two countries**
  — "In Hinglaj all kinds of sins are destroyed, and there is a rule that whoever goes there is called
  Hinglaji. After pilgrims have lived a full life of 100 years, they are not burnt on the pyre, but
  instead worshipped in a shrine [samādhi] as dev'ātmās [divine souls]. As in temples, other people
  will do pūjā for them. And whoever comes back from Hinglaj becomes a part of the Kapri sect. Like
  saints they are not simply burnt, but everybody gets a shrine, and everybody will be worshipped. On
  festival days people will come, and put siṃdūr [vermilion] on their samādhis. That is the rule!"
  (p. 182, folio not stated)
- **Kapri / dev'ātmā, as the book defines their relation.** "While Kapris can become dev'ātmās through
  the journey to Hinglaj, all dev'ātmās automatically become (half) Kapris after their successful
  pilgrimage." (p. 178, folio not stated) The author limits his own evidence: "Dayaram asserted that
  this practice is found all over India, but so far, I have only encountered it in Kutch, where many
  little shrines are found as evidence" (p. 178, folio not stated).
- **The Hinglaji title.** "Today, the epithet 'Hinglaji' is still used in the community for people who
  went on the yātrā." (p. 172, folio not stated) In Kutch too, "returnees from Hinglaj are also treated
  with awe and respect and are frequently given the epithet 'Hinglaji'" (p. 178, folio not stated).
- **The Charan obligation.** "The journey to the shrine in Balochistan ought to be made by every Charan
  once in their lifetime and is thus the sine qua non ritual for many. For the Charans, the pilgrimage
  is also a commemorative ceremony, remembering and renewing a bond they have shared with the Goddess
  for ages." (p. 172, folio not stated) Access now: "Today, only rarely can Charans obtain visas for
  the journey to Pakistan; however, when they do, they must do so with the help of agents, in many
  cases as a part of tours organized by Pakistani tour operators." (p. 172, folio not stated)
- **Visa waiting times as pilgrims report them.** "There were five of us who wanted to go—three from
  Gandhidham [Gujarat] and one from Calcutta. The guy from Calcutta had tried for over ten years to
  get the visa, the Gujaratis had even tried for fourteen years! We all went to Delhi and with the
  grace of the Goddess we got the visa right away." (p. 175, folio not stated) Generally: "Many
  would-be pilgrims try to obtain visas for years, receiving repeated rejections." (p. 184, folio not
  stated)
- **How the shrine's contact details travelled.** "About four months later, an acquaintance from Jaipur
  who had gone to Hinglaj in 1991 gave me the phone number of the Hinglaj Sheva Mandali in Karachi and

  not stated) **An invitation letter is therefore part of the visa apparatus.**
- **Video as advertising.** "The video he took of the trip was widely circulated among the devotees of

- **The shielded tour.** "The group was completely shielded from the outside world, and contact with

  `tunnel-`/`pilgrimages`[text-layer?] [tunnel-pilgrimages] are made possible through close
  collaboration between the Indian aguā and the local Pakistani tour organizers." (pp. 183–184, folio
  not stated)
- **The picnic vocabulary as a boundary practice.** "visitors to the shrine often discredit the

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The Charan epigraph on the obligation, attributed to Barhath.** "From the moment a Charan is born,
  Mother Hinglaj waits for him to come for the darśan; if he leaves the world otherwise [without having
  visited the shrine], then a stone falls from the Mother's mountain." (BARHATH 2006, 9) (p. 172, folio
  not stated).
- **Hinglaj as ādiśakti of the Charans.** "For the Charan of west India, the Goddess Hinglaj is the
  ādiśakti (Skt.), the primordial energy that has proliferated through their community for many
  generations (Westphal and Westphal-Hellbusch 1976; Samaur 1999; Kamphorst 2008)." (p. 172, folio not
  stated)
- **Self-manifestation of the Goddess in India.** Returned Hinglajis "often established Hinglaj temples
  in their cities and villages, and in a few cases even made the Goddess appear by herself locally
  (Skt. svayambhū) as a result of their devotion" (p. 172, folio not stated).
- **Return from the brink of death as a blessing.** "Homecomers were seen as a divine gift since, as the
  Charans say, the Goddess herself had blessed such pilgrims and allowed them to return safely from the
  brink of death." (p. 172, folio not stated)
- **The Goddess as the agent of the visa.** "She is the one who influences the employees working in the
  Pakistani embassy to issue visas, or who causes airport security personnel to get distracted at the
  right time." (p. 173, folio not stated) Rajendra's own version at the X-ray machine: "in the very
  moment when my bag went into the `X-`/`ray`[text-layer?] [X-ray] machine, someone came to meet the

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The border has replaced the desert as the filter on pilgrims — the chunk's central claim.** "While
  in the time before the construction of the Makran Coastal Highway the long and grueling journey to
  the shrine served as this filtering process, today, the established border has a similar role in
  keeping the journey to the Goddess 'elite.' The demanding and potentially lethal trek through the
  desert once kept access to the shrine restricted; now, this restriction remains in place for Indian
  pilgrims as a result of the fierce visa policies paired with the fear of the unknown in neighboring
  Pakistan." (p. 171, folio not stated) Restated for Kutch: "As in times past when the difficult desert
  route restricted movement to the shrine, now rigid visa regulations keep the number of the Hinglajis
  and dev'ātmās low and thus the practice alive." (p. 179, folio not stated)
- **Scarcity as the condition of a ritual status.** "It is due only to the contingencies surrounding the
  selection of pilgrims—particularly those resulting from opaque visa regulations and the legal
  ambiguity of the journey to a restricted area of Balochistan—that notions of divine selection can be
  maintained." (p. 179, folio not stated) And, in the language of power: "from the perspective of
  power, the prestige connected with the journey from India to the remote temple in Balochistan may be
  seen as the exclusion of many for the sake of a few. These exceptional few gain a certain symbolic
  capital and rise in the regard of their communities." (pp. 171–172, folio not stated)
- **Symbolic capital converted into a funerary practice** — the point of the Kapri case study: "there is
  another practice in this area that translates the symbolic capital earned on the journey into tangible
  practices" (p. 178, folio not stated); "the journey to Hinglaj has the power to change devotees'
  ritual status, not only transforming them into a Hinglaji but also changing them into dev'ātmās (Hin.
  divine soul) after their death" (p. 178, folio not stated).
- **A decline narrative running the other way across the border.** The Thatta priest's contrast is read
  by the author as "the debasement and decline within the Pakistani-Hindu community compared to the
  'real' traditions coming from India" (p. 171, folio not stated), a second axis on top of the "'real'
  and 'proper' practice" distinctions the book has already analysed (p. 171, folio not stated).
- **Stereotype as a producer of aura.** "In general, such stereotypes about Pakistan add to the
  dangerous aura and the mystification of the shrine, which, in some ways, newly articulates discourses
  of danger and difficulty found in older representations of the journey." (p. 170, folio not stated)
- **Borderlands after Purewal.** "Purewal writes that the hinterlands of the geopolitical border—the
  'borderlands'—not only extend for several kilometers into each country but also permeate opportunities
  for interaction between Indians and Pakistanis inside the country", quoting "borderlands are
  constituted not merely at geopolitical international borders, but also in day-to-day interactions and
  exchanges" (Purewal 2003, 542) (p. 184, folio not stated). Schaflechner's extension: "the borderlands
  consist of checkpoints, administration offices, and police stations" (p. 184, folio not stated); "the
  border is not only positioned at the margins of two nation states but also seems localized in multiple
  trials along the way, each of which has the potential to end their journey" (p. 184, folio not
  stated); and it "begins with the visa application process", so "the Pakistani borderlands start for
  Hinglaj pilgrims before they ever leave India" (p. 184, folio not stated).
- **Border control as a generator of business.** "the rigid control of movement at and around the
  geopolitical border can actually generate new business opportunities and prompt changes in power
  distribution" (p. 183, folio not stated).
- **A shift in what representations of Hinglaj are about, dated.** "In the centuries prior to the
  pilgrimage's institutionalization in the mid-1980s, the journey was characterized by the strenuous
  path through the deserts of Sindh and Balochistan, the stops en route, and the kinesthetic
  experience. Later representations, especially after 2001, started to show more interest in Hinglaj as
  a location and not as a pilgrimage." (p. 186, folio not stated)
- **Nostalgia as an organising principle, not merely a mood** — the book's key analytic move in §4.5:
  "Similar to the notion of solidification, I also understand this permanent reference to some lost
  unity as an organization principle with the ultimate goal to incorporate the tradition's recent
  dislocation. Here, too, proper and improper practices are marked and separated." (p. 187, folio not
  stated)
- **The tourism/pilgrimage literature invoked but held at arm's length.** "Notwithstanding the fact that
  Hinglaj is a particular case study, the debate bears similar characteristics to those of current
  discussions about the differences between tourism and pilgrimage (cf. Timothy and Olsen 2006; Urry
  1995; Cohen 1988)." (p. 187, folio not stated)
- **The author's own position inside the field as data.** The Charan community's help was itself read as
  the Goddess's will: "I received much help, and I was not permitted to give anything in return… 'I did
  not do anything, whatever you get is all the Goddess's mercy. She herself wants that your work should
  be successful.'" (p. 173, folio not stated) Rajendra turned the research question back on him: asked
  what had changed after his pilgrimage, he "simply repeated the question back to me, asking me how I
  felt as someone who had been to Hinglaj and was now staying in a Charan community" (p. 176, folio not
  stated).
- **The camera as an actor in the field.** "The video camera in particular affected the group
  dynamic"; "Many pilgrims apparently overestimated my influence on the media landscape and were of the
  opinion that they would appear on television in Europe or America"; "The interviews, therefore, had a
  double audience: an imagined 'Western' public as well as the rest of the pilgrimage group" (p. 180,
  folio not stated). Pilgrims used it to "amend stereotypes about the country in front of an implicit
  audience" (p. 180, folio not stated).
- **Different classes of pilgrim read the same trip differently.** "While some of the better-educated
  members of the group interpreted the trip in philosophical or political terms, it seemed that the
  aspects of purification, remission, and ridding oneself of sins were the main incentives for the
  members of the Ahir community." (p. 182, folio not stated)
- **Effort as the source of value, in the pilgrim's own theory.** "Whatever you want to get in this
  world, you have to work for it. The bigger the thing is that you wish for, the more you need to work
  for it. But this is not only hard work; it also means you need devotion. The old pilgrimage was
  difficult, the pilgrimage now is difficult, and it seems that in the future, too, it is going to be
  difficult." (p. 177, folio not stated) Rajendra's own gloss on why his status rose: "If the border
  were not there, then people would not value the pilgrimage so much" (p. 176, folio not stated).
- **The account is continuous with older Charan legend, not a break from it.** "his own account is also
  a tale of difficulty, faith, and devotion. In this way, his description resembles Charan mythologies
  and general representations of the yātrā dealing with tapasyā and divine intervention, and so connects
  current events to the vast body of Charan legends surrounding the shrine." (p. 177, folio not stated)
- **A pilgrim's political conclusion, reported.** One returnee said "that the trip had showed him that
  people in Pakistan were not so different from people in India and that terrorists are only a small
  part of the Pakistani population" (p. 183, folio not stated).
