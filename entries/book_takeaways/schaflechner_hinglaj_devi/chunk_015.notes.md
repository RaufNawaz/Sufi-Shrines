## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Custodianship: the HSM and the Dewani family.** "Hinglaj is the clan Goddess (Hin. kul'devī) of
  the Dewanis, a Sindhi Lohana family from Las Bela that has played an important role in organizing
  the event ever since the beginning of the institutionalized yātrā." (p. 274, folio not stated)
  "Virsimal Dewani is currently the pilgrimage's chief organizer and the face of the HSM in the
  media." (p. 274, folio not stated) "Ever since the inception of the HSM, Virsimal Dewani has been in
  charge of the organization and has thus played a central role in the Mandali." (p. 275, folio not
  stated) The Dewani clan is "currently responsible for many decisions made within the HSM" (p. 274,
  folio not stated). **No office title, no term, no formal appointment and no succession is given**:
  **(not stated)**.
- **The founder of the gathering, named.** "Even though the idea for an annual gathering at Hinglaj
  came from a Karachi devotee, Vasrup Chand Tusli, Virsimal Dewani was, from the beginning,
  significantly involved in the melā's establishment." (p. 275, folio not stated) **Tusli is given no
  dates, no caste, no office and no further appearance in the chunk**: **(not stated)**.
- **A dated change in the HSM's function.** Dewani "changed the role of the HSM from a mere travel
  operator in the 1980s to an active designer of the tradition" (p. 275, folio not stated), consistent
  with "the beginning of the institutionalized melā in the 1980s" (p. 282, folio not stated).
- **A dated first visit, and the limit of living memory at the shrine.** "Dewani himself visited
  Hinglaj for the first time when he was a child, more than forty years ago, in 1971… In fact, I could
  not find anyone else who had seen the place before 1971, and so most of my inquiries about the
  history of the valley during the twentieth century came, one way or another, back to Dewani's
  experience." (p. 276, folio not stated) **This is a stated limit on the book's own evidence base for
  the twentieth century and should be carried into any entry that cites it.**
- **The family's authority and its transnational reach.** "the family's history and long relationship
  with the Goddess adds to the HSM's overall authority and is one reason for its extensive influence"
  (p. 276, folio not stated). Dewani "has ties to Bombay and Dubai where parts of his family reside,
  and he maintains connections with various travel agents in India and internationally who organize
  trips to the Balochistan temple" (p. 276, folio not stated); his "regular correspondence with
  various spiritual experts from India, in particular, raises his prestige in the eyes of many
  devotees" (p. 276, folio not stated). The ritual tie "requires even members living abroad to return
  to Pakistan for certain ceremonies" (p. 275, folio not stated).
- **Caste composition of the custodial body, stated flatly.** The HSM is "a body consisting mainly of
  (Lasi) Lohana caste members" (p. 277, folio not stated), its institutional power "gained in large
  part through its upper-caste members and connection to a family with long-standing ties to the
  Goddess Hinglaj" (p. 277, folio not stated). "the Dewani family's influential position is, to a
  certain extent, also linked to their upper-class (caste) background" (p. 276, folio not stated).
- **The caste line inside the volunteer corps, stated as a rule without exception.** "While many
  low-caste communities 'volunteer' as masons, plumbers, or guards for the HSM, those volunteers
  posted in front of the Hinglaj temple, advising visitors on how they should perform their offering,
  are always Lohana. Prescriptions for pilgrim behavior inside the Hinglaj temple therefore remain
  firmly under the control of the Lasi Lohana community." (p. 282, folio not stated)
- **The volunteer corps and its unacknowledged payment.** "Today there is a sizable corps of HSM
  volunteers (Hin. sevā kar'nevāle)… Basically, anybody can volunteer… However, there is a cadre of
  'professional' volunteers who have worked with the HSM for many years. In interviews, these
  volunteers always emphasize that they are not employed by the HSM, but during my fieldwork between
  2009 and 2015, I noticed that they regularly received 'money donations' from the organization for
  their work." (p. 282, folio not stated) **The amounts are not given**: **(not stated)**. Their
  powers: "These volunteers have the authority to exclude visitors who misbehave, but normally their
  job consists mainly of pointing out minor transgressions" (p. 282, folio not stated); they are also
  "the pilgrims' primary source of information on sleeping options, water supplies, or food" (p. 282,
  folio not stated).
- **The annual maintenance cycle.** "Weeks before the big phālgun/caitra melā, the HSM cleans the
  valley, repaints the temples, and does minor renovation work. A few days before the beginning of the
  festival, paths are newly marked, and small arrows are painted on the ground to guide people through
  the site." (p. 271, folio not stated) The HSM "even responds to suggestions and requests by previous
  visitors" (p. 271, folio not stated), and this "refurbishing" itself "contributes to new
  interpretations of existing temples and pilgrimage paths" (p. 271, folio not stated).
- **Allocation of ground at the melā, and politics on site.** "many Hindu organizations and
  politicians approach the HSM to reserve a spot in the valley where they can set up stalls and
  stages. Hindu welfare organizations usually build small information stalls, and sometimes local
  politicians even sponsor dance and singing programs to reach out to the population and publicize
  their candidacy." (p. 272, folio not stated) **No fee, lease or rent is mentioned**: **(not
  stated)**.
- **How the festival is advertised.** "Several weeks before the April celebration, the HSM publicizes
  the exact dates and details of the festival on bulletins that are posted in Hindu temples in various
  Pakistani cities." (p. 281, folio not stated) The same sheets carry "a certain set of guidelines
  that (according to them) ought to be followed inside the valley" (p. 281, folio not stated).
- **The festival's shape.** Officially "begun with the visit to Chandrakup on the first day and ended
  with a collective havan (Hin. ceremony), in front of the Hinglaj temple on the third day" (p. 281,
  folio not stated) — i.e. three days.
- **The alcohol ban and its date.** "the strict banning of alcohol—a rule that emerged around the
  beginning of the millennium when the crowds started to swell" (p. 281, folio not stated). The rest
  of the printed guidelines "mainly address organizational issues… the parking of cars, the setting up
  of stalls for commercial purposes, and the mandatory display of identification cards during the
  festival" (p. 281, folio not stated).
- **The nine posted rules, dated 2011, in two languages.** "In 2011, the organization decided to post
  signs in Urdu and English stating the 'do's' and 'don'ts' inside the gates of the valley's enclosed
  area" (p. 282, folio not stated); "these rules appear in Urdu and English on two separate boards.
  Since the Urdu version does not deviate from the English, I only quote the English sign here"
  (p. 284, folio not stated). The nine, as the book prints them (p. 284, folio not stated): care for
  "the sanctity of Mata Mandir"; "When witnissing [sic] Mata take care of other pilgrims"; abstain
  from "creating a nuisance in the premises of Mata Mandir"; "Drinking alcohol is prohibited in the
  premises of `Mata Mandir offenders`[text-layer?] [Mata Mandir. Offenders] shall be handed over to
  the police."; "Eating of any kind of meat and fish are [sic] prohibited"; "Mata Mandir is open only
  for the purpose of paying homage to Mata."; "Don't come here for picnicking as Mata Hinghlaj [sic]
  Mandir is a sacred pilgrimage place"; "Please don't litter"; "Please cover your head when entering
  Mata Mandir." **Before 2011 "official rules of conduct were only presented during a few days of
  festive activity, and then only by oral communication"** (p. 284, folio not stated).
- **Two unposted rules, and the police as enforcer.** "the HSM also propagates two additional rules
  not mentioned on the board that are enforced by volunteers and the Balochistan police during the
  gathering days: the ban on animal sacrifices at the shrine of Kali Mata… and the official
  prohibition on Muslims entering the valley during the April festival." (p. 284, folio not stated)
  **This is the chunk's only statement of state involvement at the site**; no department, order or
  legal instrument is named: **(not stated)**.
- **Escalation of the means of control, in three stages.** Personal contact between HSM
  representatives and pilgrims "at the beginning of the institutionalized melā in the 1980s"; then
  loudspeakers "as the number of visitors began to surge"; then the 2011 boards (pp. 282, 284, folio
  not stated). At the 2012 havan "the crowds started to rush toward the fire and many people were
  pushed to the ground. Some volunteer workers took megaphones and appealed to the sanctity of the
  place and the wrath of the Goddess to calm the mob." (p. 282, folio not stated)
- **The free community kitchen as an instrument of the food rule.** "With the help of the free
  community kitchen (Urd. bhanḍārā), the HSM exerts direct influence on food consumption and
  therefore, according to widespread Hindu theories on the relationship between diet and the
  constitution of the human mind/body, on the amount of tamas (Hin.) present in pilgrims during the
  festival." (p. 285, folio not stated) **Who funds or runs the bhanḍārā, and what it serves**:
  **(not stated)**.
- **Two Hindu welfare organizations active at the melā, with founding years.** "the Pakistan Hindu
  Seva Samaj (PHSS) and the Pakistan Hindu Seva Trust (PHS). While the PHSS was established quite
  recently, in 2012, the PHS began its work in 2010." (p. 272, folio not stated) The PHS "organizes a
  wide range of initiatives and uses the various melās at Hinglaj to make strategic contacts among
  Hindus in Karachi, Sindh, and, in fact, the whole country" (p. 272, folio not stated).
- **PHS: subsidised pilgrimage for widows, and printed prayers.** "Almost every year, the PHS collects
  donations to fund the travel expenses of Hindu widows who wish to go to Hinglaj. In 2012, the
  organization printed leaflets with prayers in Sindhi and Urdu 'to help people' (logoṃ kī madad
  kar'ne ke lie) recite the correct prayers at the shrine." (p. 272, folio not stated) Volunteers
  distributing the flyers "simultaneously exchanged phone numbers and Facebook contacts with other
  devotees" (pp. 272–273, folio not stated), and the organisation "collects these contacts to mobilize
  people throughout the year" (p. 273, folio not stated).
- **PHSS: founded out of a fatal accident at the shrine.** "The PHSS is a comparatively younger
  organization, established in response to the tragic death of a walking pilgrim who was hit by a
  motorbike (see Chapter 4). The incident raised questions about the adequacy of security and medical
  facilities inside the valley and ultimately led to the founding of the PHSS." (p. 273, folio not
  stated) **The pilgrim is not named and the year is not given here** — Chapter 4 is cross-referenced
  and is outside this chunk: **(not stated)**. Initially it collected donations "in order to set up a
  medical camp at Hinglaj" (p. 273, folio not stated); "Many members also help the HSM volunteers
  clean up the valley after the festivals" (p. 273, folio not stated). "In 2013, just one year after
  its establishment, the PHSS had already begun organizing religious functions in the Narayanpur
  neighborhood and had started collecting money for the construction of an ashram in Karachi's Defence
  Colony." (p. 273, folio not stated) It uses "identification cards and yellow T-shirts sporting the
  organization's logo" (p. 273, folio not stated).
- **The PHSS's seven stated aims, from Urdu flyers distributed at the Hinglaj melā in 2012** (p. 273,
  folio not stated): "To help poor families and widows with their monthly expenses"; "To assist
  impoverished youth to arrange their marriage"; "To provide free medical treatment"; "To establish
  medical camps at pilgrimages"; "To supply poor children with education and scholarships"; "To
  establish a society for the donation of blood"; "To establish camps for flood-affected people".
- **Who the two organisations reach.** "Both organizations—the PHS and the PHSS—appeal mainly to
  members of the Dalit communities; although the PHS is patronized by high-caste philanthropists, for
  the most part, its core message of Hindu unity and its community-based social work attracts young,
  low-class urban Hindu men who are accessible through social media." (pp. 273–274, folio not stated)

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The theoretical frame, restated from Chapter 1.** The author returns to "the work of Laclau and
  Mouffe, whose theoretical concept of the nodal point is apt for analyzing the distribution of
  several truth-claims within a certain field" (p. 269, folio not stated). "Nodal points unite a group
  of people and supply it with coherency and meaning. The 'equivalential' (Laclau and Mouffe 2001)
  relationship emerging between these people stems from a common opposition to an outside, which is
  antagonized by all members. In other words, group unity will be defined through its constitutive
  outside." (p. 269, folio not stated)
- **The worked example is the 1953 Pakistani commission on the anti-Ahmedi riots** — "When the
  Pakistani government appointed a commission in 1953 to investigate the reasons behind the
  anti-Ahmedi riots, a group of Muslim legal scholars was formed to define once and for all who should
  be considered a true Muslim. After days of debate, their lack of consensus caused them to declare:
  …" followed by the block quotation (Cited in Shaikh 2009, 61; italics, brackets, and sic erat
  scriptum in the original)" (p. 269, folio not stated). The author draws from it: "it is always
  possible to add one more meaning to a universal term… Only through the negative definition—through
  the definition of what the group is not—the chain of characteristics is closed" (p. 269, folio not
  stated), and cites Farzana Shaikh's conclusion "that the anti-Ahmedi sentiment in Pakistan was also
  a means to bring a feeling of unity to the various Muslim schools in the formative period of the
  Islamic Republic (Shaikh 2009)" (p. 269, folio not stated).
- **The application to Hinglaj.** "A nodal point represents, often temporarily, a group of
  heterogeneous members through their common denial of something 'fake.' The same is true for
  truth-claims about Hinglaj, as they supply a group with coherence and closure through a commonly
  shared denial." (pp. 269–270, folio not stated) "This analysis is particularly useful after a
  tradition's dislocation or, in other words, when a group is in upheaval. Then, the contingent
  foundations of truth-claims are still visible, and their exclusionary logic is conspicuous."
  (p. 270, folio not stated)
- **The central claim of §6.5: an education/progress discourse that excludes without appearing to.**
  "the HSM—a body consisting mainly of (Lasi) Lohana caste members—propagates its own interpretation
  of the Hinglaj tradition and simultaneously denies those of many others. In this process, they
  create a hierarchical discourse of 'educated,' and thus 'proper,' behavior (that which comfortably
  coincides with the Lasi Lohana tradition) versus 'uneducated,' and thus 'improper,' behavior inside
  the valley." (p. 277, folio not stated) The author adds the contingency claim: "the selection of
  practices that are ultimately denied or supported by the temple organization is essentially
  contingent" (p. 277, folio not stated). He places the HSM "in accordance with reformist Hindu
  movements and their historical rejection of folk traditions (Fuller 1992, 99–103)" (p. 277, folio
  not stated).
- **Dewani's own formulation of the method, and the author's reading of it.** Dewani (2012 interview):
  "Educated people have the responsibility to pass on knowledge, but not by prohibiting anything!… The
  goal is the same whether you sit in an A/C coach, a car, or `a off-road vehicle`[text-layer?] [an
  off-road vehicle]. Also in a train there are three different classes; the only difference is the
  fee. But the train arrives." (p. 279, folio not stated) The author's gloss: "he does not, therefore,
  simply oppose certain practices performed at Hinglaj by marking them 'wrong;' instead, he organizes
  them according to their supposed efficacy… Dewani's words do not baldly exclude other practices;
  rather, they deny their efficacy." (p. 279, folio not stated)
- **The Ganesha installation read as a technique of closure.** "With this blunt installation of its
  own interpretation of the paṛāv—corresponding naturally to its primary membership's own tradition—
  the HSM denied other voices the power to advance their own truth-claims of this place… This example
  makes clear how the solidification of one particular tradition always entails a form of exclusion,
  but one that is represented by the HSM as a necessary correction of an 'uninformed'
  interpretation." (p. 280, folio not stated)
- **Signage as the decisive step from oral to written norm.** "the step from oral circulation to a
  written declaration should not be underestimated. While orally transported content can be changed
  easily or might simply be ignored, the bold, black-on-yellow letters at the entrance (in two
  different languages) give an impression of steadiness, stability, and normativity." (p. 284, folio
  not stated) "Just as books stabilize history by limiting the range of ways in which its signifiers
  may be interpreted, these boards stabilize practice, limiting the possible range of action by
  visitors." (p. 284, folio not stated) The signs also "visibly delineate the area as religious space
  rather than merely scenic" (p. 283, folio not stated) and extend the rules across the whole year
  rather than the festival days (pp. 283–284, folio not stated). Kim Knott is quoted in support:
  "Sacred space is not the stimulus for ritual; ritual, as sacred-making behaviour, brings about
  'sacred' space. Ritual takes place, and makes place in this sense. (Knott 2005, 43)" (p. 283, folio
  not stated), and again on the body (Knott 2005, 60f.) (p. 285, folio not stated). Latour supplies
  the vocabulary: "'easily readable' (Latour 1983, 161)" and "'inscription devices' (Latour 1983,
  161)" (p. 286, folio not stated), with Latour 2005, 30–34 on groups as traces (p. 284, folio not
  stated) and the epigraph "We cannot distinguish between those moments when we have might and those
  when we are right." (LATOUR 1993, 183) (p. 277, folio not stated).
- **The vegetarianism rule read as the production of a body.** "the Mandali not only directs how
  people should act inside Hinglaj but also actually creates an ideal of a literal body that consumes
  according to the organization's understanding of 'proper' practice" (p. 285, folio not stated). The
  author notes the rule's external legitimation: Dewani "had traveled repeatedly to India and visited
  major religious sites there, so the idea of imposing vegetarianism on a large scale at Hinglaj was
  legitimized by what he had seen at other sacred Hindu places" (p. 285, folio not stated), and that
  ahiṃsā as a long-standing pilgrimage practice "is cited by many sources… although this is difficult
  to prove (cf. Abadhut 1964, Shastri 1977, Lakhavat 2011)" (p. 285, folio not stated).
- **The Sindhi-nationalist denial of caste, and the Dalit counter-position, set out as a conflict the
  author does not resolve.** Thakur, "a historian of Sindh", in Sindhi Culture (1951): "[c]aste system
  is almost absent in Sind" (Thakur 1997, 25) (p. 277, folio not stated). "To devoted Sindhi Hindu
  nationalists, acts of caste discrimination are anathema, and in many of my conversations with them,
  such individuals told me that it exists today only in India or other provinces of Pakistan."
  (p. 278, folio not stated) Against this: "This behavior is fiercely opposed by Dalit organizations
  that strongly criticize the upper castes, especially the Hindu landowning class, and their
  involvement in the oppression of the lower jātis. Some people even went so far as to blame the
  difficulties for Hindu communities in Pakistan solely on the upper-caste Hindus" (p. 278, folio not
  stated). The author explicitly declines to settle it: "A separate study is needed to give this
  important and difficult topic its due." (p. 278, folio not stated) He adds that "many efforts by
  upper-caste Hindus to 'educate' or to bring 'development' to the lower castes are mocked as
  hypocrisy by Dalit organizations" (p. 278, folio not stated).
- **The divide is not the HSM's invention.** "This 'modern/educated/proper' and
  'primitive/uneducated/improper' divide is not only found at the shrine of Hinglaj but is also
  present in Urdu publications about Hindu communities in Pakistan more generally (cf. Jethamalani
  1994; Jajot 2011; Rathi 2010)." (p. 278, folio not stated) "Clearly, the HSM is not the sole driver
  of this brand of progress and change… Within the past decade, especially, the community has found a
  new confidence and voice as a minority in Pakistan." (pp. 278–279, folio not stated)
- **§6.7: governmentality and hegemony.** The author uses Foucault's governmentality "as an approach
  to power that analyzes the structures, practices, and institutions designed to influence people's
  behaviors" (p. 287, folio not stated), distinguishing the two senses of the term after Füller and
  Marquardt 2009 (p. 287, folio not stated), quoting Foucault 1982, 789 ("an action upon an action")
  and Foucault 1982, 790 ("[p]ower is exercised only over free subjects"; the definition of "to
  govern") (pp. 287–288, folio not stated), and Howarth 2010, 321 (p. 287, folio not stated). Applied:
  "The HSM does not directly impose its notions of 'proper' behavior onto visitors since doing so
  would only trigger (more) resistance by many. Instead, the organization connects its understanding
  of the shrine to wider notions of progress and education" (p. 288, folio not stated). "Their
  demands, spread by institutional means or inscriptional devices, were formulated in order to change
  the behavior of people visiting the shrine." (p. 288, folio not stated) The organisation's members
  "can also rely on a strong ideological backing from the Pakistani-Hindu intelligentsia and from
  several Hindu welfare organizations that validate these restrictions" (p. 288, folio not stated).
  The chunk ends as hegemony is introduced "back to the work of Gramsci" (p. 288, folio not stated).
- **A self-description of the HSM's self-understanding.** "the Mandali perceives itself as a guardian
  of truth and in the role of an educator seeking to rid the Goddess's tradition of antiquated and
  premodern conceptions" (p. 278, folio not stated); Dewani "understands the temple organization as a
  part of a progressive movement that attempts to rid the Hindu community (Urd. barādarī) of its
  backward ideas and practices" (p. 279, folio not stated).
