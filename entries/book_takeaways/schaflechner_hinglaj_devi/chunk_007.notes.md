- **chandragup-baba-chandragup** (the book's names for it: `Chandrakup`, `the mud volcano`, `Baba
  Chandrakup`; it lies on the Hinglaj route between Sonmiani and the River Aghor)
  - **Its function on the route is to grant or withhold permission.** "the group sets off in the
    direction of Chandrakup, the mud volcano, where they will ask for permission to proceed to the
    shrine of Hinglaj Mata (Abadhut 1964, 47)" (p. 128, folio not stated).
  - **It is expected to heal.** "Kunti's secret hope is that at Baba Chandrakup, Thirumal will be
    healed of his mental illness (Abadhut 1964, 48)." (p. 128, folio not stated) **This is the only
    place in the chunk where the honorific `Baba` is attached to the site.**
  - **Approach conditions.** "The area before Chandrakup is quite arid, and there is nothing to
    protect the pilgrims from the blazing heat." (p. 129, folio not stated) Earlier: "ahead lies one of
    the most demanding parts of their journey" (p. 129, folio not stated).
  - **The ritual protocol, as the ritual guide lays it down.** "Before the group ascends the mud
    volcano, Rupalal instructs them on the proper proceedings at Chandrakup (Abadhut 1964, 156–157). He
    says that to receive permission to continue their journey, they must worship Chandrakup and yell
    out their sins at the crater's edge. Only then will they be allowed to carry on with their journey.
    The pilgrims also bake a small cake (roṭ), which must be offered to the volcano." (p. 130, folio
    not stated) "After an emotional night in which many pilgrims worry acutely about their upcoming
    confessions at Chandrakup, the group begins their ascent (Abadhut 1964, 164)." (p. 130, folio not
    stated)
  - **The offering and the confession sequence.** "On the top of the volcano, Rupalal offers the roṭ
    prepared by all the pilgrims together by throwing it into the crater (Abadhut 1964, 166). One after
    another, the pilgrims are called to confess their sins." (p. 130, folio not stated) **The roṭ is
    prepared collectively and offered by the aguā, not by each pilgrim.**
  - **The volcano as lie-detector.** "At one man's turn, however, the wind stops and the volcano's
    bubbles cease to rise. This is understood as a sign that the pilgrim has not confessed to all of
    his sins, and so Rupalal implores him to repent. The man initially hesitates but then admits that
    he impregnated a servant who was subsequently sent away from his house a few years ago (Abadhut
    1964, 168). After that, the volcano's bubbles again begin to rise (Abadhut 1964, 169)." (pp.
    130–131, folios not stated)
  - **The rite broken off.** "Suddenly, Thirumal appears on the rim of the crater and with wild
    laughter jumps into the volcano, submerging beneath the mud (Abadhut 1964, 169). In horror, the
    whole group runs down the volcano's side, including those who have not yet confessed their sins
    (Abadhut 1964, 170). Exhausted and terrified, they proceed toward Hinglaj without returning to
    Chandrakup." (p. 131, folio not stated)
  - **The unabsolved passage is later judged improper.** Rupalal "says that the group repeatedly
    disregarded the proper rules of the pilgrimage (Abadhut 1964, 204). Particularly in light of
    Thirumal's suicide at Chandrakup, which prevented a few pilgrims from confessing their sins, he
    feels the group should not have been allowed to proceed—let alone cross the River Aghor." (p. 131,
    folio not stated)

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Austerity as the measure of a journey.** "means of transportation are often judged according to the
  degree of tapas/tapasyā or austerity they entail" (p. 117, folio not stated). Schaflechner's reading
  of Abadhut: "Abadhut obviously wishes to make a strong case for the necessary physical effort of
  tapasyā on a pilgrimage. For him, it clearly matters which way a pilgrim chooses to reach the
  location." (pp. 117–118, folios not stated) "Especially the metaphor of burning the senses, implied in
  the adjective dagdh, speaks of the connection of tapasyā and the blazing desert heat (cf. Shee 1986,
  187). Thus, he also distinguishes between the salvific powers of the shrine and those of the journey
  itself." (p. 118, folio not stated)
- **The purity mark, softened.** Burning on the forearm replaced by "the mark of a cold stamp" for
  hygiene reasons; "only two or three people came to the temple in the past few years to receive the
  mark" (p. 117, folio not stated). **This is the chunk's only visitor figure of any kind, and it counts
  recipients of a mark, not pilgrims.**
- **The organised party: recruitment, season, guides.** Gathering at the Nagnath akhāṛā, Karachi, "in
  the month āṣāṛh, 1946 CE"; the Nath community "guide pilgrims once or twice a year"; "After a month,
  enough people have gathered to comprise a pilgrimage party, and cameleers are arranged from the area
  around the Hinglaj shrine" (p. 127, folio not stated).
- **Who pays.** "The journey is financially supported by a Jain businessman who is himself a Goddess
  devotee and has already travelled to many places of Goddess worship, such as Calcutta's Kali temple or
  the distant śakti pīṭh of Kamakhya. The businessman also organizes a camel for an elderly Bengali
  woman, Bhairavi, who cannot walk by herself. He advises the pilgrims to carry many onions with them in
  case their water runs short (Abadhut 1964, 3)." (p. 127, folio not stated) **A named sponsorship
  practice with no sum attached: (not stated).**
- **The oath at the river, in three clauses.** "There the group gives an oath to an old priest (prauṛh
  paṃḍā) who ties an ochre-colored piece of cloth around their heads and makes them swear that they will
  refrain from any violence, that they will help each other, and that under no circumstances will they
  give any other pilgrim water from their own water bottle (surāhī)." (p. 127, folio not stated) The
  oath is broken almost at once: Bhairavi "sprinkles water on their faces, breaking the oath given just
  a few days earlier at the river's edge (Abadhut 1964, 20)" (p. 127, folio not stated).
- **The office of the aguā and the chaṛī.** "the group meets their aguās, Rupalal and Sukhalal... who
  serve as their ritual guides through the desert. Rupalal carries the chaṛī, a stick, which is
  meticulously worshipped and symbolizes the protection of the Goddess." (p. 127, folio not stated) The
  aguā's authority in the desert: "the aguā tells them they must reach the next resting spot or else
  perish in the desert" (p. 129, folio not stated). Journey's end is marked materially: "Rupalal digs
  the chaṛī into the earth to indicate the journey's completion (Abadhut 1964, 201)" (p. 131, folio not
  stated). The mytho-historic precedent is Lalu Jasraj as Rama's "chaṛīdār" (p. 121, folio not stated).
- **A state levy on pilgrimage, undated and unquantified.** "In Sonmiani, the group must report to the
  police station to pay their taxes for the pilgrimage. The officer in charge, however, is not present,
  and so they have to wait for a few days." (p. 128, folio not stated) **The amount, the authority and
  the legal basis are (not stated); the episode is set in 1946 CE by (p. 127, folio not stated).**
- **Donation to the ascetic.** "The pilgrims give him the gurudān, a donation, and cross the river
  (Abadhut 1964, 199)." (p. 131, folio not stated) **No sum: (not stated).**
- **Offering at the shrine and at the volcano.** The `roṭ` cake baked collectively and thrown into the
  crater (pp. 130, folios not stated); at Hinglaj itself the pilgrims "bathe and put on fresh clothes to
  enter the womb of the Mother", and the altar is "decorated with oleander flowers, incense, and
  candles" (p. 131, folio not stated). **No cash offering at the shrine is described: (not stated).**
- **Ritual purity as a live dispute inside a party.** Jayashankar "becomes increasingly upset with the
  pilgrimage because, according to him, the rules of ritual purity are not being followed. He complains,
  for example, that it is not proper to do a darśan at one of the many holy places along the way without
  first taking a bath. Because this is not possible in the desert, he is upset at the loss of some of
  the potential spiritual merit to be gained (Abadhut 1964, 50)." (p. 128, folio not stated)
- **Death on the route and the rites that cannot be done.** "the group is unable to perform any death
  rituals for the Brahmin, so instead, they simply bury him in the hard desert ground (Abadhut 1964,
  91)." (p. 129, folio not stated)
- **Dātun at the Aghor**, "the pilgrims clean their teeth and dig the dātun twig into the earth
  according to the required custom" (p. 131, folio not stated).
- **Marijuana among pilgrims**, reported without comment: at a local's house Thirumal "even starts to
  talk and smokes some marijuana with the other pilgrims (Abadhut 1964, 124)" (p. 130, folio not
  stated).
- **Infrastructure changing the route.** National Highway 25 shifted the start of many pilgrimages to
  Liari in Balochistan and caused the first resting places to be bypassed; Karachi's growth erased them
  (p. 121, folio not stated). **Schaflechner defers the detail: "this will be dealt with in detail later

- **Media as an institution of the pilgrimage.** The Bengali novel of 1957, the Hindi translation of
  1964, the 1959 film, and the Rāmāyaṇa's "very successful television series in the late 1980s, which
  was refilmed in 2012" (pp. 119, 126, folios not stated). "Both of these works contributed hugely to
  the dissemination of the pilgrimage narrative among the wider public." (p. 126, folio not stated)

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Rama's sin and the sentence of pilgrimage**, attributed by the book to a paraphrase of Jethamalani,
  Hart and Jareca (p. 120, folio not stated): the sage Kumbhodar refuses Rama's food because he is a
  "Brahmicide"; "by slaying the demon Ravana, Rama made himself guilty of 'brahm hatyā'... and thus
  committed one of the worst sins possible. To cleanse himself of this sin, the sage advises Rama to
  make a pilgrimage to Hinglaj Mata, the only place that can purify him." (p. 120, folio not stated)
- **The Goddess's army defeats the king of Ayodhya.** At the "Ungakhera Bherum Luk" pass, "the Goddess's
  army, which guards the entry to her empire, intercepts the group. A fight breaks out in which many of
  Rama's men die, and after twelve years of battle, the king from Ayodhya and his army must retreat."
  (pp. 120–121, folios not stated)
- **The Goddess's condition: come as a simple pilgrim.** "she responds that he must return to his first
  stop, Ram Bagh, and make the journey again as a simple pilgrim. So Rama leaves his entourage, his
  army, and vehicles behind and sets out to walk to the shrine, accompanied only by his closest
  friends." (p. 121, folio not stated)
- **The Devi's promise to the excluded**, the charter for later pilgrims: "Rama's men are devastated at
  not being allowed to accompany their lord to the Goddess's abode. The Devi thus promises them that
  their descendants will, at some point in time, all come back to do the pilgrimage." (p. 121, folio not
  stated)
- **Three attempts to make water, two failures and one success.** Hanuman's stomping "produces only a
  dry riverbed"; Lakshmana's arrow "only succeeds in detaching one of the hills"; "In agony, Sita places
  her palm down on the soil and thereby makes five wells appear, from which the group drinks." (p. 121,
  folio not stated) The dry riverbeds and wells are still pointed out in the landscape: "three dry
  riverbeds, which the pilgrims claim were from the time of Rama's journey" (p. 122, folio not stated).
  **Wilford's version contradicts Hart's**: the river "is said to have been dug out by Rama (Wilford
  1851, 249)" (p. 122, folio not stated).
- **Purification granted and commemorated.** "the Devi grants him purification of his sin. To mark his
  completed yātrā, he carves the symbols of the sun and moon on the mountain opposite the temple."
  (p. 121, folio not stated)
- **Sita's ordeal by fire**, from the Rāmāyaṇa summary: "Sita responds to this insult by burning herself
  on the pyre but when the fire does not touch her, her purity and divine nature are proven." (p. 120,
  folio not stated) And her disappearance: "the ground opens, and the princess disappears." (p. 120,
  folio not stated)
- **The volcano detects a concealed sin** — attributed to Abadhut's narrative, not asserted by
  Schaflechner: the bubbles cease at the turn of a pilgrim who has not confessed everything, and resume
  after he admits he "impregnated a servant" (pp. 130–131, folios not stated).
- **The vision in the tunnel**, attributed to Abadhut: "he has a vision of the Goddess who laughingly
  gazes at him" (p. 132, folio not stated), followed by the Aghori Baba's injunction of lifelong silence
  about it (p. 132, folio not stated).
- **Thirumal's madness, recovery and relapse** are narrated without supernatural attribution, though
  Kunti hopes "that at Baba Chandrakup, Thirumal will be healed of his mental illness" (p. 128, folio not
  stated); "Strangely, over the course of these days, Thirumal begins to recover" (p. 129, folio not
  stated).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Narrative as the carrier of orthopraxy.** "I understand narratives in their role as bringing
  together a particular set of elements into a meaningful sequential order... they address a certain
  audience that has a relationship with or simply an interest in their content (Hinchman and Hinchman
  1997, xv). A community's narratives convey what must be done and how it should be done but
  also—and perhaps most important—what should not be done. They can be case studies or examples
  simplifying abstract concepts of social behavior (Berrenberg 2007, 30)." (p. 118, folio not stated)
- **Mytho-historic narrative binds landscape to body.** "In the case of many ritual journeys,
  mytho-historic narratives connect landscapes with bodily memories. They recall the hardships of former
  travelers as well as their successes and faults during their travels. Such narratives give ritual
  journeys meaning and locate them within the wider religious discourse of a community." (p. 118, folio
  not stated)
- **Withering without canonisation — the chunk's central historical claim.** "This example shows that,
  without canonization, connections of topography and mytho-historic narratives associated with Hinglaj
  have gradually withered." (p. 123, folio not stated) With the qualification: "The reasons for this
  shift cannot be fully reconstructed on the basis of the material available to me." (p. 123, folio not
  stated) **This is the book's title thesis of `change and solidification` applied to the route's
  stories, and it is stated as a loss, not a gain.**
- **A methodological limit stated twice.** "Due to the scarcity of sources, it cannot simply be assumed
  that the pilgrimage described in them was the form generally followed in those days" (p. 122, folio not
  stated); and on Hart's `Hiṃgulā Purāṇ` claim, "a fact that I cannot confirm, at least not with the
  version available to me" (p. 121, folio not stated). **Schaflechner repeatedly marks what his sources
  will not bear.**
- **The narrating-I and the experiencing-I.** "This split within the I-narrator can be described as
  having two layers: the 'narrating-I' and the 'experiencing-I' (Vogt 1990, 71).24" (p. 123, folio not
  stated) The narrating-I "is able to weave contingent events together into a coherent story in which
  every detail fits into a flow of events, ultimately climaxing in the temporal location of the
  narrating-I. The experiencing 'I,' by contrast, acts and reacts to the events within the story's
  timeline without the benefit of hindsight." (p. 123, folio not stated)
- **Autobiography as a literary regime, after Renza.** Autobiography necessarily "borrows [ways of
  writing] from the methodological procedures of the imaginative fiction" (Renza 1977, 2), which "places
  autobiographical accounts written from the perspective of an I-narrator somewhere between literary
  'design' and historical 'truth'" (p. 124, folio not stated). "discussions about historical accuracy in
  autobiographical accounts are structurally limited as such accounts follow a certain literary regime."
  (p. 124, folio not stated)
- **Memory dated from its moment of retrospection.** "The temporal moment of this retrospection is
  crucial to the composition of memory because a coherent life narrative helps to construct the 'durable
  character of an individual' (Ricoeur 1991, 77)"; the point of recall may "'subordinate' the history to
  such an extent that the narrative represents 'the writer's present self-identity' (Renza 1977, 3).25"
  (p. 124, folio not stated)
- **Situated stories.** McLean, Pasupathi, and Pals describe autobiographical narratives as "situated
  stories", personal memories "created within a specific situation, by particular individuals, for
  particular audiences, and to fulfill particular goals" (McLean, Pasupathi, and Pals 2007); so
  "autobiographies are not stable presentations of a certain part of life but... change and grow
  alongside the author's own development" (p. 124, folio not stated). "Here, questions such as
  publication date, publishing house, and even the language in which the story is composed must be
  considered." (p. 125, folio not stated)
- **The move Schaflechner makes with all this — away from factuality.** "it may be more appropriate to
  turn one's analysis away from notions of factuality in favor of questions about why and for whom such
  authenticity might be important." (p. 125, folio not stated)
- **The `witnessing-I`, the chapter's own coinage for this corpus.** "the authorial name (the
  association of author, narrating-I, and experiencing-I) confirms the truth of the witnessed events,
  which has crucial implications for this particular corpus of texts" (p. 125, folio not stated). Two
  ways of establishing it, after Lejeune: the obvious, "when the name of the author is the same name
  mentioned in the following text" (p. 125, folio not stated) — exemplified by Jasol, "'One of them
  asked: "Where can I find Narasimha Jasol among you?" I was startled. Who could be this man asking for
  me in Pakistan?' (Jasol 2008, 92)" (p. 126, folio not stated) — and the implicit "autobiographical
  pact" (Lejeune 1989), which requires that "'vital statistics' (date of birth, ethnicity, language)
  should be identical between the narrator and the author (Lejeune 1989, 11)" and that "the story should
  relate to a world that can be linked to the vital statistics of the author" (p. 126, folio not stated).
- **The author's own working rule for the re-narrations that follow.** "Accepting obvious and implicit
  forms of the autobiographical pact, in the following re-narrations I will, for the sake of clarity,
  associate the name of the author with the narrator and the protagonist of the story." (p. 126, folio
  not stated) **This governs how every summary in §3.6 should be read, including the Abadhut summary in
  this chunk.**
- **Why the pact matters at this shrine specifically.** "For many devotees and authors I have met, this
  witnessing aspect of the autobiographical pact was more important than the texts' literary quality.
  Texts are often only judged according to this witnessing aspect." (p. 126, folio not stated)
- **Marutīrtha Hiṃlāj as a textbook case.** "The narrative implies a link between the author and the
  narrator, even though the 'I' in the text does not explicitly relate to the name on the book's cover.
  In the preface to the Hindi translation, however, the translator, Nihararanjan, refers to a personal
  meeting with Abadhut, which provides additional support for the implicit link between author and
  narrator. Without this supplement given in the translation, Marutīrtha Hiṃlāj presents a typical
  example of an autobiographical pact between the reader and the narrator (Lejeune 1989)." (p. 132,
  folio not stated)
- **The novel's three motifs, as Schaflechner numbers them.** "(a) renouncement and austerity are
  portrayed as an important part of the ritual journey (Abadhut 1964, 154); (b) the narrator is critical
  of the pilgrimage and its rigid rituals (Abadhut 1964, 49, 91); and (c) the ritual journey is
  described as a metaphor for life in which all humans are pilgrims on an unknown path (Abadhut 1964,
  132)." (p. 132, folio not stated) **The sentence that names the three motifs as a set is cut off at
  the chunk boundary.**
- **The Rāmāyaṇa's scale and date, attributed to Goldman and Sutherland, not to Schaflechner.** "an
  Indian epic consisting of about fifty-thousand lines that are divided into seven major chapters (Skt.
  khaṇḍa) (Goldman and Sutherland 1984, 4–5). The canonized text of the Rāmāyaṇa is said to have been
  composed between 750 and 500 BCE, thus predating the Mahābhārata (Goldman and Sutherland 1984, 23)."
  (p. 119, folio not stated)
