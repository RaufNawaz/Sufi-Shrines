## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Weekly `ḥāẓrī` (Urd. presence, appearance) sessions**, every Thursday, at which "the various
  goddesses and ancestors possess the `bhopā`—the community's spiritual leader and genealogical
  expert" and the two elders' names are invoked ("children of Bhao!" "family of Bhachu!") (pp. 306–307,
  folio not stated). "The `bhopā`'s wild dance to the sound of the `ḍam'rū` (Hin. a small drum with a
  playing surface on both sides) is called 'shaking' (Hin. `hāl mār'nā`), a state of trance during which
  he repeatedly addresses the community in short, excited sentences." (p. 309, folio not stated)
- **The occupations of the community**: "Most of the older members of the Devipujak community sell
  dried fruits by trade; quite a few sell their wares in Karachi's Empress Market or at the far end of
  the city, at the Sunday Bazaar in the posh Defence Housing Authority area, where they sell nuts and
  snacks. But some, especially those of the younger generation, today engage in other occupations and
  support the community with their salaries." (p. 307, folio not stated)
- **A migration claim with a stated basis and a stated limit**: "According to Vagri history, the
  community migrated back and forth between Sindh and Gujarat for many generations and, like other
  nomadic groups, were eventually forced to settle on one side of the border after the Partition. Since
  there is no written account of their movement, the clan's detailed history can only be based on
  speculation. The Ghorava family, however, estimates that their forefathers first arrived in Karachi
  about two hundred years ago." (pp. 307–308, folio not stated)
- **Caste rules the community states for itself**: "their elevated caste status forbids them to accept
  water or food from any other low-caste Hindu communities, like the Meghwar or the Hindu Kathiyavari"
  (p. 308, folio not stated). The author records that he "witnessed various examples where this
  discriminatory requirement was not strictly implemented" (p. 308, folio not stated), and gives a
  counter-instance at the Jagnath `akhārā` where the Devipujaks "were slightly annoyed with the
  uninvited guests but acted very hospitably, sharing food and water with them" (p. 309, folio not
  stated).
- **`Bali` as obligation rather than exchange**, the chunk's central institutional claim: "the ritual
  slaughter within the Vagri community is not simply a quid pro quo action used for the fulfillment of
  a wish, as seen among other Vagri communities (Mann 1980); the `bali` is in fact viewed as obligatory
  for the health and security of the whole community." (p. 310, folio not stated) The offering is "male
  goats and roosters" (p. 310, folio not stated).
- **Money at the festival, from the endnotes.** Note 15: "One particularly interesting incident
  occurred in 2010 when the organization claimed to have sold forty thousand tickets. These sales would
  have generated revenues totaling 10 million rupees, about 85,000 Euros (2012). Many visitors wondered
  where this money had gone and argued that petrol and water alone could never have cost such a huge
  sum of money." (p. 317, folio not stated, note 15) Note 14, in the author's own voice: "I personally
  would add that a certain amount of money has to be distributed among local agents and the police to
  ensure a smooth course of events during the `melā`. I obviously have no direct statement about this

  which bribes had to be paid to the local police." (p. 317, folio not stated, note 14) Note 17 records
  that the HSM "once turned down the request of a local scholar who had approached the organization for
  financial support to publish his work about the Goddess" (p. 317, folio not stated, note 17).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The origin of blood sacrifice, attributed to the community and glossed by the author against a
  text.** "For the community in Karachi, this tradition dates back to mythological times when the Devi
  was forced to consume the blood of demons during a battle in order to counteract their continuous
  reproduction. In my conversations with them, the Devipujak never directly referred to the
  `Devī Māhātmaya`, but their version of the episode closely parallels the story of the Goddess's
  struggle with the demon Raktabija (Devadatta 2003, 130–135). According to Lali Bhua, it was because
  of this fight that the Goddess developed a taste for blood as sacrifice from her worshippers (Hin.
  `xūn caṛhānā`)." (p. 310, folio not stated) — attributed to Lali Bhua and to the community, not
  asserted by the author.
- **The sanction for failing to sacrifice.** "If the community ceases to do so, I was frequently told,
  the Goddess would herself take the life of a member of the community. If it came to this, they
  explained, the Goddess would cause accidents, or bring disease upon the community." (p. 310, folio
  not stated) Note 29 gives the community's own words: "`Ham agar na deṃ, to hamāre meṃ se koi ek
  jāegā. mātā bali xud leṃgī!`" (p. 317, folio not stated, note 29). The author's inference is his own:
  "it seems it is not simply the consumption of blood the Vagri Goddess is interested in, but the
  general life energy of her subjects" (p. 310, folio not stated).
- **Kali as gatekeeper, in a 2010 testimony.** Note 30: "In 2010, one member of the community told me:
  'Before our forefathers came here on camels. At that time Kali was really here [`svayaṃ thī`]. People
  needed to give the sacrifice here; it was necessary . . . . If you went to Hinglaj without giving a
  sacrifice to Kali she came after you and slapped you.'" (p. 317, folio not stated, note 30)
- **Trance utterances recorded at the Thursday sessions**: "I will kill those who cast a spell on you!"
  and "Ramesh, you only called on me one time, but I will answer you twenty-one times!" (p. 310, folio
  not stated). "These Thursday meetings also commemorate particular incidents in the community's past,
  as when the Goddess saved the Ghorava's ancestors from misfortune, or how she once abandoned one of
  the family's `bhopā`s." (p. 310, folio not stated) **Neither incident is narrated or dated**:
  **(not stated)**.
- **The threat attributed to the Mother if sacrifice ceases**: "She will even destroy the whole
  universe [`sarva naṣṭ kar degī pure brahmāṃḍ ko`]." (p. 312, folio not stated, with note 32 "From an
  interview in 2012.", p. 318)
- **Accidents read as the Goddess's anger**, in a 2012 Devipujak's words: "Since the Hinglaj Sheva
  Mandali has prohibited animal sacrifice, every year there are some accidents... In one way or
  another, the Mother takes her gift [`bheṃṭ`]." (p. 312, folio not stated) — attributed to a community
  member, not to the author.
- **Survivors of the old journey received as the Goddess's gift.** "those returning alive from the
  dangerous pilgrimage were often celebrated as gifts from the Goddess herself, who protected them on
  their way" (p. 321, folio not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The author's core claim about how the ban became a marker of Hindu identity**: "A 'we' becomes
  constructed as a community binds together through its common denial of one particular practice as
  foreign to an actual tradition (cf. how Khatri [1973, 10] called the practice a foreign element)."
  (p. 306, folio not stated) He immediately limits it: "it is not possible to assume only one single
  nodal point within one particular discourse (Laclau and Mouffe 2001, 139). There is a multitude of
  nodal points and truth-claims that make up Hinglaj." (p. 306, folio not stated)
- **The role of institutions in making an exclusion stick**: "To be effective, any such exclusion
  requires a network of tools and institutional means endowing it with the power to successfully repeat
  the rejected practice. At this point, the agency of authors, reform movements, and temple committees
  becomes important. In the case of Hinglaj, the HSM possesses the primary ability to create an
  atmosphere of acceptance or denial of certain practices." (p. 306, folio not stated)
- **The author's charge of historical amnesia among visitors**: "Many visitors at Hinglaj portrayed
  animal sacrifice as an Islamic practice and thus in contradiction to their own Hindu identity,
  oblivious to the long tradition of `bali` among many Hindu communities in the area, particularly at
  the Kali shrine in the same valley." (p. 306, folio not stated)
- **Plurality without democratisation** — the thesis of section 6.13: "The increasingly plural religious
  landscape at Hinglaj—seen in the vast crowds it attracts from a wide range of castes, languages,
  ethnicities, and nationalities—has not led to the democratization of authority over the interpretation
  of the shrine's traditions." (p. 313, folio not stated)
- **Retroactive meaning**: "The case study of the Hinglaj shrine shows how meaning is retroactively
  assigned to contingent circumstances, which then transform into significant truth-claims inside the
  valley." (p. 314, folio not stated) And: "while the prohibition may have begun as a result of
  unintentional influences, the HSM utilizes these incidents and solidifies them (through annual
  repetition) into the 'true' Hindu practice at the shrine." (p. 314, folio not stated)
- **The discourse is explicitly declared not closed**: "the banning of `bali` does not represent a
  stable marker for 'Hinduness' as some Pakistani Hindus, and the HSM, would wish it to be. In other
  words, the sliding of meaning has not been suspended—the discourse is not closed, and the tradition
  at the shrine not (yet) 'sedimented' (Butler 2011; Laclau 1990)." (p. 315, folio not stated)
- **The shrine's popularity explained partly by insecurity, not only by antiquity**: "The shrine's
  growing popularity, therefore, stems not only from its ancient history and references in some Sanskrit
  texts but also from the state of insecurity for Hindus as citizens of the Pakistani nation state."
  (p. 319, folio not stated) "Hinglaj's success as a site secluded from urban disturbances also
  indicates to some degree the current precarious circumstances of Hindus living in the Islamic
  Republic." (p. 319, folio not stated)
- **The two decisive events, named as such**: "two elements stand out as particularly important: the
  institutionalization of the pilgrimage by the Hinglaj Sheva Mandali (HSM) in 1986 and the completion
  of the Makran Coastal Highway (MCH) at the beginning of the new millennium." (p. 319, folio not
  stated)
- **An explicit limit on what can be known about the shrine's past**: "details of this old journey, let
  alone one continuous way of engaging with the Goddess through the centuries, cannot decisively be
  established. The events of the past thirty years, however, have shown how actors at the shrine aim to
  establish precisely such a historic continuity by rallying behind certain practices." (p. 321, folio
  not stated)
- **Truth-claims as performative**: "These truth-claims, however, are constative statements that not
  only hold true or not but also reveal their performative character. Truth-claims make things happen
  and thus bear an influence on people and their practices." (p. 323, folio not stated)
- **A caveat the author enters about his own textual evidence**: "It is difficult to measure the
  influence of such texts and ritual manuals on the shrine and its practices... while I doubt that many
  visitors at the shrine of Hinglaj read these texts and manuals themselves, religious authorities often
  seem to gain their respectability through the study of such texts." (p. 315, folio not stated, note 1)
- **A comparative frame, cited not asserted**: "Fierce debates about the relative positions of Dalit
  sub-castes is a widespread phenomenon, as shown by Moffatt (1979) in his study of several such
  communities in Tamil Nadu." (p. 309, folio not stated) The author then differentiates the Karachi
  case: "such differentiation seemed more important on an imaginary or ideological level... In their
  daily contact with other Hindu communities, such segregation was not very obvious." (p. 309, folio
  not stated)
