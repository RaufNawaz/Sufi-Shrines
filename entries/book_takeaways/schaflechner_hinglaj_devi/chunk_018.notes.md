## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The book's central term, defined here.** "what I have witnessed during my research and what is
  still obvious today is the tradition's dynamics of becoming solid" (p. 324, folio not stated); "The
  HSM's and other individual actors' efforts to present certain practices and narrative elements as
  intrinsic, solid, or natural parts of the Goddess's tradition represent tangible forms of what is
  here termed solidification." (p. 324, folio not stated)
- **Solidification distinguished from sedimentation.** "Important at this juncture is that, unlike an
  already sedimented tradition, solidification describes a phenomenon that has not yet had the time to
  coagulate into a (fairly) uncontested terrain. To succeed, such processes require time and
  facilities for repeated propagation." (p. 324, folio not stated)
- **The mechanism the author names for it**: "a continuous interpellation of certain characteristics
  as the tradition's natural elements" (p. 324, folio not stated); a rhetoric that "follows a certain
  regulative norm that refrains from crudely direct inclusion or exclusion of certain elements" yet
  "manages… to establish a certain normative ideal of the Hinglaj tradition by linking itself to the
  notions of enlightenment and progress" (p. 324, folio not stated).
- **The tiling case, stated as the author's paradigm.** "This is a paradigmatic example of attempts
  that aim to solidify a certain truth-claim as universal through the systematic exclusion of its
  contesting elements." (p. 325, folio not stated)
- **The bali case, argued explicitly against a top-down reading.** "The case study shows how the
  solidification of tradition is not a top-to-bottom endeavor but rather the outcome of a negotiation
  process between crowd, landscape, and institution." (p. 325, folio not stated) "This example shows
  that the solidification of the Hinglaj tradition is not (only) the work of omnipotent actors and
  institutions but rather the outcome of a set of regulating norms, which are products of, but also
  producing, the engagement of various communities interacting with each other at the shrine."
  (p. 325, folio not stated) The author concedes the long-running character of the wider argument:
  "Undoubtedly, a long controversy exists regarding the affirmation or rejection of blood rituals."
  (p. 325, folio not stated)
- **The book's stated scope, and what it declines to do.** "This work portrays Hindu thought and
  practice in Pakistan through the lens of one particular religious site. It offers just a brief
  glimpse into the Pakistani-Hindu community's current situation and therefore, needs to leave broader
  questions unanswered." (p. 325, folio not stated)
- **The author's research agenda for others**, set out in four moves: "Future research needs to build
  upon these foundations by investigating the impact of recent political changes for Hindus in the
  Islamic Republic." (p. 325, folio not stated); "I hope that this work triggers additional research
  that addresses the widely unstudied field of Hindu rituals and religious imaginaries in Pakistan."
  (pp. 325–326, folio not stated); "Finally, future scholarship should examine the topic of Hindu
  political and economic engagement, particularly in the Sindh province." (p. 326, folio not stated);
  and "I, furthermore, wish that other such studies into the rich cultural landscape of Pakistan will
  give the Islamic Republic a place within South Asian studies beyond the dominant narratives of a
  failed state, ethnic violence, and terror." (p. 326, folio not stated) He also reports "The surge of
  interest that I have already encountered during my research at Hinglaj" (p. 326, folio not stated).
- **What the Works Cited shows about the book's evidence base** — a structural observation, not a
  claim about any shrine. The list combines (i) European critical theory in quantity — Althusser,
