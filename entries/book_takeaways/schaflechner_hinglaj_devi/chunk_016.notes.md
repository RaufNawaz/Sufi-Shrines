## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Ticketing, and its abolition.** "As long as the HSM was organizing the transportation, the
  necessity of the fee was more explicable to pilgrims." (p. 290, folio not stated) After 2001
  self-organised travel widened "the communication gap between the HSM and the melā attendees, many
  of whom would encounter members of the Mandali only as they checked for tickets, gave orders, or
  organized the crowds" (p. 290, folio not stated). "With the imposition of the fee, the utilization
  of this money became less transparent, and many yātrīs became increasingly irritated by the
  expense." (p. 290, folio not stated)
- **Who could not pay.** "many visitors attending the festival are peasants from Sindh who come along
  with their entire family, usually consisting of eight to ten people. For them, it was impossible to
  afford the entrance costs for all of their family members, so many tried to gain access without
  paying the fee." (p. 290, folio not stated)
- **Enforcement and friction.** "Frequently, it came to little skirmishes between the visitors and
  these volunteers, some of whom ... took their job a bit too seriously." (p. 291, folio not stated)
  "these unpleasant micro contacts were often the only direct interaction that visitors had with the
  HSM" (p. 291, folio not stated).
- **Accounting and its opacity, after abolition.** "Even its decision to put an end to the ticketing
  system could not stop the rumors about the proceeds the HSM had received before 2013; to this day,
  people still wonder aloud how the organization is suddenly able to manage the supposedly vast
  expenses." (p. 292, folio not stated) "When I inquired about how the HSM was able to deal with the
  deficit following the abolition of the ticket system, I received the answer that they now have
  donors supporting them. Nobody was willing to tell me who these donors were and how they are able to
  organize the necessary amount of money." (p. 292, folio not stated) **The donors are
  **(not stated)**.**
- **Caste framing of the money dispute.** "many Hindus in Pakistan (especially from the scheduled
  castes) saw the imposition of an entry fee at any time of year as typical profit-orientated behavior
  by the HSM, which consists mainly of members of the Lohana caste" (p. 292, folio not stated); "such
  accusations were usually baldly linked to stereotypes of caste identity and behavior" (p. 292,
  folio not stated).
- **A challenge to the custodian's religious expertise.** The HSM is accused of "allegedly shallow
  knowledge of the history and the rituals at Hinglaj and its surroundings, a direct challenge to the
  organization's hegemonic position and religious expertise at the shrine" (p. 292, folio not stated);
  "others are skeptical about this Lohana version of history at the shrine" (p. 292, folio not
  stated).
- **The sacrificial economy before the ban, in the custodian's own account.** "In the initial days of
  the established annual melā in the mid-1980s and early 1990s, many visitors bought goats from the
  local Muslims living in tiny villages around the valley (Dodiya 1989, 52). This led to variability
  in price and quality of the sacrificial animals." (p. 295, folio not stated) "Later, particularly in
  the mid-1990s, when the number of visitors was steadily increasing, the demand for goats expanded
  while the supply stayed low." (p. 295, folio not stated) Goats were then "sold for high prices
  but—according to the HSM—were actually not suitable for the sacrifice", i.e. "either female, too
  young, or not healthy enough" (p. 295, folio not stated).
- **The custodian takes over the supply, then abandons it.** "the Mandali began taking the supply of
  sacrificial goats into its own hands, transporting suitable animals on trucks from the nearby city
  of Las Bela. Dewani explained that the goats were sold at one rate and on one day only so that the
  visitors had an incentive to carry out the bali on one particular day—the day designated by the HSM
  for the worship of the Goddess Kali." (p. 295, folio not stated) "the Mandali was no longer able to
  keep up the supply of goats and eventually also had to stop the transport from Bela." (p. 295, folio
  not stated) A throughput ceiling is given: "it was simply not possible to sacrifice more than forty
  goats a day in accordance with the traditions at the shrine." (p. 296, folio not stated)
- **The traditional sacrifice day.** Visitors "began slaughtering the goats according to their
  availability rather than according to the traditional days and times propagated by the HSM (usually
  the eighth day of the pilgrimage)" (p. 295, folio not stated).
- **Petitions that produced the ban.** "in those years, members of the Vishnu paṃth—in this case
  mostly the Maheshvari community and followers of the ISKCON—had repeatedly requested that the
  organization stop the slaughter of the goats." (p. 296, folio not stated) Because the easier path
  ran past the Kali Mata temple, "many pilgrims had to pass by the bloody rituals to reach Hinglaj's
  sanctum sanctorum. Disgusted by the act, some appealed to the HSM to stop the activity." (p. 296,
  folio not stated)
- **Enforcement of the ban.** "the HSM did not react only superficially to the demands of certain
  visitors but actually became the most active agency in the ban's enforcement through the
  establishment of a network of volunteers and ritual experts who help the Mandali to implement it."
  (p. 296, folio not stated)
- **A substitute rite adopted by the custodian's own family.** "While the Dewanis previously gave the
  blood of a goat to the Goddess Kali during the young men's upanayana saṃskāra (Skt.) ceremony—an
  initiation ritual for the community's male members in which a thread is given to symbolize
  maturity—now on such occasions, the family simply cuts the animal's ear and sets it free in front of
  Kali's temple." (p. 296, folio not stated)
- **Sacrifice continuing after the ban.** "for some time after the ban was enacted, members of the HSM
  secretly continued sacrificing goats in the mountains for the Goddess Kali. This was done in the
  name of all visitors to prevent misfortune, but eventually this slaughter was also brought to an
  end, I was told." (p. 296, folio not stated) **The book gives no date for that ending and attributes
  it only to hearsay ("I was told")**: **(not stated)**.
- **`baqra cīd`[text-layer?] [baqra ʿīd] as a reason to travel to the shrine.** "in response to the
  question of why so many people had gathered in Hinglaj/Nani during `baqra cīd`[text-layer?] [baqra
  ʿīd], a frequent answer was that people wanted to escape the open slaughter of animals in their
  towns, a custom with which Muslims celebrate Abraham's willingness to sacrifice his first-born son."
  (p. 294, folio not stated) Most visitors then "had come from urban areas like Karachi or Hyderabad"
  (p. 294, folio not stated).
- **Pyālā denā, a healer's rite, defined by the author.** "'Pyālā denā' literally means 'to give a
  cup,' a practice carried out on particular occasions such as navarātri, when the bhopā embodies the
  Goddess and consumes a cup (Hin. pyālā) of fresh blood from a rooster or a male goat." (p. 304,
  folio not stated) The pujārī's own account: "Every kālī caudas I had to go to the temple and there I
  had to give the pyālā." (p. 304, folio not stated), later replaced with "I started to offer coconuts
  to Kali Mata" (p. 305, folio not stated).
- **Distribution of religious literature as an institution.** ISKCON "publications are
  disproportionately present at festivals and in temples where religious literature is sold."
  (p. 305, folio not stated)
- **Nineteenth-century merchant patronage, as economic history.** "wealthy merchants took on the
  patronage of temples—mainly in the Vaishnava tradition—or cow shelters (Hin. gauśālā)" (p. 301,
  folio not stated); "a certain moral code of dharma had at that time become increasingly important
  among traders for the sake of their reputation and 'credit-worthiness' (Bayly 1999, 217)" (p. 301,
  folio not stated).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The Goddess takes her own sacrifice if denied one** — attributed to the Devipujak-Vagris, not
  asserted by the author. "every time a festival is organized at Hinglaj and the Mother is deterred
  from her sacrifice, she causes accidents and disease among the pilgrims, thereby taking her own
  sacrifice ('mātā xud bali letī')" (p. 294, folio not stated). "To support their point, the Vagris
  described many incidents in which people had horrible accidents or fell sick during the melā in
  Hinglaj." (p. 294, folio not stated) **No incident is dated or named**: **(not stated)**.
- **Secret sacrifice as prophylaxis** — the HSM's post-ban goat sacrifices in the mountains were "done
  in the name of all visitors to prevent misfortune" (p. 296, folio not stated).
- **Spirit-keeping by a Kali pujārī** — reported in dialogue by Sanjesh, in the pujārī's presence:
  "He kept spirits [bhūt pā'lnā] for a long time. If he let one loose, then it might also catch you.
  He captured many." (p. 304, folio not stated) The pujārī's reply: "No, no, they won't catch you!
  People must be very frightened." (p. 304, folio not stated)
- **Protection by scriptural study** — "you have your energy, and if you study the śāstras [Skt.
  religious texts], then the spirits won't catch you" (p. 304, folio not stated).
- **A substitution accepted by the Goddess** — "I would take a coconut with me. So, I started to offer
  coconuts to Kali Mata and I said: 'Mother, accept this now instead.'" (p. 305, folio not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Hegemony as the frame for custodianship.** Following Simon, hegemony is "a relation, not of
  domination by means of force, but of consent by means of political and ideological leadership"
  (Simon 1991, 24) (p. 289, folio not stated); with Griggs and Howarth, it is "the production of
  'common sense' in a particular site or sphere of the social" and "the winning of 'intellectual and
  moral leadership' in society, and not just the achievement of political power" (Griggs and Howarth,
  2008, 130) (p. 289, folio not stated). The author applies this directly: "It is precisely this
  production of 'common sense' through 'moral leadership' and a combination of coercion and persuasion
  that characterizes the HSM's modus operandi at the shrine of Hinglaj." (p. 289, folio not stated)
- **The HSM universalises a caste tradition.** "The tradition of the HSM—that is, of the Lasi
  Lohana—seeks to elevate itself to the level of the universal tradition rather than simply being one
  of many. It eliminates other claims to truth by labeling them 'less useful,' 'fake,' or 'outdated.'"
  (p. 289, folio not stated)
- **"Hindu unity" named as the site's central myth.** "'Hindu unity'—a concept implying the idea that
  there exists an 'actual,' 'eternal,' or 'real' way of practicing at the shrine—is the central myth
  at Hinglaj." (p. 289, folio not stated) Consensus there is "a 'temporary result of a provisional
  hegemony' (Mouffe 2000, 104) brought about by the HSM" (p. 289, folio not stated), and that power
  "is not a widely sedimented autonomous agency but rather in constant dialogue with the landscape and
  the crowds at the shrine" (p. 289, folio not stated).
- **The ban is contingent, then retroactively naturalised** — the chunk's central historical claim.
  "An examination of the history of the ban will show how contingent events were retroactively
  assigned with meaning, which ultimately led to the representation of animal sacrifice as 'improper'
  Hindu behavior." (p. 293, folio not stated) "contingent—possible but not necessary—events led to the
  formation of a prohibition which then was retroactively interpreted by visitors and the organization
  as an 'actual' element of the tradition." (p. 296, folio not stated)
- **Solidification is struggle, not a top-down process.** "the solidification of the Hinglaj tradition
  is not a top-down process but a form of struggle emerging from the clashes between different
  truth-claims." (p. 293, folio not stated)
- **Two ideological positions built on the same symbol.** "In their affirmation or rejection, both
  parties introduce the same element, bali, as a primary symbol of what it means to be a 'true'
  Hindu." (p. 293, folio not stated) "Animal sacrifice, thus, is a clear case in point for how alleged
  natural practices at the shrine reveal dynamics of exclusion and contingent foundations at deeper
  scrutiny." (p. 293, folio not stated)
- **Unity produced by exclusion.** "the rejection of bloody sacrifices conjures up a sense of
  'Hindu-unity' built on a perceived opposition to the Muslim sacrificial practice" (p. 297, folio not
  stated); "This effort to produce unity through not only the establishment but also the exclusion of
  certain truth-claims is a clear case in point for what I term a solidification of the Hinglaj
  tradition." (p. 297, folio not stated)
- **An observed counter-example to the identity marker.** Having heard that Hindus came to escape
  slaughter, the author reports witnessing a rooster sacrifice at the Kali Mata temple and a goat
  offered the night before, concluding: "the non-sacrifice of animals was apparently not such a stable
  marker for Hindu identity as many visitors I spoke to had initially presented it." (p. 294, folio
  not stated)
- **The Indological debate, summarised by the author.** Alsdorf (2010), whose "seminal piece on the
  practice of non-violence and vegetarianism in 1962" (p. 298, folio not stated) is cited, "assumes
  that Vedic traditions did not know the concept of ahiṃsā and that the link between adhering to a
  certain diet and salvation was established only later in the Manusmr̥ti (200 BCE–200 CE)" (p. 298,
  folio not stated), and reads Manusmr̥ti chapter 5 (at 5, 48–49 against 5, 56) as uniting "three
  different positions ... an initial stage where the eating of animals is allowed, a second stage
  where it is forbidden outside a ritual framework, and a third stage, where vegetarianism is
  propagated and animal sacrifice is completely outlawed (Alsdorf 2010)" (p. 299, folio not stated).
  The author adds the caution: "Whether these stages appeared in subsequent historical order and
  eventually evolved into ahiṃsā cannot, however, be definitively established." (p. 299, folio not
  stated)
- **Schmidt against Alsdorf.** Schmidt "disagrees with Alsdorf's thesis that there was no concept of
  ahiṃsā in the Vedic times", understands ahiṃsā as an oath "not expected of the common man" (Schmidt
  2010, 103) binding only on "the Vedic brahmacārin", and argues "that Vedic sacrifice developed into
  symbolic, or interiorized, sacrifice and eventually to renunciation (Schmidt 2010, 124)" (p. 299,
  folio not stated).
- **Heestermann.** He locates the roots of non-violence in ritual regulation: violence "became
  replaced by some 'system of ritualism that minimized' brute force (Heestermann 1984, 122)", visible
  in the śrauta rituals where "the (animal) victim was no longer beheaded but instead was led away
  from the sacrificial stake to a different location where it was then suffocated" (p. 299, folio not
  stated); and he claims "the connection between `ahṃisā`[text-layer?] [ahiṃsā] and vegetarianism was
  not at all natural in the beginning and emerged only out of a Brahmanical ritual", via the dīkṣita's
  meat abstention (pp. 299–300, folio not stated). "Buddhists and Jains, both strong advocates of
  non-violence, had—according to Heestermann—no particular connection to vegetarian philosophy
  (Heestermann 1984, 126)." (p. 300, folio not stated)
- **Doniger.** She "posits that a vigorous debate on the morality of animal slaughter was already
  present at the time of the Brāhmaṇas (800–600 BCE)" and that criticism became open "in later works
  such as the Mahābhārata (Doniger 2011, 150–151)" (p. 300, folio not stated).
- **The author's own dissent from Urban.** Urban holds that bali survived only through "the existence
  of śākta and tantric traditions (Urban 2011, 52)"; the author replies: "I believe this position is
  too general since not all traditions in which blood sacrifice plays a role can be subsumed under the
  concept of tantra. The case study of the Devipujak-Vagris—introduced below—is just one such
  exception." (p. 300, folio not stated)
- **The author's critique of an Indological generalisation.** Of Lodrick's sentence (Lodrick 1981, 55)
  he writes that it and similar statements "exclude many Indian communities who practice bali or at
  least do not adhere to vegetarianism" and imply "that Islam is excluded from the possibility of
  'expressing Indian conceptions'"; "the virtues defined here can be seen as the views of a particular
  elite" (pp. 297–298, folio not stated).
- **Bayly's economic explanation, endorsed.** "a history of non-violence or vegetarian politics must
  take into account economic developments of the nineteenth century" (p. 301, folio not stated); "The
  rejection of meat and alcohol was already a trend among commercial communities starting from the
  late eighteenth century (Bayly 1999, 217)"; "By the early twentieth century, different clusters of
  such trading communities had formed a distinct 'bania-merchant culture' (Bayly 1999, 217)" (p. 301,
  folio not stated). The author's summation: "it was as a result of these economic and social
  advantages that ahiṃsā as a way of life began to flourish among the trading middle class." (p. 301,
  folio not stated)
- **Reform movements and colonial reason.** Brahmo Samaj and Arya Samaj "found common ground in their
  strong opposition to elements of 'folk religion' widespread within Hindu practice. Blood sacrifice
  was 'a clear case in point' (Fuller 1992, 101)—a conspicuous remnant of an alleged superstitious
  past that, in the reformers' view, must be overcome by reason." (p. 302, folio not stated)
- **Bali as a nodal point, the chapter's closing analytic move.** "bali had shifted from a mere moment
  within a set of various practices into a nodal point (or truth-claim), with the ability to separate
  'good' from 'bad' Hindus" (p. 305, folio not stated); "As a nodal point, animal sacrifice creates an
  equivalential chain of attributes, for example, uneducated, low caste, and even Muslim." (p. 305,
  folio not stated) **The sentence that follows is cut off by the chunk boundary.**
- **The author's reading of "awareness" and "education".** Of the Meghwar interviewee he writes: "I
  interpret this statement as suggesting that sacrificial rituals are a part of the community's
  superstitious past, which is to be overcome gradually through progress." (p. 303, folio not stated)
  Of the pujārī: "the denial of animal sacrifice is connected to the study of religious texts, in
  particular the Bhagavad Gītā" (p. 305, folio not stated), an interpretation that "may have its roots
  in the presence of religious texts sponsored and distributed in Pakistan by the ISKCON society"
  (p. 305, folio not stated).
