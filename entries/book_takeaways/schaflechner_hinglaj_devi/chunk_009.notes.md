## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The Makran Coastal Highway as the cause of everything in this chapter.** "The journey's
  kinesthetic experience, for example, has been significantly changed by the construction of the MCH,
  which shaped a new pilgrimage route and created new rituals and patterns of mobility. On the one
  hand, the practice of walking to the temple has notably diminished because of bus and tour
  operators." (p. 150, folio not stated) "Their path no longer cuts through the arid desert but simply
  leads along the highway almost directly up to the shrine. This route has proven advantageous as it
  eliminates two of the main problems that pilgrims once faced on the traditional route: the
  possibility of getting lost in the barren desert and the scarcity of water sources." (p. 150, folio
  not stated)
- **A roadside economy created by the traffic.** "The traffic on the much-traveled MCH triggered the
  opening of little stalls and petrol pumps that sell water and snacks on the side of the road. Not
  infrequently, cars also stop to offer the walkers drinking water." (p. 150, folio not stated)
- **The revival of walking, and who walks.** "The practice of walking to the shrine underwent a revival
  in the past decade as a result of the construction of the MCH." (p. 152, folio not stated) "It is
  evident, however, that most people do not follow old family traditions when walking to Hinglaj but
  instead have only recently decided to go on the tour." (p. 152, folio not stated) Motives "frequently
  involve the fulfillment of wishes, certain vows, or, simply, wanderlust" (p. 152, folio not stated).
- **The walking regime of the Kohli group**: "The group started their daily walk at around 2:00 a.m.
  and kept going until noon, when the heat became too difficult for them to bear. On the way, they
  stayed at temples or private houses and also in the courtyards of mosques where people often supplied
  them with food and water." (p. 154, folio not stated)
- **Caste and class at the festival, stated as structure.** "In Sindh, the Kohli community is mainly a
  disempowered scheduled caste whose members work as agricultural peasants on the fields owned by
  Sindh's feudal elite." (p. 154, folio not stated) "This class and caste division is also tangible in
  the organization of the festival, where a part of the upper-class Lohana community, embodied in the
  HSM, has taken on the responsibility to 'educate' the low caste visitors. Such measures, however,
  often lead to sneers and tension between the castes, who mutually deny each other's ability to know
  the 'real' tradition." (p. 154, folio not stated)
- **The ritual offices the walking groups do or do not keep.** The Kohli group "did not have an aguā or
  chaṛīdār, nor did they follow the practice of wearing the ochre-colored clothes that are donned by
  many at the Liari temple. There was no obvious ritual hierarchy… The group had no stern practice of
  penance, nor did they follow any particular codes of conduct. They also consumed meat, if they got
  the chance." (p. 156, folio not stated) "the men did not seem to be familiar with the concept of the
  chaṛīdār" (p. 154, folio not stated).
- **The chaṛī and the chaṛīdār, as the Narayanpur group describes them beforehand.** "the pilgrim group
  is guided by one or two chaṛīdārs who carry with them a chaṛī, a walking stick shaped like Shiva's
  trident, the triśūla." (p. 160, folio not stated) The chaṛīdār "must walk a few hundred meters ahead
  of the group and is responsible for the community's general well-being… Raj Kumar is the pilgrims'
  representative, accepting donations of food and water given to the group along the way." (p. 160,
  folio not stated) **The author records the rules and then records that they were not kept**: "Raj
  Kumar did not carry the chaṛī in his hand, nor was he walking in front of the others." (p. 160, folio
  not stated) "Raj Kumar also did not become the chaṛīdār of the group through some kind of ritual
  initiation, but, as he himself said, he became so simply because of the 'order of the Mother' (mātā
  kā hukm)." (p. 161, folio not stated)
- **Rules actually observed on the road**: "The only rule that all of the yātrīs seemed to follow was
  that no one consumed any meat or fish on the journey. However, many smoked a substantial amount of
  gāṁjā (Hin. marijuana), which they considered necessary to appease Shiva. The older men in the
  community also had some whiskey with them, which they drank as the Goddess' prasād (Hin. food
  offered to an idol)." (p. 160, folio not stated) Meat resumes only at the end: "After cooling down
  the chaṛī in Thatta, the pilgrimage ends, and the pilgrims are allowed to eat meat again." (p. 165,
  folio not stated)
- **The rath (cart), its contents and its economy.** "The ornamented cart contains a 30-centimeter tall
  mūrti (Hin. idol, statue) of Sheravali Mata (literally 'the Mother with the lion') and a picture of
  the Goddess, the oldest the Narayanpur Gujarati community possesses. Before the framed print, there
  is a small glass cabinet containing a flame from the Santoshi Mata temple in Narayanpur… The rath is
  painted ochre and bears ochre-colored flags on each of its four corners. Ochre is traditionally the
  color that pilgrims wear for the journey and so resembles the practices described in older accounts
  (Khatri 1973, 19)." (p. 163, folio not stated) "Inside the cart, the yātrīs also store their food,
  which consists mostly of dāl, some vegetables, and rice. Many within the multireligious neighborhood
  of Narayanpur consider donating food for this journey to be spiritually beneficial and thus give
  provisions irrespective of their religious affiliation. The rath also contains a small generator that
  produces light for walking during the night and electricity for a small amplifier that plays
  devotional songs." (p. 164, folio not stated) "Two chaṛīs, instead of being carried by the leader as
  in the traditional accounts, are instead attached to the two front corners of the cart." (p. 164,
  folio not stated)
- **The jāg'raṇ and the departure rite.** "The night before the group leaves Karachi, the chaṛīdār Raj
  Kumar organizes a jāg'raṇ, a religious wake in the local Ram Pir temple… The departure of the cart is
  initiated with the cracking of a coconut and a small ceremony attended by many in the neighborhood.
  In 2012, the group intended to leave after the jāg'raṇ at 4:00 a.m., but because of the neighborhood
  gunfire and shooting mentioned above, the group was forced to postpone their departure. Usually,
  influential local people are present at this occasion to bid the group farewell." (p. 163, folio not
  stated)
- **The ticketing system and the resistance to it — the chunk's hardest institutional fact.** "In 2009,
  the HSM decided to introduce an elaborate ticketing system in place of the former, rather loose
  arrangement of years past. The new system forced visitors wanting to enter the main part of the
  valley, where the Kali and Hinglaj temple are located, to wear a plastic wristband called the bhag'vā
  paṭṭī (Hin. ochre ribbon). According to HSM officials, the main purpose of the nontransferable
  wristband was to prevent several pilgrims from sharing one ticket, a common practice in the past. The
  plastic wristband had to be purchased in the valley for 250 rupees. This caused outrage among many
  pilgrims who fiercely opposed this ticketing system up until 2013 when the HSM decided to put an end
  to the practice." (p. 165, folio not stated) The counter-practice: "One attempt to resist this
  ticketing system came from the Narayanpur yātrīs who positioned the rath on the way to the ticket
  checkpoint. They explained that by putting the cart there, those who were not able to pay to enter
  the inner part of the valley could still receive a genuine darśan from the cart's mūrti." (p. 165,
  folio not stated)
- **Medical provision at the festival, and an institution founded because of its absence.** A young
  pilgrim was "seriously injured when a motorbike ran into the pilgrim group on the road" in 2012; the
  Edhi foundation ambulance stationed in the valley was refused him by "the doctor on call, a member of
  the Lohana community… since the boy's condition was not critical" (p. 158, folio not stated). "A
  private car was found to carry the boy back to the city—but only after the intervention of an
  influential local member of the Hindu Seva Trust… this incident also demonstrated the significant
  lack of medical facilities at the festival and led, in the subsequent weeks, to the establishment of
  a Gujarati community-driven organization in Narayanpur, the Pakistan Hindu Seva Samaj, which aimed to
  improve medical services in the valley." (p. 159, folio not stated) By the autumn "some members of
  the Narayanpur pilgrim group proudly showed me their medical supplies and an emergency car they had
  organized for the transportation of patients (see Chapter 6)." (p. 159, folio not stated)
- **The modern route, stop by stop** (§4.3.1): Karachi (departure from the outskirts in 2012 because of
  a police operation) → "the village of Hab Chawki" → "Vindar, a small village on the way" → "the next
  paṛāv in Liari" → Chandrakup → "Kanda Devi stop" → "the Hingol River" → the valley (pp. 159–160,
  folios not stated). "Their pilgrimage route does not deviate from the current most popular stops,
  which arose along with the construction of the MCH and together represent the widely accepted modern
  pilgrimage route (see Fig. 4.2)." (p. 157, folio not stated)
- **The Narayanpur walk, sized and dated.** It "started among the Gujarati community of Narayanpur in
  2006 and has since become an annual feature taking place a few days before the April melā"; the 2012
  group was "about twenty-five male pilgrims… among them several youngsters between the ages of 12 and
  16 (Schaflechner 2012)" (p. 157, folio not stated). "The journey along the road is about 240
  kilometers long; the group originally intended to cover the entire distance in six days but
  ultimately ended up taking twice as long. Some pilgrims also stopped walking due to exhaustion and
  instead rode on one of the many buses carrying people to the Hinglaj Valley in the days leading up to
  the melā." (p. 158, folio not stated)
- **Narayanpur as a place**: "Narayanpur Colony is a multireligious neighborhood in the center of
  Karachi, north of Saddar town. It is one of the biggest Hindu colonies in the city, with a few Muslim
  and Christian families also residing there. The neighborhood is poor and notorious for crime and
  violence. Most Hindu families hail from Gujarati or Marwari communities, and many work as sweepers
  for the city of Karachi. Narayanpur is rich in history and houses a variety of temples, gurudwaras,
  and churches." (p. 157, folio not stated)
- **Border bureaucracy as the Indian pilgrim's institution.** "one of the biggest hurdles for Indians
  who want to do the yātrā today is getting a visa for Pakistan. Further, they must obtain an
  additional visa allowing travel to Balochistan, a province that has been in a state of a latent
  insurgency since the establishment of Pakistan (Axmann 2012). At the time of writing, to obtain such
  a visa—aside from the general formalities like a valid passport, visa fee, and so forth—an Indian
  applicant was also required to present evidence of a sponsor in Pakistan to be responsible for their
  safety. Further, at the time of writing, once in Pakistan, an Indian visa holder must register at a
  police station within twenty-four hours of arrival, after any change of place, and again twenty-four
  hours before their departure.5" (p. 169, folio not stated) "Access to Balochistan is further limited
  by its legal ambiguity since 'Non-Objection Certificates' (NOC) must be obtained from the government,
  but the legal status of an application for such a certificate is unclear." (p. 169, folio not stated)
- **The pilgrimage as a market for intermediaries.** "Since such orders are apparently not strictly
  enforced, the journey has no proper legal status and thus is likely to involve bribery and nepotism.
  Discussions began in 2007 regarding the inclusion of Hinglaj in a list of religious places that can
  be mutually visited by citizens of each country, but so far, those discussions have produced no
  successful outcome.6 At the time of writing, both parties were in the process of amending the visa
  formalities for group traveling, but relations between the two countries remain volatile. Such
  logistical challenges may be problematic for pilgrims to Hinglaj, but, on the other hand, they also
  present an opportunity for many travel agents with good connections to embassies and consulates to
  make some money from devotees who are keen on traveling to the shrine." (p. 169, folio not stated)

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Shah Abdul Latif's visit**, attributed to the Kohli pilgrim Putu, not to the author: "Long ago Shah
  Abdul Latif also came here with a group of Yogis. He came here to ask the Mother to help him."
  (pp. 154–155, folios not stated)
- **A wish granted for offspring**, in Giresh's words: "I came since I wanted my sister to have a
  child. I had already come twice, once on a bus and then on a motorbike, but my Mother told me that I
  must come on foot. Then [the Goddess] heard my wish and my sister had a child. This is why I am now
  walking to her shrine." (p. 153, folio not stated)
- **The Goddess guiding the group out of a gunfight**, attributed to the pilgrims: "some pilgrims
  recounted their dangerous way out of Karachi and proudly added how the power of the Goddess had
  guided their path through sensitive neighborhoods and how she protected the rather large group from
  being targeted." (p. 159, folio not stated)
- **Protection from wild animals by the triśūl, chaṛī and flame**, quoted from one pilgrim: "There are
  many wild animals on the way, but the Mother always helps, and never has anyone been hurt. Nothing
  has ever happened to us. This is because we have the power of the triśūl, the chaṛī, and the jhoṃk,
  the flame that burns all the time in the rath. This flame is like our friend; it is sent by the
  Mother." (p. 164, folio not stated)
- **The cart as a generator of strength**, attributed to the pilgrims: "They say that people who are
  tired from walking need only to hold on to the cart, and they immediately receive renewed strength
  for the journey." (p. 164, folio not stated) And the cart as "charged": "The rath becomes 'charged'
  (from a 2012 interview; original in English) with divine energy, they say, which, once it reaches the
  valley, can also be transferred to the bypassing people." (p. 165, folio not stated)
- **The cart as a miraculous substitute temple**, in a pilgrim's words, March 2012: "So many people
  think that this cart is miraculous and do their prayers there. They think it is their cart." (p. 166,
  folio not stated)
- **Two dream commands transferring Hinglaj's power to Indian sites.** Anoop Dan "claimed some decades
  ago that he had a dream in which the Goddess appeared to him saying that since her shrine in
  Balochistan was no longer reachable, the site of Temra Rai should be visited by Charans in its place."
  (p. 167, folio not stated) In Gujarat, a devotee "announced that the Goddess had appeared to him in a
  dream and advised him of the temple's remote desert location, after which he founded a temple
  committee to run it." (pp. 167–168, folios not stated)
- **The garbha's purifying power**, given as pilgrims' belief: "it was the power of the Mother's garbha
  (Hin. womb, lap)—a tunnel underneath the image of Hinglaj that is said to possess purifying
  qualities" (p. 157, folio not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The chapter's thesis**: "Building on my interaction with pilgrims in Pakistan and India, I will
  show how certain 'traditional' elements have been rearticulated to fit the novel circumstances."
  (p. 150, folio not stated) And for the Indian half: "I will show that the journey's salvific
  potential for Indian devotees is best understood from the perspectives of power, 'symbolic capital'
  (Bourdieu 1998), and restricted mobility." (p. 151, folio not stated)
- **The questions the chapter closes on**: "How do Hindus at the shrine cope with the gap between
  traditional representations of the pilgrimage and current practices at Hinglaj? What kinds of new
  narratives are created to explain the change in ways of getting there?" (p. 151, folio not stated)
- **Walking as caste self-assertion.** Putu on the Lohanas: "See, the Lohanas come in their cars because
  they are not strong—they are too fat for walking! We work in the fields all day so we are strong, we
  are able to go on this journey, but they are too weak!" (p. 156, folio not stated) The author's
  reading: "The walking signified a means by which the group defined itself and also undermined a more
  powerful community… Usually culturally and financially overshadowed by the dominant Lohana Hindu
  community, the Kohlis I spoke to drew a sense of power and self-confidence from the physical
  practice. From this perspective, the kinesthetic part of the journey represents strength and
  distinguishes the Kohlis from the physically weaker upper-caste Hindus." (p. 156, folio not stated)
- **The Hajj comparison is about redemption, not obligation.** "This comparison, however, does not seem
  to relate to the mandatory aspect of the Hajj for devout Muslims but rather to its quality of
  redemption and cleansing of sins." The author grounds its availability in the public sphere: "because
  the Hajj is one of the most prominent forms of pilgrimage in Pakistan's public sphere, often
  mentioned, for example, in schoolbooks and television programs, the frequent comparative references
  to it are not surprising." (p. 155, folio not stated)
- **Walking as satsaṃg and sociality, against the car.** "For Putu, the walking practice is like a
  satsaṃg (Hin. congregation to sing devotional songs; a gathering of those seeking the truth), lifting
  up the hearts of the pilgrims." (p. 155, folio not stated) In Putu's words: "The walking is also a
  place to meet [mi'lne kī jagah hai]. People get much more when they walk. This is our satsaṃg… Those
  who sit in their cars are actually trapped in them [gāṛī meṃ baṃd haiṃ]. We all help each other. We
  have the chance to serve each other [sevā kar'nā]." (pp. 155–156, folios not stated)
- **Where the salvific power sits for these pilgrims — not in the walking.** "For them, it was not
  necessarily the salvific powers of the walking that were able to rid the pilgrims of their sin.
  Rather it was the power of the Mother's garbha… For Putu, the experiences on the way and the
  wanderlust that drove them were at the root of their walking." (p. 157, folio not stated) Against
  that, for Giresh "the kinesthetic aspect of the yātrā was the carrier for the journey's salvific
  powers" (p. 153, folio not stated). **The two readings sit side by side in the same section.**
- **Ritual offices dissolving, stated as a finding.** "Thus, the role of the chaṛīdār has, for these
  pilgrims, lost some of its importance when compared to the representations of the traditional
  pilgrimage. The ritual expertise once embodied by the aguā is no longer the central characteristic of
  his role… Thus, among the Narayanpur pilgrims at least, the role of the aguā has effectively
  dissolved, shifting toward a wider distribution of power among the elders of the group." (p. 162,
  folio not stated)
- **Method note the author makes against his own informants.** "It was obvious that the pilgrims had
  given a very traditional description of their journey to me beforehand, which was, however, not
  sustained along the way." (p. 160, folio not stated) The walking practice is "loosely modeled on codes
  of conduct adhered to in previous journeys described by authors such as Shastri (1977) and Hart
  (1839)" (p. 160, folio not stated).
- **The cart substitutes for the stick and concentrates the walk's merit.** "some of the previously
  introduced elements of the traditional yātrā resurface in the design of this cart. The primary
  position of the chaṛī is substituted by the central position of the cart." (p. 164, folio not stated)
  "The Narayanpur pilgrims insist that the long and demanding journey fills the cart with the blessings
  of the Goddess." (p. 164, folio not stated) "This role as an alternative temple cannot be filled by
  just any kind of religious object; it requires use of an entity associated with an exceptional
  practice, like the pulling of the cart from Karachi." (p. 166, folio not stated)
- **The rath as a direct challenge to the temple organization.** "By offering the cart to those who were
  not able to afford tickets into the melā, the Narayanpur yātrīs thereby extended the blessings of the
  Goddess—gained from their own physical efforts—to other pilgrims whose plans to enter the valley had
  been thwarted by the ticketing system. In this way, the Narayanpur pilgrims directly challenged the
  monopoly of the HSM as the sole access point to the Goddess until the ticketing system was aborted in
  2013." (p. 166, folio not stated)
- **The border as a producer of symbolic capital — the chapter's theoretical core.** "borders may also
  play a role in strengthening the 'symbolic capital' of a particular place, that is, the value it
  bestows upon those who cross a border to reach it (Bourdieu 1998, 47–52)." (p. 166, folio not stated)
  "While traditionally it was the fierce journey through the desert that bestowed huge symbolic capital
  onto the pilgrims, today, this restriction of Hinglaj is rearticulated for Indian devotees through
  the fierce India-Pakistan border." (p. 166, folio not stated) "Narratives of longing and separation
  can further validate the spiritual merit of the journey due to the heightened emotional sacrifice
  connected to the task and hence elevate a place's symbolic capital." (p. 168, folio not stated)
- **Proxy shrines do not replace the original.** "Even though such 'proxy shrines' are widely distributed
  all over South Asia, they rarely live up to the fame of the sites they represent." (p. 168, folio not
  stated)
- **Border studies literature the author positions himself in**: "This analysis of geopolitical borders
  and their impact on particular communities at and in contact with them forms an independent field of
  study that aims to understand identity formation within modern nation states (cf. Hastings and Wilson
  1999; van Schendel 2005; Gupta and Ferguson 1992)." (p. 167, folio not stated) Also Althusser 1971 on
  "interpellation", Purewal 2003, 546, Flynn 1997, Hastings and Haller 2000, and "borders can also
  reshape the routes of ritual journeys and affect practices at pilgrimage sites (Harder 2013, 151)"
  (p. 167, folio not stated).
- **The Indian pilgrim's difficulty is displaced, not removed** — Depavat's argument, quoted at length:
  "The problems for which the pilgrimage to Hinglaj is considered difficult are no longer present today.
  But it is necessary to make it clear that to this day this pilgrimage is still difficult to
  accomplish. Earlier there were certain kinds of difficulties and now there are other kinds. Today
  there are the formalities of the passport and the visa, the problems of Islamic countries like
  Pakistan, there is some doubt in guaranteeing the security of the Indian travelers, and [also] due to
  local terror attacks the pilgrimage cannot be considered easy . . . . Without the help of the Shri
  Hinglaj Sheva Mandali this pilgrimage is impossible. (Depavat 2010, 153)" (pp. 168–169, folios not
  stated) The chapter title of the Hindi source is "'The Hinglaj Pilgrimage Is Not Easy' (Depavat 2010)
  is a chapter in the Hindi book Śrī Kar'ṇījī Durlabh Darśan" (p. 168, folio not stated).
- **An internal contradiction the author flags in his source.** Singh Ratnu "emphasizes in his travelogue
  how dangerous it is to travel to Balochistan with a visa that is valid only for Karachi. However, in
  the very next paragraph, he writes that the government of Pakistan actually does not issue visas for
  the journey to Balochistan and that in fact the High Commission has explicitly forbidden the handling
  of such applications (Ratnu 2007, 28)." (p. 169, folio not stated)
