- **chandragup-baba-chandragup** (the book's name for it: `Chandrakup`, "the mud volcano

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The pilgrimage tax and the only visitor number in the chunk** — the single most usable economic
  datum here: "A 1907 Baluchistan Gazetteer (ed. McConaghey) mentions that at a Karachi outpost
  (possibly Sonmiani), the pilgrims were required to pay a tax of between 1 and 5 rupees, depending
  on their income and ability. The state earned around 600 rupees annually from these taxes, which
  would imply a rough average of two hundred visitors to the shrine yearly via the Sonmiani route."
  (p. 147, folio not stated) **The inference "two hundred visitors" is the author's own, drawn from
  the revenue figure; the Gazetteer is not quoted as giving it.** In the body text the same
  institution appears as current practice in 1940: "the yātrīs register their names and pay the
  pilgrimage tax" at Sonmiani (p. 140, folio not stated).
- **Group formation and size** — pilgrims "wait for a group of twenty to twenty-five people to gather"
  at the Nagnath akhāṛā (p. 138, folio not stated); Shastri is told "he will have to wait for at
  least another month for enough people to gather and for the cameleers to arrive" (p. 138, folio not
  stated); in the event "twenty-seven pilgrims have gathered" (p. 138, folio not stated), among them
  one woman (p. 138, folio not stated).
- **The office of aguā / chaṛīdār, hereditary and rotating**: "The pilgrim group is then led by the
  aguā (Hin. leader, guide), or the chaṛīdār (Hin. stick bearer), the spiritual head of the journey,
  traditionally belonging to the Nagnath clan (Shastri 1977, 4). Every year, the responsibility for
  guiding the pilgrims through the desert and orchestrating the ritual performances is assumed by a
  different man from one of the Nagnath families (Shastri 1977, 4)." (p. 138, folio not stated)
- **The chaṛī, its manufacture and its rule of precedence**: "These aguās also carry the chaṛī, a bar
  in the shape of a triśūla (Skt. trident), taken from the Hinglaj Valley and wrapped in yellow,
  ochre, and red cloth (Shastri 1977, 5). This chaṛī is a symbol for the Goddess and plays a central
  role during the ritual journey.31 So, for example, it is not allowed to travel in front of the one
  who carries it (chaṛīdār). The same is true even during the stops along the way at each paṛāv (Hin.
  holding place, camping ground), where the chaṛīdār sticks it in the earth. This indicates the point
  beyond which no one is allowed to go in the direction of the pilgrimage route. In addition, the
  chaṛī must be worshipped and honored by all the pilgrims." (p. 138, folio not stated) Note 31
  quotes "Chaṛī kā bhī viśeṣ mahattva aur svarūp, hotā hai" (Shastri 1977, 5) (p. 148, folio not
  stated).
- **The oath sworn at the River Hab, in four clauses**: "the ritual journey begins with an oath by each
  of the pilgrims: Until they return once more to this river following the pilgrimage, they will not
  engage in any sexual activities; they will help each other; they will avoid thoughts of envy, hate,
  or blame; and under no circumstances will they let anyone drink from their own water jar (Hin.
  surāhī). The oath is accompanied by the tying of an ochre-colored piece of cloth around the head of
  each pilgrim (Shastri 1977, 11)." (p. 139, folio not stated)
- **Payment in kind to cameleers and water guards** — a wage structure stated twice: "For this service
  every pilgrim is bound to give one roṭī (Hin. bread) a day to the camel drivers. Pilgrims are also
  obliged to give one roṭī to those people who safeguard the drinking water in this barren
  environment (Shastri 1977, 14).35" (p. 139, folio not stated) The norm is tested when "the starving
  guard demands not just one roṭī from the pilgrims, according to the tradition, but insists on having
  two from each of them (Shastri 1977, 26). The pilgrims refuse and leave the hungry man behind."
  (p. 140, folio not stated) One such guard is later found dead of starvation at another well, and
  "according to tradition, they put some flour on the watchman's dead body and leave" (p. 141, folio
  not stated).
- **Camels as navigational infrastructure**: "To find the proper route, the pilgrims must depend on the
  intuition of the camels, who play an important role as navigators.34 The camel guiders are able to
  find the way at night according to the position of the stars, but during the day the animals' sense
  of orientation is vital for finding the way and locating sources of necessary drinking water
  (Shastri 1977, 12)." (p. 139, folio not stated) The route itself is dictated by water: "the
  westbound path toward Chandrakup proceeds mostly according to the location of wells in the area"
  (p. 140, folio not stated), with note 41 quoting "Gantavya vahī hai, jahāṁ kuāṁ mil jāye" (Shastri
  1977, 25) and adding "Hart also mentions that the route followed the location of the wells (Hart
  1839, 81)" (p. 148, folio not stated).
- **Offerings and their materials**: the roṭ cake prepared overnight under a fast (p. 141, folio not
  stated); "gāṁjā (Hin. marijuana), coconuts, and cilams" thrown into Chandrakup (p. 141, folio not
  stated); "a cilam (Hin. clay pipe)" smoked at the River Hab "to appease the God Shiva (Shastri
  1977, 11)" (p. 139, folio not stated); "dān-dakṣiṇā (Hin. gifted food)" given to Aghori Baba
  (p. 142, folio not stated); two nīm (Lat. Azadirachta indica) twigs broken at the Hingol, "One they
  use to clean their teeth before crossing the river, and the other must be planted there at the
  shore" (p. 142, folio not stated); a mālā taken from the vedī (p. 142, folio not stated).
- **Purity and caste on the route, twice tested**: Jivraj "hits her and yells that she must not spoil
  her dharma (Shastri 1977, 17). Jivraj explains that they are from the Pushtimarg Vaishnava sect,
  which does not allow the consumption of onions." (p. 139, folio not stated) And at the well: "The
  pilgrims are afraid of becoming impure by touching the body of a dead Muslim. In the end, only

  (p. 141, folio not stated) By the next day, "all of the Gujarati Vaishnavas start to leave their
  sectarian constraints aside and ask Shastri for onions, too" (p. 139, folio not stated).
- **Burial customs as a research resource**: "in Gujarat … among certain communities, it is a custom to

- **The Nath–Goddess link asserted**: "Also 'Gopichand Raja' is a figure associated with the Nath

  Devadatta Shastri's book, and he made me promise to give him the book as soon as possible"; and

- **Film and translation as distribution channels**: the 1959 film adaptation of `Marutīrtha Hiṃlāj`

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The two worshipped stones of the guru and the student**, told by the aguā on the mountain before
  Chandrakup: a guru asked his student for water; "Because the student was a guru-bhakta, a devout
  follower of the master, he gave all of his drinking water away until there was none left for
  himself"; the guru refused him water in turn, the student died, the guru's bottle "shattered into
  pieces on the ground, spilling all the water into the sand", and the guru died beside him. "Since
  that time, these two stones have been worshipped in memory of this incident." (p. 140, folio not
  stated) Attributed to the aguā within Shastri's narrative; note 40 adds "A similar story is also
  found in Abadhut (Abadhut 1964, 56)" (p. 148, folio not stated).
- **Chandrakup withholds its bubbles from an unconfessed sin** — reported in both books. In Abadhut:
  "one of the pilgrims fails to fully confess his sins, and the bubbles of the volcano immediately
  cease to appear. With this episode, the author leaves open the possibility of divine intervention."
  (p. 134, folio not stated) In Shastri: "When the time comes for a devotee from Barmer to make his
  offering, the bubbles and the wind suddenly stop, and his gifts do not submerge in the volcano's
  mud. Only after he yells out his sins is his offering accepted and sinks down into the mud (Shastri
  1977, 45)." (p. 141, folio not stated)
- **A possession and an exorcism at the crater rim**, attributed to Shastri's account: "another pilgrim
  from Gujarat becomes possessed by a spirit. As he speaks, the man's voice changes to that of a
  female. The female ghost blames the pilgrim for having taken her virginity and then murdering her
  afterward. In revenge for his acts, the jinn announces that she will now kill the Gujarati man by
  throwing him into the volcano. But the chaṛīdār manages to cast out the spirit with mantras and some
  of the volcano's mud, which he smears on the man's forehead." (p. 141, folio not stated) **The book
  uses "spirit", "female ghost" and "jinn" of the same being within four sentences — see Doubts.**
- **The volcano answers Shastri**: "At the conclusion of his speech on the difficulty of acting
  morally, a huge eruption spews from the volcano, spitting out two coconuts. Pleased, the chaṛīdār
  taps Shastri on the shoulder and explains this strange incident to him as a sign of Chandrakup's deep
  satisfaction with Shastri's words (Shastri 1977, 47)." (p. 142, folio not stated)
- **The vision in the tunnel**, in Abadhut only: the I-narrator "describes his experience in the tunnel
  leading around the bedī as a vision of the Mother Goddess. This reported image basically resembles
  her common iconographic form (Abadhut 1964, 210)." (p. 134, folio not stated) Shastri by contrast
  "goes inside but writes nothing in his account about the 'indescribable' experience.44" (p. 142,
  folio not stated), note 44 quoting "Anirvacanīy yah anubhav!" (Shastri 1977, 54) (p. 149, folio not
  stated).
- **Rama's sin and its marks on the landscape**: the sun and moon "inscribed on the mountains opposite
  the temple … were created by Ramachandra himself when he came to Hinglaj to cleanse himself of his
  sin of killing a Brahmin" (p. 142, folio not stated); the Conclusion calls this "the mytho-historic
  narrative of Rama" (p. 146, folio not stated). **The Brahmin is not named**: **(not stated)**.
- **The Ganges in the Anil pond**: "at the Anil pond, the waters of the River Ganges are said to be
  flowing" (pp. 146–147, folios not stated).
- **Sin turned to smoke, rain and cloud** — Abadhut on Chandrakup, quoted in note 28: "Then this very
  sin [which has been thrown into Chandrakup] turns into smoke and dissolves in the endless sky. It
  becomes a cloud and rains down on the whole world; in the form of water [this sin] falls down on
  peoples' heads" (Abadhut 1964, 147) (p. 148, folio not stated). "This impression of the smoking
  crater was later adopted in the visual representation of the same scene in the film Marutīrtha
  Hiṃlāj." (p. 148, folio not stated)
- **Aghori Baba's tenure in the valley** as a called vocation: "Following the order of the Goddess,
  Aghori Baba had stayed behind" (p. 142, folio not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The chapter's governing claim about texts and the shrine**: "Āgneya Tīrtha Hiṅglāj, therefore, not
  only is a product of but also produces discourses on the desert Goddess." (p. 136, folio not stated)
  And of both books together: "Both novels, Marutīrtha Hiṃlāj and Āgneya Tīrtha Hiṅglāj, exert strong
  influence on public perception of the ritual journey and form, undoubtedly a significant part of the
  Hinglaj discourse." (p. 144, folio not stated)
- **Fieldwork used as evidence that a book makes the tradition** — the Rajendra Singh episode, in which
  the author's own gift of Shastri's text is quoted back to him as oral testimony a few hours later
  (pp. 136–137, folios not stated). The author frames it as demonstration, not anecdote: "An example
  from my fieldwork demonstrates this." (p. 136, folio not stated)
- **Authenticity as the criterion devotees apply**: "For many devotees in India and Pakistan, the
  narrative presents an accurate account of the 'authentic' or 'traditional' yātrā (mainly due to its
  descriptive style), and thus people frequently refer to the text." (p. 136, folio not stated) Against
  Abadhut: "the work is often judged according to a certain standard of accuracy due to which the
  strong narrativization of the account appears obstructive and seen as a sign of 'mere storytelling'"
  (pp. 135–136, folios not stated); "for many, Marutīrtha Hiṃlāj represents a work of fiction rather
  than a reliable account of the pilgrimage" (p. 136, folio not stated).
- **The "witnessing-I" and the "autobiographical pact"** — the chapter's analytic device. Abadhut's
  romantic plot means "the 'witnessing-I' to lose his credibility" (p. 133, folio not stated); the
  tunnel scene "constitutes a significant break from the 'autobiographical pact'" because the narrator
  publishes what the ritual authority forbade him to speak of, so "the witnessing-I either does not
  respect the rules set out by the Aghori Baba or, more significantly, does not fear the Goddess's
  potential wrath and hence is not a good devotee. This contradiction undermines the notion of the
  witnessing-I." (p. 134, folio not stated) In Shastri by contrast "there is no distinct line between
  the author and the protagonist/narrating-I" and the line "is kept as malleable as possible
  throughout, since the identification of the narrating-I with the author stands for the uniqueness and
  authenticity of the story" (pp. 142–143, folios not stated).
- **Secrecy as symbolic capital**, via Harder on the Maijbhandari: the paradox is one where "a certain
  code of secret knowledge [here, the image of the Goddess] is exposing itself to the non-initiated
  public [here, the reader], while insisting all the while on its marked property as a secret code
  [here, Aghori Baba's advice]" (Harder 2011, 279) (p. 135, folio not stated). "This language of
  secrecy carries a certain 'symbolic capital' (Bourdieu 1998) gained through the indication of a
  potential additional element without any further elaboration (Urban 1998). The author validates the
  implied content precisely by referring to its secrecy." (p. 135, folio not stated) The inverted
  reading is offered too: "the reading of the text would assume the same status as actually undertaking
  the journey, and so the initiated reader gains some share in the Goddess's darśan." (p. 135, folio not
  stated)
- **Shastri's protagonist as rationalist-devotee**, the author's central character reading: "On the one
  side, he paints a picture of himself as a spiritual seeker deeply rooted in the teachings of the
  Bhagavad Gītā, a character who believes in the agency of volcanoes and does not question the presence
  of spirits. On the other side, though, he is a fierce agitator against blind faith, orthodoxy, and
  traditional interpretations of the classical Hindu scriptures (Shastri 1977, 20)." (p. 143, folio not
  stated) What distinguishes him is "not his devotion to the Goddess" but "his practice of ātmaviśvās
  (Hin.), his self-confidence" (p. 143, folio not stated). "While all the others are passive
  sinner-subjects of the volcano's agency, the protagonist is active, even receiving the volcano's
  approval for his words." (p. 143, folio not stated)
- **Why that matters for the yātrā's reach** — the chapter's most transferable argument: "The
  protagonist's endless doubts about the useless rituals and cruel oaths lead to a unio mystica which,
  in my opinion, appears even more 'genuine' because it happens to a skeptic, an event that even the
  rationally inclined can believe in. Thus, the yātrā emerges as a mystical experience for all; it has
  meaning not only for those stuck in blind faith—that is, people who believe in purity and rituals—but
  also for those who follow humanist principles." (p. 144, folio not stated)
- **The indescribable as a structural device**: "This mystical aspect finds its climax in the final
  darśan at the shrine, an event far from any known worldly experience and thus indescribable."
  (p. 144, folio not stated)
- **Communalism critique attributed to Shastri, not to the author**: "Shastri argues that the reality of
  the dharma can't be bound by the limitations of communalism.37 The saṃpradāy (Hin. established
  doctrine) for him is merely a system of useless laws and rites, whereas only the Gītā offers solutions
  for the real predicaments of human beings and thus provides an explanation of dharma (Shastri 1977,
  20)." (p. 140, folio not stated) Note 37 gives "Dharmatattva sampradāyoṃ kī sīmā se baṁdhā nahīṃ hotā
  hai" (Shastri 1977, 20) (p. 148, folio not stated).
- **Exoticization named as such**: "an exoticization of the noble Balochi cameleers is found in both
  accounts (cf. Shastri [1977, 10] and Abadhut [1964, 4])" (p. 143, folio not stated).
- **The influence question, deliberately left open**: "This coincidence of very closely corresponding
  themes … might suggest that Shastri copied parts from Abadhut's much earlier work. However, there is
  no definite proof of such borrowing." (p. 145, folio not stated) The author's preferred explanation is
  ritual sedimentation: "certain themes were commonly present on the pilgrimage and expressed in
  different fashions by different writers" (p. 145, folio not stated); "the assumption that Abadhut's
  writing influenced Shastri's work to some degree is likely, but the idea that Shastri copied his
  description directly from Abadhut's is much less so" (p. 145, folio not stated). And he declares the
  question immaterial to his own project: "Notwithstanding the fact that the mutual influence is an
  intriguing question, it ultimately does not have an important impact on this study. I am interested in
  the various ways the yātrā was represented and how certain common themes arose that eventually
  sedimented into an allegedly 'true' pilgrimage—or, in other words, into one of the tradition's most
  conspicuous truth-claims." (p. 145, folio not stated)
- **Which themes carried over**: "Themes such as the carrying of onions, the idea that the camels are
  the actual guides along the way, or the breaking of the oath given at the River Hab reappear in
  Shastri's work." (pp. 144–145, folios not stated) "The closest similarities in the two stories are
  found in the description of the oath at the River Hab and the appearance of the Aghori Baba at the
  shrine of Hinglaj.45" (p. 145, folio not stated), note 45 giving the precise pairs: "Compare here
  Shastri (1977, 11) with Abadhut (1964, 6), and Shastri (1977, 54) with Abadhut (1964, 211)." (p. 149,
  folio not stated)
- **Austerity as the through-line of the chapter's sources**: "Representations of the pilgrimage, such
  as the travelogue by Abadhut, the account given by Shastri, and the mytho-historic narrative of Rama,
  emphasize the necessity of austerity and kinesthetic practice, captured by the concept of tapas or
  tapasyā, along the way." (p. 146, folio not stated) The term is defended in note 12: "The primary
  meaning of the noun tapas, from the root 'tap,' is 'warming up' or 'heating up.' … Because it lacks a
  moral or ethical implication, tapas cannot be used synonymously with the Western idea of asceticism.
  However, due to a lack of any equivalent term, I use tapas in the wider sense of 'asceticism' or
  'austerity' (Shee 1986, 187)." (p. 147, folio not stated)
- **"Kinesthetic" chosen over Turner's "kinetic"**: "Edith Turner called pilgrimage a 'kinetic ritual'
  (Turner and Turner 1978, xiii). The adjective 'kinetic' actually means the motion of material bodies.
  In this work, however, I use the term 'kinesthetic' to emphasize not only physical motion—often a part
  of pilgrimages—but also the meaning associated with that physical motion." (p. 146, folio not stated)
- **Tīrtha as an elastic category**: Eck "argues that the word tīrtha, as it is found in the older
  classical texts—like the Vedic Saṃhitās, the Brāhmaṇas, and the early Upaniṣads—does not mean a place
  of pilgrimage but was rather associated with purity and ritual practice (Eck 1981, 326)" (p. 146,
  folio not stated); "it seems that every stop along the way to the temple can potentially become a
  tīrtha. Jethamalani demonstrates this very wide understanding of the term tīrtha in his book Hinglāj
  Tīrth. For him, all stops on the way to Hinglaj can be called tīrthas (Jethamalani 1994)." (p. 146,
  folio not stated)
- **A methodological caution the author enters about memoirs**: "Such is often the case in the memoirs
  of public or politically active people, where certain kinds of actions are retrospectively justified
  (cf. Musharraf 2006)." (p. 148, folio not stated)

  `Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir`, location "Hingol Balochistan National Park, Road، Asha Pura,
  Pakistan", **with the id cell empty, and with category, principal_figure, figure_died and silsila all
  empty as well**. No id was invented for it; every Hinglaj bullet above is headed with the literal
  string `**(no id — Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir)**`. **A human must mint that id before this
  book can be consolidated**, since it is the row that will carry almost everything in the volume. Two
  further cautions on that row: its location string contains an Arabic comma (U+060C) after "Road",
  which will break naive splitting; and the "Asha Pura" in it **must not be merged with the Goddess
  Ashapura or her temples**, which this book treats separately — no Ashapura material appears in this
  chunk, so nothing here forces the question, but the collision is live for other chunks.
