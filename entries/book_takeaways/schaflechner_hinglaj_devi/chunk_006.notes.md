## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The pilgrimage tax at Sonmiani, and route choice as an economic decision.** "Isthtiaq[text-layer?]
  [Ishtiaq] Ansari counts ten stops between Karachi and the port of Sonmiani (Ansari 2001, 29–32),
  where every pilgrim was required to pay a tax." (p. 113, folio not stated) "According to oral
  narratives, this tax at Sonmiani eventually caused many pilgrims to change their route, taking a more
  difficult path over the mountains of Sehwan in order to avoid the port (and the port tax)." (p. 113,
  folio not stated) The book's own conclusion: "Such accounts make it evident that then, as now,
  pilgrimage routes to Hinglaj were often chosen not only according to particular religious motivations
  but also to accommodate time constraints, financial circumstances, and the physical condition of the
  pilgrims." (p. 113, folio not stated)
- **A festival fee that moved from year to year** (Chapter 2 note): "This is also documented on the
  announcement sheets of that time. However, during the 1990s, this fee varied from year to year."
  (p. 100, folio not stated) **The fee's amount and what it was for are not stated in this chunk**:
  **(not stated)**.
- **A lesser pilgrimage to the port itself**: "Apparently, there used to be a small yātrā only to
  Sonmiani, which was called choṭī māī (Joshi 2010, 21)." (p. 113, folio not stated)
- **Guides as an occupation**: "Pilgrim groups were usually guided by aguās (Hin. guide; also aguvā)
  who originated from areas close to Hinglaj (Brohi 1998, 30)." (p. 113, folio not stated)
- **The three land-and-sea options from Sonmiani.** "Wilford writes that from the town of Sonmiani
  onward, pilgrims had three options: one sea route, which was the least frequented; one route that
  followed along the shore, the path chosen by most pilgrims; and one inland route, traversable only
  with the help of camels (Wilford 1851, 243)." (p. 113, folio not stated) Wilford "not only describes
  the paths originating in Karachi but also includes routes from Thatta and even as far away as Bhuj.
  Between Karachi and the port of Sonmiani, the paths were fairly similar." (p. 113, folio not stated)
  A mixed route is also recorded: "some pilgrimage groups walked as far as Sonmiani and then took a
  boat from there to cross the bay (Wilford 1851, 250f.)" (p. 115, folio not stated).
- **Wilford's informants, and a journey dated to c. 1770**: "Wilford gained this information from
  various pilgrims and aguās, but above all from a devotee from Thatta who must have already visited
  the site around 1770 (Wilford 1851, 233)." (p. 113, folio not stated)
- **The sea route, attested and contested.** "There are not many accounts of the sea route, which
  apparently took pilgrims up to the mouth of the Hingol River where they disembarked and continued on
  foot." (p. 114, folio not stated) "Abro, too, confirms that in the 'former days' (Sin. 'avā'ilī vaqtan
  meṉ') of the yātrā, the easiest way to reach the shrine was via the sea route." (p. 114, folio not
  stated) Against that: "most of my informants insisted that the most frequented route for pilgrims
  from Gujarat was the coastal route described above via the Rann of Kutch; this is confirmed in
  Kshatriya's writings (Kshatriya 2005, 75)." (p. 115, folio not stated)
- **The akhāṛās of Thatta as the only medieval archive.** "Only a few records from the akhāṛās (Hin.
  wrestling ground, arena; here: pilgrim shelter) in the city of Thatta are able to shed some light on
  the practice in medieval times. Various Sindhi authors quote these annals as historical evidence of
  royal visits to the temple (Chhabria 2000; Jethamalani 1994; Ansari 2001)." (p. 112, folio not
  stated) "Ansari adds that in addition to these caravans, many pilgrims from Kutch and Jaipur used to
  visit the shrine (Ansari 2001, 22)." (p. 113, folio not stated)
- **Branding as a certificate of pilgrimage, and its two explanations.** "At one time, pilgrims in
  Koteshwara, especially followers of the Gorakhnath sect, would have the sign of Shiva and Shakti
  burned onto their forearms. This marking bore witness to their successful journey to Hinglaj."
  (p. 115, folio not stated) Briggs' reading: "the branding actually represented the reinstating of the
  Hindu pilgrims back to their original Hindu faith following the pilgrimage (Briggs 1938, 109)."
  (p. 115, folio not stated) The priests' reading: "They equate this practice to the rituals that are
  performed in modern times after the pilgrimage, which are necessary to purify the homecoming pilgrims
  after their journey to Pakistan." (p. 116, folio not stated) "Because the area in which Hinglaj is
  located is full of 'meat eaters' and the pilgrims on their way necessarily have to stay at the homes
  of Muslims, Kohlis, or other communities considered 'impure' (Hin. bhraṣṭ), purification rituals are

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Shah Abdul Latif, the milk and the Mother**, attributed to legend: "Legend has it that after making
  a long and difficult journey, Latif had reached the shrine of the Goddess and was offering the Mother
  some milk from his palm. In this moment, the image came alive, and the Mother drank from the hands of
  the saint. This evoked the envy of the Yogis, who then decided to kill him. Latif fled the scene and
  disappeared into the earth only to re-emerge at Thatta, some 350 kilometers away." (pp. 108–109,
  folio not stated)
- **The ship turned to stone at "Goorab-i-Sung"**, quoted from Hart at length: "A wealthy Shet named
  Kowaljee freighted a vessel from Hindoostan to carry himself and a large party to Hinglaj… when the
  Goddess, justly indignant at his arrogance and presumption, transformed the boat and every soul on
  board into stone. (Hart 1839, 91)" (p. 114, folio not stated) The book reads it as a sanction on
  route choice, not only as a wonder (p. 114, folio not stated).
- **Rama's pilgrimage to Hinglaj**, named twice as a story the chapter will return to: "Some oral
  traditions and mytho-historic narratives about Hinglaj—like the story of Rama's pilgrimage (Hin.
  yātrā)—demonstrate the importance of the specific means of arriving at the shrine to the spiritual
  merit thereby gained." (p. 104, folio not stated) "This separation also exists today and will become
  even more obvious in the story of Rama's pilgrimage." (p. 114, folio not stated) **The story itself
  is not told in this chunk**: **(not stated)**.
- **The Muslim woman priest of the Goddess, reported and then doubted.** "According to Briggs, there
  exists a legend that a Muslim woman was the priest of the Goddess in Hinglaj in those days. By
  ritually applying the ṭīkā (Hin. ornamental or sectarian mark) on the forehead of the (Hindu)
  pilgrims, she turned all of them into Muslims. Thus, on their way back, the pilgrims had to be
  reconverted to their original faith—hence the branding (Briggs 1938, 106–110)." (p. 115, folio not
  stated) Schaflechner's verdict: "Due to the fact that no similar account is mentioned in any other
  sources, it is difficult to confirm Briggs' suggestion… Thus, the story of converting pilgrims into
  Muslims may simply be another in the huge corpus of unverifiable legends surrounding the aloof shrine
  of the Goddess." (p. 115, folio not stated)
- **The Saraswat Brahmins and Ramacandra's ṭīkā**, from Burton: "many of these Saraswat Brahmins were
  considered low caste and were also called 'Sindhur,' from the vermillion ṭīkā (Hin. mark on the
  forehead), which—according to legend—they had gotten from Ramacandra. Rama was in need of a priest
  and since he could not find any in Balochistan he simply applied a ṭīkā on a mlecch's (Hin. barbarian)
  forehead. These Saraswat Brahmins were known for their devotion to the Devi who, in most areas of
  Sindh, was called Hinglaj (Burton 1851, 312)." (p. 100, folio not stated)
- **Ali's camel in the Lahoot cave**, given as allegation: "a curiously shaped stone that is allegedly
  the image of Ali's camel" (p. 108, folio not stated).
- **The Jasyasena narrative of the Hiṃgulā Purāṇ**, cited only by chapter number and disputed: "Jareca,
  however, begins the narrative of Jasyasena with chapter 77 (Jareca n.d., 1), while Shastri, claiming
  to be the translator of the Hiṃgulā Purāṇ, states that the story begins with chapter 79 (Shastri
  1905, 258) of the said text." (p. 99, folio not stated)
- **The Karna and Parashurama parallel**, offered by the author as a structural comparison rather than
  as a Hinglaj legend: Karna "disguises himself as a member of the sage Bhrgu's family"; an insect
  bites his thigh while Parashurama sleeps in his lap; Parashurama "curses Karna, foretelling that he
  will forget the powerful knowledge of the brahmā weapon in the moment when he will need it the most
  (compare chapters 2 and 3 of the Śānti Parva)." (p. 99, folio not stated)
- **The brahmarandhra, glossed**: Apte translates it as "an aperture on the crown of the head through
  which the soul is said to escape on its leaving the body" (Apte 1992) (p. 98, folio not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The chapter's thesis, mobility against locality**: "I argue that mobility and locality are best
  understood as two mutually influential, albeit often imbalanced, aspects of pilgrimage sites in
  general and of the Hinglaj temple in particular." (p. 104, folio not stated) The case is stated
  first: "The case of the ritual journey to Hinglaj clearly shows that both aspects—mobility and
  locality—significantly affect the creation of mytho-historical narratives and ritual practices at the
  shrine." (p. 103, folio not stated) The literature it corrects: Ingold and Vergunst "criticize that
  ethnographical accounts of religious localities often still describe places and destinations and less
  frequently include the means by which people reached them (Ingold and Vergunst 2008, 3)" (p. 103,
  folio not stated).
- **Why the old sources matter as sources**: "Because many devotees in Pakistan and India consider
  these sources as some of the few authentic documentations about the pilgrimage's former reality,
  these texts form a significant part of Hinglaj's sedimented history. Particularly, more recent
  sources create forms of authenticity upon the truthfulness of the autobiographical accounts." (p. 104,
  folio not stated)
- **A loss of austerity read as a loss of quality**: "Many visitors emphasize the physical endeavor and
  hardship in such allegedly 'traditional' journeys and often connect the current absence of tapasyā
  (Skt. also tapas), austerity, or simply the kinesthetic experience, with a decrease in spiritual
  quality at Hinglaj." (p. 104, folio not stated) "Visitors at the shrine often brought up this
  discord, emphasizing that the site is decaying as a result of people not adhering to the traditional
  practices; this will be the topic of a later chapter." (p. 110, folio not stated)
- **"Pilgrimage" defined by family resemblance, not by feature.** The term "cannot be easily defined by
  specific physical or ritualistic features" (p. 105, folio not stated); Bharati has emphasized that
  the tīrtha and tīrthayātrā "may also be expressed metaphorically (Bharati 1970, 85)" (p. 105, folio
  not stated); Wilke's Lalita Tripurasundari has her place "not a physical location but rather within
  the śrīcakra, a mental representation of the Goddess's dwelling… sacred landscapes are not missing;
  they have merely shifted from outer to inner spaces, thus becoming allegories (Wilke 2010, 82)"
  (p. 105, folio not stated). Hence Wittgenstein's "family resemblances" (Ger.
  "Familienähnlichkeiten") (Wittgenstein 2003, §67; Staten 1986, 82–83) (p. 105, folio not stated),
  and the author's own usage: "The term yātrā, which I will translate as 'pilgrimage' or 'ritual
  journey,' shares a family resemblance with many practices, but there is no definite element
  permeating all expressions of the concept." (p. 105, folio not stated)
- **Tīrtha, defined and un-bounded**: "The Sanskrit term tīrtha—literally meaning 'passage,' 'ford,' or
  'bathing place'" (p. 106, folio not stated); "Tīrthas are often found at or near certain natural
  features like springs, caves, or volcanoes, but can also be at the graves of saints and their
  disciples. Hence, the term tīrtha is not bound to any one type of site, but rather suggests a place
  that promises some kind of benefit to the visitor." (p. 106, folio not stated) **That sentence is the
  book's warrant for treating a Hindu tīrtha and a Muslim dargāh in one frame.** "The earliest
  extensive noting of tīrthasthāns is found in the Tīrthayātrāpārvaṇa of the Mahābhārata" (p. 106,
  folio not stated). Eck: travel to the tīrthas is "one of the oldest and still one of the most
  prominent features of Indian religious life" (Eck 1981, 323) (p. 106, folio not stated); Jacobsen
  "goes so far as to say that Hindu religiosity is often more centered around sacred space than around
  sacred text (Jacobsen 2013, 7)" (p. 106, folio not stated).
- **Salvific benefit as a category, with a comparison to sacrifice**: Jacobsen's "salvific" "includes
  desired goals that might pertain to worldly wealth and health in life but also to rebirth and
  salvation"; "many sites promise visitors benefits similar to those that could be gained from more
  elaborate ritual practices such as the aśvamedha (Skt.) or the agnihotra (Skt.) (Eck 1981, 338)"
  (p. 106, folio not stated). Clientele varies: "some might be visited mainly by particular jātis (Hin.
  caste, community), others by women or by low-caste communities (Gold 1990, 5). Yet it is rare to find
  a fully consistent clientele in one or another of these places of worship." (p. 106, folio not stated)
- **Networks, imagined landscapes and the śākta pīṭha**: "tīrthas do not exist in isolation but rather
  form a network of many different sacred sites all over the subcontinent" (p. 106, folio not stated);
  "One could say, for example, that the fifty-one (or one-hundred eight) places of the Goddess (Skt.
  śākta pīṭha) mentioned previously belong to such a category of connected tīrthas" (p. 107, folio not
  stated). **The two counts, "fifty-one (or one-hundred eight)", are given exactly as printed.** Eck's
  "duplication and multiplication" of sites (Eck 1998, 166–168) and mythology that "take[s] place" in a
  network (Eck 1998, 169–170) (p. 107, folio not stated); Feldhaus's regions, made of sites "people
  bring together with their bodies . . . with their minds and imaginations" (Feldhaus 2003, 100)
  (p. 107, folio not stated).
- **The claim that matters for this archive**: "Hence, salvific places do not necessarily have to be
  bound to religious affiliation and can comprise different communities or different faiths." (p. 107,
  folio not stated) And, after the Latif legend: "Such stories connect Hindu and Muslim sacred spaces
  in Sindh and show that imagined landscapes do not necessarily have to be separated using clear-cut
  religious taxonomies." (p. 109, folio not stated)
- **Sindh as a landscape of coexistence, and who says so**: "The south of Pakistan, and especially
  Sindh, offers a whole range of salvific spaces that are frequently visited by different religious
  communities and form a network of pilgrimage routes or ritual journeys. Sindhi nationalists often
  emphasize these characteristics of Sindh as a landscape of 'coexistence' (see Chapter 2)." (p. 107,
  folio not stated) The Sindhi claim quoted in Chapter 2's notes: "Sindh xūśqismatī'a sān jī'un
  tahcqīdan[text-layer?] [taḥqīqan] jī mcāmulī[text-layer?] [maʿāmulī] men sekyūlar rahe āhe." (p. 101,
  folio not stated) **No English gloss is printed for this sentence in the chunk**: **(not stated)**.
- **A Sindhi claim of universal mother-worship**, quoted without gloss: "Asān ke dhartī te insān jo
  d̤āke ba d̤āke śacūrjaṇ[text-layer?] [śaʿūrjaṇ] jo tasalsal ai rābt̤o naz̤ar ache tho. Ītrī qadr jo
  hinan jo pīro khaṇje tho tah sabhnī jūn pāṛūn pātāl tā'īn vañi hik jag̈h te milan thyūn, yacnī
  [text-layer?] [yaʿnī] sabhnī jo b̤ij hik mādar parastī āhe" (Sin.) (p. 101, folio not stated).
  **Printed with no translation in this chunk**: **(not stated)**.
- **Mobility as the medium of memory**: Lorimer and Lund, "[S]torying with events can render landscape
  memorable" (1908 [Lorimer and Lund 2008], 192) (p. 109, folio not stated); Ingold, "cognition should
  not be set off from locomotion, along the lines of a division between head and heels, since walking
  is itself a form of circumambulatory knowing" (Ingold 2004, 331) (p. 109, folio not stated); Olwing
  [text-layer?] [Olwig], landscape takes form through the "repetition of passages walked" (Olwing 2008,
  87) (p. 109, folio not stated); Shields, "Over time, spatial practices, the habitual routines of
  'place ballets' are concretised in the built environment and sedimented in the landscape" (Shields
  1991, 53) (p. 109, folio not stated). The reverse process is named too: "A process in the opposite
  direction—the ceasing of habitual routes—disassociates inhabitants from their (imagined)
  geographies." (p. 110, folio not stated)
- **Cresswell's three-part mobility, restated for ritual journeys**: "(a) that ritual journeys always
  imply some kind of movement; (b) that this movement is located in discourse as well as in space and
  that it can be represented, interpreted, or valued in various ways; and (c) that the journey is
  experienced through the body, which makes bodily practices significant (Cresswell 2010, 160)."
  (p. 110, folio not stated)
- **The gap the book is really after**: "A gap between representation and the actual bodily experience
  of reaching Hinglaj has had an especially significant influence on the dynamics at the shrine in
  recent years. It is here that the imaginaries of the pilgrimage, sedimented in many texts and
  perpetuated in oral histories, collide with current practices relating to the travel." (p. 110, folio
  not stated)
- **Dating the shrine's own scriptures, sceptically.** "Thus, according to my research, the Hiṃgulā
  Purāṇ did not appear in writing before the twentieth century. Other texts presenting the same story
  are even more recent." (p. 99, folio not stated) "The mere fact that Jareca prints a Sanskrit text he
  proclaims as ancient does not necessarily prove that the text is not a Sanskrit translation of an
  older Gujarati version. This assertion stems from the fact that I have not been able to find the
  Hiṃgulā Khaṇḍa anywhere, nor have I ever met anyone who has laid hands on this version of the Skanda
  Purāṇa." (p. 99, folio not stated) An expert opinion is entered: "Professor Isaacson from the
  University of Hamburg estimated that the text was written in the past two or three centuries (from a
  personal email communication January 2, 2009)." (p. 100, folio not stated)
- **A plagiarism finding against a standard source.** "The following paragraph corresponds almost
  verbatim with Crooke's article: 'The title of Bibi Nani may be identified with Nanea, the
  mother-goddess worshipped in Syria, Persia and Armenia, and other parts of Asia under the names
  Anaiti, Anaea, Aneitis or Tanais, the primeval Babylonian Goddess Nana, the lady of the temple E-anna
  of her city Uruk.' … Consequently, I think it highly likely that this part was taken directly from
  the ERE (compare ERE 1913, 715 with Billimoria 1944, 93)." (p. 101, folio not stated)
- **Nana, Durga and the limits the author sets himself.** "I am not in the position to judge the
  connection of the Goddess Hinglaj with other ancient goddesses of the Middle East, but I would like to
  mention the work of D. T. Potts (2001)" (p. 101, folio not stated); Potts quoted: "The possibilities
  for [Nana's] conflation with Durga are particularly tempting given the fact that, on Kushan coinage,
  Nana sometimes 'appears with Oesho (Siva) as her consort,' and the lion-riding goddesses of some
  seventh century A.D. silver bowls from Choresmia 'may be related to Nana or Durga'" (Potts 2001, 27)
  (p. 101, folio not stated); and "[h]er worship in Bactria . . . bespeaks a long period of profound
  influence which has lasted until the modern day, as the shrines in Afghanistan to 'Bibi Nanni' surely
  attest" (Potts 2001, 31) (p. 102, folio not stated).
- **The methodological refusal that closes Chapter 2**: "Although I do not doubt the ancient history of
  the Hinglaj shrine in Balochistan, I want to emphasize that at this point, I am more interested in the
  current usage of such topoi of origin (or the 'chimera of origin' as Foucault says) and how they shape
  Pakistan's and Sindh's recent religious and nationalist landscape." (p. 102, folio not stated)
- **Colonial ethnography as a co-production, acknowledged and set aside**: "There are many works that
  hint at the entanglement of British and local informants in the production of what is generally
  understood today as 'Hinduism.' Since this is not the topic of this study, I will simply refer here to
  a few influential works (Sen 1993; Kopf 1969; Dirks 2001; King 1999)." (p. 98, folio not stated)
- **The author's own source failures, stated openly**: "Unfortunately, I was not able to obtain a copy

  the expertise to locate the Sanskrit text by Jareca in a particular time period." (p. 100, folio not
  stated) "Unfortunately, there is little in the precolonial literature regarding the actual practices

- **Which text the story is translated from, and why**: "Since the Hiṃgulā Purāṇ is available to me in a
