## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The bhag'vā, the pilgrimage's marker of renunciation, and where it is put on.** Shastri has the
  journey begin at the Hab River, "where the pilgrims say an oath and put on an ochre-colored piece of
  cloth (Shastri 1977, 11)" (p. 207, folio not stated). Today: "pilgrims coming from the south also put
  on the ochre-colored piece of cloth in Liari, which previously was done at the River Hab. This cloth,
  called the bhag'vā (Hin.; in Urd. bhagvā), was tied around the head or worn around the neck (Khatri
  1973, 19).7 It assured the yātrīs the Goddess's protection from wild animals or other obstacles along
  the way (Jethamalani 1994, 7)." (p. 208, folio not stated) The organiser's gloss: "The bhag'vā means
  that the pilgrims become Yogis and ascetics at this place [Liari]. When they wear the bhag'vā, they
  leave the mundane world behind. It is actually a sign of the faqīrs [ . . . ]. Here their pilgrimage
  starts—this is their first stop [paṛāv]. This means that they now focus completely on the pilgrimage
  and that they are protected from animals like snakes and scorpions [ . . . ]. By wearing the bhag'vā
  they receive a protective covering [kavac lag jātā hai]." (p. 209, folio not stated, note 8)
  "Today during the phālgun/caitra festival, this traditional bhag'vā is also worn in the form of an
  ochre-colored cap, which is sold by locals around the Liari temple." (p. 209, folio not stated)
- **The ticketing system and the bhag'vā paṭṭī, a religious idiom put to a commercial use.** "Inside
  the Hinglaj Valley, this bhag'vā tradition was rearticulated between 2009 and 2013, when the HSM
  introduced a new way of dispensing tickets to the temple. Before 2009, there had been another
  ticketing system in place that, according to members of the HSM, had proven very easy for visitors to
  bypass. This led the organization to introduce nontransferable, ochre-colored plastic wristbands,
  which they called the bhag'vā paṭṭī. Many visitors were upset with this procedure and mocked the way
  in which the HSM attempted to connect the selling of tickets to the traditional practice of the
  bhag'vā. In response to the protests, in 2013, the HSM decided to abolish the ticketing system"
  (p. 209, folio not stated). **A second telling of the same system dates it to 2008 and prices it**:
  "in 2008, the Mandali introduced a new ticketing system requiring the purchase of an ochre-colored,
  plastic wristband called the bhag'vā paṭṭī (Hin. ochre cloth/ribbon) in place of the former paper
  tickets. During the time of the phālgun/caitra melā, visitors were required to wear the 250-rupee (in
  2012) wristband visibly if they wished to cross into the western part of the valley where the Kali
  and Hinglaj temples are located. While in years prior, the ticketing system had been rather loosely
  regulated, the new system strictly required the display of the paṭṭī at two separate checkpoints for
  access to the western part of the valley." (p. 219, folio not stated) **The two dates conflict; see
  Doubts.**
- **The fee, converted, and its social weight.** "Since its establishment, the HSM has always charged a
  fee for the organization of the pilgrimage (transport, food, water, etc.). The Mandali sees itself as
  the successor of the chaṛīdār, who also received money for his services." (p. 219, folio not stated)
  "Notwithstanding the fact that 250 rupees (about €2 in 2012) might not seem a large sum, for simple
  peasants, this amount equals a day's wage for work in the fields." (pp. 219–220, folios not stated)
  "Since the wristbands are not transferable to others, families tend to send the male members inside
  with the funds they have available, leaving the female members outside of the pay-only area."
  (p. 220, folio not stated)
- **Liari's economy of the stop.** "Moreover, financial advantages in the form of donations and
  proceeds from the sale of coconuts and ochre-colored cloth caps at the Liari stop certainly also
  plays a major role in the HSM's attempt to keep the paṛāv a significant visiting site." (p. 209,
  folio not stated) The HSM, "whose members consist almost entirely of Lohanas from Las Bela, still
  advertises the Liari paṛāv as the starting point of the journey, which is evident from the
  announcement posters of the pilgrimage." (p. 209, folio not stated)
- **Chandrakup's economy.** "During the phālgun/caitra festival, however, locals offer the pilgrims
  lifts in trucks and off-road vehicle up to the foot of the main volcano to make some extra money."
  (p. 211, folio not stated) "In addition, locals immediately catch the coconuts with long poles and
  pull them out of the mud in order to resell them at the foot of the slope. Chandrakup volcano is
  generally used by many local Balochis to make some money by selling coconuts and tea or by begging
  for donations." (p. 214, folio not stated)
- **The valley's commerce and who is licensed to trade inside it.** "Most shops and tea stalls are not
  allowed to be set up in the valley, and so there is a clear separation between the commercial area
  and the sacred area. Only the descendants of Dost Mohammad, the Muslim caretaker at the shrine before
  the HSM gained its influence, were allowed to sell cigarettes and snacks inside the valley during the
  time of my fieldwork. In recent years, the commercial area outside the wall has expanded, and the HSM
  has had to clear out additional space for shops and parking lots." (p. 219, folio not stated)
- **State policing of the festival, and the Hindus-only order.** "this 'threshold' of the (Hindu) sacred
  space, as one enters from the nonsacred space, is also marked by the presence of the Balochistan
  police standing guard at the valley's gate. The police are under an official order to allow only
  Hindus to enter the festival. Due to the huge crowds, however, the mandate represents an impossible
  task; there is no way to determine the visitors' religious affiliations from afar. Yet the police
  still show their presence through selective searches at the entrance as well as uniformed and
  ununiformed patrolling inside the valley." (p. 218, folio not stated) The author's counter-instance:
  "I myself spent some time with a Muslim traveler from Karachi at Hinglaj who had no problems entering
  the valley whatsoever during the festival." (p. 218, folio not stated)
- **The two signboards and the code of conduct.** "Specific guidelines are suggested by two signs—one
  in English and one in Urdu—that lay out the 'do's' and 'don'ts' of the sacred geography." (pp.
  217–218, folios not stated) "These rules reflect some of the traditional behaviors required of
  pilgrims on their way to Hinglaj (Shastri 1977, 11) but in some ways also appear to respond to new
  challenges that evolved out of the temple's increased accessibility." (p. 218, folio not stated)
  "Although they are only suggestive in nature, the boards still indicate the existence of particular
  'ideal' behaviors and practices that are not required outside the walls of the confined area."
  (p. 218, folio not stated)
- **The festival date, and how it was set by hydrology rather than by calendar.** "The Hingol's water
  level is partially responsible for the phālgun/caitra festival's date being chosen in the mid-1980s.
  Initially, when the annual festival was established, the HSM needed to select a weekend when the
  river's low water level could be anticipated so that the pilgrims would be able to safely cross. For
  this reason, the date of the festival was not fixed according to a religious calendar but was
  scheduled in consideration of local weather conditions. With the construction of a bridge over the
  Hingol in 2012—a project that was funded by the Balochistan government—the date of the festival no
  longer depended on the water level of the river and thus was shifted by the HSM to a date considered
  to be 'more accurate.'19 At the time of writing, the HSM had recently decided to organize the yātrā
  on the weekend after the caitra navarāti." (p. 215, folio not stated) **No calendar date and no
  duration is given for the melā**: **(not stated)**. **The chunk names no ʿurs of any kind.**
- **Roads, and what they did to the route.** "It was the construction of the N 25 between Karachi and
  Quetta in the 1960s that transformed Liari into an important site, even for pilgrims coming from the
  south traveling over Karachi." (pp. 207–208, folios not stated) "Liari was located in a strategic
  position, close to the N 25 and at the beginning of the west-leading desert road, rendering the
  village an important organizational stop. At this juncture the means of transportation changed and
  scattered groups traveled to Hinglaj on camels or in off-road vehicle." (p. 208, folio not stated)
  Figure caption: "FIGURE 5.1 The journey from Liari village after the construction of a road between
  Quetta and Karachi" (p. 208, folio not stated). "In 2001, after the MCH had been constructed up to
  the Hingol River, Liari lost its functional and strategic position as the last major settlement
  before the desert journey." (p. 209, folio not stated) Shelter-building follows the road: "Nowadays,
  there is an increasing number of pilgrimage shelters inside the valley's confined area, almost all of
  which were built after the completion of the Makran Coastal Highway in 2001." (p. 220, folio not
  stated) "By 2009, many more shelters had been built in the area around this stop, and construction of
  additional buildings to accommodate the thousands of pilgrims was ongoing the last time I visited the
  shrine in 2015." (p. 221, folio not stated)
- **Pre-road logistics, as reported from before Partition.** "Hopeful pilgrims often waited several
  weeks at the akhāṛā until a group of twenty to twenty-five people had assembled and camel drivers
  hired from Las Bela had arrived." (p. 206, folio not stated) "Abadhut also mentions that the Naths
  started small businesses around the akhāṛā since they could not make a living solely from guiding
  pilgrims to Hinglaj (Abadhut 1964, 1)." (p. 206, folio not stated) In the 1970s "the camels still had
  to be left at Ashapura while the pilgrims continued on foot since the remainder of the path was too
  dangerous for the animals (Khatri 1973, 24f.)" (p. 221, folio not stated).
- **The chaṛī and the chaṛīdār, the office the HSM claims to have replaced.** The sticks are "cut from a
  tree in the Hinglaj Valley and had the power to protect the group on their dangerous journey,
  provided they properly worshipped it on the way" (p. 207, folio not stated). At Chandrakup the augury
  "was once done by the chaṛīdār and is done today either by someone from the HSM or any ritual expert
  traveling with the group" (p. 212, folio not stated); the chaṛīdār also read whether sins were
  forgiven (Shastri 1977, 41) (p. 213, folio not stated) and received the dān-dakṣiṇā at the Hingol
  (p. 216, folio not stated). "The Mandali sees itself as the successor of the chaṛīdār, who also
  received money for his services." (p. 219, folio not stated)
- **Offerings at Chandrakup, then and now.** "Traditionally, the pilgrims bake a type of cake, or
  sweetbread, called the 'roṭ,' before ascending the slope of Chandrakup (cf. Hart 1839, 89; Shastri
  1977, 43; Abadhut 1964, 157). The roṭ is made out of ingredients contributed by all the yātrīs and
  serves as a shared offering to Chandrakup or, as Hart says in his description, to the God Rama (Hart
  1839, 89)." (pp. 212–213, folios not stated) "Aside from coconuts, other oblations include gāṁjā (Hin.
  marijuana) and cilams (Hin. pipe to smoke marijuana), which are also thrown into the volcano (Shastri
  1977, 42; Abadhut 1964, 157; Lakhavat 2011, 138).15 Nowadays, the suggested offerings consist of
  coconuts (Urd. nāriyal), betel nut (Urd. supārī), coins (Urd. sikkah), and dal (Urd. dāl)
  (Jethamalani 1994, 44)." (p. 213, folio not stated) "Yet the ritual of throwing coconuts into the
  crater seems to be the most commonly practiced over the years.14" (p. 212, folio not stated)
- **The vigil and the confession of sins.** "The night before ascending Chandrakup, the group stays
  awake (Hin. jāg'raṇ), fasting and meditating on the sins they will confess at the rim of the crater.
  At the top, the yātrīs first have to introduce themselves with their full name and place of origin
  and then call out their sins in front of the group." (p. 213, folio not stated) "Nowadays, there are
  no such strict regulations at the volcano, and it is impossible to verify the extent to which
  restrictions were actually enforced in the past. Furthermore, during the phālgun/caitra festival,
  pilgrims, eager to continue on their journey, do not have the time to wait for their offerings to sink
  into the mud (even if its consistency would allow it on that day)." (pp. 213–214, folios not stated)
- **Rites at the River Hingol.** "Historically, as well as today, pilgrims take their last bath in the
  Hingol before they enter the valley of Hinglaj. Today, depending on the water level, this is often
  only a symbolic gesture in which the face is washed or some water is sprinkled on the forehead."
  (p. 216, folio not stated) "The riverbank is also used by male and female devotees for the pitr̥tarpaṇ
  (Hin. the ancestor worship) (Abadhut 1964, 197), and many come here to shave their heads as a sign of
  mourning after a family member has died (Abro 1991, 58)." (p. 216, folio not stated) The twig rite:
  "the pilgrims use the nīm or babūl twig (Hin. dātun) to clean their teeth after they have bathed in
  the river (Shastri 1977, 51). After this, they give a donation (Hin. dān-dakṣiṇā) to the chaṛīdār…
  After the donation, the twig is then dug into the earth on the other side of the Hingol." (p. 216,
  folio not stated) Abadhut's variant uses two twigs (Abadhut 1964, 196) (p. 216, folio not stated).
  Kshatriya's variant makes it an oracle of partnership: "Each pilgrim picks a twig and digs it into the
  earth while saying the name of his or her chosen śaraṇ'bhāī. On the way back from the tīrtha, the
  pilgrims must pass by the twigs again. Only when the twigs have started to blossom can the pilgrim be
  sure that his or her choice of śaraṇ'bhāi has been accepted by the Goddess (Kshatriya 2005, 77–78).
  This concept of the śaraṇ'bhāī is not bound to any specific gender constraints and is often taken very
  seriously by the pilgrims." (p. 216, folio not stated)
- **Lohana life-cycle rites at the river.** "The Hingol is also of special importance for certain parts
  of the Lohana community that perform the ceremonies of the jane'o (Urd.), the bestowing of the sacred
  thread, and the munḍan pāṭh (Urd.), the shaving of the head, at the shore of the river (Jethamalani
  1994, 7). Many Sindhi Lohana communities wear this string of cloth (Burton 1851, 315), and some
  perform a ceremony at the River Hingol for young males being initiated into the community. The age of
  those to be initiated is not fixed, and many young Lohanas undergo the ceremony just before their
  wedding." (p. 216, folio not stated) "In general, however, most members of the Lasi Lohana community
  perform their rituals at the River Hingol. Similar to other paṛāv, so too at the Hingol it is mainly
  the ritual practice of the Lasi Lohana community that is promoted by the HSM and that increasingly
  prevails among visitors." (p. 216, folio not stated)
- **Animal sacrifice at the Kali Mata shrine, its history and its suppression.** "There are numerous
  sources indicating that animal sacrifice was once a commonly practiced part of the ritual activities
  inside the valley, but it was restricted to the area of this shrine only. Hart (1839) and Goldsmid
  (1861) write that in the nineteenth century, pilgrims would sacrifice goats the day before finally
  proceeding to the shrine of Hinglaj." (p. 221, folio not stated) "The amount of time required for the
  blood to dry on the warm sandstone was then interpreted as a sign of the Goddess's satisfaction or
  dissatisfaction with the sacrifice (Hart 1839, 95)." (p. 222, folio not stated) A witnessed survival:
  "I witnessed the sacrifice of a rooster during one of the festivals, the blood of which was thrown
  into the cavity on top of the shrine by a group of Devipujak-Vagris from Karachi (see Chapter 6)."
  (p. 222, folio not stated) Who did the killing: "In the nineteenth century, the pilgrims' sacrificial
  goats were slaughtered by local Muslims, very likely belonging to the Zikri Muslim community, who,
  according to Hart, also kept the carcasses (Hart 1839, 95). It is not clear how long this tradition
  continued or how strictly it was practiced, so one can only speculate if this was a special
  arrangement or not." (p. 222, folio not stated) Today: "the practice of animal sacrifice, or bali, is
  ideologically charged and is banned during the festivals. Certain members of the HSM have also
  expressed their wish that the tradition be discontinued completely." (pp. 222–223, folios not stated)
  "In any case, these days, most pilgrims opt to offer coconuts at the Kali temple instead of animals."
  (p. 223, folio not stated)
- **Hair offering as a stated motive for pilgrimage** — Thaomal's, as Hart reports it: "his main reason
  for undertaking the pilgrimage was to have his son's head shaven at Ashapura paṛāv and to offer the
  hair to the Goddess (Hart 1839, 94)" (p. 220, folio not stated).
- **The WAPDA dam and the state's retreat, dated.** "In 2008, the Balochistan Assembly abandoned a plan
  put forward by the Water and Power Development Authority of Pakistan (WAPDA) to build a dam in the
  Hingol River close to the MCH (Lakhavat 2011, 147–148). The dam would have flooded the accommodation
  roads to the temple and endangered the locality and its associated festivals. So the WAPDA initially
  suggested relocating three holy places to a higher elevation and guaranteed the construction of a new
  access road.20 This proposition was rejected by the HSM" (p. 217, folio not stated). "In 2008, the
  lawmakers in the Balochistan Assembly reacted to the concerns and protests of the Hindu community and
  asked the federal government to stop the project. In 2009, after a year of suspension, the WAPDA chose
  to continue with the controversial plans but decided to shift the site of the dam a few kilometers
  north in order to protect the temple. This resolution was in keeping with a consensus between the
  WAPDA, the Balochistan Assembly, and the Hindu community." (p. 217, folio not stated) **The three
  holy places are not named**: **(not stated)**.
- **State funding of the site.** "many buildings inside the Hinglaj Valley were financed by the
  provincial government of Balochistan" (p. 226, folio not stated); the Hingol bridge was "funded by the
  Balochistan government" (p. 215, folio not stated); against which the HSM says the temple's own
  reconstruction "was supported mainly by donations from the Hindu community" (p. 226, folio not
  stated).
- **The statue programme as an instrument of interpretation.** The HSM says the Ashapura statue "was
  built by the HSM itself at the end of the 1990s", replacing "a small stone at that location, smeared
  with siṃdūr (vermilion), which was once worshipped as the Goddess" (p. 220, folio not stated); the
  Hanuman statue was built there "so that people would know who is worshipped there." (p. 220, folio
  not stated) "I want to add that this is not the only example where the HSM erects a statue of a
  certain deity for the sake of telling people 'who is worshipped there.'" (p. 221, folio not stated)
- **Institutional names as the chunk gives them**: **HSM** (never expanded in this chunk: **(not
  stated)**), also called **the Mandali**, described as "the temple committee" and "the temple
  organization" (pp. 209, 215, 219, 220, folios not stated); **MCH**, expanded once as "the Makran
  Coastal Highway" (p. 220, folio not stated); **WAPDA**, expanded as "the Water and Power Development
  Authority of Pakistan" (p. 217, folio not stated); **the Balochistan Assembly** and **the Balochistan
  police** (pp. 217, 218, folios not stated).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Gorakhnath and the limestone**, attributed to Lakhavat: the piled khic'ṛī turned to limestone, and
  "anyone who returned from Hinglaj wearing a necklace of these limestone pebbles would be freed from
  the fetters of this world (Lakhavat 2011, 35)" (p. 206, folio not stated).
- **Parvati, the demon and the defiled khic'ṛī**, attributed to Briggs: "Shiva and Parvati were on their
  way to Hinglaj, and they stopped for a rest in the area of Thatta… a demon approached Parvati and
  tried to attack her, but Parvati killed the demon with a trident… he told Parvati to throw away the
  khic'ṛī. Thus, the grains of the food turned into the limestone found at Thatta today (Briggs 1938,
  104–105)." (p. 206, folio not stated)
- **The eighty-four Chandra-Kups**, quoted from Hughes, *The Country of Baluchistan, Its Geography,
  Topography, Ethnology and History*: "In the 'Sat Jug,' or golden age, Suda Shiwa, or Siva, and
  Parbatti, his wife, visited the Makli hills near Tatta… He arrived at Hinglaj, but could not find the
  Devi there. It is said that she took the shape of a fly, and so remained on the ball of ashes which
  Siva held in his hand. For twelve years he searched for her, but in vain, when, in a paroxysm of rage
  he dashed the ball to the ground, where it lay broken in eighty-four pieces. These eighty-four parts
  of the ball became the hills known as 'Chandra-Kups.' (Hughes 1877, 142)" (p. 214, folio not stated).
- **The eighteen Koops**, quoted from Hart's 1839 travelogue: "'After the abduction of Seeta by Rawan,'
  [sic] among others engaged in endeavouring to discover the place of her concealment was Sedasheo (a
  form of Mahadeo.) For twelve years did he unceasingly prosecute the search without success… he dashed
  his 'Biboot' (cake made of the fine ashes of cow-dung, for rubbing on the neck and arms, or marking
  the forehead) on the ground. It split into eighteen pieces, and formed as many Koops. Seeta, in the
  form of Shree Mata instantly appeared… she ordained that previous to visiting her Temple, every
  pilgrim should pay his devotions at one of the 'Koops,' which Sedasheo named, after her husband 'Rama
  Chandra,' and his spirit is supposed to inhabit them. (Hart 1839, 88f.)" (pp. 214–215, folios not
  stated). **The `[sic]` is the book's own.** The author's comment: "While this story clearly associates
  the mud volcano with the story of Rama and Sita, today, stories involving the prince from Ayodhya and
  his wife are rarely found in relation to the volcano." (p. 215, folio not stated)
- **The coconut augury**, attributed to tradition: "According to tradition, the number of bubbles that
  rise, together with the intensity of the gas emission in response to the offering, defines the
  pilgrim's destiny." (p. 212, folio not stated) "Abadhut mentions that the offerings must submerge into
  the mud as a sign that Baba Chandrakup has accepted the oblation; however, this practice is not
  consistently followed.16 Even Hart indicates that at the time of his own journey, the offerings did
  not sink due to the thickness of the mud (Hart, cited in Hughes 1877, 145)." (p. 213, folio not
  stated)
- **The unforgiven pilgrim left to die**, attributed to Abadhut and Shastri: "According to the bubbling
  of the mud and the reaction of the wind, the chaṛīdār is able to tell if the pilgrim's sins are
  forgiven (Shastri 1977, 41). If anyone is unwilling to confess (or if his or her transgressions are
  too cruel to be forgiven), then the bubbles stop rising and the offering is not accepted (Lakhavat
  2011, 138). According to Abadhut and Shastri, in such situations, the yātrī is not allowed to proceed
  on the journey and must be left there in the desert to die (Abadhut 1964, 204; Shastri 1977, 41)."
  (p. 213, folio not stated)
- **The bubbling water and the call on Mātā**, quoted by Briggs from Haji Abdula Nabi and referred to
  "the Chaurasi or Anil Kund" rather than to Chandrakup: the water "rises up at times with a bubbling
  noise, discolored like a river fresh swollen after rain, and carrying mud in suspension. The Hindu
  pilgrims, when this takes place, throw in betel-nuts, cloves, cardamoms and coconut. Should there be
  delay in the rising, pilgrims in the most abject manner will call on Mātā to give them a sight of
  herself, exhorting each other to reveal their sins and inwardly repent. (Briggs 1938, 108)" (p. 213,
  folio not stated).
- **Chandrakup as a wishing well**, attributed to Brohi: "Brohi writes that Chandrakup today is used as
  a wishing well where pilgrims ask the residing deity for favors (Brohi 1998, 32–33)." (p. 212, folio
  not stated)
- **The Goddess Ashapura welcoming pilgrims**, attributed to Dayaram Kapri: "The Goddess Ashapura used
  to sit there to welcome the pilgrims. Since at this point, the devotees' wish to accomplish the yātrā
  was finally fulfilled and the shrine of Hinglaj was near, the Goddess took the form of Ashapura to
  congratulate them. In memoriam of the days when the Goddess still made herself visible in this way to
  the pilgrims, a statue of Ashapura was erected there by a Gujarati businessman." (p. 220, folio not
  stated) **The businessman is not named**: **(not stated)**.
- **The camels' swelling feet**, attributed to Abro: "Another legend says that camels are not allowed to
  cross into the valley since their feet would swell from the presence of the Goddess (Abro 1991, 58)."
  (p. 221, folio not stated)
- **The chaṛī's protective power**: each stick "had the power to protect the group on their dangerous
  journey, provided they properly worshipped it on the way" (p. 207, folio not stated).
- **The blossoming twig as the Goddess's consent** to a chosen śaraṇ'bhāī (Kshatriya 2005, 77–78)
  (p. 216, folio not stated).
- **The snake as a form of the Goddess**: at the Jagnath temple "On the inner temple wall is an image of
  a snake, a representation of one of the Goddess's many forms." (p. 207, folio not stated)
- **Lalu Jasraj at Liari**, a narrative the author found thin: "Within another corpus of narratives
  concerning the shrine, Liari is also associated with the story of Lalu Jasraj (Hart 1839, 80),9 the
  chaṛīdār (Hin. stickbearer) of Rama and his entourage. However, I came across this narrative mainly
  during my conversations with Dayaram Kapri from Gujarat… who claimed that this reference is found in
  the Hiṃgulā Purāṇ. Unfortunately, I was not able to locate the story there, and only a few of my other
  informants in Pakistan mentioned this myth about Liari, so it is questionable how widespread it is
  outside the Gujarati Kapri community." (p. 209, folio not stated)

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The pilgrimage's stops are historically contingent, and the author dates the contingency.** Of
  Liari: "Judging from these historical accounts, it is evident that Liari was included in the southern
  route only fairly recently. This supposition is also supported by Samaur, who writes that pilgrims
  used to travel the road via Sonmiani, but currently people take the route via Liari (Samaur 1999,
  504f.)." (p. 207, folio not stated) He explains the older silence as an artefact of where the sources
  started: "Since all of the sources mentioned above traveled to Hinglaj from Karachi, it comes as no
  surprise that they would not have considered the stop." (p. 207, folio not stated)
- **Motifs float between sites because the network was not institutionalised.** "This association of
  certain motifs—here, the delay in the rising of bubbles and the renunciation of sins—with different
  places is paradigmatic for the pilgrimage network where, due to the lack of institutionalization, such
  attributes were for a long time not fully attached to certain places." (p. 213, folio not stated)
- **The roads shrank the sacred geography.** "While traditional travelogues and accounts of pilgrims
  walking to the shrine generally demonstrate an extended conception of the Goddess's sacred
  geography,22 the disappearance of the old pilgrimage paths led to the elimination of many stops en
  route and subsequently also to a change in the scale and perception of Hinglaj's 'imagined landscape'
  (Eck 1998). This development reflects a general shift from the kinesthetic experience of the
  pilgrimage route to a more 'place-centered' or 'sedentary' tradition in which the valley and its
  localized rituals have become increasingly prominent." (p. 217, folio not stated)
- **The wall and the signs both produce and are produced by sacred space**, argued through Knott, who is
  quoted: "[r]itual practice itself is interesting when seen from the perspective of spatial practice as
  it is none other than spatial practice transformed by religious meaning, and often—though not
  always—performed in the context of a space set apart as sacred and by an appropriate ritual
  practitioner. (Knott 2005, 43)" (p. 218, folio not stated). The author's own formulation: "In this
  way, the wall, together with these signs posted in the valley, do both: they contribute to producing
  the valley's space as religious space by regulating its activities, but they are simultaneously also a
