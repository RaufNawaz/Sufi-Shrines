## Arguments and interpretations

- **The book's key concept, defined here.** "During the course of this process, certain practices at
  Hinglaj became labeled as the 'real,' 'true,' or 'proper' ways to engage with the Goddess, whereas
  others were termed as 'fake' or 'notional.' In this book, I call these intricate dynamics of inclusion
  and exclusion 'a solidification of tradition,' and one of my major goals is to utilize this concept for
  my case study." (p. 29, folio not stated)
- **The chapter's governing question, asked twice.** "How is it possible to organize a field of voices in
  which everyone lays claim to speak 'the truth' about the Goddess Hinglaj?" (p. 29, folio not stated);
  "How is it possible to organize a plurality of overlapping—but also opposing—truth-claims visible in
  myriad interviews, texts, or videos?" (p. 31, folio not stated)
- **"Truth-claims" defined.** "In the following, I will call such references, which define what Hinglaj is
  through exclusion, that is, through what Hinglaj is not—'truth-claims.' Such truth-claims are an
  important characteristic of many (learned and/or devoted) opinions on Hinglaj." (pp. 30–31, folios not
  stated)
- **The evidence base, stated by the author.** "In many of the sources I analyzed, whether written or
  oral, claims about Hinglaj were often introduced with words such as the 'real,' the 'actual,' or the
  'proper' meaning of the tradition. Many authors and interviewees sought to point out some 'true'
  practice at the shrine or an 'actual' story behind certain events." (p. 30, folio not stated) Chapter 6
  rests on "my own extensive ethnographic material" (p. 27, folio not stated); the newspaper figures are
  "from my own study of minority-issue reporting in English newspapers" over "1987–2011" (p. 28, folio not
  stated). **No fieldwork dates, no interview counts and no informant names appear in this chunk** — the
  one dated interview is given as "a 2010 interview with a Pakistani-Hindu devotee" (p. 31, folio not
  stated), unnamed: **(not stated)**.
- **The two-step theoretical move the chapter makes**, announced at p. 31 and executed over pp. 31–45:
  "First, I will engage with the so-called ontological turn, which promises a novel approach to a
  multitude of truth—or ontological—claims. In a second step, I will engage with the discipline of
  'post-foundationalism' to emphasize how such claims are produced through hegemony and political
  struggle." (p. 31, folio not stated)
- **The ontological turn, adopted in part.** Its appeal is to "take others and their real difference
  seriously" (Candea in Carrithers et al. 2010, 175) and to "describe the ontologies of non-Western
  peoples without recourse (or with as little recourse as possible) to our own ontological assumptions"
  (Course 2010, 247) (p. 32, folio not stated). Applied to the case: "The ontological turn, thus, shows
  how a variety of practices produce the shrine and its tradition instead of merely providing us with
  different opinions on an object 'out there.' Instead of only one Hinglaj that is discovered by the
  anthropologist through assembling and evaluating various truth-claims, we now need to speak of many
  Hinglajs and many ritual journeys produced out of a multitude of truth-claims." (pp. 33–34, folios not
  stated) — sharpened with a bracketed substitution into Law and Lien's sentence: "different and multiple
  [Hinglajs] subsist in different and multiple worlds" (Law and Lien 2013, 4) (p. 34, folio not stated).
- **The ontological turn, then refused.** "If there are ontologies in plural, are we not creating an
  ontology of plural ontologies—a meta-ontology? Furthermore, how can these ontologies be differentiated
  if there is no meta-ontology providing their differentiating principle? In other words, why are
  ontologies multiple yet not arbitrary?" (p. 34, folio not stated) "the discipline fails to further
  engage with the notion of multiple ontologies, a step that, even though it aims to break with
  foundational claims, produces yet another one—this time the claim that ontologies, in fact, are
  multiple." (p. 34, folio not stated) **He keeps its performativity and drops its flatness.**
- **Post-foundationalism, adopted as the method.** It "puts forward a theory of hegemony and political
  struggle explaining the production and interaction of multiple truth-claims by rendering them as 'nodal
  points'" and "avoids the highly charged term 'ontology' and its recent inflationary use for the sake of
  the concept 'foundational'" (p. 35, folio not stated). Its paradox: "the absence of an uncontested
  foundation makes repeated attempts to establish such a singular and unified foundation necessary" (p.
  35, folio not stated); "the absence of an ultimate foundation leads to its necessary production
  (Marchart 2010, 63–64)" (p. 36, folio not stated).
- **Derrida applied directly to the Hinglaj archive — the single most useful passage in the chunk for a
  researcher.** "Derrida's first argument relates to the impossibility of any subject position to master a
  field's empirical richness. Applied to this case, no research, no institution, and no individual would
  ever be able to overlook the totality of information that has ever been produced on the Goddess Hinglaj
  and her tradition." (p. 36, folio not stated) "even if we were able to cover the totality of voices
  engaged with the shrine at Hinglaj and its practices, no situation would be imaginable in which they all
  would agree on one central element as its undisputable axis mundi." (p. 36, folio not stated)
- **The methodological payoff, stated plainly.** "Translating this into a methodology for this work shifts
  my attention to the history of truth-claims and their contingent foundations. Because the establishment
  of undisputed foundations is impossible, however, dynamics at the shrine can only be identified as
  processes of solidifying such foundations. Due to the relatively recent dislocation of the tradition,
  the contingency within many of these truth-claims is conspicuous and so is the attempt to objectivize or
  solidify them." (p. 37, folio not stated)
- **What the book explicitly does NOT claim** — worth carrying into any entry that cites this book: "I do
  not claim, therefore, the absence of a homogeneous Hindu tradition at the shrine but rather show how the
  production of precisely such a unified identity has repeatedly been attempted by many actors." (p. 37,
  folio not stated) Restated: "the events at the shrine are not only a proof for the absence of a
  homogeneous (Pakistani-) Hindu identity but also show the various ways in which the creation of
  precisely such a unity is attempted by ways of exclusion." (p. 43, folio not stated)
- **The method's scope condition, in the author's own words:** "this methodology is particularly useful in
  cases where traditions have recently undergone significant changes and are now in the process of
  re-evaluating their central concepts." (p. 38, folio not stated) **This is the sentence that tells a
  future user of this book which other shrines the framework is claimed to travel to.**
- **Laclau and Mouffe's apparatus as the book uses it** — articulation, discourse, moments, elements (the
  block quotation from Laclau and Mouffe 2001, 105, pp. 38–39, folios not stated); nodal points as
  Lacanian "point de capiton", "Often translated as a 'quilting point'… the fixation button in upholstery,
  which shapes and grips the fabric underneath" (p. 40, folio not stated); and the bridge to the case:
  "Similar to how nodal points structure their discursive environment, truth-claims, too, aim to organize
  the field of possibilities around them by excluding ambivalence and polysemy." (p. 41, folio not stated)
- **Logic of equivalence / logic of difference, and why groups need enemies.** "The representation of any
  group (and groups are also a particular organization of symbols and representations) requires the
  establishment of at least one 'anti-group' or 'enemy' so that its key concepts may be organized… the
  identity of the chain of equivalence is not provided by some common denominator that is found in its
  equivalent elements but rather through its border, that is, through a common negation of some excluded
  other." (p. 41, folio not stated) **The author applies this reading to caste and religious community
  directly:** "This rejection of any given foundation within a society can also be applied to supposedly
  'fixed' identities such as castes or religious communities." (p. 38, folio not stated)
- **Hegemony as the substitute for the absent foundation**, from Gramsci's "power over 'subordinate
  classes' through combining coercion and persuasion (Simon 1991, 24)" (p. 41, folio not stated), freed by
  Laclau and Mouffe of "class consciousness and the singularity of hegemonic centers" (p. 41, folio not
  stated), to "hegemony needs to take its place as a de facto objectivity" (p. 42, folio not stated).
- **Materiality defended against the charge that discourse theory is only about words** — the Laclau and
  Mouffe block quotation on the earthquake and the falling brick, whose specificity "as 'natural
  phenomena' or 'expressions of the wrath of God,' depends upon the structuring of a discursive field"
  (Laclau and Mouffe 2001, 108) (p. 42, folio not stated). **A theological example chosen deliberately,
  and the hinge to the book's later treatment of landslides and renovations at the shrine (p. 27, folio
  not stated).**
- **Sedimentation, and the author's criticism of it.** Husserl's concept via Laclau — routinisation in
  which "the original intuition which gave rise to them is completely forgotten" (Laclau 1990, 34) (p. 43,
  folio not stated); "Through the repetition of this exclusion, truth-claims… become historic facts, and
  their original contingent foundation is covered up (Bergunder 2014, 266–269)" (p. 44, folio not stated);
  "the sedimentation of power relations leads to the construction of objectivity (Jørgensen and Phillips
  2002, 38)" (p. 44, folio not stated). **Schaflechner's own verdict on his borrowed tool:** "Laclau fails
  to clarify what he means by a tendency to objectivize, and at no point is a detailed description of the
  dimensions and texture of sedimentation given." (pp. 44–45, folios not stated) "All of these attempts
  show that the Laclauian theory of sedimentation lacks in explanatory force and, hence, needs to be
  adapted to each individual case study." (p. 45, folio not stated) **He is building his own concept
  because he judges the borrowed one insufficient — this is why "solidification" exists as a term.**
- **Butler on sedimentation**, the last argument before the chunk breaks: sedimentation "describes the
  production of foundations, which themselves are the outcome of prior sedimentations… Every sedimentation
  is, therefore, always already a second sedimentation (Marchart 2013b, 192)—which makes its origin a moot
  point." (p. 45, folio not stated) With the same criticism levelled: "Similar to Laclau, Butler, too,
  omits the question of when exactly we are able to speak of sedimented structures" (p. 45, folio not
  stated).
