## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **(no id — Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir)** (the book's names for it: "the shrine of
  Hinglaj", "the Goddess's abode", "the mountain at Hinglaj", "the Hinglaj temple in Balochistan",
  "her shrine in Balochistan", "the main shrine in Balochistan", "the shrine of Hinglaj/Nani in
  Balochistan"; the deity is "the Goddess Hinglaj", "Hinglaj Devi", "Hinglaj Mata", "Mata Hinglaj",
  "the Mother", "the Devi", "the Mahashakti Hinglaj"):
  - **Initiation precedes the journey.** In the Brahmakshatriya origin story, Dadhichi tells the five
    princes that "the five princes must go to the shrine of Hinglaj to be initiated into her
    tradition. So the princes fast, become initiated into the mantra of the Goddess Hinglaj, and then
    set out on the pilgrimage to the Goddess's abode." (p. 64, folio not stated)
  - **The route, as the story gives it** (p. 64, folio not stated): "they cross the River Gandki and
    then travel on to Narayanpur to worship the Goddess Ashapura. Dadhichi and the princes next pass
    through Nagar Sthan [Thatta], reaching the five wells; they then proceed to Chandrakup, where
    they take a bath and present a gift of 60,000 cows to the local Brahmins. Later they reach
    Karavirak, in the middle of the Hingol River, where they fast and meditate for six years." The
    bracketed gloss "[Thatta]" is the author's. Then: "they eventually ascend the mountain at Hinglaj,
    where they fast and meditate for another twelve years." (p. 64, folio not stated)
  - **What the Goddess grants and withholds.** She refuses to undo Parashurama's curse — "How could
    the words of the mighty Parashurama become a lie? But I will help you and do what is possible" —
    then "instructs the princes in worshipping her as their clan Goddess [Hin. kul'devī] and adds that
    he who enters her cave will gain the dharma of an ascetic. She furthermore advises them to always
    worship the Saraswat Brahmins." She promises that their descendants "would be prosperous and
    fortunate in the future" even after they forget the art of weaponry (p. 64, folio not stated).
  - **Hinglaj as the point where fate can be bent**: "The pilgrimage of Hinglaj also plays a
    significant role within this plot: it is here where the divine and the mundane meet and where the
    humans can exercise some agency over their fate, eventually even altering the course of the
    curse." (p. 66, folio not stated)
  - **Hinglaj as caste guarantor**: "The Goddess Hinglaj serves here as the community's creator and as
    the guarantor of 'Kshatriyadom.'" (p. 68, folio not stated)
  - **The Charan bond and its obligation.** "The Charan in particular claim to have a special bond
    with Hinglaj in Balochistan, and the pilgrimage to her shrine at some point in one's life is
    mandatory for many of them (Westphal and Westphal-Hellbusch 1974, 311)." (p. 69, folio not stated)
    "Charans often say that when a boy is born into the community, a bell rings at the Hinglaj temple
    in Balochistan. Furthermore, it is the responsibility of every Charan male to visit the Goddess at
    Hinglaj before his 60th birthday; otherwise a rock will tumble down into the sacred valley,
    eventually burying the shrine." (p. 70, folio not stated)
  - **Hinglaj inside the Charani Sagati, and contested there.** "according to Charan narratives,
    Hinglaj herself was once a Charani (Samaur 1999, 503). In fact, she is said to have been the first
    of countless incarnations of the Goddess in this world." (p. 70, folio not stated) Kamphorst,
    quoted: "it is the Mahashakti Hinglaj herself who is credited with the heroic deeds of all her
    avatārīs. (Kamphorst 2008, 244)" (p. 70, folio not stated). But: "the notion that Hinglaj is
    associated with the circle of Charani Sagati is also contested among Charans today. While some
    are proud to include Hinglaj into this circle of incarnations… others are of the opinion that
    Hinglaj is the ādiśakti, the primordial divine power that can only ever partially incarnate into
    this mundane world." (p. 71, folio not stated)
  - **A "historical" Hinglaj, in three incompatible accounts.** Samaur: her origins lie in the
    "gaur'viyā cāraṇ" branch from Thatta, "her father's name was Haridas", and that Thatta connection
    made the saṃnyāsīs of the Charan and the Udasin community hold "that the temple in Thatta was as
    important as the main shrine in Balochistan (Samaur 1999, 503)" (p. 71, folio not stated).
    P. P. Payaka, in reproduction: "Hala in Sindh was called Kohala in the former times, and the
    Goddess Hinglaj was once the queen of the Kohala or Kohana area. In this account, Hinglaj is
    portrayed as a Charani and the leader of the Tumbel Charan, whom she guided from Sindh to the
    Makran. P. P. Payaka also mentions that she remained a virgin all her life and was well versed in
    the religious texts (Westphal and Westphal-Hellbusch 1974, 315)." (p. 71, folio not stated)
    Kamphorst: "the woman's name was 'Charani Deval'", dated to the eighth century (p. 71, folio not
    stated).
  - **How the shrine came to be where it is, per Kamphorst**: "the Tumbel spread the Goddess's fame in
    Las Bela, and so Hinglaj decided to settle in a cave close to the Hingol River, where she has been
    worshipped by the Charan and other communities since as early as the ninth century (Kamphorst
    2008, 237)." (p. 71, folio not stated)
  - **Dating of the cult, left open by the author.** Tambs-Lyche "could have been a part of a cult
    that originally began in the fifth century", on Colonel Tod, "but also acknowledges that Tod's
    dating tends to be too early"; with the Devī Māhātmaya reaching Rajasthan "in the sixth or seventh
    century", "a rough estimate between the fifth and seventh century could be an accurate guess
    (Tambs-Lyche 1999, 30n7)" (pp. 71–72, folios not stated). The author's own summary: the cult "was
    already active between the seventh and ninth centuries, possibly incorporating rituals that might
    have already existed at the shrine of Hinglaj/Nani in Balochistan. Unfortunately, no definite
    proof is available." (p. 74, folio not stated) **"Hinglaj/Nani" is the only alternative name the
    chunk gives the shrine itself.**
  - **Hinglaj as ādiśakti and as the anchor of the regional Goddess map**: "the Goddess Hinglaj is
    understood by some as the first incarnation, the ādiśakti, and thus the center for many variations
    of Goddess worship in western India (Tambs-Lyche 2004, 64)"; "these various local incarnations
    became anchored to the shrine of Hinglaj and further interpreted in its light. With the
    establishment of these links to the place in Balochistan, a continuous history was created that is
    reiterated and perpetuated today in the stories and the poetry of the bards." (p. 74, folio not
    stated)
  - **The Kapri relation.** "The Encyclopedia of Religion and Ethics only mentions that the Kapris are
    followers of the Goddess Ashapura and that they frequently visit the shrine of Hinglaj in
    Balochistan (Hastings and Selbie 2003, 716)." (p. 75, folio not stated) "For the Kapri community,
    anyone who emerges from Hinglaj's cave in Balochistan is automatically initiated into the group
    and can assume the title 'Kapri.'" (p. 75, folio not stated) Depavat: "everyone who comes out of
    the Goddess's womb (Hin. garbha) also attains the title 'kāpaḍiyā' (Depavat 2010, 147)"; Kamphorst:
    "In this way all travellers become religious brothers and assume the title Kapadiya" (Kamphorst
    2008, 238) (p. 75, folio not stated).
  - **Rama's pilgrimage as the charter.** "The sage Vashishta advised Rama to go on the pilgrimage to
    Hinglaj, which was the only place where he could be purified of his horrible offense." (p. 76,
    folio not stated) "The remaining ninety-six Brahmins performed a sacrifice for Rama and accepted
    Hinglaj as their clan Goddess." (p. 76, folio not stated)
  - **The ritual programme a pilgrim must complete**, from Mekan Dada's own words: "I will go to
    Chandrakup, I will climb up to Anil Kund, I will do the pūjā at your temple, I will put siṃdūr
    [Hin. vermilion] on the place where Rama sat down, and I will see the moon and the sun carved into

    home." (p. 78, folio not stated) **This is the fullest inventory of shrine features the chunk

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Pilgrimage as initiation, twice over.** Into the Hinglaj tradition: the princes "fast, become
  initiated into the mantra of the Goddess Hinglaj" before departing (p. 64, folio not stated). Into a
  caste: "anyone who emerges from Hinglaj's cave in Balochistan is automatically initiated into the
  group and can assume the title 'Kapri'" (p. 75, folio not stated), and so "anybody professing Hindu
  religion can become a Kapadi" (Mohidden 2003, 603) (p. 75, folio not stated).
- **The limits of that initiation, from the author's fieldwork.** "people who come back from
  Balochistan only become 'half Kapris.' They are, for example, not allowed to marry into the
  community. The pilgrimage, however, does bring a certain amount of prestige and even certain lasting
  changes for the yātrīs. According to this tradition, even as 'half Kapris,' they are not burned on
  the pyre after death as they normally would be, but instead are buried in a samādhi (Hin. tomb)."
  (p. 79, folio not stated) **A change in funerary practice is the concrete, stated consequence of the
  pilgrimage.**
- **Mandatory pilgrimage with a deadline and a sanction** — every Charan male "before his 60th
  birthday; otherwise a rock will tumble down into the sacred valley, eventually burying the shrine"
  (p. 70, folio not stated).
- **Offerings and gifts recorded on the route** — "a gift of 60,000 cows to the local Brahmins" at
  Chandrakup (p. 64, folio not stated); siṃdūr placed "on the place where Rama sat down" (p. 78, folio
  not stated); the Goddess gives Mekan "a triśūla and a hat with a ṭhum'rā wrapped around it" (p. 78,
  folio not stated).
- **Shrine governance.** "the temple committee, the Hinglaj Sheva Mandali (HSM)" and "the construction
  of the Makran Coastal Highway (MCH), which incidentally connects Hinglaj with urban Pakistan", named
  as the two things through which Lohana authority over "the organization and the authority to
  interpret the traditions and practices at and to the shrine" "solidified" (p. 79, folio not stated).
- **Cross-border pilgrimage economy and the visa regime** — "each year, several groups from India
  manage to obtain visas and travel to Pakistan to visit the shrine" (p. 69, folio not stated);
  organisers such as Dayaram Kapri (p. 79, folio not stated); "so many low-caste Gujarati communities
  are drawn to make the journey to Hinglaj, even after the Partition" (p. 79, folio not stated).
- **Traga as a legal and credit instrument.** "Charans were also warrantors for contracts, guaranteeing
  adherence to certain agreements between two parties with their lives. This was possible by
  threatening to commit traga (self-immolation)… The threat of traga served as a guarantee for
  everyday agreements like loans or the settlement of disputes, but also for politically sensitive
  issues such as treaties made between two kings (Tambs-Lyche 2004)." (p. 69, folio not stated) "The
  ghosts of the deceased were feared, especially those of females, because they embodied the horrific
  aspect of the Goddess." (p. 69, folio not stated)
- **The bardic profession.** "Many Charan men were employed as bards and poets, serving kings and
  aristocrats as genealogists and, even more significantly, assembling and composing the histories of
  their masters' dynasties… Their poetry connected the rulers of their day to those of mythological
  times, placing current administrations inside a timeless dharma and thereby legitimizing the rulers'
  claims to power." (p. 69, folio not stated) "While the Charans legitimized Rajput authority by
  facilitating a connection to an eternal order, the Rajput were instrumental in the Charans' upward
  social mobility." (p. 70, folio not stated)
- **Apotheosis as an institution** — the Charani Sagati (Kamphorst 2008), "a tradition of apotheosis,
  that is, the deification of Charan women, which continues today" (p. 69, folio not stated); "many
  Charan women who were exceptionally honored during their lifetime would eventually become important
  local Goddesses in areas where Charans lived (Westphal and Westphal-Hellbusch 1976, 167)", and
  Enthoven, cited there: "[s]everal of the most popular goddesses of North Gujarat are the spirits of
  Charan woman who sacrificed themselves to guard the privileges of their caste" (p. 70, folio not
  stated).
- **The trades prescribed by a god.** Vishvakarman "instructs them in producing red color, printing
  cloth, and selling goods, with the exception of ghee, honey, and oil. He also tells them that
  although they can no longer use weapons themselves, they should now utilize their Kshatriya knowledge
  to sell them to others." (p. 65, folio not stated)
- **Caste mobility through the colonial census.** "the Khatris, among many other merchant communities,
  sought to improve their varṇa status by writing petitions to H. H. Risley, the commissioner in charge
  of the 1901 census" (p. 67, folio not stated); "the colonial commissioners ultimately recognized the
  Khatris as belonging to the Kshatriya varṇa", and Jareca's book "reprints one of H. H. Risley's
  letters in its entirety" (p. 68, folio not stated). The Khatris were "originally described as members
  of the Vaishya varṇa (Baines 1893, 201)" (p. 67, folio not stated).
- **Brahmins for hire.** Rowe, cited: during upward mobility "many—often 'low-ranking'—Brahmins had a
  symbiotic relationship with the castes seeking to alter their status, especially the Khatri
  community. Such Brahmin groups supported claims of higher lineage in the form of 'learned opinions'
  (Rowe 2007, 203)." (p. 67, folio not stated)
- **The community book as an object with a funding model.** Brahmakṣatriyotpatti Evaṃ Māṁ Hiṃgulāj "was
  written and published by the author Bhamvarlal Jareca, who was supported by the donations of the
  Khatri community in Rajasthan"; it contains "devotional songs (Hin. bhajan) and ritual manuals for
  the worship of the Goddess (Jareca n.d., 116–137)" and reports on the "'brahmakṣatriya samāj,' an
  organization aiming to preserve the traditions of the Khatri community (Jareca n.d., 155)" (p. 67,
  folio not stated).
- **Annual gathering figures — for Temra Rai, not for Hinglaj.** The "42-kilometer-long
  circumambulation" draws "tens of thousands of people" (p. 74, folio not stated). **The chunk gives no

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- Indra, disguised as a bumblebee, stings Jayasena's thigh to expose him as a Kshatriya, and Jayasena
  "stabilizes his mind and ignores the pain" rather than wake Parashurama (p. 63, folio not stated) —
  told as the narrative of Jareca's book.
- Parashurama's curse: "You will forget all that I have taught you, and from now on, you and your
  ancestors will be known to the world as Brahmakshatriyas!" (p. 64, folio not stated)
- The Mother appears after twelve years' fasting on the mountain at Hinglaj and grants a wish, but
  refuses to unmake the curse (p. 64, folio not stated).
- The curse strikes generations later in battle: "their arrows begin turning into grasslike sticks"
  (p. 65, folio not stated).
- A bell rings at the Hinglaj temple in Balochistan whenever a Charan boy is born — "Charans often say"
  (p. 70, folio not stated).
- A rock will bury the shrine if a Charan man fails to come before his 60th birthday (p. 70, folio not
  stated).
- The Goddess "grants Charan men the ability to write and compose stories and poems. She is their muse,
  and they imagine her sitting on a poet's tongue, quickening his creativity." (p. 70, folio not stated)
- The Devi "promised to incarnate as a Charan woman periodically in order to bring change and prosperity
  to the community" (p. 70, folio not stated).
- Avar Mata reveals herself as the Goddess, kills "Bakha, the buffalo demon", and the taste of its blood
  "sent the Goddess flying into a frenzy" (p. 72, folio not stated); she kills fifty-two Hun-demons
  (p. 72, folio not stated).
- Karni Mata "miraculously drew the bows of the army of Bikaner, allowing them to defeat their enemies
  from a safe distance (Kamphorst 2008, 248)" (p. 73, folio not stated); the Goddess appeared to Karni's
  mother "While Karni's father was undertaking the pilgrimage to Hinglaj" (p. 73, folio not stated).
- Mekan Dada's buried pilgrim clothes, laid down in a former life as Lakshmana at "the village Kombhri,
  in Nakatrana", and rediscovered when his parents dig where he forbade (pp. 76–77, folios not stated) —
  "paraphrases from interviews conducted with members of the Kapri community in 2010 and in 2011"
  (p. 76, folio not stated).
- The milk miracle: the Goddess, "disguised as a normal shepherd, wearing a Balochi dress and accompanied
  by a herd of goats she had created through her mantra power", cannot fill Mekan's cup, "so that a
  hundred thousand liters came out, but still she could not fill the glass" (pp. 77–78, folios not
  stated); she then grants him the guruship of all who come to her place (p. 78, folio not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The narrative's social work.** "the story of the five princes explains the rupture between the
  glorious past and the present state of the (Brahma) Khatri community… their fall from the ruling class
  to the status of (mere) traders is not the community's fault but rather the will of the gods… The story
  assures that the Brahma Khatris are both mighty kings by blood and prosperous merchants by the will of
  the gods." (p. 66, folio not stated)
- **Local adaptation of a pan-Indian theme**, with Gail quoted: "The content of the Parashurama story is
  a clear case in point of how an old and formerly local story remains alive by moving through India
  being adapted to various local traditions" (Gail 1977, 206) (p. 65, folio not stated).
- **Foucault's genealogy as the method.** The author applies "Foucault's reading of Nietzsche" from
  "Nietzsche, Genealogy, History" (1977): "Instead of beginning at some distant point in the past and
  trying to follow its traces into the present, Foucault suggests analyzing how supposed past events are
  rearticulated to fit into a present framework", against "a search for unknown 'chimeras of origin'
  (Foucault 1977, 144)" (p. 66, folio not stated), with the block quotation at Foucault 1977, 146
  (pp. 66–67, folios not stated). Consequence: "the work containing the text about the Brahma Khatris'
  origin becomes central to the narrative's analysis" (p. 67, folio not stated).
- **The text as a "learned opinion", offered as a hypothesis and marked as such.** "it is possible that
  the aforementioned text centering around the Goddess Hinglaj was produced for this exact purpose—as a
  'learned opinion' in this time of upward social mobility. Taken as a given, this would also explain the
  repeated demand found in the texts—uttered by different deities such as Hinglaj or Vishvakarman—to
  always respect the Saraswat Brahmins." (p. 68, folio not stated) The author also states the limit: "A
  definite answer as to the presented mytho-historic text's exact date of production… cannot be offered
  here" (p. 68, folio not stated).
- **Two authorities braced together.** "the ongoing discussion about the 'highly speculative'
  Khatri/Brahmakshatria identity (Singh 1998, 1721) is connected to two powerful and authoritative
  entities: Hinglaj, as divine assurance for the community's aristocratic bloodline (Foucault's
  metaphysical ground of history par excellence) and Risley, as the census commissioner for the British
  Raj." (p. 68, folio not stated)
- **A source claim the author refuses to accept.** "The association is bolstered through the claim that
  this narrative is part of the eighteen Purāṇas, namely the Skanda Purāṇa; I have not been able to
  verify this claim." (p. 65, folio not stated)
- **A published error corrected.** Mohidden's "Hingadajki", placed by that author "somewhere in today's
  Saudi Arabia": "It is very likely that this is simply a misreading of 'Hinglaj.'" (p. 75, folio not
  stated)
- **A gap in the literature stated outright.** "aside from some short descriptions in a few publications,
  not much has been written specifically about the small Kapri community of Gujarat, and, thus, this
  chapter is mainly informed by oral histories gathered in India and Pakistan." (p. 75, folio not stated)
- **Devi traditions as a technology of dynastic legitimation.** "The mytho-historic narratives of the
  bards and the Rajput communities reveal a strong connection between the establishment of ruling
  dynasties in the area and the apotheosis of many Charan women… The deification of the Charani was often
  related to their social and material status and was closely linked to their relationships with local
  rulers." (p. 74, folio not stated) And on Avar: "The mytho-historic corpus around Avar Mata is a telling
  example for the general relationship between the local Charan communities and the former (Muslim) king…
  Friction, suppression, and eventual release are motifs found in many such stories revolving around west
  Indian Goddesses." (p. 73, folio not stated)
- **Pilgrimage as a route to social mobility, flagged forward.** "This interesting possibility of altering
  one's community background through the pilgrimage to Hinglaj—often used to gain prestige and upward
  social mobility in today's Gujarat—will be discussed in detail in Chapter 4." (p. 76, folio not stated)
  and again at p. 79 (folio not stated).
- **The Lohanas as the type of the Sindhi Hindu**, via two colonial witnesses: Burton — "a description of
  them [the Lohanas], therefore, will be a general portrait of the Sindh Hindoos" (Burton 1851, 314) — and
  McMurdo — "The Lowannas or Lohannas compose the great body of Hindus in Sindh" (Mubarak 1985, 55)
  (p. 79, folio not stated). The author qualifies: "In terms of ritual practices or sectarian interests,
  however, the Lohanas are quite a heterogeneous community, sharing few universal characteristics aside
  from the name." (p. 79, folio not stated)
