    settle.** The Hevajra Tantra, Sādhanamāla and Kālikā Purāṇa, "the oldest known texts dealing with

    folio not stated). `Kulārṇava Tantra`: "Hiṅgulā" is found there and, "when allegedly quoting the

    **Schaflechner refuses (a):** "I cannot share Agrawala's conviction about the location of the

  - **Pīṭhanirṇaya (PN) — the text on which the shrine's primacy rests.** "In the Pīṭhanirṇaya (PN), the

- **Custodianship is committee-based, not hereditary, on the evidence here.** The priest "gained his
  position as a pujārī through appointment by the local temple committee (the HSM) in 2008" (p. 47,

- **A ritual abolished by organisational decision, then re-read as religious.** Animal sacrifice inside
  the valley stopped for "organizational difficulties" and was banned officially "as recently as 2004";
  "Only after the ban was put into effect was the prohibition increasingly reinterpreted as a
  `religions`[text-layer?] [religious] decision and became an important part of the tradition." (p. 47,
  folio not stated)
- **The tunnel rite and its gatekeeping** — pilgrims "crawl through a small tunnel, signifying the womb
  of the Goddess from which they are born again"; entry is conditional on "a rigid pattern of
  purification and worship" (p. 57, folio not stated).
- **Pilgrimage as an organised tour with a state and media dimension.** The 2010 Gujarat party was
  organised in Pakistan by the HSM with a named organiser accompanying; "Local and national news picked
  up the story of the Indian pilgrims, and so two television teams came to the temple" (p. 48, folio not
  stated). **Mass media is here an active participant in shrine interpretation, not an observer.**
- **Security and the Hindu public sphere:** a Jinnah Institute official branded Hinglaj a "high risk for
  catastrophe" in 2012, yet "it is one of the few places in Pakistan that offers a relatively secure
  public space for a massive Hindu festival to occur" (p. 50, folio not stated).

  construction of a road—many powerful actors are currently in the process of producing an objective
  tradition at the site. An increasing institutionalization was conspicuously taking place." (p. 49,
  folio not stated) Earlier in the same section: "The already briefly introduced institutional and
  infrastructural changes around the shrine rather caused a variety of sedimented objectivities from
  different traditions to collide." (p. 46, folio not stated)
- **A devotional print economy in four vernaculars** — ritual manuals, mytho-historical narratives,
  travelogues, video documentations and institutions produced by Brahmakshatriya, Charan, Kapri and
  Lohana devotees (p. 58, folio not stated), with three specific books dated 1905 (Gujarati), 1973
  (Sindhi) and c. 2006 (Hindi) (p. 61, folio not stated), and a 1934 information-gathering date behind

  stated).
- **Colonial classification as an institution with material stakes.** Colonial law courts "decided

  bloodline of certain communities" (p. 60, folio not stated).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The second face in the effigy, told two ways by the same priest** — the demon Hingol, "a local
  tyrant killed by the Devi in mythological times" (2009); a vīr of Durga "who killed the demon
  Mahishasura" (2012). Attributed by the book to Gopal Maharaj in each case (p. 46, folio not stated).
- **The dismemberment of Sati, narrated at length as the myth "frequently heard at the shrine today"**
  (p. 55, folio not stated): the Goddess incarnates as Sati, daughter of king Daksha; marries Shiva
  against her father's will; Daksha performs "the aśvamedha (Skt.)" and invites every god "except
  Shiva"; Sati "jumped into the sacrificial fire"; Shiva kills Daksha, then dances "the tāṇḍava (Skt.),
  a frantic dance that threatened to destroy the whole world"; "the God Vishnu took his discus and cut
  Sati's body into many parts that fell out of Shiva's arms and scattered all over the subcontinent",
  and each landing place "became known as śākta pīṭha (Skt.), or seats of the Goddess's power."
  **A variant is recorded at the shrine:** "although Vishnu's intervention is sometimes omitted; in this
  version, the wild motion of Shiva's dance alone caused the body's dismemberment." (p. 55, folio not
  stated)
- **Which part fell at Hinglaj — two answers.** The brahmarandhra, "upper part of the head", per the
  Pīṭhanirṇaya (p. 55, folio not stated); the navel, per the Caṇḍīmaṅgala (p. 57, folio not stated);
  and at the shrine today a minority argues for "a part of her belly" because the garbha tunnel lies
  below the image (p. 57, folio not stated).
- **The siṃdūr etymology** — Sati "wore vermillion (Hin. 'siṃdūr') in her hair", so after her head fell
  the place became Hiṅgula, then Hinglaj; attributed to Billimoria 1944, 93 (p. 57, folio not stated).
- **The BVP's promise** — a devotee "reaches the end of the rebirth circle by seeing the idol of Durga
  at Hinglaj on the eighth day after the new moon in the month āśvin" (p. 54, folio not stated).
- **Carcikā's wandering** — born of Shiva's sweat in the fight with Andhaka, she "roamed the earth only
  to eventually settle at 'the best of all places—the Hingulata mountain'" (p. 53, folio not stated).
- **The Brahmakshatriya origin story, paraphrased by the author from three devotional books** (pp.
  61–62, folio not stated): Parashurama's vow to kill all Kshatriyas; Ratnasena's flight with his five
  pregnant wives; Dadhichi's ashram where "due to his spiritual power, even the wild animals live there
  in peace"; the protective forest in which "they would be safe from Parashurama's arrows"; the boar
  hunt that draws the king outside it and his death; the wives' self-immolation; Dadhichi's lie that the
  princes "were the sons of Brahmins"; the sacred threads and the Veda recitation. **The chunk breaks
  off before Parashurama's test is narrated.**
- **The Khatri/Kshatriya escape legend** — "the Kshatriya kings had to adopt the pseudonym 'Khatri' to
  escape the wrath of Parashurama (Mohidden 2003, 670)" (p. 58, folio not stated); and a rival account,
  "that the Kshatriya rulers began referring to themselves as Brahmakshatrias in the twelfth century to
  avoid the conversion to Islam by Muslim invaders (Mohidden 2003, 670)" (p. 59, folio not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The book's own coinage, and why sedimentation would not serve.** "None of these metaphors, however,
  is well suited to help describe the events documented at Hinglaj." (p. 46, folio not stated) "It is
  this attempt by many actors to produce norms and a fairly uncontested history, which I suggest to call
  a solidification of tradition. A solidifying tradition, thus, has not (yet) convinced most of its
  critics, its contingent foundations are still very much visible, while a sedimented tradition has
  already succeeded in creating a reasonably undisputed terrain." (p. 47, folio not stated) The
  distinction is temporal: "I suggest to understand solidification as acts that follow recent moments of
  dislocation, while sedimented social practices pertain to already fairly naturalized phenomena whose
  last dislocating moment has long since passed." (p. 47, folio not stated) He declines to harden it:
  "Though I am not proposing to establish a hard and fast border" (p. 47, folio not stated).
- **The charge against the theorists.** Laclau and Butler "avoid introducing the operational means of
  sedimentation"; "A way to measure the concatenation of repeated articulations, necessary to speak of
  sedimented structures, is not supplied." (p. 46, folio not stated) Laclau's version is "a process in
  which the `“forgetting of the origins’`[text-layer?] ["forgetting of the origins"] tends to occur"
  (Laclau 1990, 34) (p. 46, folio not stated); Butler "utilizes sedimentation for criticizing the
  naturalization of power relations through sex" (p. 46, folio not stated). Torfing's definition is
  quoted in full as "an institutional ensemble of rules, norms, values and regularities, which we take
  for granted in our everyday life. [Sedimented structures are] not subjected to the ongoing practices
  of constitution and subversion. (Torfing 2003, 70)" (p. 46, folio not stated).
- **Reflexivity: the book counts itself as an actor in the process it describes.** "As the production of
  written or recorded material is often a step within the solidification of tradition, due to its
  production of central nodal points, naturally, the research for this work also left its traces on the
  object under investigation." (p. 47, folio not stated) "any kind of social study should not be blind
  toward its effect on the studied object" (p. 48, folio not stated), citing Latour: "[A]ny study of any
  group by any social scientist is part and parcel of what makes the group exist, last, decay, or
  disappear." (Latour 2005, 33f.) (p. 48, folio not stated) And: "the present study plays its own role
  in the solidification of the Hinglaj tradition and thus also produces—intentionally and
  unintentionally—another truth-claim." (pp. 48–49, folio not stated)
- **The methodological question the chapter set out to answer:** "What could be a proper way to
  represent the plurality of truth-claims encountered when dealing with the Balochistan shrine and its
  many practices?" (p. 49, folio not stated) The ontological turn's "emphasis on practice and
  performativity is helpful for engaging with such truth-claims at eye-level… it does not explain how
  they engage with each other"; post-foundationalism supplies "the central paradox—namely, that the
  absence of any last foundation is necessary" and "assumes that last foundations are constantly
  attempted but never completely attained." (p. 49, folio not stated)
- **Normative vocabulary as politics:** "The events at Hinglaj constantly display normative categories,
  for example, adjectives like the 'actual,' the 'real,' and the 'proper,' which already pose a
  political decision. The repeated exclusion happening through such truth-claims, ultimately, sediments
  social practices into naturalized objectivities." (p. 49, folio not stated)
- **Mytho-history as the chapter's frame.** "a concept which emphasizes how memories of a mythological
  past become significant moments of present day politics"; "What makes a narrative mythical is 'not its
  truth or falsity, but the fact that it [is] concerned with order in a fundamental, cosmological sense'
  (Malkki 1995, 55)"; the term is "inspired by the work of Lawrence Babb ('mythohistorical knowledge,'
  Babb 2004, 24) and Liisa Malkki ('mythico-history,' Malkki 1995)." (p. 51, folio not stated)
- **Sanskrit attestation is symbolic capital, not evidence of antiquity.** "Sanskrit sources, however,
  are of limited use in dating Hinglaj to a particular era. The Tantras and Purāṇas are notoriously
  difficult to date; there is no universally accepted canon, and many important texts have been lost."
  (p. 52, folio not stated) Rocher is quoted: "On numerous occasions scholars have taken notice of the
  existence of manuscripts the contents of which did not correspond to those of any of the published
  versions" (Rocher 1986, 61) (p. 52, folio not stated). "A direct link between the mentioning of the
  name in Sanskrit sources and the site in Balochistan today is difficult to prove." (p. 52, folio not
  stated) And on the living side: "Discussions about the earliest occurrence of the name 'Hinglaj' in
  Sanskrit sources have, arguably, a limited influence on the perception of the Goddess among visitors
  at the shrine today. Sanskrit certainly has a strong symbolic value for Pakistani (and Indian)
  devotees, but the actual content of many of the aforementioned texts is often secondary." (p. 57,
  folio not stated)
- **Caste categories as political instruments.** "It is important to note that categories of identity
  always embody a certain kind of reduction. Such caste names—in the form of jāti or varṇa—are best
  understood as political instruments (as will be demonstrated) and thus malleable." (p. 58, folio not
  stated) The People of India series is called "a version of the census introduced by the British and
  unfortunately reproducing the worst aspects of colonial ethnography" (pp. 58–59, folio not stated).
- **The colonial-census argument, with its documents.** Dirks is quoted at length: "Risley noted several
  major struggles, including an attempt by Khatris of the Punjab and United Provinces to be classified
  as Rajputs [Kshatriyas] that was ultimately successful, asserting that these efforts vindicated his
  belief that 'the sole test of social precedence . . . was native public opinion.' (Dirks 2001, 222)"
  (p. 60, folio not stated). Risley's premise: he "saw India as a perfect environment for his studies
  since, in his thinking, the 'races' on the subcontinent had not mixed to the same extent as they had
  in Europe (Dirks 2001, 214)" and "believed strict marriage codes in the subcontinent had helped to
  preserve the physical characteristics of the different races" (p. 60, folio not stated). The Kayastha
  parallel: the case "Raj Kumar Lal v. Biseshwar Dayal, heard at the Calcutta High Court in 1884", in
  which "a Kayastha man could not legally adopt his sister's son due to the declaration of the Kayasthas
  as Shudras in the 1891 census (Carroll 1978, 239)", led to "the production of pamphlets, genealogies,
  and publications with which the community attempted to prove its Kshatriya descent (Rowe 2007, 202)"
  (p. 60, folio not stated). Varma's hostile verdict is quoted: "The Khatri who would fain rank himself
  with the Sesodias of Meywar, with the Jadus of Karauli, or with the Bhatis of Jaisalmere, still remains
  a Punjabi Bania; and no species of argument will ever transform the quill-driving caste of Kayasthas
  into a Rajput clan." (cited in Rowe 2007, 204) (pp. 60–61, folio not stated) The author's conclusion:
  "These and similar discussions were common in the early twentieth century, and they triggered waves of
  self-reflection and identity-searching in many communities. It is with this background that I will
  analyze the mytho-historic corpus consolidating the genealogies of the Khatri, Brahmakshatriya, and
  associated communities through their relationship with the Goddess Hinglaj." (p. 61, folio not stated)
- **A note on the Sanskritising mechanism**, chapter 1's note 8: "Such associations are frequent in South
  Asia's religious landscapes. Eck calls these 'duplication' or 'multiplication' processes the
  'geographical Sanskritisation' (Eck 1998, 166–167)." (p. 50, folio not stated) Chapter 1's note 9
  grounds the coinage in dictionary definitions of "solidify" and "sedimentation", "Both entries are
  taken from Merriam-Webster http://www.merriam-webster.com." (p. 50, folio not stated)
