## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The pilgrimage was institutionalized in 1986, and the book dates the whole transformation from
  there.** "This temple committee and the impact of the pilgrimage's institutionalization in 1986 will
  be introduced below." (p. 80, folio not stated) "it is evident that since the middle of the 1980s, one
  finds a network of increasing infrastructure, publications, and ritual journeys that have enabled a
  fast-paced development of the whole Hinglaj phenomenon." (p. 84, folio not stated)
- **The HSM's fee history, in full, with every figure as printed.** "In the second half of the 1980s,
  the HSM started organizing tours and charged pilgrims 500 rupees for transportation, boarding, and
  lodging per trip. Later, from the 1990s forward, the price was raised to 1,025 rupees for the whole
  trip." (p. 82, folio not stated) "However, those who traveled in their own vehicles still had to pay
  525 rupees to the Mandali for their stay in Hinglaj." (p. 83, folio not stated) "This caused the HSM
  to completely cease their business of arranging domestic transportation in 2009 and instead charge 250
  rupees per person for entry to the shrine during the annual festival in April (Hin. phālgun/caitra
  melā). They eventually quit charging this fee in 2013." (p. 83, folio not stated)
- **What the HSM sold, and why people needed it.** "In its early days, the HSM organized tour packages
  that included transportation, boarding, and lodging. Apparently, as little as thirty years ago, only a
  handful of people actually knew the exact location of the shrine." (p. 82, folio not stated) "The
  unknown location of Hinglaj posed a problem for which many devotees depended on local help, a niche
  that HSM members—mainly Lohanas originating from the region around the shrine—were able to fill." (p.
  82, folio not stated) "But already in the mid-1990s, when the exact location of the shrine was
  apparently more widely known, many pilgrims stopped depending on the HSM's support, instead organizing
  their own trips." (pp. 82–83, folio not stated)
- **The foreign-pilgrim business the HSM kept.** "To this day, in spite of having ceased to organize
  transportation from within Pakistan, the HSM continues to coordinate transportation for visitors
  coming from abroad. The Mandali caters in particular to devotees from India who often wish to book a
  fully organized trip due to visa and security concerns." (p. 83, folio not stated) "The ease of
  journeys organized with the help of the Mandali is frequently described in newer Hindi travelogues,
  helping to increase the HSM's profile and contributing to the influx of visitors (Barhath 2006; Ratnu
  2007). The role as a link between the Pakistani-Hindu world and a more international clientele adds to
  the Mandali's aura and its authority among Hindus in Pakistan." (p. 83, folio not stated)
- **Donations, state subsidy and a costed building project.** "Over the years, the HSM has also become
  increasingly responsible for the distribution of large amounts of money received by the temple as
  donations or (until 2013) as fees. A significant amount is also allocated by the provincial
  administration, which subsidizes the construction of pilgrimage shelters and roads in the valley. In
  2012, the HSM supervised the construction of a bridge over the Hingol River with a total cost of 11
  crore Pakistani rupees (about €920,000), funded by the Balochistan government." (p. 84, folio not
  stated) "This capacity for overseeing the allocation of funds also adds to the HSM's local influence
  in Las Bela since they not only create jobs in the area—albeit with the help of the provincial
  government—but also possess significant contacts in the business communities of Las Bela and Karachi."
  (p. 84, folio not stated)
- **A visitor number, sourced to the committee's own records.** "This culminated in the April 2010
  gathering during which, according to HSM's ticket sale records, over forty thousand people visited the
  shrine." (p. 84, folio not stated)
- **The festival's scale and standing.** "Various developments over the past few decades have turned the
  melā into one of the largest gatherings for Hindu communities in Pakistan—for most of my interviewees,
  it was even considered the biggest. It now annually attracts devotees from all over the country and
  even from abroad." (p. 83, folio not stated) "Mainly through the HSM's work, the annual festival has
  become well known and many Hindus I met all over the country had gone at least once." (p. 83, folio
  not stated) "The institutionalization of the pilgrimage, combined with infrastructural developments
  and the completion of the MCH, have transformed the pilgrimage into one of the largest annual social
  gatherings of Hindu communities in Pakistan today." (p. 97, folio not stated)
- **The festival is in April and is named**: "the annual festival in April (Hin. phālgun/caitra melā)"
  (p. 83, folio not stated); also "the annual phālgun/caitra gathering" (p. 84, folio not stated). **No
  day, no duration and no ritual programme is given in this chunk**: **(not stated)**.
- **Fixed dates as a mark of institutionalization.** "Also, the festivals are institutionalized and
  fixed to a certain time and date. While traditional paths were marked by insecurity, even to the
  extent that return from the journey was not a guarantee, now there is a certain sense of regulated
  mobility not only at the Hinglaj shrine but also on the journey there. This is visible in the
  solidification of different pilgrimage paths into one prevailing route following a certain set of
  chosen stops on the way." (pp. 84–85, folio not stated)
- **The Makran Coastal Highway, with its dates and dimensions.** "The completion of the 650-kilometer
  MCH from Liari to Gabd at the Iranian border in 2003 (Hasan 2009, 161) represented not just a major
  infrastructural development for the coastal area but also a significant contribution to the Hinglaj
  shrine's increasing visibility" (p. 85, folio not stated). "The MCH was built in consecutive stages,
  and the first part, linking the Hingol valley with Karachi, was completed and in use by 2001." (p. 85,
  folio not stated) "After the first part of the Makran Coastal Highway (N 10) was completed in 2001,
  the area around the River Hingol became connected to the National Highway 25, a heavily traveled road
  that links Karachi with Quetta." (p. 83, folio not stated)
- **The road's purpose and its politics.** "The project aimed to develop industries along the coastal
  areas and to link Pakistan's commercial metropolis, Karachi, with the ports of Omara, Pasni, and
  Gwadar in the far west of the country. With the help of the Chinese, the Pakistani government, under
  General Musharraf (in office 2001–2008), invested in the deep-water port at Gwadar, intending to
  develop it into a major commercial center at the entrance to the Persian Gulf (Lieven 2011, 343)." (p.
  85, folio not stated)
- **Travel times before and after, as printed.** "Before the turn of the millennium, the journey from
  Karachi to Gwadar took two to three days and required cross-country vehicles, causing many to prefer
  the longer route via Quetta. After the construction of the road, the Hinglaj Valley, at the mouth of
  the Hingol River, became connected to urban Pakistan, making a trip from Karachi possible in just
  three and a half hours." (p. 85, folio not stated) "Today, buses run on a schedule connecting Karachi
  and Gwadar—and therefore—Karachi and the Hinglaj Valley." (p. 84, folio not stated) "From that time
  onward, various private companies increasingly assumed the transportation to the valley." (p. 83,
  folio not stated)
- **The road produced a revival of walking, not only of busing.** "Because of the new road, the
  inevitably demanding and arduous journey by foot, ship, or, more recently by off-road vehicle,
  suddenly became optional for visitors and a matter of choice." (p. 85, folio not stated) "But, as will
  be discussed in more detail later, the road has not only influenced the number of visitors at the
  shrine but has also triggered a renewed tendency toward the walking tradition. Unburdened by the
  scarcity of water and the lingering possibility of getting lost in the desert, many pilgrims have
  started to peregrinate along the highway itself to reach the shrine of Hinglaj." (p. 85, folio not
  stated)
- **Zikri practice, as the book describes it.** "Zikris mainly subscribe to the tenets of Sunni Islam,
  but practice the ẕikr (Urd.), the recitation of the name of Allah instead of the common prayer. Not
  many details on the community have been documented, and the most extensive research to date can be
  found in Baloch (2010)." (p. 90, folio not stated) "In my experience, Zikris in Las Bela and Makran
  take a different approach to their religious practice than Zikris in Karachi, who adhere to more
  common interpretations of Islam." (p. 90, folio not stated)
- **Caste, priesthood and intermarriage.** "Thakur writes that the Lohanas share many attributes with
  the Arora, the Khatri, and the Bhanushali communities, who all recruit the Saraswat Brahmins as
  priests (Thakur 1997, 61)." (p. 81, folio not stated) "Lari supports this finding, writing that the
  Bhatias, the Khatris, and the Lohanas were very close communities and even intermarried (Lari 1994,
  211)." (p. 81, folio not stated)
- **A commensality rule with a mythological warrant.** Kshatriya women hiding from Parashurama "had to
  prepare food for the Brahmin men, who since that time accept food prepared by Kshatriaya (Khatri)
  women (Cook 2010, 144)" (p. 81, folio not stated).
- **Census classification of the Lohanas.** "Hence, in the 1881 census, Lohanas from Shikarpur and
  Hyderabad were counted as Sikhs, while ten years later, in the 1891 census, they were classified as
  Hindus (Lari 1994, 211f.)." (p. 80, folio not stated)
- **Trade, rulers and the merchant economy of Sindh.** "Lachaier claims that in the nineteenth century,
  the Lohanas were the most important trading community in the areas of Sindh, Kutch, and Saurashtra
  (Lachaier 2008, 82). One reason for this was the established rule of the Kalhoras in Sindh in the
  eighteenth century, which brought new business opportunities for merchant professions (Cook 2010,
  140)." (p. 80, folio not stated) "Sindh's location between trading routes connecting Afghanistan,
  Khorasan, Arabia, Persia, Turkey, and other places made the province appealing to a wide array of
  merchants from the entire continent." (p. 81, folio not stated)
- **The Amil/Bhaiband split, with a proportion.** "Around the eighteenth or nineteenth century, a
  separation occurred within these merchant groups of Sindh. A subset of Lohanas began working for local
  rulers and soon become known as 'Amils,' while those who retained their merchant profession were
  called 'Bhaiband' (Lachaier 2008, 83). The Amils, initially 10 to 15 percent of the Lohanas (Lari
  1994, 211), soon acquired a higher status than the general Lohana and Khatri communities and thus
  became an independent group." (p. 81, folio not stated)
- **Publication as infrastructure.** "Together with the HSM's publicity work in relation to the annual
  phālgun/caitra gathering, various authors writing in Hindi, Urdu, and Sindhi also began promoting the
  shrine in Balochistan and often refer to the HSM in their publications." (p. 84, folio not stated)
  "This growth is also evident in the lengthening list of publications about the temple, which
  correspond to the completion of the first part of the Makran Coastal Highway (MCH) up to the River
  Hingol." (p. 84, folio not stated) "The organization certainly has done most of the work in
  propagating the image of the place as an important tīrtha yātrā for all Hindus." (p. 84, folio not
  stated)
- **Partition demography in Sindh.** "The partition of the subcontinent brought major changes to the
  province of Sindh. The departure of the Hindu elite and the influx of Muslim refugees ('Muhajirs')
  from India dramatically altered the local demography, and the Sindhi community became increasingly
  defined by its perception of being ostracized and subjugated by Muhajir power." (p. 87, folio not
  stated)

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Eve at Hinglaj**, attributed by Schaflechner to Wilford and through him to the local Muslim
  community: Eve "fell from heaven, landed in one of the ponds inside the valley, and later settled
  there and became known as Bibi Nani or as Hinglaj" (Wilford 1851, 259; Abro 1991, 66) (p. 89, folio
  not stated).
- **Hazrat Ali splits a rock**, attributed to Hart and through him to Muslims in the area: he "split a
  rock in two with his sword as he was on his way into the valley" (Hart 1839, 94) (pp. 89–90, folio not
  stated).
- **Hinglaj as the wife of Ali**, attributed to an informant of Aurel Stein (Stein 1943, 202) (p. 90,
  folio not stated); the book elsewhere reports a "grandmother" version alongside it (p. 90, folio not
  stated).
- **Hinglaj as a wish-fulfilling Balochi pīr**, attributed to present-day Zikris: she "has the power to
  fulfill wishes" (p. 90, folio not stated).
- **The iron fort of the Rathors**, attributed to Thakur as a Lohana origin story: "Varuna built the
  Rathors an iron fort that saved them from the Raja's attack. Even though the fort disappeared
  twenty-one days after the attack, the Rathors were from then onward called Lohana, which the community
  translates as 'those of iron' (Thakur 1997, 56)." (p. 80, folio not stated)
- **Descent from Lava**, attributed to Thakur and to the author's own Sindhi interlocutors (p. 80, folio
  not stated).
- **Kshatriya women saved by Brahmin households**, attributed to Khatri mythology via Cook (p. 81, folio
  not stated).
- **Sun and moon hewn on the rock**, attributed to Masson as hearsay: "There are also said to be figures
  of the sun and the moon hewn on the rock, in an accessible site." (Masson 1977, 390f.) (p. 91, folio
  not stated) The same passage gives "a well, of unfathomable depth, above the mat".
- **The shrine's salvific power, named but not described.** "The construction of the road meant the
  displacement of former practices and introduced new possibilities for seeing the shrine and
  experiencing its salvific powers." (p. 97, folio not stated)

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The chapter's governing claim: the HSM and the road made modern Hinglaj.** "Thus, the influence of
  the HSM and, in a broader sense, of the Lasi Lohana community, on the politics of information,
  mobility, and ritual practices at the shrine cannot be underestimated." (p. 84, folio not stated) "For
  the pilgrimage network of Hinglaj, the work of the HSM along with the construction of the Makran
  Coastal Highway are the main factors pushing ritual journeys to Hinglaj into modernity." (p. 84, folio
  not stated) The theoretical warrant is Cresswell: "one sign of the advent of high modernity is the
  increasing organization of mobility through 'timetables and mechanizations' (Cresswell 2006, 10)" (p.
  84, folio not stated).
- **Schaflechner declines to name a prime mover.** "It is difficult to state what might have been the
  primary catalyst, but it is evident that since the middle of the 1980s, one finds a network of
  increasing infrastructure, publications, and ritual journeys" (p. 84, folio not stated). "However, in
  medias res of all of these events, one finds time and again traces of the HSM's efforts." (p. 84,
  folio not stated)
- **The Hinglaj case is recent and therefore unfinished**, against Jacobsen's Indian railway
  comparison: "As the means of transportation has improved, starting with the development of the Indian
  railway, pilgrimage has become easy and this has influenced the expectation of the rewards of
  pilgrimage. In many pilgrimages, but still with a number of exceptions, there is no longer any
  similarity between the wandering of ascetics and the journey of pilgrims. (Jacobsen 2013, 21)" (p. 86,
  folio not stated). "However, while the means of travel at these other pilgrimage locations have
  steadily evolved over many decades, events around Hinglaj are fairly recent, and the incorporation of
  the new practices of mobility at the shrine have not yet become completely final." (p. 86, folio not
  stated)
- **Accessibility destabilized meaning, not just numbers.** "This new accessibility dramatically changed
  the representations and practices at the shrine, and the newfound ease with which Hinglaj was quickly
  reachable stirred up discussions of identity and religious merit at the place." (p. 85, folio not
  stated) The sudden connection of the site "currently occupies the time and energy of many who aim to
  coherently incorporate this into the religious imaginary and the rituals at the shrine." (p. 86, folio
  not stated)
- **"Syncretic Sindhi nationalism", the chapter's coined term.** "Within this discourse, Hinglaj emerges
  in a completely different role—as the hallmark of a phenomenon I will term 'syncretic Sindhi
  nationalism.' Such texts portray Hinglaj as an intrinsically Sindhi religious place, appropriating and
  rearticulating it as an instrument of Sindhi nationalism that aspires to transgress religious
  boundaries." (p. 86, folio not stated) His working definition of nationalism: "a kind of group
  formation in which the so-called unity of a large and heterogeneous (or even 'imagined') group is
  evoked through the articulation of certain commonly shared nodal points, gaining their influential
  role in opposition to some excluded other." (p. 86, folio not stated) "As will be demonstrated, nodal
  points of Sindhi nationalism emphasize religious harmony and thus subsume the difference between
  Hindus and Muslims under the superior signifier of ethnic identity." (pp. 86–87, folio not stated)
- **He defends the word "syncretism" while conceding it is discredited.** "I am conscious of the fact
  that the term 'syncretism' does not hold to current theories of culture and has been widely criticized
  due to its implicit hierarchization of traditions (Ringgren 1969). I nonetheless deem its conceptual
  logic precise in the context of Sindhi nationalism." (p. 89, folio not stated) "While syncretism has
  become obsolete due to its inherent assumption of a clear-cut border between religions traditions,
  Sindhi nationalism, on the contrary, needs such hard and fast separations. It must emphasize the basic
  differences between Islam and Hinduism in order to show its outstanding ability to unite them. Within
  this discourse, the shrine of Hinglaj emerges as a materialized form of Sindhi religious unity."
  (p. 89, folio not stated)
- **The three-part structure of the nationalist claim on Hinglaj**, given twice. First at p. 90 (folio
  not stated): the shrine is "a part of the Mohenjo-Daro civilization and thus a five-thousand-year-old—
  but intrinsically Sindhi—cultural heritage site"; and "even though Hinglaj is older than both these
  religions, Muslims and Hindus have both been worshipping at the shrine for many centuries", proved by
  Latif's journey. Then in summary at p. 96 (folio not stated): "First, the equation of Hinglaj with
  Persian and Chaldean deities—mainly through the writing of colonial authors—furnishes the shrine with
  an ancient history. Second, the association of the place with Mohenjo-Daro historically binds it to a
  culture that is pre-Aryan (or pre-Hindu) and pre-Islam, but intrinsically Sindhi. And third, the
  poetry of Shah Abdul Latif presents Hinglaj as an important religious site for both Islam and
  Hinduism".
- **The harmony claim is called a utopia.** "These writers emphasize the common notion among Sindhis
  today that in the history of Sindh, there is no religious tension between the two
  communities—Muslims and Hindus. This notion of the harmonious coexistence of the religions, however,
  is merely a nationalist utopian ideal, as Aggarwal (2013) has shown." (pp. 90–91, folio not stated)
- **A source-critical demolition: the "ancient Goddess" claim is a citation loop.** Of the Las Bela
  Gazetteer's account: "Scrutinizing the quote within Ansari's argument, one finds that the Gazetteer's
  account does not build upon new, independent research in this area but is, in fact, also directly
  derived from Thomas Holdich's book India (1904)." (p. 93, folio not stated) Of Billimoria: "Although
  Billimoria's contribution seems, at first, to build upon new research, he also quotes Holdich (1910)
  when illustrating the Goddess's history… Billimoria also refers extensively (often without citation of
  direct quotations) to Crooke's 1913 article in the ERE… Crooke quotes Masson's argument to provide
  support for his own thesis, thus creating a direct connection between Abro's synopsis and Masson's
  writing from 1844. Billimoria also cites more than five pages directly from Captain Hart's 1839
  travelogue. His contribution to the study of the Goddess Hinglaj is therefore, in fact, an
  amalgamation of older sources rather than a new approach to the question of Hinglaj's origin." (p. 94,
  folio not stated) And of the root of it all: "Masson apparently was the first who hypothesized a
  connection to an ancient Near Eastern Goddess, an idea that from that time onward became integral to
  the description of Hinglaj." (p. 92, folio not stated) "However, it is primarily the writings of
  Masson and Holdich that exert significant influence on contemporary literature concerning the shrine
  in Balochistan and that also have been ultimately integrated into contemporary Sindhi publications
  about the Goddess Hinglaj." (p. 92, folio not stated) Masson himself "provides no further evidence for
  this belief" (p. 91, folio not stated).
- **What the antiquity claim buys.** "With this statement, Holdich proclaims the shrine's antiquity to
  surpass both Islam and Hinduism, placing the Goddess in an independent position, untouched by any
  present religious current. The work of many later writers bolstered this claim." (p. 92, folio not
  stated) Of Ansari: "Thus, in his estimation, the Goddess Hinglaj predates Hindu and Muslim invasions
  into Sindh and thus renders her immune to religious and sectarian skirmishes." (p. 93, folio not
  stated) Of Abro: he "places the Goddess in a distant sphere where she is 'related to the human past'
  (Sin. insān jo māẓī)—that is, detached from any specific religion—and hence removed from any kind of
  communal conflict (Abro 1991, 42)." (p. 94, folio not stated) "Since, Hinglaj is thereby associated
  with almost every Goddess west and east of Balochistan; she is all-embracing, transcending, and
  surpassing communal differences." (p. 94, folio not stated)
- **G. M. Syed's evolutionary scheme and its paradox.** "He asserts that starting from primitive
  Hinduism and Buddhism, mankind developed toward Christianity and Islam and, in his view, eventually
  gained a scientific approach to existence, which ultimately excels in Sufism (Verkaaik 2004, 33). Syed
  then finds Sufism, as the universal goal of the human race, in its purest form in the province of
  Sindh. For him, this is proven by the various shrines of Sufi saints that are, to this day, visited by
  Hindus and Muslims alike." (p. 89, folio not stated) "Syed's approach, however, is paradoxical in its
  basic assumption: on the one hand, it is universal to all, transcending petty religious boundaries…
  but on the other hand, it is geographically deeply rooted in Sindh and its inhabitants." (p. 89, folio
  not stated) Verkaaik, quoted approvingly: "In short, both Sindhi Hindus and Muslims were influenced by
  mysticism and religious tolerance. This was the Sindhi essence that both Hindus and Muslims shared"
  (Verkaaik 2010, 210) (p. 89, folio not stated).
- **Sindhi nationalism dated and sourced.** "Verkaaik describes Sindhi nationalism as a relatively new
  phenomenon that began with the struggle for independence from the Bombay Presidency in the 1920s and
  1930s (Verkaaik 2010, 197). Important for the further development of this discourse was, according to
  Verkaaik, the 1947 publication, Save Sind, Save the Continent, by Ibrahmin Joyo." (p. 88, folio not
  stated) Joyo's distinction: communalism is "a 'ridiculous idea with absurd consequences,'" while true
  nationalism "'is not based on religion, but history, culture, and geography'" (Verkaaik 2010, 202) (p.
  88, folio not stated).
- **Decline narratives with two culprits.** Against Pakistan: "Malkani's book teems with such rants
  against Pakistan and everything broadly associated with it… Here, Sindh is described as solid,
  ancient, and refined, while Pakistan and its 'Urdu-wallas' are portrayed as temporary, shallow, and
  uncultured." (p. 87, folio not stated) Against the British: "They also aim at British rule, which they
  view as having victimized Sindh. Here, another kind of intruder, the British colonizer and not the
  Muhajir, holds responsibility for the slow destruction of Sindh's glorious past." (p. 87, folio not
  stated) "Soomro also renders the history of Sindh as a story of decline in which the province lost
  some 'golden past,' which it had enjoyed before the invasion of outsiders." (p. 88, folio not stated)
- **Abro's "real Sindh".** "At one point, he writes that Sindhis once lived in the valleys between Rohri
  and east Balochistan, an area he defines as the 'real Sindh' (Sin. aṣal sindh) that was once
  'prosperous' (Abro 1991, 22; Sin. sukhī satābī). According to Abro, the whole Sindhi civilization
  originated in these mountains." (p. 94, folio not stated) "Since Sindh remained under the influence of
  Sufi mysticism (Sin. taṣavvuf), the area is 'fortunately in terms of faith, secular [in English]'
  (Abro 1991, 23; italics added). This means that religious places in the region are not bound to
  particular communities or religious affiliations, but are a part of the collective heritage of a 'real
  Sindh.'" (p. 94, folio not stated)
- **Ansari's pre-Aryan Mother Goddess.** "To broaden his argument of Sindh's 'golden past,' Ansari
  claims that the composition of the Veda occurred on the shores of the Indus." (p. 93, folio not
  stated) "The veneration of the Goddess was, he emphasizes, already in place in Sindh before the Aryans
  conquered the area 'five thousand years ago' (Ansari 2001, 154). Hence, the cult of the Mother Goddess
  predates the coming of the Aryans (Ansari 2001, 161)." (p. 93, folio not stated) "In this view of
  history, Hinglaj was worshipped in Sindh before the coming of the Aryans and was only later adopted by
  the (Aryan) Kshatriyas and hence by Hinduism (Ansari 2001, 322)." (p. 93, folio not stated) The global
  reach of the claim: Mother worship "connected to other Goddess veneration in, for example, ancient
  Egypt, Greece, and Austria (Ansari 2001, 144–162)", and "besides Egypt, Greece, and Europe also in
  places like Brazil and Turkey", indicative to Ansari of an "evolution of human consciousness" whose
  roots are found in "matriarchy" (Ansari 2001, 15) (p. 93, folio not stated).
- **Caste history read as political instrument, the chapter's other conclusion.** "The Brahmakshatriya
  community, for example, employed so-called learned opinions by the Saraswat Brahmins, including
  mytho-historic genealogies, to advance their upward political and social mobility in the early
  twentieth century. In Sindhi nationalist discourse, on the other hand, the shrine of Hinglaj is linked
  to a 'real' Sindh that prospered before outsiders began suppressing the province. Within such
  nationalistic notions of a 'golden past,' the shrine is appropriated to fit modern concepts of an
  ideal Sindhi nation." (p. 97, folio not stated)
- **Absorption as the mechanism of Lohana caste formation**, quoted from Thakur: "The case of Lohana
  caste is very peculiar. Unless a person is a member of any of the non-Lohana castes, he must be
  assumed to be a Lohana, members of which caste form the general body of the Sind Hindus . . . [P]eople
  . . . who migrated from Rajputanta and the Punjab and professed Lohana occupations have acquired
  membership of the Lohana caste. (Thakur 1997, 68f.)" (p. 80, folio not stated) "Zahid-Khan attributes
  this merging of different groups to the small number of non-Muslim Sindhis who united in the face of
  the majority (quoted in Cook 2010, 134)." (p. 81, folio not stated)
- **The HSM as a platform for negotiating tradition, and the forward reference.** "In this way, the HSM
  has become an institutional platform in which Lohana traditions are spread and also negotiated." (p.
  82, folio not stated) "One important player within this development is the Lohana community from Las
  Bela, who institutionalized their traditions with the establishment of the HSM, a development that
  will be my main interest in Chapter 6." (p. 97, folio not stated)

## Doubts

- **The Hinglaj row carries no id in `shrine_index.tsv`.** Line 107 of the index gives the name
  `Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir` and the location `Hingol Balochistan National Park, Road، Asha
  Pura, Pakistan`, but **the id column is blank**, and no category, principal figure, death year or
  silsila is filled either. **No id was invented.** Everything this chunk says about the shrine — which
  is most of the chunk — therefore sits under the literal head
  `- **(no id — Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir)**` and is **unmapped pending Rauf's ruling**. The
  index row's location field also carries a stray Arabic-script comma (`Road،`), which a human should
  fix when the id is assigned.
- **One archive id is used at a bullet head: `bhit-bhit-shah`.** It is copied character-for-character
  from column 1 of `shrine_index.tsv`. The identification rests on the book's own repeated naming of
  "the Sindhi national poet Shah Abdul Latif 'Bhittai' (1690–1751)" (pp. 90, 95, folio not stated), who
  is the row's principal figure. **The 18 September 2026 ruling is what makes this a head rather than an
  "other" entry** — the book names him only as a poet, a traveller and a nationalist emblem, never in
  connection with Bhit Shah or with any tomb. **The evidence is not a bare name** (there is a dated
  life, a journey, a named work and a named section of it), but a human should note that **nothing in
  this chunk touches the shrine at Bhit Shah itself**, so this material belongs to the saint, not to the
  site.
- **Death-year discrepancy on `bhit-bhit-shah`.** The book prints `1690–1751` twice (pp. 90, 95, folio
  not stated). The archive row's `figure_died` is `1752`. **The book's figure is recorded as printed and
  the archive's is not overwritten**; a human must decide which the entry carries.
- **Three spellings of the poet's name and title across two pages.** The author's own form is `Shah
  Abdul Latif "Bhittai"` (pp. 90, 95); the quoted Mangi text prints `Shah Abdul Lateef Bhitai` and
