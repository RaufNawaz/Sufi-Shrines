## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The HSM as the institution that makes and unmakes stops.** "With the reinstitutionalization of the
  yātrā in the mid-1980s, however, this became the work of the HSM, which ultimately chose to
  highlight certain stops along the way and leave others out. This process is easily traceable
  through a study of the advertisements for the pilgrimage, which are printed by the HSM and describe
  the pilgrimage route to be followed in the year of publication. These posters, which, since 1986
  have been annually distributed in the temples of major Pakistani cities and in rural Sindh,
  demonstrate the extent to which a developing infrastructure and institutionalization have influenced
  the change and even production of stops on the pilgrimage to Hinglaj." (p. 204, folio not stated)
  **`1986` is cleanly printed.**
- **The Khari stop, dropped — a dated worked example.** "when Jethamalani published his book in 1994,
  transportation to the shrine was arranged mainly by the HSM. It is thus likely that many visitors
  in the 1990s visited that stop since at that time, the Khari paṛāv constituted a decisive part of
  the ritual journey, visible on the HSM's pilgrimage program. In that same year, however, the HSM
  stopped announcing the Khari stop, and the paṛāv vanished from the advertisements. According to one
  official, this was due to the fact that, by then, pilgrims were already traveling in off-road
  vehicle and no longer needed to bathe in the ocean to feel refreshed." (p. 204, folio not stated)
  The outcome: "Throughout the entire course of my fieldwork at the shrine—including hundreds of
  interviews about the yātrā, the sacred valley, and the shrine—not a single person mentioned the
  Khari stop or the Rama pūjā." (p. 204, folio not stated) **The official is not named**: **(not
  stated)**.
- **The route's shrinkage, counted.** "Jethamalani writes that thirteen paṛāv and twenty-three places
  for pūjā (Skt. ritual worship) once comprised the traditional pilgrimage from Karachi to Hinglaj
  (Jethamalani 1994, 10)… He adds that today, pilgrims' time constraints have reduced this number of
  stops to just three paṛāv, which contain seven pūjās." (p. 202, folio not stated) The three-paṛāv
  list as printed: 1 Khari Nadi (a. Khari Nadi, b. Chandrakup); 2 Aghor River (a. The River Aghor,
  b. Ganesh); 3 Ashapura (a. iśṭādev, b. Kali Mata, c. Hinglaj temple) (pp. 202–203, folios not
  stated). **Note the count: the printed list carries seven lettered items, matching "seven pūjās".**
- **Roads as the motor of the whole argument.** "In the early 1970s, the RCD Highway N 25 connecting
  Quetta with Karachi was completed. In the 1960s, the road had already been constructed up to the
  city of Bela, several kilometers north of Uthal, the present capital of the province (Khan Baloch
  1970, 125)." (p. 202, folio not stated) And, for the later road: "With the construction of the MCH
  came not only the modernization of local transportation but also the reinvention of the walking
  practice to Hinglaj." (p. 191, folio not stated)
- **Walking as a revived, not surviving, practice.** "most of the walking pilgrims today began their
  practice of traveling on foot after the completion of the MCH. The infrastructure that developed
  along with the road, the easier access to water sources, and the reduced chance of getting lost on
  the way led to a revitalization of walking patterns." (p. 191, folio not stated)
- **Leadership dissolved by the road.** "Whereas before the construction of the road, pilgrim groups
  needed a clearly appointed leader with substantial knowledge of the surroundings, today, a
  formalized hierarchy is no longer necessary." (p. 191, folio not stated)

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The "tourist" is the chapter's central construct, and the author says it is empty.** "the label of
  the picnic manānevalā `[text-layer?]` [manānevālā — the macron appears on every other occurrence in
  the chunk] or the 'tourist' is used to capture a certain discontent with the current pilgrimage
  experience. It represents the projection of a deficit of some sort, creating the defining quality
  of an other at the shrine—a quality that is found only in fellow visitors and never in oneself."
  (p. 189, folio not stated) "certain visitors use these two descriptions in a manner that is void of
  any positive content but use them instead as a vehicle for expressing their resentment over the
  all-too-obvious gap between representations of Hinglaj and the actual practices at the shrine."
  (p. 190, folio not stated) "the tourist is always some other that cannot be described by any
  positive set of characteristics" (p. 192, folio not stated).
- **Why kali yuga is refused as the explanation.** "such notions of deterioration are rarely
  interpreted through the kali yuga (Skt.), the cosmic age of decay, given by many Hindus as the
  reason for spiritual degeneration all over the globe. I assume that due to the very recent changes
  at Hinglaj, kali yuga does not serve as an apt explanation for the gap between representation and
  current practice of the yātrā." (p. 188, folio not stated) Restated with a date: "As some visitors
  claim to remember a better time, kali yuga—which allegedly started in the fourth millennium
  BC—is not an apt concept to explain Hinglaj's spiritual decline." (p. 189, folio not stated)
  **"the fourth millennium BC" is given as an allegation, not as the author's own dating.**
- **The contribution claimed against the pilgrim/tourist literature.** Citing Stausberg 2010 on the
  "fairly blurry distinction between pilgrimages and tourist activities" and Humphrey and Laidlaw
  1994 on people imposing "various meaningful narratives onto ritualistic behavior on their own"
  (p. 189, folio not stated): "Interesting in the Hinglaj case is that the line is drawn by local
  actors, such as pujārīs, organizers, and the visitors themselves in order to distinguish the serious
  from the non-serious—that is, the pilgrims from the tourists. In this way, the pilgrimage site at
  Hinglaj adds a new dimension to current discussions of what counts as 'tourist' or 'pilgrim'
  activity and reintroduces it with the distinction of the 'picnic manānevālā' and the 'yātrī.'"
  (p. 190, folio not stated)
- **The line is not drawn by orthopraxy.** "This distinction, however, is not drawn along gritty lines
  of orthopraxy. The performance of certain rituals is not sufficient to serve as a marker of
  'seriousness,' a quality that is denied the tourist. Devoted pilgrims might follow all the necessary
  rituals and bhajans … and still be seen by others as 'picnic manānevāle.'" (p. 190, folio not
  stated)
- **The MCH's double effect, named as ambivalence.** "Over the past few years, the highway has been
  responsible for giving many people the chance to experience the shrine. On the one hand, the
  increasing number of visitors draws more attention to the Goddess and the various narratives at
  Hinglaj; on the other hand, such an accumulation of individuals, together with such smooth access
  to the shrine, contradicts themes of danger and exclusiveness in the pilgrimage experience as it is
  perpetuated in many narratives." (p. 190, folio not stated)
- **Austerity as the implicit theory the complaints rest on.** The complaints are "consistent with an
  implicit theory of pilgrimages according to which tapasyā and renunciation generate merit, purify
  the soul, and result in a deeper and more abiding spiritual experience" (p. 189, folio not stated);
  "These accounts attribute the pilgrimage's merit to the long and arduous path and deem physical
  demand and tapasyā critical components of the experience." (p. 189, folio not stated) The
  underwriting text is note 18's Abadhut passage: "The secret is indeed that the difficulties during
  the pilgrimage are also tapas, austerity. From austerity, one can get a glimpse of the Brahma.
  Therefore, it is said that austerity is Brahma [tapohī brahma]." (p. 194, folio not stated) And:
  "If you mount a train for a pilgrimage and you come back without any physical toils, then you might
  see the pilgrimage place [tīrtha], but the austerity is left out." (p. 194, folio not stated)
- **The chapter-4 thesis in one sentence.** "This gap must be filled with shape and meaning, a fantasy
  structure that has laid all the guilt upon the figure of the 'tourist,' thereby accounting for the
  lack of spiritual merit" (p. 190, folio not stated); "the tourist serves as a principle that helps
  to organize the tradition's recent dislocation. It stands as an obstacle between devotees at the
  shrine and the promised spiritual union they hope to achieve there." (p. 192, folio not stated)
- **Exclusivism survives on the Indian side.** "the kind of exclusivism surrounding the pilgrimage to
  Hinglaj—only known in Pakistan before the construction of the MCH—is still very much alive in
  India"; "those who come back from Pakistan stand out as exceptional and are treated differently in
  their social environment." (p. 191, folio not stated) "Many of the oral histories presented above
  can also be read from the perspective of restricted mobility and power." (p. 191, folio not stated)
- **Chapter 5's programme, stated.** "The following, therefore, will ask how discourses around Hinglaj
  and the pilgrimage stops take place… For an apt approach to this important nexus between space and
  culture, I will critically engage with theories broadly associated with the 'spatial-turn.' This
  aims at achieving two goals: first, to analyze how Hinglaj is rendered as a meaningful religious
  space and, second, to emphasize the material aspect of the solidification theory through concepts
  of spatialization." (p. 195, folio not stated)
- **The spatial turn, summarised and sourced.** "the developments surrounding the study of space and
  place since the 1980s—the so-called spatial turn—can be summed up as the rejection of any stable
  Euclidean entity and its replacement with the notion of space as continually being produced
  (Cresswell 2004; Keith and Pile 1993; Urry 1995). Space is now perceived as a 'product of
  interrelations,' 'coexisting heterogeneity,' and 'always under construction' (Massey 2005, 9)."
  (p. 195, folio not stated) "For Massey, such considerations first and foremost mean that the
  'spatial is political' (Massey 2005, 9)." (p. 196, folio not stated)
- **Lefebvre, and the author's frank rewriting of him.** "For Lefebvre, 'space' (l'espace) is not
  merely a three-dimensional area in which life happens but rather is something more complex,
  produced out of representations and discourses about space." (p. 196, folio not stated) Via
  Shields: "a 'spatialisation of social order' and a way in which 'abstract structures such as
  "culture" become concrete practices and arrangements in space' (Shields 1998, 155)." (p. 196, folio
  not stated) The triad "was not fully conceptualized by Lefebvre himself and therefore is widely
  discussed by scholars", with Merrifield quoted: "Unfortunately—or perhaps fortunately—[Lefebvre]
  sketches this [spatial triad] out only in preliminary fashion, leaving us to add our own flesh, our
  own content, to rewrite it as part of our own chapter or research agenda. (Merrifield 2006, 109)"
  (pp. 196–197, folios not stated) The author concedes: "This will naturally result in my own
  interpretation and thus rearticulation of Lefebvre's concepts." (p. 197, folio not stated)
- **The author's renaming of the triad, which governs the rest of the book.** "For the sake of clarity
  and to avoid confusion between 'spatial practice,' 'representations of space,' and 'spaces of
  representation,' in the following I render these three formants as practice, conceptualization, and
  imaginary of space, respectively." (p. 198, folio not stated) **A consolidator will need this: all
  later uses of "practice / conceptualization / imaginary of space" are Lefebvre's terms renamed.**
- **Svayaṃbhū as the book's key religious concept, located in the triad.** "this 'continuance' is
  represented through the concept of svayaṃbhū (Hin.), of being 'self-existent' or having
  'self-generated.' The perpetuation of this notion requires providing the place with an 'air of
  permanency,' which, at the same time, also calls for opposing a place's dynamic becoming." (p. 199,
  folio not stated) "Svayaṃbhū is an important feature of many tīrthas (Urban 2011)" (p. 199, folio
  not stated). "With the help of the aforementioned triad, the notion of svayaṃbhū can clearly be
  located within Lefebvre's format of imaginary of space (Lefebvre 2012)." (p. 201, folio not stated)
  Its practical effect: "it makes sense that, for example, construction work at the shrine is
  backgrounded or that the promise of engaging with a 'real' or 'self-generated' tīrtha sthān is
  causing people to go on the long journey to the Balochistan shrine." (p. 201, folio not stated)
- **Bremer, quoted for the air of permanency**: "places regarded as sacred often have an air of
  permanency that transcends the contingencies of human mortality; the sacred places of a religious
  tradition will survive long after individual religious adherents have passed away. In fact, sacred
  sites serve as an enduring symbol of, and orientation for, the continuance of the religious
  community itself. (Bremer 2006, 30)" (p. 199, folio not stated)
- **Spaces of representation as the emotional register, and as a site of violence.** They are "the
  location of social 'fantasy' (Glynos and Howarth 2007), 'myth' (Laclau 1990), or 'mythohistorical
  knowledge' (Babb 2004) and establish coherency between place and group"; "It is in this realm that
  pilgrimage sites acquire the potential to become the axis mundi of a people—a process, as we have
  seen before, that builds on the rejection of other possible options. Such spaces of representation,
  therefore, might also compete with each other, at times even resulting in violent struggles over
  their interpretation." (p. 198, folio not stated) Merrifield again: "'[spaces of representation]
  don't involve too much head: they're felt more than thought' (Merrifield 2006, 110)." (p. 198,
  folio not stated)
- **The book's own limit, stated.** "Because there is no end to this production of space but rather
  only stages of contestation and decontestation between the three modes, I think it is more accurate
  to speak of the shrine's (and the annexed route's) spatiality rather than only its religious space.
  Due to the tradition's fairly recent dislocation, this work is limited to providing a snapshot of
  current developments around Hinglaj" (p. 201, folio not stated).
- **Why the stop-list is not a canon.** "The meaning of each has been repeatedly redefined over time,
  resulting in an assortment of different features and names found at particular stops… While some
  stops have gained increasing importance over the years (primarily those closer to the temple),
  others have lost significance or even slipped into complete oblivion." (p. 202, folio not stated)
  "The diverse descriptions and enumerations of important stops found in texts and oral traditions
  find their roots in the pilgrimage's long-standing lack of far-reaching institutionalization."
  (p. 204, folio not stated) The list the author is about to give is disclaimed in advance: "This
  list is not exhaustive, but at least it provides a sense of the relationship between landscape,
  route, and the individual halting places in the area." (p. 205, folio not stated)
- **Translation policy stated for one word only.** Note 12: "Ātman (Skt.) or ātmā (Hin.) is a
  sophisticated concept within Hindu cosmology and cannot be discussed here at length. However, due to
  my engagement with this particular tradition in Gujarat, in my opinion, translating it here as
  'soul' is fairly accurate." (p. 193, folio not stated)

## Doubts

- **The Hinglaj row carries no id.** Line 107 of `shrine_index.tsv` reads
  `Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir`, location `Hingol Balochistan National Park, Road، Asha
  Pura, Pakistan`, and **its id cell is empty, as are its category, principal-figure, died and
  silsila cells**. No id was invented for it; its material sits above under the literal head
  `- **(no id — Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir)**`. **A human must supply an id for this row
  before any Hinglaj fact from this book can enter an entry**, and this chunk is almost entirely
  Hinglaj material, so the defect blocks most of the chunk's value.
- **The archive row's location string contains an Arabic comma (U+060C)** after "Road", which will
  not round-trip through naive string handling. Recorded so a later worker does not "correct" it.
- **"Asha Pura" in that location string must not be merged with the book's Ashapura.** The book uses
  Ashapura in three distinct ways in this chunk: as the third paṛāv of the Jethamalani route, whose
  asthāns include Kali Mata and the Hinglaj temple (p. 203, folio not stated); as a water source that
  visitors say has dried up (p. 188, folio not stated); and as one of three historical names for the
  Mata Bhawani temple at Makli near Thatta, "Ashapura (Briggs 1938)" (p. 205, folio not stated).
  **The third of these is a different temple several hundred kilometres away from the Hinglaj
  valley, and the Goddess Ashapura is not the Goddess Hinglaj in that sentence — the book's whole
  point there is that the naming varies by community.** No id is assigned to any Ashapura here, and
  the coincidence with the archive row's location field is not treated as an identification.
- **`chandragup-baba-chandragup` — mapped, but on a spelling the book does not use.** The book writes
  `Chandrakup` (pp. 200, 203, folios not stated), the archive `Chandragup (Baba Chandragup)`. The

  convention (a) does not apply.
