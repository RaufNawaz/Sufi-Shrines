## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The organising body is named throughout as the Mandali or the HSM**: "the Mandali began renovations
  in the temple" (p. 227, folio not stated); "the construction work done by the HSM" (p. 227, folio not
  stated); "the work of the Mandali and their design of the whole temple" (p. 245, folio not stated);
  "the temple committee" (p. 245, folio not stated); "the temple organization" (p. 244, folio not
  stated). **Its full name is not spelled out anywhere in this chunk**: **(not stated)** here.
- **Custodial control over the image, stated as an institutional act.** "the HSM felt the need to change
  the image in a way that would lessen its ambiguity. Today, the temple organization interprets the
  refurbishing work as a way to crystalize the 'actual' practice at the shrine. Due to the svayaṃbhū
  characteristic of the central image, however, work at the sanctum is often downplayed publicly."
  (p. 244, folio not stated)
- **The stated purpose of the changes, in the HSM's own account.** "As one member of the HSM said in an
  interview, the constructional changes made around the image have been done with the purpose of making
  the worship (read: the ritual practices) of the Goddess easier." (p. 245, folio not stated) The eyes
  and noses "according to the organization, are intended to help the devotee approach the divinity in a
  proper way and allow his or her gaze to wander in a certain direction" (p. 245, folio not stated).
- **Two conflicting reasons for removing the liṅga, one official and one private.** "The HSM's official
  reasoning for the choice is that the liṅga caused an obstruction for pilgrims to reach the shrine;
  however, one elderly HSM volunteer from Karachi told me that the liṅga had to be removed since it was
  a very graphic image of a penis that had disturbed the visitors" (p. 238, folio not stated).
- **The havan ceremony, the melā and crowd pressure as the driver of building.** "Over the past few
  decades, the havan ceremony has become the climax of the phālgun/caitra melā, with thousands of
  visitors attending. Due to the ceremony's popularity, the inner temple could no longer accommodate the
  crowds, requiring the construction of the havan kuṃḍ outside." (p. 233, folio not stated) **No
  absolute visitor count and no melā date are given here**: **(not stated)**.
- **Access control at the garbha.** "The doors leading into the tunnel are usually locked, and visitors
  must ask the pujārī or the volunteer workers at the shrine to have them opened. At the time of the
  major melā, the doors are only open for a certain period of time in the middle of the night."
  (p. 248, folio not stated)
- **The rebirthing ritual as the pilgrimage's core.** "The primary feature of the journey to Hinglaj and
  the pilgrimage's sine qua non ritual is the crawling through a narrow U-shaped tunnel underneath the
  image of the Goddess, called the garbha (Hin. womb, lap), or sometimes the garbhaguphā (Hin.
  'womb-cave'). This circumambulation is said to be a rebirthing of the devotee out of the body of the
  Devi." (p. 247, folio not stated) "Some reports also show that after coming out of this tunnel,
  devotees used to roll on the floor and make sounds like a newborn as part of the ritual (cf. Wilford
  1851 and Jethamalani 1994)." (p. 247, folio not stated) "In most cases, however, the ritual is
  associated with purification and the repentance of sins." (p. 247, folio not stated)
- **Hart's 1839 account of the rebirthing rite, as quoted.** "At midnight all the women and children
  were directed to bathe in the pool … sent two by two, the right hand of one sister being placed on
  the leg of the other, on their hands and knees into the narrow opening in the rock under the platform.
  On coming out on the opposite side they again bathed, and then resumed their clothes." (p. 250, folio
  not stated, citing Hart 1839, 97) Ornaments were surrendered: "even the nose ring was obliged to be
  ransomed" (p. 250, folio not stated) — **a payment, though no sum is given**: **(not stated)**.
- **Parikramā named as the practice.** Hart's raised earthen platform "'between which and the rock is a
  narrow passage forming the segment of a circle' … This is the earliest available description I found
  of the practice of parikramā (Hin. circumambulation) underneath the image and around the earthen
  platform." (pp. 249–250, folio not stated)
- **Donor inscriptions exist elsewhere in the valley but not in the temple.** "while many new pilgrimage
  shelters and water tanks inside the valley bear a visible inscription somewhere mentioning who or
  which organization donated the money for its construction, aside from the ambiguous inscriptions
  mentioned earlier in this chapter, no such signs are found inside the temple" (p. 249, folio not
  stated).
- **Funding politics as a constraint on the custodians.** Cooperation with the Balochistan government
  "creates an element of uncertainty within the conceptual design and limits the agency of the HSM, for
  example, by the difficulty of gathering funds for roads and shelters or a change in political
  patronage after an election" (p. 245, folio not stated). The wider list of actors: "the provincial
  government, the federal government, Hindu welfare organizations, authors, international clientele,
  television documentaries, and many others" (p. 247, folio not stated).
- **Measurements of the older structure, as quoted from the travellers.** Hart describes the image as
  resting on a 20 foot by 12 foot "mud edifice" (p. 250, folio not stated, citing Hart 1839, 95) and
  the temple as "a mud edifice that contained two rooms in which music was played", with "a doorway
  [that] opened to another room where the image of Hinglaj was found" (p. 249, folio not stated, citing
  Hart 1839, 95). Goldsmid: "The shrine was on a kind of raised mud platform, perhaps three feet from
  the level." (p. 250, folio not stated, citing Goldsmid 1863, 208)
- **Goldsmid on the temple's architecture.** It lacked "architectural magnificence or beauty" and the
  wooden construction "appeared as something that 'an infant taste for architecture would create out of
  wooden toy-bricks'" (p. 234, folio not stated, citing Goldsmid 1863, 207); "in the middle of the
  nineteenth century, [it] consisted of two rooms" (p. 234, folio not stated).
- **Khan Baloch on the 1961 fabric.** He "refers to the architecture of the temple and the platform
  (Sin. chap) inside, which had already been lined with timber by the time of his visit. Atop the
  platform were scattered several instruments used by the pilgrims for pūjā; Baloch estimated them to be
  around half a century old (Khan Baloch 1970, 132)." (p. 240, folio not stated)
- **Abro on the pre-renovation liṅgas.** "He mentions that on one side, six or seven liṅgas were kept,
  and that another one, about a foot tall, was located at the base of the platform, which is called
  'sadā śivo' (Sin.) (Abro 1991, 62f.)." (p. 241, folio not stated)

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The Hingol legend, paraphrased by the author from three written sources** — "The following is a
  paraphrase from different written sources (Abro 1991; Khatri 1973; Chhabria 2000)" (p. 229, folio not
  stated). "The demon Vichitra had two sons. One of them was called Hingol and the other was named
  Sindur." (p. 229, folio not stated) Sindur loots China; the people of China, "worshippers of Shiva",
  petition him; Shiva promises that "within three days, Ganesha would come and relieve them from their
  afflictions"; "After three days, `Ganesh`[text-layer?] [Ganesha] came indeed, and killed the tyrant
  Sindur." (pp. 229–230, folio not stated)
- **Hingol's boon and his death.** He "retreated to a mountain, raised his hands toward the sky, and
  stood on one toe for thousands of years"; Brahma grants that "he could not be killed in war, by any
  weapon, or by any human being and also not under the sky nor on the earth" (pp. 229–230, folio not
  stated). Parvati appears as a beautiful woman, is pursued from the Himalaya to the River Indus and
  into Sindhudesh, and "at the place where the shrine is located today, the Goddess absorbed him into
  her body" (p. 230, folio not stated).
- **The naming of the place, in the Goddess's own words as the book gives them.** Hingol's last wish:
  "You should remain here at this very place under my name and fulfill all wishes that people bring to
  you!"; the Goddess answers "From now on I will be called Hinglaj Devi, and this place should be my
  place of worship. I will stay here forever with Hingol." "Since this day, both of their faces are
  found inside the cave." (p. 230, folio not stated)
- **The author's own placing of this legend.** "This story, which is clearly a reference to the legend
  of Narasimha in the Bhāgavata Purāṇa, however, is rarely heard at the shrine today. Devotees often
  simply state that the other face is Shiva's or Rama's." (p. 230, folio not stated) "no particular
  myth had, as of the end of my research, fully solidified around this enigmatic part of the statue"
  (p. 230, folio not stated).
- **The skull tradition (Pīṭhanirṇaya).** "This garnishing of the stone heaps corresponds to a certain
  interpretation of the shrine from the Pīṭhanirṇaya (seventeenth to eighteenth century), where it is
  written that the upper part of the head, the brahmarandhra (Skt. a suture or aperture in the crown of
  the head), of the Goddess Sati fell at Hinglaj (Sircar 1973, 35). According to this interpretation,
  one of the globular stones on the eastern side is Sati's skull, which came to rest there after Vishnu
  cut her body into several pieces." (p. 229, folio not stated)
- **The navel tradition (Dakṣayajñabhaṅga), given as a competing account.** Mukundarama "instead states
  that the navel rather than the Devi's brahmarandhra has fallen at Hinglaj (Sircar 1973, 33)"
  (pp. 230–231, folio not stated); "since the womb (garbha) is directly underneath the sanctum it must
  have been the navel that has fallen in Hinglaj" (p. 231, folio not stated). **The author states which

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The central claim of the section: the renovation was an act of interpretation, not maintenance.**
  "the manner in which the HSM carried out the renovations significantly changed the way visitors are
  able to approach the image, which has subsequently also influenced ritual practices at the shrine"
  (p. 229, folio not stated); "the changes around the mūrti are paradigmatic for the politics of
  'Hinduization' around the construction of religious space at Hinglaj (see also Chapter 6)" (p. 236,
  folio not stated).
- **The method is Critical Discourse Analysis, named and sourced.** "I wish to borrow from Critical
  Discourse Analysis (CDA), mainly associated with the work of Norman Fairclough (2001b) and Ruth Wodak
  (2009). Studies in CDA concern themselves with how information is conferred by the absence of
  details, implicit statements, and propositions (Riggins 1997, 10–11)." (p. 236, folio not stated) The
  operative move is Fairclough's foregrounding/backgrounding (Fairclough 2001a) (pp. 229, 236, folio
  not stated), and the author converts Riggins's question about texts into one about images (p. 236,
  folio not stated).
- **The finding that follows from it.** "the shape of the rebuilt sanctum suggests that those parts that
  did not benefit the prevailing Hindu interpretation of the image—or the HSM's version of it—were
  backgrounded, while others that are more in concert with this concept were foregrounded. Through this
  process, the physical shape of the oblong stone, described in older accounts and frequently
  interpreted as the top of a grave, was made less prominent." (p. 236, folio not stated)
- **A careful negative: absence in the sources is not absence in practice.** "the absence of a
  particular interpretation of the image in the given sources does not necessarily mean that such an
  interpretation was not assumed by some devotees who came to the shrine; however, it does denote that
  before certain circumstances led to the shrine becoming a central stage for Pakistan's Hindu
  communities, there was no obvious and self-explanatory central image." (p. 244, folio not stated)
- **The colonial-source caveat, stated in the author's own voice.** "the descriptions quoted from Hart,
  Goldsmid, and Stein are not simple phenomenological accounts within a vacuum, but represent a certain
  discursive background in which these descriptions were written. In other words, all these authors
  operated as colonial masters under the guidance of locals or religious experts who influenced their
  writing." (p. 237, folio not stated) He adds that full recovery of intention is impossible "since not
  even an extensive account of the social context could provide the means to fully understand their
  authors' intention (Skinner 1969, 46)" (p. 237, folio not stated).
- **The observed disappearance of a whole register of description.** "These descriptions of the sanctum
  as 'tomb shaped' or as a 'Muslim grave' do not appear in current accounts of the image, where such
  alternative interpretations are no longer present." (p. 237, folio not stated)
- **The Lefebvrian trialectic, applied as the section's frame.** "Lefebvre stresses in his work the idea
  of a mutual dependency of practices, conceptualization, and imaginary of space that together produce
  meaningful sites. I have suggested understanding this interdependent trinity as (ritual) practices,
  conceptual space, and religious imaginary" (p. 244, folio not stated); parts of the mūrti that fit
  "in Lefebvre's terminology—the 'imaginary of space' at the shrine were emphasized" (p. 236, folio not
  stated). The three numbered moments are set out at pp. 245–246 (folio not stated).
- **Hegemony over the conceptual space, flagged forward.** "the aspect of hegemony is important since
  not all groups exercise equal control over the conceptual space of the image—this will become
  particularly important in the next chapter" (p. 245, folio not stated).
- **svayaṃbhū as the claim that denies the whole analysis.** "the notion of svayaṃbhū—the image's (and
  in fact the whole valley's) quality of having generated out of itself. At times, this notion is in
  conflict with the two other modes—especially the conceptual space—since it denies human agency at the
  shrine. To what extent the image has been altered over the past two hundred years, therefore, was
  often a moot point when talking to devotees and organizations about Hinglaj." (p. 246, folio not
  stated)
- **A methodological limit the author states about his own snapshot.** "the current perception of the
  religious spaces inside the Hinglaj Valley can only provide a snapshot of such continuous processes of
  spatialization. There is, however, also a state of affairs that increasingly tends toward an
  equilibrium in the trialectic" (p. 246, folio not stated), with "decontestation" (Freeden 2006) as the
  term for what institutions now achieve (p. 246, folio not stated).
- **The argument from photographs, and its limit.** "such changes have rendered accurate assessments of
  the figure's age all but impossible" (p. 232, folio not stated); the author therefore reads
  travelogues and "a few old photographs" (p. 232, folio not stated) and concludes the image "actually
  had a different shape just a few decades ago" (pp. 232–233, folio not stated).
- **A self-correction against the author's own evidence.** Of Stein's 1943 photograph: "No one from the
  HSM or from the local villages could remember having seen the image in such a form during their first
  visits to the shrine, and it was not possible to find anyone today who could recall a śiva liṅga on
  this spot." (p. 238, folio not stated)
