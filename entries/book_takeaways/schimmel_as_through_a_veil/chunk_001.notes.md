## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Samāʿ as a dated institution.** `as early as in the mid-ninth century, the institution of
  samac` [text-layer?: samāʿ]`, mystical concert, was known at least in Baghdad, and verses were
  recited or sung in connection with music and often whirling dance. This was a starting point for
  one kind of Sufi poetry.` (p. 27, folio 17).
- **Formative-period gatherings before the orders.** `Even during its formative period, during the
  ninth and early tenth centuries, when only a small number of worthy disciples gathered at the
  masters' houses and regular fraternities had not yet developed, it was common to discuss mystical
  problems among initiated friends` (p. 27, folio 17). Novices `would be warned not to care too
  much for the turuhat afi-tiiiffya` [text-layer?: reading uncertain; see `## Doubts`]`, the
  half-technical, verbose talk of the Sufis` (p. 27, folio 17).
- **Poetry as performed shrine-adjacent practice.** Rumi's rhythmical forms `are thus much closer
  to popular poetry as it was used in the dervish tekkes during the samac` [text-layer?: samāʿ]
  `than to the ideals of classical, courtly poetry` (p. 17, folio 7). His order is named `the
  Whirling Dervishes`, and Schimmel notes the possibility of tracking `his influence on the poetry
  written in Persian or Turkish among the members of his order` (p. 17, folio 7).
- **Vernacular mystical poetry is sung by order members.** `It should always be kept in mind that
  this poetry is mostly sung by members of Sufi orders and fraternities` (p. 18, folio 8) — the
  chunk's most directly usable statement for shrine practice, though it names no shrine.
- **Transmission by illiterate performers.** `illiterate bards are also found, who took up the
  popular tradition founded by the masters, and helped the ideas of mystical poetry reach into
  popular literature, to lullabies and wedding songs, riddles and even jokes. But the songs of
  illiterate dervishes and their styles of recitation have only rarely been studied in detail.`
  (p. 18, folio 8).
- **Epigraph convention.** `even the worldly poets usually headed their collections of verse with
  the epigraph "In the name of God," thus later writers, especially in the Persianate tradition,
  began every work with hymns to praise the Creator and the beloved Prophet.` (p. 22, folio 12).
- No urs, mela, langar, offering, endowment, custodianship, revenue figure, visitor number,
  dispute, building date or state involvement appears anywhere in this chunk — `(not stated)`
  throughout.


## Legends and miracles (karamat)

Reported as the book reports them, i.e. as motifs of poetry and of Koranic narrative, not as the
author's own claims.

- **The miʿrāj as proof of Muhammad's uniqueness.** `the experience of the micriij`
  [text-layer?: miʿrāj]`, the heavenly journey, serves to prove the uniqueness of Muhammad's
  experience: he returned from the Divine Presence, which he endured without fainting, while Moses
  swooned at the vision of only so much as the Burning Bush` (pp. 19–20, folio 9 and folio 10).
- **Muhammad's miraculous birth and ascension as poetic topics.** `notwithstanding the endless
  descriptions of his miraculous birth, or his ascension into the presence of God` (p. 19,
  folio 9); and the programme of `collecting poems about Muhammad's birth, marriage, family life,
  miracles, victories, his ascension, and his family` (p. 20, folio 10).
- **The scent of Yusuf's shirt.** Rumi `compared his poem to the scent which makes the absent
  Friend present, and gives information about him (as the scent of Yusuf 's shirt gave good tidings
  to his blind father)` (p. 13, folio 3).
- **Inspiration as the poets' defence.** `Regarding themselves as inspired by some higher power,
  they gave lively descriptions of the "birth" of poetry which poured out spontaneously.` (p. 22,
  folio 12); and the maxim `"God has hidden treasures under the tongues of the poets"` (p. 22,
  folio 12).
- **Poetry as lawful magic.** `Was not poetry lastly sihr haliil` [text-layer?: siḥr ḥalāl]`,
  "lawful magic"?` (p. 24, folio 14).


## Arguments and interpretations

- **The historiographical opening thesis.** `THE MYSTICAL POETRY of the Islamic countries,
  particularly Iran, has attracted European scholars for some 200 years; it was, in fact, the first
  source for their acquaintance with the spiritual aspects of Islam.` (p. 11, folio not stated).
- **Three named hostile precursors, with dates as printed.** `Friedrich A. D. Tholuck's verdict in
  his thesis of 1821, Ssufismus sive theosophia persarum pantheistica, which paved the way for the
  constantly repeated misunderstanding that Sufism was nothing but pantheism` (p. 11, folio not
  stated); Trumpp in `1867` on the `Risiil6` [text-layer?: Risālō] (p. 11, folio not stated); and
  `Another thirty years later, Rene` [text-layer?: René] `Basset spoke in the introduction to his
  very useful translation of Busiri's` [text-layer?: Būṣīrī's] `Burda of "Soufisme qui
  commen�ait` [text-layer?: commençait] `des lors a exercer son influence si nefaste`
  [text-layer?: néfaste] `sur la poesie` [text-layer?: poésie] `orientale" (p. x).` (p. 11, folio
  not stated).
- **Why the mystical strand appealed to outsiders.** `The mystical aspects of Islam understandably
  appealed more to non-Muslim readers than did the legal and dogmatic aspects of Islam` (pp. 11–12,
  folio not stated at p. 11; folio 2 at p. 12).
- **A short canon of the scholarship.** `Reynold Alleyne Nicholson`, credited with `excellent
  editions of classical Sufi works in both Arabic and Persian`; `Louis Massignon`, `though often
  difficult to read`; and `Among the German-speaking pioneers in the field of Islamic mystical
  poetry the name of Hellrnut Ritter` [text-layer?: Hellmut Ritter] `outshines all others` (p. 12,
  folio 2). Also `the Scottish clergyman William Hastie`, who regarded Rumi as `an antidote to Omar
  Khayyam's frivolous verses, which were so amazingly successful in their very free renderings by
  FitzGerald`, and `Riickert's` [text-layer?: Rückert's] `free but congenial translations` (p. 12,
  folio 2).
- **Metaphor and the theory of mystical language.** Schimmel endorses `Evelyn Underhill's
  Mystidsm` [text-layer?: Mysticism] as `still outstanding`, holding that for Underhill `mysticism
  is a practical life process, in which the One is the object of love` (pp. 12–13, folio 2 and
  folio 3). She then argues that Karsten Harries's claim — `all metaphor that is more than an
  abbreviation for more proper speech gestures toward what transcends languages; thus metaphor
  implies lack` — `says in scientific terms what Rumi meant` (p. 13, folio 3). She cites `Hermann
  Pongs` on `metaphor` versus `symbol` and `Bild` in German (p. 13, folio 3), and glosses
  `Einbildung` as `coined by the medieval German mystics to point to the act of forming images of
  (imagining) the Divine in the mystic's mind` (p. 14, folio 4).
- **A Christian comparison drawn deliberately.** `The medieval Christian mystical poet found his
  images, Bilder, in "God's two books, in Nature and Scripture,"` and Alan de Lille's verse
  (quoted in Latin, six lines) `can be applied to a large part of medieval Islamic mystical poetry
  as well` (p. 14, folio 4). Schimmel calls `Hans Heinrich Schaeder` the author of `the best
  description of this lyrical poetry` (p. 14, folio 4).
- **Periodisation and geography of the tradition.** `Islamic mystical poetry is, at least during
  the first seven centuries, many-faceted. It begins with the simple ascetic songs of the early
  mystics and soon develops into chaste and lucid verses` (p. 14, folio 4), `finally reaching full
  bloom in the grand odes of Ibn al-Farid` (p. 15, folio 5). In Iran the forms `grew first in the
  eastern fringe of the country, present-day Afghanistan, and later in the central south, in
  Shiraz, with its influence soon extending to India and later to Turkey` (p. 15, folio 5).
- **Stated exclusions from the book's scope** (useful to a consolidator deciding what this book can
  be cited for): Nasir-i Khusrau's philosophical poetry, the `marthiya` complex, and Hurufi poetry
  are all named and set aside (p. 15, folio 5).
- **Why Rumi is singled out, and the claim that he is not quite "classical."** `it is because of
  his tremendous influence on most writers in subsequent centuries` (p. 15, folio 5); `Although
  nobody has ever attempted to establish a chronology of his more than 40,000 lyrical verses, one
  can perhaps draw a few guidelines` (p. 15, folio 5). Then: `I feel that he does not belong
  completely to the "classical" Persian tradition` (p. 16, folio 6), because `Rumi's ghazals usually
  constitute a unity` where `the ideal ghazal … consists of verses which follow each other like
  pearls on a string without needing a "logical" sequence` (pp. 16–17, folio 6 and folio 7), and
  because `Rumi's vocabulary is taken from all walks of life and he does not shun crude or obscene
  words` (p. 17, folio 7). Conclusion: `Thus, Rumi … belongs, in a certain way, more to the popular
  than to the "high" tradition.` (p. 17, folio 7), a reading she attributes in part to `The noted
  Turkish scholar Abdulbaki Golpi'.narli` [text-layer?: Abdülbaki Gölpınarlı]`, who is certainly
  one of the greatest experts in the Mevlevi tradition` (p. 17, folio 7). She also compares Rumi to
  `Beethoven's music` and cites `Dr. Samuel Johnson's statement comparing Shakespeare to other,
  truly "classical" poets` (p. 16, folio 6).
- **The vernacular argument — the chunk's most archive-relevant thesis.** `While Western scholars
  devoted much time to the study of mystical poetry written in Arabic and even more to that in
  Persian, and to a lesser extent that in Turkish, the mystical poetry in the regional languages
  has largely been neglected.` (p. 18, folio 8). Schimmel refuses the term `folk poetry` `because
  as much as this literature is meant for the masses, many of its authors were literate people, who
  were proud of their acquaintance with Islamic learning and sometimes liked to show off` (p. 18,
  folio 8). The mechanism she proposes: each such mystic `knew that the nonurban Muslims needed
  religious instruction in an idiom which they could easily understand, and in images with which
  they could identify. It is largely thanks to them that languages like Panjabi, Sindhi, Pashto,
  etc. grew into vehicles for poetry and literature.` (p. 18, folio 8). And the resulting
  uniformity: `popular poetry all over Islamdom is very similar in its character, but it still
  preserves the local peculiarities which reflect the landscape out of which it grew` (pp. 18–19,
  folio 8 and folio 9).
- **Eros- versus nomos-oriented religion.** Following `Joseph N. Bell`, the love-feeling of
  mystical poetry is `typical of the eros-oriented type of religion, which basically contrasts with
  the nomos-oriented core of Islam` (p. 19, folio 9); and `Logically, the nomos-oriented attitude
  becomes most conspicuous in poetry addressed to the Prophet` (p. 19, folio 9). She grounds the
  distinction in the shahada: it is `the second half of the profession of faith, i.e., "Muhammad is
  the messenger of God," which makes Islam a distinct religion` (p. 19, folio 9).
- **Naʿt poetry as the key to Muslim piety.** `To understand Muslim piety in full, one has to take
  into account the poetry written in honor of the Prophet, for much of the "mystical" poetry
  composed in all Islamic languages bears strong similarities to mystical poetry in other religious
  traditions; but in the trusting and dedicated songs and hymns written in honor of the final
  Prophet … Islamic literature has gained a very distinctive flavor.` (p. 20, folio 10).
- **Chapter One's thesis: the Koranic verdict against poets and the Sufi answers to it.** `THE LAST
  VERSES of Sura 26 (v. 224 ff.) in the Koran contain a sharp criticism of the poets "who wander
  distracted in every valley and say what they do not do."` (p. 21, folio not stated), supported by
  `alleged traditions` condemning poetry as `nafth ash-shaitan, "what Satan has spit out"` (p. 21,
  folio not stated). Schimmel reads the prohibition as directed at `poetry in praise of sensual
  love` and of wine, and notes that `The utterances of Indo-Muslim reformers of the late nineteenth
  century against flirtatious or dreamy Urdu poetry echo the Koranic and Prophetic aversion to
  "worldly" poetry` (p. 21, folio not stated). The counter-moves she catalogues: Rumi's own
  disavowal, the inspiration defence, the `IJadfth` [text-layer?: ḥadīth] `inna min ash-shier
  '1ikma` [text-layer?: inna min ash-shiʿr ḥikma] `("There is wisdom in poetry")`, and the maxim
  `"the best poetry is that which is most untrue"` (p. 22, folio 12). Residual unease persists:
  biographers wrote `"the learned scholar condescended to committing to paper some verse, which is
  beneath his dignity."` (p. 23, folio 13).
- **Prose over poetry in Arabic, and Massignon on iʿjāz.** `in the Arabic tradition proper
  religious prose literature is on the whole more expressive than most religious poetry`, the
  masterpieces named being `Hallaj' s Kitiib af-tawiisin` [text-layer?: Kitāb aṭ-Ṭawāsīn]`, the
  Nahj al-balagha, and Ibn c Ata'ullah's Ijikam` [text-layer?: Ibn ʿAṭāʾullāh's Ḥikam] (p. 23,
  folio 13). `Louis Massignon has correctly underlined the central place of the icjiiz qur'iinique`
  [text-layer?: iʿjāz qurʾānique]`: the most essential feature of Islam is, in his view, the Arabic
  language of the Koran.` (p. 23, folio 13). Schimmel's causal claim: it was the Koran's
  `linguistic unsurpassability` together with `its verdict against poets` and `the Arabs' pride in
  the great heritage of pre-Islamic poetry which slowed down the development of Islamic Arabic
  poetry in the full sense of the word` (p. 24, folio 14).
- **Goethe on poet and prophet.** The tension between revelation and poetry is `stated in a
  beautiful image by Goethe in the Noten und Abhandlungen zum West-Ostlichen`
  [text-layer?: West-Östlichen] `Divan, where he says that poet and prophet are both obsessed by
  the same spirit, but while the poet squanders his talents in multifaceted, colorful verse, the
  prophet concentrates on the one truth` (p. 23, folio 13).
- **From asceticism to love mysticism, dated.** `around the year 800. This was the period when
  early Islamic asceticism, with its austere and world-detesting outlook, began to turn into love
  mysticism`, led by `Jae far as-Sadiq` [text-layer?: Jaʿfar aṣ-Ṣādiq] and `Rabic a
  al-c Adawiyya` [text-layer?: Rābiʿa al-ʿAdawiyya] (p. 25, folio 15). Early Sufism was
  `Persiinlichkeitsmystik` [text-layer?: Persönlichkeitsmystik]`, a personal relation between God
  and man`, and `voluntaristic mysticism, aiming at the complete unification of man's will with the
  Divine will, not yet a gnostic approach as it became later` (p. 25, folio 15).
- **The Koran as the matrix of mystical language.** Citing `Pere Nwyia`, a complete `qoranisation
  de la memoire` [text-layer?: mémoire] was achieved (p. 25, folio 15); `The experience of the love
  that exists between God and man was found expressed in Sura 5/59: "He loves them and they love
  Him"; and slowly, "the language of experience became," as Nwyia says, "the language of love."`
  (p. 26, folio 16). The Moses episode `formed the first step to the Sufi theory of the shathiyat,
  the theopathic locutions` (p. 26, folio 16).
- **The constitutive problem of mystical poetry: solitude against sociability.** `The mystic is
  basically a solitary wayfarer` who calls not `"O my Lord!"` but `"O my Beloved!"` (p. 26,
  folio 16); against which `The poet, however, is a sociable person, "wandering in every valley"; he
  is the mouthpiece of the society` (p. 27, folio 17). Schimmel poses the problem as `How then could
  a Sufi be a poet?` (p. 27, folio 17) and resolves it through the Sufis' wish `to attract people`
  and through samāʿ.
- **A three-part typology of early Arabic mystical verse.** `descriptive poems, in which the Sufis
  tried to tell of their experiences and their mystical "states" (ahwtil` [text-layer?: aḥwāl]`);
  technical poems with often complicated word plays, puns, and allusions; and popular poems which,
  as will become clear, often prefigure the mystical poetry in the non-Arabic areas.` (p. 28,
  folio 18).
- **A candid aesthetic judgement on Rabiʿa.** `Such lines cannot be called great poetry-the
  author's feeling is stronger than her art.` (p. 28, folio 18).
- **Form history: the Sufis adapted the short Abbasid genres, not the qaṣīda.** `instead of-or
  besides-high-sounding Bedouin poetry, which culminates in the long, sonorous qas.fda`
  [text-layer?: qaṣīda]`, … shorter love poems and wine poems and especially the art of descriptive
  poetry (was.f` [text-layer?: waṣf]`) became fashionable in the cities of Iraq, Syria, and Egypt.
  It was this kind of poetry which the early Sufis adapted to their own purposes; as far as we can
  see the classical form of the qas.fda` [text-layer?: qaṣīda] `with its traditional framework was
  used by them only much later.` (p. 29, folio 19).
- **The `dalāʾil` genre and its prose model.** `Long poems are devoted to the dalii'il`
  [text-layer?: dalāʾil]`, the signs by which the true mystical lover can be recognized` (p. 30,
  folio 20), and `These ppems` [text-layer?: poems] `take up a prose form which was sometimes
  regarded as a hadith or even a hadith qudsi, such as, "Verily God has servants who . . ."`
  (p. 30, folio 20). Schimmel's verdict: the prose is `in much more poetical words than in most of
  these rhyming exercises` (p. 30, folio 20).
- **The technical-vocabulary genre and tauḥīd.** A genre arose `in which the Sufis skillfully used
  their recently developed technical vocabulary and played on all the possible derivations of
  Arabic words, with their three radical consonants, to attain a highly complicated web of words
  which could be disentangled only by the initiated` (p. 31, folio 21), and `the experience of the
  central concept of Islam, that of taulJid` [text-layer?: tauḥīd]`, was hidden under verbal play`
  (p. 31, folio 21). The chunk breaks off mid-exposition of the declarative and factitive meanings
  of `tauhid` [text-layer?: tauḥīd] and of `Islamic predestination` (p. 31, folio 21) — see
  `## Doubts`.

  Pakistan-referring phrase in the chunk is the collective `the various languages of the

  `Panjabi, Sindhi, Pashto` (p. 18, folio 8), which are languages and not places. Ruling (a) is
