## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Naming practice.** The Prophet's name "or one of its equivalents (Ahmad, Mustafa, Taha, etc.) was
  commonly given to boys, but in order to pre­serve both its sanctity and its power, the word
  Muhammad is vocalized, in some languages, differently when applied to nor­mal human beings (thus
  Mehmet in Turkey, Mihammad or Mahammad in Morocco), while the full and correct vocaliza­tion is
  preserved for the Prophet" (p. 192, folio 174). In later times "he, like God, had 99 names", and
  "Sometimes, even more honorific names-up to 200!­were invented for him" (p. 192, folio 174).
  Sacdi's eulogy "has also contributed to Islamic nomenclature, par­ticularly in India, for all four
  qualities-qasfm, jasfm, nasfm and wasfm [text-layer?: qasīm, jasīm, nasīm, wasīm]-are found as
  proper names, sometimes in compounds, such as Jasimuddin" (p. 214, folio 196); "yiisfn (Sura 36) or
  tiihii (Sura 20) ... became proper names in the Muslim world" (p. 214, folio 196).
- **Blessing on the Prophet as a devotional office.** "the !ja/awat-i shanJ [text-layer?: ṣalawāt-i
  sharīf] or durud came to constitute an important element of the dhikr in some mystical groups, and
  is repeated hundreds of times as a protective prayer by innumerable faithful" (p. 195, folio 177),
  on the strength of a tradition that "God would bless ten times a person who blessed the Prophet
  once" (p. 195, folio 177). "Some reciters would repeat after each of its lines a blessing over the
  Prophet" when reciting the Burda (p. 204, folio 186).
- **The `maulid` as a calendar festival.** "Celebrations of his maulid, birthday, on 12 Rabic al-awwal
  [text-layer?: Rabīʿ al-awwal], the third lunar month, are mentioned first about 1200 and soon
  spread into the various Islamic lands" (p. 197, folio 179). "In Morocco the Merinid ruler Abu Yac
  qub Yusuf introduced maulid cele­brations in 1291, while the Mamluks in Egypt celebrated the
  Prophet's birthday with great splendor during the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries" (p. 197,
  folio 179); "In Turkey, such celebrations must have been known in the early four­teenth century"
  (p. 197, folio 179).
- **How a `maulid` is kept.** "One lights candles, puts on good clothes; sometimes incense is burned
  and from the Maghreb to Turkey and East Africa it is customary to distribute sweets" (p. 199,
  folio 181). "In Egypt, the maulid of the Shadhili Sufi al-Munawi is particularly well loved, and is
  often played on records" (p. 199, folio 181). Chelebi's poem "is still sung on the Prophet's
  birth­day, but also at the anniversary of a bereavement or in fulfill­ment of a vow" (p. 197,
  folio 179) — the chunk's only mention of a death anniversary or a vow as an occasion for recitation.
  During the Turkish recitation, "when this line is recited in the Turkish mevlad [text-layer?:
  mevlud] all those present gently touch their neighbor' s back" (p. 198, folio 180).
- **The `maulid` in Indo-Pakistan.** "In Indo-Pakistan maulid literature proper is rather recent, for
  the 12 Rabic al-awwal was formerly mainly remembered as the day of Mu­hammad's death. Major maulid
  in the Subcontinent were gen­erally composed in Persian; the genre was introduced into Bengali not
  long ago... In Panjabi and Sindhi, the miracles of the Prophet's birth are told in balladesk or
  lyrical forms" (p. 199, folio 181). Separately, the "holy geography" of Mecca and Medina "is also
  found in poetry composed in honor of the Prophet in Indo-Pakistan, in sf]Jarjfs [text-layer?:
  sīharfīs] and biirahmasa [text-layer?: bārahmāsa]" (p. 205, folio 187).
- **Reform and reaction around the festival.** "some modernists have at­tacked the custom of reciting
  this kind of poetry" (p. 199, folio 181), and "Lately however maulid celebrations everywhere are
  put in the service of reformist movements and serve to remind the Muslims not so much of the
  traditional miracles but of the wonderful, heroic and nation-building acts of their beloved Prophet"
  (p. 200, folio 182).
- **Silsila and genealogy.** "Some maulid con­tain the genealogy of the Prophet, for the nobility of
  his de­scent is important; this feature is also found in the Sufi tradi­tion where the silsila of
  the Pir is led back to Muhammad and from him to Adam" (p. 200, folio 182).
- **Devotional objects, images and material culture.** The `hilya` descriptions "were later written in
  fine calligraphy to adorn the houses of the pious, and were in turn again elaborated in poetical
  form in Arabic, Turkish, and Urdu" (p. 194, folio 176). Burda verses "were believed to cure certain
  ailments or to protect from plague or poverty, and were therefore used in amulets or written on
  walls" (p. 204, folio 186). "Pictures of Buraq are found today on trucks and tank waggons in
  Pakistan" (p. 201, folio 183). The ascension "inspired marvelous miniatures by the artists of Iran
  and Central Asia" (p. 201, folio 183). Poets "sometimes specialized in praising certain items
  connected with the Prophet, such as his sandal or his footprint" (p. 206, folio 188).
- **Transmission, printing and the book trade.** The Burda "was soon translated into almost every
  Islamic language... The poem is available in Turkish, Urdu, Pashto, and Panjabi. In the sixteenth
  century a Malay trans­lation was produced, while several Swahili recensions of more recent date are
  in use in East Africa. The Burda has often been printed, and fine illuminated manuscripts exist in
  Cairo and elsewhere" (pp. 204–205, folios 186–187). "in Egypt alone not less than eighty quintuplet
  versions (takhmfs [text-layer?: takhmīs]) were made out of it, not to men­tion the variations in
  the non-Arabic countries like India" (p. 205, folio 187); the splitting-and-filling technique is
  named "(tashfir [text-layer?: tashṭīr])" (p. 205, folio 187).
- **Recitation in ritual settings.** "Recitations of the Burda in a festive set­ting are still
  practised in the Deccan" (p. 205, folio 187). Rumi's most famous eulogy "is sung in c ltri's
  beautiful tune, at the beginning of the ritual dance of the Mevlevis" (p. 212, folio 194). Sana'i
  "improvised" his finest eulogy "in a religious meeting" (p. 208, folio 190), and Shibli's verse "is
  still sung today in religious meetings to praise the Prophet" (p. 195, folio 177).


## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- A tradition promises that on Doomsday it will be announced: "He who is called Muhammad should get
  up and enter Para­dise thanks to the blessing of the Prophet's name!." (p. 192, folio 174).
- Before the Prophet was begotten "his father had been seen with a white light shining from his
  forehead", and "this light-later inter­preted as the creative, pre-eternal light-was carried in
  Amina's womb until it illuminated the world to the palaces of Bostra in Syria" (pp. 197–198,
  folios 179–180). A Swahili preacher explained the light "It was 'like television, for it brought
  nearby and showed clearly cities far far away'" (p. 198, folio 180).
- At the birth "A white swan came to touch Amina's back", and "c Asiya [text-layer?: ʿĀsiya]
  (Pharaoh's believing wife), Mary, and a houri appeared to act as midwives. The Prophet was born
  purified and circumcised and was wrapped in white wool and green silk, and every­thing created
  began to greet him with the great Merhaba (Wel­come)" (p. 198, folio 180).
- "The idea that Muhammad radiated light and did not cast any shade is, in a certain way, an
  exteriori­zation of his cit?ma [text-layer?: ʿiṣma], his freedom from faults and sins" (p. 196,
  folio 178).
- The "miracle of 'splitting the moon'" — "a miracle by which, according to legend, some contemporary
  Indian kings were converted to Islam" (p. 196, folio 178). "The speaking gazelle and the sighing
  palm trunk equally belong to the repertoire of popular preachers and so­phisticated poets all over
  the Muslim world" (p. 196, folio 178).
- The ascension: the Prophet flies "on his mount Buraq with a female head and a peacock tail"
  (p. 201, folio 183); Gabriel must remain outside, "like a nightingale separated from his rose" and
  says "If I would go one step further my wings would get burnt" (p. 202, folio 184); "It was said
  that Muhammad's bed was still warm when he returned, and the water in the pitcher which had tumbled
  over at his departure had not yet leaked out completely" (p. 202, folio 184); "The Indian tale of
  the seeker, who, submerged in the river, lived through a whole life in a few moments, was sometimes
  applied to explain the Prophet's experience" (p. 203, folio 185).
- The Burda's own origin miracle: "the ailing poet composed it in honor of Muhammad and was
  mi­raculously cured by a dream in which the Prophet put his cloak, burda, over his shoulders as he

  recitations all over the Islamic world" (p. 199, folio 181). Qadi ʿIyad's `Ash-Shifāʾ` "assumed
  al­most the character of a sacred book, charged with protective power" (pp. 196–197, folios
  178–179).
- Common Muslims "were certain that he was able to guide them and even cure their ailments through
  his apparition in dreams" (p. 195, folio 177).


## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Hassan ibn Thabit is the seed of the whole tradition.** "Thus, Hassan ibn Thabit's verse indeed
  contains the germs of most later poetry in honor of the Prophet" (p. 194, folio 176); the author
  traces the `hilya`, the intercession motif and the light motif back to him (pp. 193–194, folios
  175–176).
- **The veneration of Muhammad is chiefly a Sufi development.** "While the common Muslims deeply
  trusted in the Prophet and his message... the veneration of Muhammad as an almost super­human
  figure grew predominantly in Sufi circles" (p. 195, folio 177), and the Light of Muhammad was
  elaborated "possibly in connection with trends in Shia imamology" (p. 195, folio 177) — the author
  hedges this genealogy herself with "possibly".
- **A measured lull between 900 and 1200.** "Between 900 and 1200 one witnesses a certain dearth of
  outstanding religious poetry in the Arabic world although im­portant theological works were
  composed" (p. 196, folio 178).
- **Birth legends of a religion's founder are a comparative category.** "it is common to surround the
  birth of the founder of a religion with garlands of wonder­ful events. The motif of light is central
  in them because often the birth of a saviour (Hei/sbringer) [text-layer?: Heilsbringer] and his
  epiphany fall to­gether" — with parallels drawn to "the story of Bethlehem", "the wonderful birth
  of the Buddha as told in the Lalita Vi1?tara [text-layer?: Lalita Vistara]", Zoroaster, and "the
  fanciful stories of Krishna' s birth" (p. 200, folio 182).
- **Universalising of the `maulid`.** "allusions to the local Arabic environment and typically Arabic
  traditions, as they are found in the oldest part of the srra [text-layer?: sīra], were left out in
  order to give the maulid literature a more universal appeal" (p. 200, folio 182).
- **Love of the Prophet is not erotic love.** "this love never enters the realm of cishq [text-layer?:
  ʿishq], erotic love, but remains in the sphere of respect. It resembles the loving veneration one
  feels toward a senior member of the family" (p. 207, folio 189).
- **Persian eulogy outweighs Arabic and shaped Turkish and Urdu.** "There are an even greater number
  of nac tiyya poems in the Persian than in the Arabic tradition. The descriptions are cer­tainly
  more colorful" (p. 207, folio 189); the genre "largely influ­enced the Ottoman Turkish and the Urdu
  tradition" (p. 207, folio 189).
- **An antiphilosophical strain is characteristic of the East.** "And Maulana Rumi continued in this
  antiphilosophical style, which is very typical for poets and mystics in the Eastern Is­lamic world"
  (p. 210, folio 192); Sana'i turns Avicenna's own book titles against him (p. 210, folio 192).
- **The author's own first-person judgements.** "The ever-growing in­terest in the technical aspects
  of nac t poetry cuhninates [text-layer?: culminates], I think, in Yusuf an-Nabhani's attempt to
  write sixteen praise poems on Muhammad in the sixteen classical Arabic meters" (pp. 205–206,
  folios 187–188); "Nowhere else does Islamic religious poetry come so close to myth as in these
  poems" (p. 201, folio 183); Khaqani's poetry "deserves a spe­cial, extensive study" (p. 215,
  folio 197) and Jami's "would deserve a special discussion" (p. 213, folio 195).
- **A scholarly identification claimed.** "Thanks to Sacdi's use the mutiic [text-layer?: muṭāʿ],
  whose identity had caused some embarrassment to scholars, is indeed identified as the Prophet
  Muhammad" (p. 214, folio 196).

## Doubts

**Archive mapping.**

- **`mazar-e-iqbal` rests on a bare literary citation.** Iqbal is named only as the author of two
  quoted lines (p. 192, folio 174). The chunk gives no tomb, no city, no date of death and no
  silsila. The 18 September ruling makes this a legitimate mapping, but the evidence is an
  author-only mention and the consolidator should weigh it as such.
- **"Sind" is recorded but not mapped, and this is the one open mapping question in the chunk.**
  Three places give Sindh-side material at province or language level: "Such formulas were to be
  repeated through the cen­turies down to modern Sindhi folk poetry" (p. 195, folio 177); "In Panjabi
  and Sindhi, the miracles of the Prophet's birth are told in balladesk or lyrical forms" (p. 199,
  folio 181); and "An eighteenth-century Persian poet of Sind, Mir c Ali Shir Qanic" (p. 207,
  folio 189). Ruling (a) is keyed to `toponym_map.txt`, and that file has **no** line for Sind or
  Sindh under either spelling — the check BRIEF §7 demands was run for both. A province is also a
  wider unit than the ruling's worked example ("Multan"), and attaching these three sentences to
  every Sindh row in `shrine_index.tsv` would be a much larger claim than the ruling states. **No id
  is therefore claimed, and the decision is left reversible**: if Rauf extends ruling (a) to
  provinces, the rows waiting for it are all the Sindh rows of `shrine_index.tsv`, and "Panjabi" and
  "Panjab"-side rows would raise the same question. Note also that "Panjabi" and "Sindhi" here are
  **languages**, not places, which is a second reason to hold off.
- **"Pakistan", "Indo-Pakistan" and "the Subcontinent" are country- and region-level and take no
  id.** "Pictures of Buraq are found today on trucks and tank waggons in Pakistan" (p. 201,
  folio 183) and the Indo-Pakistan passages (p. 199, folio 181; p. 205, folio 187) would, read as
  toponyms under ruling (a), attach to all 169 rows at once, which empties the ruling of meaning.
  Flagged so the consolidator can decide, not claimed.
- **Two silsila identifications are deliberately refused as mappings.** Amir Khusrau was "initiated
  into the Chishtiyya by his friend Nizamuddin Auliya" (p. 214, folio 196), and several archive rows
  carry a Chishti silsila; "Ahmad Sir-hindi's mystical theories... form the basis of his revivalist
  theol­ogy" (pp. 211–212, folios 193–194), and Sirhindi is not the principal figure of any archive
  row. In both cases the chunk names no shrine, no site and no connection to Pakistan, so **no id is
  claimed**. Recorded only so the silsila is not later mistaken for evidence (the same refusal the
  schimmel_pain_and_grace notes made for Naqshbandi-Mujaddidi).
- **Ambiguous-name check run and clear.** No name in this chunk could plausibly match two archive
  rows, and none of the archive's many Shah/Shah Jamal/Makhdoom-type names appears at all.
