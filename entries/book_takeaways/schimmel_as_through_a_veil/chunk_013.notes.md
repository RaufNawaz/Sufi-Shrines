## Arguments and interpretations

- Schimmel defends the localisation of the nativity in South Asian poetry by analogy with Europe: "This attitude is not different from that of German or Dutch painters who placed the Nativity in a northern European landscape, or that of popular Christmas carols where Bethlehem is transferred into a Ger­man rural environment"; she adds "it is the feeling of being very close to the blessed child who will emerge as the savior of all those who follow him." (p. 298, folio 280).
- She makes the second half of the shahada the marker of Islamic distinctiveness: "One has always to keep in mind that it is the second half of the profession of faith, 'Muhammad is the Messenger of God,' which determines Islam as a distinct religion and was understood to point to the legal aspect of Islam, while the first half, 'There is no deity save God,' could be accepted by most believers in the various religious traditions." (p. 303, folio 285).
- She reads the Moses/Jesus/Muhammad triad as a doctrine of balance: "Moses sym­bolizes the predominance of the Law, while Jesus represents the predomi­nance of love and otherworldliness, while Muhammad is regarded as the prophet who preached the healthy equilibrium between these two extremes." (p. 305, folio 287).
- She diagnoses an Arabising reflex among Indian Muslims: "this is typical of the attitude of many Indian Muslims who often felt like foreigners in the Subcontinent and found that the remedy for all ills is the Arabic tradi­tion, as the great theologian of Delhi, Shah Waliullah, remarked." (p. 305, folio 287), with the Shah Waliullah quotation "Arabs should be given mastery of [people of all] reli­gions, and their customs should be substituted by the customs of the Arabs" (pp. 305–306, folios 287–288).
- She records Hindu participation in Muslim devotional poetry as a fact of the Sindhi and Urdu record: "Even Hindus joined the Muslims in composing `na<ts` [text-layer?: naʿts], thus Sufi Bhai Asuram (d. 1941) in Sindhi … The first scholarly biography of the Prophet in Sindhi was also written by a Hindu teacher, Lalchand Jagtiani." (p. 307, folio 289).
- She rejects the Hindu commentarial reading of Shah ʿAbdul Latif outright: "These patronizing remarks (pp. 37-38) are certainly out of place." (p. 308, folio 290).
- She reports a live Indian theological dispute over whether blessings on the Prophet are meaningful "since he is already perfected", resolved by Shah Waliullah with a Burda citation (p. 300, folio 282); and a second dispute in which "Fazlul Haqq Khairabadi, who defended the first possibility, died in 1862 in the Andamans where he was exiled after his participation in the 'Mu­tiny' of 1857" (p. 306, folio 288).
- Evaluative judgements on her own sources, useful for weighing them: Abdul Ghani's volume is cited "unfortunately with wrong trans­lations of the key concepts" (p. 305, folio 287); Rasheed's article "is a useful compilation of material" (p. 302, folio 284); A. S. Husain "highly praises lbn Daqiq al-e Id al-Manfaluti, probably because he belongs to the Egyptian tradition" (p. 301, folio 283); Nicholson's Literary History of the Arabs "devotes only a brief paragraph to the poem" (p. 300, folio 282); Cejpek's "Iranian Folk-Literature" is annotated "[mentions poetry only in passing . ]" (p. 317, folio 299); Furuzanfar "is the best introduction to the tra­ditions used by Rumi" (p. 304, folio 286).
- She notes that a poetic tradition can be traced by name-form alone: a Spanish poem on the sandals "by a woman, ash-shaikha Sa'duna umm Sa'd al-Andalusiyya (d. `64o/1238` [text-layer?: 640/1238])" (p. 302, folio 284).
- The bibliography is organised author-alphabetically with repeat-author dashes and gives publisher, place and year for nearly every item; for this chunk's A–C span it is the book's most usable finding aid for Sindhi, Panjabi, Urdu and Pashto primary sources (pp. 311–317, folio not stated to 299).
