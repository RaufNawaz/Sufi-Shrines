## Practices, institutions, economy

- Sung recitation at a dargah, in two languages and in sequence: at Maneri's dargah "after the
  noonday prayer, and the minstrels began to sing, first in Persian, the language of higher
  education, and then in Hindwi" (p. 153, folio not stated).
- Vernacular preaching as the institutional strategy of the orders: "Everywhere outside the center
  of the Islamic world it was the goal of mystical preachers and leaders of fraternities to reach
  the masses and infuse into them the love of God and His Prophet in words which they could easily
  comprehend, using images from their daily life" (p. 155, folio 137).
- The authority of the Pir, named by the book as an effect of the poetry itself: "The role of at
  least one branch of mystical folk poetry in cementing the tremendous influence of the Pir over
  his followers should never be forgotten" (p. 159, folio 141).
- The madrasa curriculum, named and mocked: "Kanz Qu duri Kafiya [text-layer?: Kanz, Qudūrī,
  Kāfiya], the handbooks of hadith, jurispruqence [text-layer?: jurisprudence], and Arabic
  grammar, which were taught in Indian madrasas for centuries" (p. 158, folio 140).
- Graded dhikr taught through women's work: "As you take the cotton, you should do dhikr-i jali, /
  As you separate the cotton, you should do dhikr-i qalbi, / And as you spool the thread you should
  do dhikr-i 0aini [text-layer?: dhikr-i ʿainī]: / The threads of breath should be counted one by
  one, 0 sister" (p. 163, folio 145).
- Women as the transmitting institution: images are "taken from the world of women, who were even
  less educated than men but were, and still are in rural areas, the best transmitters of religious
  poetry, songs, and proverbs, and the depositaries of mystical lore" (p. 163, folio 145).
- Genres as pedagogical instruments — "charkhf niima [text-layer?: charkhī nāma], spinning songs",
  described as "some of the earliest Urdu poems for mystical instruction" (p. 163, folio 145);
  "chakkf niima [text-layer?: chakkī nāma]", taking "inspiration from the most important occupation
  of the Indian housewife, the grinding of grain" (p. 164, folio 146); "The Golden Alphabet, known
  from the Ancient Near East and the Old Testament", "used among the Muslims in Pashto, Panjabi,
  and Sindhi poetry as well as in Turkish dervish circles and Swahili religious verse" (p. 165,
  folio 147); "many Panjabi si1]arfr [text-layer?: siharfī] ('thirty letter poems')" which "use
  indigenous words for each letter" (p. 165, folio 147); and the "Biirahmiisa [text-layer?:
  Bārahmāsa] ('Twelve-Months-poem')", "restricted to the Indian tradition" (p. 165, folio 147).
- The ritual year inside the Barahmasa: "Starting with the tragic events of Muharram, the
  soul-represented as a young woman-lives through the festive days of the year until it finally
  finds, in Dhu'l-hijja, the way to Mecca, the center of its longing, where the Divine Friend
  dwells, or to Medina, the beloved Prophet's resting place" (p. 165, folio 147). The book adds
  that "The Muslim poets sometimes replaced the Indian months by the Muslim lunar months (in recent
  times even Christian months are sometimes found in Sindhi biirahmiisa [text-layer?: bārahmāsa])"
  (p. 165, folio 147).
- Mevlevi dress and dance: "The symbolism of the white robes-garments of resurrection-which appear
  after the Mevlevi dervishes have cast off their black coats at the beginning of the whirling
  dance, points to this deep secret of transforming Love" (p. 151, folio 133).
- Patronage and political use of vernacular religious verse: Lor Chanda was "written for Feroz Shah
  Tughluq's vizier around 1370" (p. 154, folio 136); and "That religious poetry in the folk idiom

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Love as alchemy, and destruction as the precondition of finding.** "Love is the great alchemy.
  Its power can transform the base copper of man's lower potencies into gold, provided he endures
  all afflictions in its crucible" (pp. 141–142, folios 123–124). Because "according to Persian
  poetical conventions treasures are always found in ruins, the heart has first to be completely
  destroyed before the hidden treasure can be found", a composition the author says was helped by
  "the rhyme of ganj (treasure) with ranj (pain)" (p. 141, folio 123).
- **A deliberate refusal to read Rumi's ascent philosophically.** Of the upward movement "from
  mineral and plant to man and angel" ending in "cadam [text-layer?: ʿadam], 'positive
  Not-being,'" she insists "one should interpret these verses not as pertaining to certain
  philosophical theories or to metempsychosis but rather as another expression of his conviction
  that no part of creation can reach a higher level of existence without sacrificing itself in
  Love" (p. 141, folio 123).
- **Wine and fire as the master metaphors, and a claimed source.** "Thrown out of the
  well-established professorial way of life by the heady wine of Love, he uses this imagery more
  frequently than any other metaphor, except for fire" (p. 142, folio 124); and "Maulana had
  probably read Ibn al-Farid's great ode to wine, allusions to which can be detected in some of his
  verses" (p. 142, folio 124) — the author's own hedge ("probably") is hers.
- **Love against Intellect, read as a satire on legalistic Islam.** "In Maulana's favorite, and
  most convenient, comparison Intellect is the qiiqi [text-layer?: qāḍī], the judge representing
  legalistic Islam" (p. 146, folio 128); the ghazal she quotes stages "Mufti First Intellect"
  issuing a fatwa and "The preacher Love" arriving at "the c;dgah [text-layer?: ʿīdgāh] of Union"
  (p. 146, folio 128). She identifies the pulpit-versus-gallows contrast as "a topos which was to
  become commonplace for almost all later Persianate poets" (p. 145, folio 127), resting on the
  pun "Only by becoming a martyr (shahid) man can be a witness (shiihid [text-layer?: shāhid])"
  (p. 145, folio 127).
- **Love placed outside law and ritual, with the biographical qualification kept.** "Maulana in his
  personal life continued to follow the injunctions of religious law, very carefully observing
  fasting and prayer. Yet all these lively images point to the fact that Love is outside the
  religious rites and the seventy-two sects of Islam" (p. 147, folio 129); summed in "Love is free
  from the narrowness of prayer niche and cross" (p. 148, folio 130). Compare her earlier note that
  the "without proper etiquette" position "Such ideas were expressed more prosaically as early as
  c. 900 by some Baghdadian Sufi leaders" (p. 147, folio 129).
- **The dance read as the practice from which the poetry came.** "Rumi, whose poetry was born out
  of the whirling movement of ecstatic dance, often saw Love as the great musician" (p. 150,
  folio 132); "In this whirling movement the lover experiences the secret of fanii' and baqii'
  [text-layer?: fanāʾ and baqāʾ]: he is taken out of the gravitational field of the material world
  and annihilated in the spiritual sun's magnetic field" (p. 151, folio 133). She reads the cosmic
  dance comparatively, alongside "the dance of the soul (Suso)" (p. 151, folio 133).
- **Maneri's remark dated as a first, with the practice dated earlier than the remark.** It is
  "one of the first known remarks by a mystical master about religious poetry in the folk idiom,
  although the use of local languages in Sufi circles goes back to the thirteenth century"; and if
  Baba Tahir's quatrains count, "mystical poetry outside the 'high' literary traditions began as
  early as the eleventh century in Iran, and a century later in Central Asia" (p. 153, folio not
  stated).
- **The chapter's chronology of the vernaculars.** "About the same time-the late thirteenth and the
  early fourteenth centuries-we find the first songs about mystical love in Anatolia in simple
  Turkish verse and in India in Hindwi. In the Arab world too popular mystical poetry developed
  during that period. Somewhat later Panjabi, Sindhi, and Pashto followed" (p. 155, folio 137).
- **The claim that most concerns this archive.** Of the regional epics that took up "popular love
  tales and transformed them into vehicles for mystical teaching": "But only in the centuries after
  his death did mystical poets in Sind and the Punjab develop the art of utilizing simple folk
  tales as media for mystical instruction" (p. 154, folio 136) — "his" being Bada'uni's.
- **A regional differentiation among the vernaculars.** "However, Pashto religious poetry is on the
  whole like Balochi, more ascetic in outlook, and it rarely uses the sweet generalizations of
  all-embracing unity which are common to Sindhi, Panjabi, and Bengali Sufi songs" (p. 155,
  folio 137).
- **Vernacular mysticism as the bypassing of a priestly class.** "The use of the folk language
  enabled the mystics to teach the core of religion without the mediation of a priestly cast (as in
  the Hindu case where in fact the most ardent poets in the folk idioms were shudras), and without
  relying upon the writings of erudite scholars" (p. 156, folio 138) — the scholars being those
  Ghazzali said were "well informed about the most complicated forms of divorce but could not tell
  what 'trust in God' or 'piety' really meant" (pp. 156–157, folios 138–139).
- **An anti-intellectual bias claimed as general, and grounded doctrinally.** "mystical folk poetry
  throughout the Muslim world has a strongly anti-intellectual bias and is often directed against
  the ulema and their claim to be the true depositaries of the Divine Law" (p. 157, folio 139).
  Its doctrinal anchors are "cilm ladunni [text-layer?: ʿilm ladunnī] (5ura 18/65 [text-layer?:
  Sura 18/65])"; the Prophet "who was called in the Koran ummi (Sura 7'157-158 [text-layer?: Sura
  7/157–158]), generally translated as 'illiterate'"; and the folk mystics' claim "that their
  poetry consisted of waridat, inspired words, which descended upon them without any intellectual
  activity" (p. 157, folio 139).
- **Koranic Arabic as the mark of the Muslim vernaculars.** "These commonly used words and
  sentences are found practically all over the Islamic world, and it is this Koranic heritage that
  gives the Muslim branches of Indian languages their distinct flavor" (p. 157, folio 139).
- **Folk poetry is not exempted from fossilization.** "Yet, in the history of mystical folk poetry
  too the process of repetition and fossilization of forms, topics, and images is as visible as in
  high poetry" (p. 160, folio 142) — set against most Persian mystics, who "composed their verse in
  the precious style, which, though often developed out of generally used images, soon crystallized
  into more and more abstract patterns" (p. 159, folio 141).
- **Imagery is read off the landscape, and the author makes local knowledge a condition of
  reading.** The poets "resorted to an imagery taken from daily life and from the landscape that
  surrounded them" (p. 159, folio 141), following "the Koranic injunction to find God's signs 'in
  the earth and in yourselves-do you not see?' (Sura 51'21 [text-layer?: Sura 51/21])" (p. 160,
  folio 142); "One enjoys these poems best when one knows the country and realizes how true to life
  their imagery is" (p. 163, folio 145). Her worked examples include the tree of life (p. 160,
  folio 142), the water wheel "doliib [text-layer?: dūlāb]", whose shriek is "the tree's complaint
  about its separation from the forest" (p. 161, folio 143), the yellow flower as "a perfect
  dervish who expects nothing from this world" (p. 162, folio 144), the fading "color saffron"
  against "the red dye (lak) that stays even when the cloth is worn to rags" (p. 162, folio 144),
  and the substitution of birds by region: "In Sindhi and Panjabi the kang, crow, serves as
  messenger instead of the more urbane pigeon", while "the Papiha bird in the Indian plains asks
  during the rainy season in Hindi, piu kahiiii [text-layer?: piu kahāṇ?], 'Where is the
  beloved?'", and "Sindhi poetry recognizes the swan that feeds on pearls as the symbol of the true
  Sufi" (pp. 162–163, folios 144–145).
- **The alphabet poem's letter-meanings are given as a shared code.** "The alif for every Muslim is
  the letter pointing to God, the One and Unique. Them [text-layer?: The m] was usually connected
  with Muhammad. The cain [text-layer?: ʿain] was associated with cishq [text-layer?: ʿishq], love,
  or with c Ali ibn Abi Talib [text-layer?: ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib], the fourth caliph and first imam
  of the Shia, whose veneration plays an important role particularly in Turkish Bektashi poetry"
  (p. 165, folio 147).
- **Mercy imagery is tied to the Prophet.** Realistic descriptions of "clouds gathering and finally
  distributing ra/Jma [text-layer?: raḥma], mercy, to the world" are "ingeniously connected with
  praise of the Prophet who too was sent rafJmatan lilciilamfn [text-layer?: raḥmatan
  lil-ʿālamīn], 'as mercy for the worlds' (Sura 21'10'7 [text-layer?: Sura 21/107])" (p. 164,
  folio 146). The Barahmasa's soul hopes for union "either with the Divine Beloved or with the
  haqfqa muhammadiyya [text-layer?: ḥaqīqa muḥammadiyya], the archetypal Muhammad" (p. 165,
  folio 147).
- **Form is explained functionally.** "Poetry that was meant for the masses had to be memorable and
  had to have simple rhyme patterns. For this reason certain forms were favorites with the mystical
  folk poets" (pp. 164–165, folios 146–147); the Golden Alphabet "helped to memorize the sequence
  of the alphabet, and each letter was charged with a certain meaning" (p. 165, folio 147).

## Doubts

**Damage class inventory (ruling (b), inventoried once here and flagged inline above).**

- **Transliteration is destroyed throughout, exactly as BRIEF §2 measured.** In this chunk: ʿayn
  prints as `c` (`cadam`, `caql`, `cilm ladunni`, `cishq`, `cain`, `c Iraqi`, `curyan`,
  `cushshaq`, `Ismacil`, `Mascud`, `micraj`, `c;dgah`, `lilciilamfn`, and, load-bearing here,
  `Shah c Abdul Latif`); `ī` prints as `f` (`hadfth`, `Mathnawf`, `al-muhyf`, `charkhf`, `chakkf`,
  `haqfqa`, `si1]arfr`); final `ā` prints as `ii` (`niima`, `siiqi`, `ammiira`, `shathiytit`,
  `Biirahmiisa`, `Kiifiya`, `doliib`, `IJayii`, `fanii'`, `baqii'`, `miitu ... tamiitii`); `ḥ`
  prints as `IJ` or `/J` or `f:l` (`IJayii`, `ra/Jma`, `rafJmatan`, `f:likam`, `l}adith`); and
  `ṣ`/`ḍ` collapse to `$`, `q` or U+FFFD `�` (`qiiqi`, `qii'1i`, `�arf`). **None of it is
  reversible token by token**, so no name above was silently normalised.
- **Digit and number damage.** Three-digit folios split (`12 5`, `13 0`, `1 37`, `14 1`); `0` prints
  as `o` or `O` (`195o's`, `0 Hu!`, `0 sister`, `0 cloud`, `0 Love`); `8` prints as `s` (note
  marker `3s2` at p. 146, folio 128); `9` prints as `C)` (`Sura C)/112` at p. 163, folio 145); and
  the Sura **slash prints as an apostrophe** (`Sura 7'157-158`, `Sura 51'21`, `Sura 21'10'7`) or is
  lost entirely (`Sura 50116`). Sura references are therefore only as reliable as the flag on them.
- **Note-reference numbers are unusable and none is cited above.** They are split, transposed or
  broken across lines throughout: `3 4t` (p. 140), `3 74` and `3 35` where the running sequence
  wants 347 and 353 (p. 140, folio 122; p. 142, folio 124), `. . 8 35.` for note 358 (p. 142,
  folio 124), `garments.so` for note 50 (p. 163, folio 145), `.4 3 .` for note 43 (p. 162,
  folio 144). **No footnote number from this chunk may be carried anywhere.**
- **No macron, dot-below, ʿayn, hamza, curly quote, em dash or ligature survives.** Every
  apostrophe in `fana'`, `Ja'isi`, `Da'ud`, `Khata'i`, `Bada'uni`, `Dhu'l-fiqar`, `Dhu'l-hijja` is
  a flattened hamza; every dash is a plain hyphen, so `the soul-represented as a young woman-lives`
  (p. 165, folio 147) and `white robes-garments of resurrection-which` (p. 151, folio 133) are em
  dashes, **not** compounds.
- **One word is genuinely unrecoverable and no reading is offered.** "The old contrast between
  khabar (information) and mcyir [text-layer?: unrecoverable] (vision)" (p. 145, folio 127) — the
  printed form cannot be resolved with confidence from this chunk, so it is left as printed. Two
  others are flagged with low confidence and should be re-checked against a page image: `piu
  kahiiii` (p. 163, folio 145) and `Yusuf Jal ikhti` (p. 156, folio 138).
- **Original script.** No Arabic, Sindhi, Panjabi or Urdu script survives anywhere in this chunk's
  transcription. Everything the book prints in the original — including the Sindhi line "Lokan nahw
  a �arf" (p. 158, folio 140) and the Panjabi and Bijapuri verses (pp. 160–164, folios 142–146) —
  reaches us only as damaged transliteration. Per BRIEF §3 it is **not reconstructed here**. The
  German of the Rückert couplet is also damaged (`tOte` for `töte`, `Alliih` for `Allāh`, p. 151,
  folio 133).

**Archive mapping.**

- **All four archive mappings rest on a literary reference and nothing more.** For
  `bhit-bhit-shah`, `mazar-of-bulleh-shah`, `shrine-of-sachal-sarmast` and
  `garh-maharaja-shorkot` the chunk gives a name, a language, a verse, and in three cases a
  relative date. It gives **no shrine, no tomb, no ʿurs, no custodian and no death year for any of
  them**. A consolidator should weigh these as poet-attestations, not as site material.
- **Name forms differ from the archive's, and the difference is not resolved here.** The book writes
  "Shah c Abdul Latif" and "Shah Latif" with no "Bhittai" and no Bhit; "Bullhe Shah" with no
  "Abdullah Shah Qadri" and no Kasur; "Sultan Bahu" with no Garh Maharaja or Shorkot; "Sachal
  Sarmast" with no Daraza. The identifications above are made on the strength of language, century
  and the poems quoted. None was enriched.
- **Sultan Bahu's order is given as "Qadiriyya", which is not word-for-word the archive's field.**
  The book says only "as used in the Qadiriyya order, to which Sultan Bahu belonged" (p. 161,
  folio 143); the archive row `garh-maharaja-shorkot` reads "Sarwari Qadiri". Recorded as the
  book's wording, flagged so the difference is not silently merged.
- **"Bedil Rohriwaro" is a judgement left open.** The nisba points to Rohri, and Rohri appears in
  the `location_short` of `sadh-belo-sadh-belo-island-temple` and
  `gurdwara-pehli-patshahi-jind-pir-sukkur`. But the book names a **poet**, not a place, and gives
  nothing else about him (p. 157, folio 139). **No id is claimed** — a nisba is not a bare toponym,
  and reading it as one would be an inference, not a fact from the page.
- **"Shams" / "Shamsuddin" in the Rumi chapter is Rumi's master, not a Multan saint.** The chunk's
  "Shams appears as the qalandar" (p. 143, folio 125), "the sun of 'Be, and it was'" (p. 145,
  folio 127) and "The love of the Sun (i.e., Shamsuddin)" (p. 150, folio 132) must **not** be
  mapped to `shrine-of-shah-shams-ud-din-sabzwari`. Flagged only so the consolidator does not do so.
- **"qalandar" here is a type, not the Sehwan saint.** "the kingdom of the qalandar, and the
  qalandar is beyond that!" (p. 143, folio 125) is a figure in Rumi's Persian ghazal. **No mapping
  to `lal-shahbaz-qalandar`.**
- **Region-level attachment is refused, and the refusal is recorded so it stays reversible.** Ruling
  (a) governs a bare toponym; this chunk offers only the region names "Sind" and "the Punjab"
  (p. 154, folio 136; p. 158, folio 140; p. 160, folio 142; p. 161, folio 143). `toponym_map.txt`
  contains **no province or region entry at all**, which is why no id list is taken from them here.
  If Rauf rules that a province-level mention also carries ruling (a), the material affected is
  identifiable and small: the four poet bullets above already carry it at person level, and the only
  additional facts are the tree-and-unbeliever image "in Sind" (p. 160, folio 142), the Banyan-tree
  and "first known Sindhi poems" line (p. 161, folio 143), the crow-as-messenger and pearl-eating
  swan (pp. 162–163, folios 144–145), and the "Sind and the Punjab" thesis sentence (p. 154,
  folio 136). Nothing else would move.

**Internal tensions and gaps.**

- **The chapter dates its own "first" three ways within three pages.** Maneri's mid-fourteenth-
  century remark is "one of the first known remarks by a mystical master about religious poetry in
  the folk idiom", yet on the same page "the use of local languages in Sufi circles goes back to the
  thirteenth century" and vernacular mystical poetry "began as early as the eleventh century in
  Iran, and a century later in Central Asia" (p. 153, folio not stated); then p. 155 (folio 137)
  dates "the first songs about mystical love in Anatolia ... and in India in Hindwi" to "the late
  thirteenth and the early fourteenth centuries". These are different claims about different things
  (remark, use, poetry) but they are easy to conflate; a reader should keep them apart.
- **Every date for the four archive poets is relative, not absolute.** Shah ʿAbdul Latif is
  "eighteenth-century" (p. 158, folio 140); Bullhe Shah is only "his Panjabi contemporary" (p. 158,
  folio 140); Sachal Sarmast is "250 years later" than Qadi Qadan, whose death is the one absolute
  date in the run, "(d. 1551)" (p. 158, folio 140); Sultan Bahu is "some 300 years later" than a
  "late fourteenth century" figure (p. 160, folio 142) and "A century and a half" after Qadi Qadan
  (p. 161, folio 143). **Do not convert any of these into a year.**
- **Two pages carry no folio and are cited accordingly.** p. 152 is `[blank page]` — one of the nine
  genuine blanks listed in BRIEF §3, not a plate page. p. 153 is the chapter-opening page and prints
  no running header; the chapter number reaches us as `Four I`, in which the `I` is the rendering of
  the divider rule and not a word.
- **The chapter title appears in two forms.** The opening page prints "The Voice of Love: MYSTICAL
  POETRY IN THE VERNACULARS" (p. 153, folio not stated) while every running header from p. 154
  onward reads only "Mystical Poetry in the Vernaculars" (p. 154, folio 136). Headers are treated
  as apparatus and are not quoted as content anywhere above.
- **Two quotations in the chunk are quoted by Schimmel from an unnamed intermediary.** Maneri's
  paragraph (p. 153, folio not stated) and Bada'uni's paragraph (p. 154, folio 136) are block
  quotations whose source is carried only by a footnote number, and the footnote numbers in this
  chunk are unusable (see above). Their wording is quoted exactly; their provenance is
  **(not stated)** here.
