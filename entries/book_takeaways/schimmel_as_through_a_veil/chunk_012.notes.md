## Practices, institutions, economy

- Invocation for a son: Lal Shahbaz Qalandar of Sehwan "is often implored when a woman wants a son",
  in women's poems collected as "Hochzeitslieder der Frauen im Industal" (p. 287, folio 269).
- Address to saints in Sind uses "endearing diminutives (Piral, Ghauthal)" (p. 287, folio 269).
- Seasonal migration carries devotional verse: "examples for Lal Shahbaz and Pir Murad of Thatta are
  also found in Balochi because Baloch tribes migrate in winter into the Indus Valley"
  (p. 287, folio 269).
- Pilgrimage to Mt. Hinglaj: "the pilgrims travel to Mt. Hinglaj, which is situated west of Sind, they
  are said to "go East," i.e., to find illumination." (p. 288, folio 270).
- Interior pilgrimage: "the combination of exercise and interior pilgrimage in the work of Khwaja
  Farid" (p. 286, folio 268).
- Order-founding practice: "Sometimes the founder of an order would leave a rhymed waliiya
  [text-layer?: waṣāya — reading uncertain, see Doubts], an exhortation for his followers"
  (p. 287, folio 269).
- Dhikr become a genre: "In Gujarat, a special form of poetry called jikrr [text-layer?: jikrī], from
  dhikr, developed around 1500 ... and "the qawwtils [text-layer?: qawwāls] of Gujarat sang these
  jikri which bore traces of rapture and love"" (p. 283, folio 265).
- Instrumental accompaniment: "For the use of the flute see Qadi Abdul Mannan, Literary Heritage of
  Bangladesh, Medieval Period" (p. 283, folio 265); Shah Abdul Latif "praises also the instruments of
  the Yogis for their bewitching power" (p. 283, folio 265).
- Muharram observance: "For a Bengali jarf [text-layer?: jārī] celebration, which is accompanied in
  the Mymensingh area by dancing" (p. 285, folio 267); "The genre of maqtal l;iusain
  [text-layer?: maqtal Ḥusain] was popular in India from at least the thirteenth century, as can be
  concluded from Amir Khusrau's remarks, and even in a predominantly Sunni country like Egypt the
  feast of the Hasanain is celebrated with great enthusiasm." (p. 285, folio 267).
- The hilya as a devotional object: "Whosoever sees my l1ilya [text-layer?: ḥilya] after my death will
  be as if he had seen me, and whosoever sees it, longing for me, God will make Hellfire forbidden for
  him" (p. 295, folio 277).
- Dream-vision as initiation: "among Sufis a dream of the Prophet was sometimes regarded as a kind of
  initiatory experience. It was commonly accepted that Satan can never assume the form of the Prophet"
  (p. 296, folio 278).
- Handbooks of blessings in current use, named: "c Ali al-Muttaqi's Kanz al-'ummtil
  [text-layer?: Kanz al-ʿummāl], c Ali al-Qari's Al-Jiizb al-a'4Qm [text-layer?: al-Ḥizb al-aʿẓam],
  Giimiishkhanawi's [text-layer?: Gümüşkhanawī's] Majmu<at al-aurtid wa'el-ahzab, and Jazuli's
  Dalti'il al-khairtit" (p. 295, folio 277); and the modern anthology "Mohammad Elias Burney, Mishkaa t
  us-salawaat. A Bouquet of Blessings of Muhammad the Prophet (Peace be upon Him)" (p. 295, folio 277).
- A ṭarīqa built round Prophet-veneration: "c Abdallah al-Mirghani, in whose fariqa
  [text-layer?: ṭarīqa] the veneration of the Prophet found its outlet in a large literature of
  devotion" (p. 294, folio 276).
- An institutional event: "the proceedings of the Seerat (sfrat) Conference in Karachi 1975"
  (p. 292, folio 274).
- The author's own fieldwork claim: "I have learned more about Islamic piety from women in Anatolia
  and Sind than from most of my learned colleagues or from sophisticated books-they still cling to
  "the faith of the old women" as it was praised in early Islam" (p. 281, folio 263).


## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- Attributed by the book to "the tradition": 'Ali "conquered the fortress of Khaibar on his white mule
  Duldul" (p. 282, folio 264).
- Attributed to "the tradition": Muhammad ibn al-Hanafiyya was "a son of ' Ali by another wife than
  Fatima (according to the tradition, a "blackish slave girl from Sind")", whose story "is found in
  the Panjab, in Bengal, South India, and Malaysia" (p. 284, folio 266).
- A saying ascribed to 'Abdul Qadir "in the Middle Ages": "My foot is on the neck of every saint"
  (p. 287, folio 269).

## Arguments and interpretations

- On vernacular idiom and women: "An interesting account of the relation between the use of the
  indigenous idiom and women in c. A . o . 850 [text-layer?: c. A.D. 850] is found in Tirmidhi's
  Arabic autobiography ... when the angel speaks to the Sufi's wife in Persian in her dream"; with
  Dammann's remark "that popular religious poetry in Swahili is often sung by women"
  (p. 281, folio 263).
- A hypothesis the author marks as her own speculation: "I wonder i f i n some of the spinning songs
  an implicit allusion to Hallaj, the cotton-carder, may be intended; in high poetry, thus in Rumi's
  Diwan, he is sometimes connected with cotton; that is the case especially in later times, after
  Shabistari had spoken of the "cotton of existence"" (p. 281, folio 263).
- A contrast of two poets' use of one image: "The millstone occurs frequently in ' Attar's and Rumi's
  verse, but in different settings: c Attar sees the turning millstone which will then be broken and
  finally rest, while Rumi sometimes perceives the stone, too, as dancing, or uses the image of
  "grinding the grains" for man's purification by suffering." (p. 281, folio 263).
- On the golden alphabet: "In Panjabi, Indian words are often used in Siharfis, not so much Arabic
  expressions; thus alif may begin the line ek alak'h be-ant data, "One endless, unlimited Lord,"
  d may stand for dikh, "see," sh for shih, "lion,"" (p. 281, folio 263). The form "is also common in
  Turkish" and "occurs rarely in classical Persian" (pp. 281–282, folios 263–264).
- On 'Ali in vernacular poetry: he "plays a particularly important role in Turkish Bektashi poetry
  because the Bektashis are largely Shia-oriented", yet "as the early hero of Islam ... c Ali also
  looms large in Sindhi, Panjabi, and related popular poetry, even in a non-Shiite environment."
  (p. 282, folio 264).
- On the calendar poems: "In Panjabi, I found the Indian months almost exclusively, while Sindhi uses
  both Indian and Muslim and lately even Christian months (as does the contemporary poet Talib
  al-Maula)" (p. 282, folio 264); and "In high Persian literature, the genre seems to have been used
  only in the Subcontinent" (p. 282, folio 264). Christian parallels are drawn to "Mechtild of
  Magdeburg" (p. 282, folio 264).
- On the letter-writing motif: "The imagery of tears as ink is very common in both high and popular
  Sufi poetry ... It is indeed amazing to see the illiterate women in these poems complain time and
  again that no letter has reached them", and "in mystical interpretation the girl is the soul,
  imprisoned (like Marui in the Sindhi tradition) in this colorful but treacherous world, who longs
  for a sign of Divine grace." (p. 284, folio 266).
- On the gendering of desire, the tripartite saying "talib ad-dunya mu'annath-talib al-akhira
  mukhannath-talib al-maula mudhakkar, "Who seeks the world, is a female; who seeks the Hereafter, is
  a catamite; who seeks the Lord, is a man,"" traced to "Zubaid Ahmad, Contribution of Inda-Pakistan
  [text-layer?: Indo-Pakistan] to Arabic Literature" (p. 284, folio 266).
- On a European misreading: "the idea that "Hallaj was a Brahmanical thinker of purest water" was
  popularized in Europe particularly by Max Horten, Indische Stramungen [text-layer?: Strömungen] in
  der islamischen Mystik" — the book calls the accompanying *Padmavat* footnote "confusing"
  (p. 286, folio 268).
- On "Turk" as a label: "Turk is the general designation of the Muslim in Indian popular tradition ...
  After all, the first conquerors of North West India and many of the later rulers, including the
  Moghuls, were of Turkish extraction; besides, the motif of the beautiful but cruel Turk was
  commonplace in higher Persian poetry." (p. 286, folio 268).
- A hypothesis offered for testing: Victor Turner's "Mystical rhetoric . . . is very often
  characteristic of movements of egalitarism, popular protest during liminal periods of history" — "It
  would be worthwhile to explore to what extent [it] ... is valid for Sufism; it is certainly relevant
  for the recently growing interest of progressive writers in Islamic countries in the mystical
  tradition, in which they discover the predominance of Love over law" (p. 288, folio 270).
- On "mystical philology": the case of "suryani" is "discussed in great depth" by Goldziher; the
  author speculates "given the connection of the East, sharq, in the tradition of Sufism, with the
  area of illumination, ishriiq [text-layer?: ishrāq], this Divine pfirbi [text-layer?: pūrbī] may
  also belong to the "inspirational" idioms rather than being a veritable Indian dialect."
  (p. 288, folio 270).
- A research gap the author names: "A study of Rumi's paradoxes would be most welcome."
  (p. 288, folio 270).
- On the topsy-turvy world: it "is common to all peoples", found in English and German lying songs and
  "in pictorial representations from the Middle Ages onward, as in thirteenth-century English tiles
  that show a hare riding out to hunt" (p. 290, folio 272); Das Gupta "combines this inverted, u/ta
  [text-layer?: ultā] way of speaking with the Yoga ideal of the inversion of the bodily functions."
  (pp. 290–291, folios 272–273).
- Niyazi Misri's own exegesis is quoted at length, including "'Poplar' is a fragrant tree without
  fruit. Intended is the dry ascetic who claims to possess gnosis, and who is captured by the wish for
  authority ... and sells these wise words as his own goods" (p. 290, folio 272); the closing Arabic
  verse "has been quoted time and again by Sufis when they complained about the degeneration of
  Sufism, of which only the outward traces are left" (p. 290, folio 272).
- On the Prophet's names: "the name Ahmad, considered to be the Prophet's heavenly name, was
  elaborated by pious writers since it was connected in its assumed meaning, perikletos, with the
  parakletos of the Christian tradition; hence the importance of Sura 61/6" (p. 293, folio 275);
  "slowly the number of names grew to more than two hundred, and Horten ... mentions a thousand names"
  (p. 294, folio 276).
- Bibliographic judgements: "The most important study of the development of the veneration of the
  Prophet is still Tor Andrae, Die person Mohammeds in glaube und leh re seiner gemeinde"
  (p. 292, folio 274); "The basic Arabic source of na<tiyya [text-layer?: naʿtiyya] poetry is Yusuf
  an-Nabhani, Al-majmaca an-nabhtiniyya fi'l-madti'i/1 an-nabawiyya" (p. 292, folio 274);
  "Christopher Shackle, "The Multani marsiya," offers an excellent analysis" (p. 285, folio 267).
- On the Burda's reception: "Some orientalists have doubted the authenticity of this story because it
  is not found in lbn [text-layer?: Ibn] Ishaq's sira"; "The Burda was one of the first Arabic poems
  ever edited and translated in the West: G . J. Lette, Cannen [text-layer?: Carmen] panegyricum in
  /audem [text-layer?: laudem] Muhammedis (Leiden 1748)" (p. 293, folio 275).
- On the doctrine of light: Bowering's work is cited "particularly about the elaboration of the theory
  of the nar Muf]ammad [text-layer?: nūr Muḥammad] by Sahl at-Tustari" (p. 296, folio 278); the
  Prophet "can be seen in dreams by many different people in the same hour . . . because he is like
  the sun whose light covers many countries, east and west" (p. 296, folio 278).

**Archive mapping.**

- **Place-level attachments (ruling (a)) are place-level, not site-specific.** The Multan bullet
  (8 ids, the union of the map's "Multan" 7 rows and "Multan City" 3 rows per BRIEF §7) and the
  Karachi bullet (9 ids) attach material about the *city* to every archive row in it. The Karachi
  evidence is especially thin: the city appears once, as the venue of the "Seerat (sfrat) Conference
  in Karachi 1975" (p. 292, folio 274), and once more only through the resulting volume's title
  (p. 296, folio 278). A consolidator should weigh both accordingly.
- **"Sind" and "the Panjab" are treated as regions, not toponyms for ruling (a).** Both occur often
  (e.g. "women in Anatolia and Sind", p. 281, folio 263; "which is found in the Panjab, in Bengal,
  South India, and Malaysia", p. 284, folio 266), as do "Gujarat", "Bengal" and "Bijapur". Following
  the calibration precedent for "the Panjab", no ids are claimed from a region name. If Rauf rules
  that a province name also takes every row in it, this chunk would map most of the archive, so the
  decision should be made explicitly rather than inferred from these notes.
- **"Sehwan" is taken as an epithet of the saint, not as independent place-level material.** The
  phrase is "Lal Shahbaz Qalandar of Sehwan" (p. 287, folio 269), so the person ruling of 18 September
  governs and only `lal-shahbaz-qalandar` is claimed. Note that "Sehwan Sharif" also appears in the
  `location_short` of `shrine-of-lakhi-shah-saddar` ("Laki (Lakhi), near Sehwan Sharif"); if a
  consolidator reads "of Sehwan" as a bare toponym, that second row would join the head.
- **"Shams Tabriz of Multan" is not identified with any archive row.** The book calls him "the
  mysterious figure of Shams" and cites only Ivanow's title (p. 285, folio 267). It does **not** say
  he is the archive's Shah Shams-ud-Din Sabzwari of Multan, nor that he is Rumi's Shams-i Tabriz.
  `shrine-of-shah-shams-ud-din-sabzwari` is nevertheless in the Multan head under ruling (a); that id
  is there because of the place, not because of this identification, and the distinction should not be
  collapsed.

  Prophets of al-Kisa'f" (p. 285, folio 267) stands among Koranic-prophet material and refers to a
