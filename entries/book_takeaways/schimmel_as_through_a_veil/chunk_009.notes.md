## Arguments and interpretations (academic book)

- The regional character of Indian naʿt: Qudsi's poem "is a typical expression not only of the
  veneration of sacred names but also of the Indian tendency to stress the Arabic aspect of
  Muhammad's biography" (p. 218, folio 200).
- Urban vs. folk register: "While the urban poets in India expressed their veneration of the Prophet
  in grandiose hymns to display their erudition, their contemporaries in the plains of Sind and the
  Punjab wrote tender little poems" (p. 222, folio 204).
- Where the rain-cloud image lives: "The comparison of the Prophet with the rain seems to occur more
  frequently, however, in folk poetry than in high poetry" (p. 220, folio 202).
- Comparative reach across religions, offered explicitly as the author's own association: "One may
  think here of the comparison of the Buddha with the rain cloud which waters trees and grass alike,
  as elaborated in the Saddharmapundarika (chapter 5, 1-27), and even of Christian Rorate hymns"
  (p. 220, folio 202).
- Two registers of naʿt distinguished by the tradition itself: "Urdu writers do not in vain
  distinguish between the traditional nact and a more existential kind of poetry the depth of whose
  contents can barely be grasped by the uninitiated" (p. 221, folio 203).
- Hagiographic priority over historicity: of Makhdum Muhammad Hashim, "The loving portrayal of the
  Prophet was more important for him and his followers than historical truth, information about
  which, in any case, was difficult to obtain from the literature at their disposal" (p. 223,
  folio 205).
