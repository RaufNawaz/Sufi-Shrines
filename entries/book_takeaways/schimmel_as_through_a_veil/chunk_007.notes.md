## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Samaʿ as the original setting of vernacular mystical verse.** "That mystical poetry in folk
  idioms was first composed for samac [text-layer?: samāʿ] assemblies is known also from the
  Subcontinent." (p. 168, folio 150) The Burhanpur migrants sang in Sindhi during the samac, and
  "the Sindhi musicians who, around 1580, tore the historian Bada'uni's heart 'with grating tunes'
  may well have belonged to this group." (p. 168, folio 150)
- The formal consequences of that use: "throbbing, repetitive sounds with the swaying motion of the
  body and the strong rhythmical element, which can be underscored by the use of drums" (p. 168,
  folio 150).
- **Performance form.** "Soon the kaff [text-layer?: kāfī] developed, in which the first one or two
  lines are repeated over and over again by a chorus while the main lines of the poem are recited or
  sung by a single person." (p. 166, folio 148) Folk poetry "was meant to be recited or sung and not
  to be read or studied by scholars ... it was intended for the illiterate masses and was written
  down, if at all, only at a very late stage." (p. 166, folio 148)
- **Didactic and calendrical genres.** "both sifJarJf [text-layer?: sīharfī] and biirahmiisa
  [text-layer?: bārahmāsa], as well as imitations of the Arabo-Persian qa�fda
  [text-layer?: qaṣīda] with a very simple form of monorhyme, were used not so much for enthusiastic
  love songs but rather for didactic purposes (n�ihatniima [text-layer?: naṣīhatnāma]):
  descriptions of Muhammad's life and miracles, especially his ascension, the suffering of the
  martyrs of Kerbela, and the teaching of Islamic values, customs, and rituals." (p. 166, folio 148)
  The Bengali "jiiriniima (from Persian ziir, 'lamentation')" [text-layer?: jārīnāma / zār] are the
  Kerbela form (p. 174, folio 156).
- **Indigenous metres.** "the poets used indigenous forms such as the two-lined d6ha
  [text-layer?: dohā] with its 24 (11 plus 13, or 13 plus 1 1 [text-layer?: 11]) miitras
  [text-layer?: mātrās]" (p. 166, folio 148).
- **Shrine-centred singing.** "There are many songs in honor of other saints too, because each
  community has hymns in praise of the mystical guide at whose shrine the qawwali gather. Here was
  and still is the center of popular religious life." (p. 179, folio 161)
- **Genealogical verse and salutation formulas.** "Sometimes such poems are merely rhymed accounts
  of the spiritual or family lineage (silsila, shajara) of the Pir, or they contain blessings over
  the saint and his family, repeating dozens of times in sweet tunes and enrapturing rhythms a
  formula like as-saltimu calaika ya Bandanawaz [text-layer?: as-salāmu ʿalaika yā Bandanawāz]"
  (p. 179, folio 161).
- **Spiritual territory.** "the Indian Sufi practice of assigning to each saint, and he in turn to
  his khalzya [text-layer?: khalīfa], a spiritual territory, wiltiyat [text-layer?: wilāyat]"
  (p. 179, folio 161).
- **A Sindhi geographical praise-poem.** "The first geographical poem in Sindhi, which enumerates
  dozens of cities and countries as c Abdul Qadir's domain, was written in the early eighteenth
  century in his praise ." (p. 179, folio 161) The cities are not listed in this chunk.
- **The Pir's centrality and missionary function.** "mystical verse in the popular languages was
  largely composed by members of the fraternities for missionary purposes, and thus a special genre
  developed which centers around the veneration of the founder of the order or the patron saint."
  (pp. 178–179, folios 160–161)
- **Ismaili ginans.** "It is also generally used in the ginans of the Ismaili community where the
  beloved for whom the bridal soul is waiting is the Imam." (p. 173, folio 155)
- **Living folk performance in Sind.** "It is moving to listen to illiterate folk musicians in
  remote corners of Sind" repeat Hallaj's name (p. 175, folio 157).
- **Qawwali described.** "ONE OF THE most moving experiences in the Subcontinent is to listen to a
  good qawwali. The musicians recite Persian or Urdu ghazals, beginning with a solo voice to which
  the group responds" (p. 189, folio not stated).
- **The forty days' seclusion** is mentioned as a practice, glossed by the book itself: "steamed in
  the oven for forty days (i .e ., in the forty days' seclusion)" (p. 185, folio 167).
- **Contemporary village "saints".** "in the private prayers of contemporary village 'saints' one
  can still hear extremely daring, if not coarse addresses to God or the Prophet" (p. 182,
  folio 164).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Music, not grammar, explains the form of folk poetry.** "Its use in music is the reason for
  grammatical irregularities, strange forms, and unusual rhymes and wordings which at times defy
  logical analysis . Its logic is peculiar because it aims to create a certain mystical mood rather
  than to explain, in plain words, rational facts ." (pp. 166–167, folios 148–149) "It is the
  mnemonic aspect of poetry which makes it a much more useful means of instruction than prose."
  (p. 166, folio 148)
- **Attribution is irrecoverable because the verse was performance material.** "The fact that such
  poetry was meant to be recited in the gatherings of dervishes accounts for the difficulty of
  finding which poems are really by Yunus . ... A similar phenomenon can be observed in Sindhi and
  Panjabi religious literature." (p. 168, folio 150) The credit for first seeing the samaʿ use of
  Yunus' verse is given to "John R. Walsh" (p. 167, folio 149).
- **Dating claim about the genre.** "since folk poetry developed only after 1300, the influence of
  lbn c Arabi's theories of wa]Jdat al-wujud [text-layer?: waḥdat al-wujūd], Unity of Being, as well
  as the typical vocabulary of the Sufi fraternities, form an integral part of this literary genre."
  (p. 169, folio 151)
- **A single trans-regional idiom.** "The contents of mystical poetry between Istanbul and Delhi are
  therefore so similar that one can almost translate a Sindhi poem by Sachal Sarmast into Turkish
  ... or transplant some Bektashi verses into Panjabi and declare them to be compositions of Bullhe
  Shah." (p. 169, folio 151) The borders "between 'high' and popular poetry became blurred"
  (p. 169, folio 151).
- **The woman soul is the Indo-Muslim specialty, and the book attributes it to Hindu influence.**
  "the Indo-Muslim folk tradition has developed another peculiar facet under the influence of Hindu
  neighbors-the symbol of the woman soul ." (pp. 169–170, folios 151–152) The book contrasts this
  with Arabic (a beautiful maiden) and Persian (a "beautiful young shiihid" [text-layer?: shāhid])
  usage (p. 169, folio 151), and notes that classical Sufism made the nafs feminine "since the word
  nafs is, luckily for the ascetics, feminine in Arabic", which "leads generally to a most
  deprecatory description of everything feminine, women being on the lowest rung of the human race"
  (p. 170, folio 152).
- The heroines are read both as souls and as allegories of the nafs' education, "from lowly a
  beginning as nafs ammiira [text-layer?: nafs ammāra], 'inciting to evil' (Sura 12/59)" to "nafs
  mutma'inna 'the soul at peace' (Sura 89'27 [text-layer?: 89/27])" (p. 170, folio 152).
- **The poets' own defence of female protagonists.** "they therefore repeatedly state that these
  women were in reality 'men,' true 'men of God .'" (p. 173, folio 155)
- **Against reading this poetry as pantheism.** "Sufism is nothing but measureless pantheism-an
  impression which is certainly not correct but can easily be deduced from this poetry if its images
  are taken at face value." (p. 176, folio 158) Of Hallaj's reception the book is blunter: the
  wahdat al-wujud reading is "an interpretation which is historically wrong but formed his image in
  the tradition of Turkish, Persian, and Indo-Muslim singers" (p. 176, folio 158).
- **Alternative explanations the book offers for ecstatic identification verse:** the soul
  traversing "the stations of all prophets on its way towards the haqfqa mufiammadiyya
  [text-layer?: ḥaqīqa muḥammadiyya]" (p. 177, folio 159); the state of "bast, expansion, which may
  amount to a kind of cosmic consciousness" (p. 177, folio 159); and the caution that "probably not
  every illiterate or semiliterate poet was fully acquainted with these speculations" (p. 177,
  folio 159).
- **The topos thesis, which is the ground of the Iqbal point.** "In later Sufi tradition, the
  multicolored imagery of all-embracing unity and indifference toward ritual became a mere topos and
  could be used even by otherwise perfectly legalistic Muslims" (p. 178, folio 160).
- **Secrecy as a structural cause of obscurity.** "Secrecy is an integral part of mysticism, and as
  the number of followers of a Sufi order grew greater, the greater was the danger of vulgarizing
  the lofty truths" (p. 180, folio 162); hence "cryptic languages, like the mystical Suryani", the
  "balabailiin language, or lisiin al-mu/Jyf [text-layer?: lisān al-muḥyī] from the sixteenth
  century, which was first discovered in an Ottoman manuscript by Sylvestre de Sacy", and a
  "'mystical kitchen vocabulary,' discussed by Hellmut Ritter" (p. 180, folio 162). The book also
  proposes "intellectual shock therapy ... as is the case with the koan in Zen Buddhism"
  (pp. 180–181, folios 162–163).
- Frameworks borrowed from others: Henry Corbin's "paradoxes" for the shat'1iyat
  [text-layer?: shaṭḥiyāt] (p. 181, folio 163); Adonis, who "stresses the surrealistic character of
  early Sufi literature" (p. 181, folio 163); Hellmut Ritter's "Muslim mystics' strife with God"
  (p. 182, folio 164); D. T. Suzuki on language becoming "warped" (p. 184, folio 166); Charlotte
  Vaudeville on Kabir's "ultabha11si [text-layer?: ultabhānsī] ('inverted') images" (pp. 182–183,
  folios 164–165).
- **Two explicit research proposals, both pointing at Pakistan.** "It would be worthwhile to study
  the relationship between poetry used in the samac and the remarkably rich tradition of imagery
  connected with music." (p. 168, folio 150) And: "it would be worthwhile to study Sindhi or Pashto
  riddles and conundrums with a view to the paradoxical (and probably often mystical) meanings which
  they may convey to the initiated." (p. 183, folio 165) A third: "It might be interesting to study
  Sufi legends as projections of ideas expressed in such paradoxical verses and riddles ."
  (pp. 183–184, folios 165–166)
- **Non-Muslim participation.** The Islam taught to the masses "was not the exclusive religion of
  the immutable Law but rather a religion of warm love-a love in which non-Muslims might also share,
  as in India Hindus participated in writing mystical poetry in honor of the Prophet and the saints."
  (p. 186, folio 168)
- **On the chapter that opens at p. 189:** "The love of the Prophet has been called the strongest
  binding force in Islam" (p. 189, folio not stated), and "Wilfred Cantwell Smith has lucidly shown
  how important the person of the Prophet became particularly during the last hundred years."
  (p. 190, folio 172) Hassan ibn Thabit's verse is called "an important constituent of the srra
  [text-layer?: sīra], Muhammad's biography" (p. 191, folio 173).

**Archive mapping — name and spelling questions.**

- **"Warith Shah" (p. 171, folio 153) is mapped to `mausoleum-of-waris-shah` on the strength of the
  Hir-Ranjha attribution and the "late eighteenth century" date, but the book's spelling is not the
  archive's ("Waris Shah").** The chunk gives him no tomb, no site and no order, so the mapping
  rests on the name and the work alone.
- **Spelling variance on four other mapped names, none of them resolved here:** the book's "Bullhe
  Shah" against the archive's "Bulleh Shah (Abdullah Shah Qadri)"; "Madho Lal Husain" against
  "Madho Lal Hussain"; "Shah c Abdul Karim of Bulrri" against "Shah Abdul Karim Bulri"; "Sacha!
  Sarmast" / "Sachal Sarmast" against "Sachal Sarmast (Abdul Wahab Faruqi)". In each case the
  identification is as secure as a name can make it, but the printed form is what I quoted.
- **The book's order for Madho Lal Husain conflicts with the archive's silsila field.** The book
  calls him "the ecstatic mystic of the Qadiri order" (p. 170, folio 152); the `madho-lal-hussain`
  row records the silsila as "Malamati". Recorded side by side, not reconciled.
- **Two archive rows carry a name close to "Shah c 1nayat".** I mapped p. 175 (folio 157) to
  `dargah-roza-sufi-shah-inayat-shaheed` because the book says "of Jhok" and "executed by the Moghul
  governor in early 1718", which matches that row's place and its `figure_died` of 1718. The other
  candidate, `shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri` (Lahore, c. 1728), is **not** claimed. A consolidator
  should confirm the choice rather than assume it.
- **The Multan shrine of "Shams-i Tabriz" is NOT equated with any archive row's figure.** The book
  says the shrine of "Shams-i Tabriz, Maulana Rumi's spiritual beloved" is "still venerated" in
  Multan (p. 175, folio 157). The archive has `shrine-of-shah-shams-ud-din-sabzwari` in Multan, and
  that row is inside the place-level Multan head above for that reason alone. **The chunk nowhere
  states that the two are the same person**, so no identification is made here; this is exactly the
  two-row case the protocol says to flag rather than settle.
- **Goraknath (p. 183, folio 165) is the principal figure of two archive rows and the book names no
  site.** Candidates: `gorakhnath-goraknath-temple` (Peshawar) and `tilla-jogian` (Salt Range, whose
  principal figure reads "Guru Gorakhnath (Nath yogis)"). Per BRIEF §4 this person sub-case is still
  open, so the material is parked under "Other saints, sites and events" with both candidates named
  here. Note that the mention is of Bengal, not of either site.
- **Krishna is likewise the principal figure of two rows** — `krishna-mandir-kabari-bazar`
  (Rawalpindi) and `krishna-mandir-ravi-road` (Lahore) — and the book names no site for him
  (pp. 169, 170, 172; folios 151, 152, 154). Same open sub-case, same treatment.
- **`shri-panchmukhi-hanuman-mandir-karachi` rests on a single bare name inside a literary
  formula.** "Abu Hanifa and Hanuman are one and the same" (p. 176, folio 158) is the whole of the
  evidence; the row's principal figure is "Hanuman (Panchmukhi)" and the book says nothing of a
  temple, a site or Karachi. The 18 September person ruling makes this a legitimate bullet, but the
  evidence is a bare name and is flagged as such.
- **`mazar-e-iqbal` also rests on author-only mentions** (pp. 176 and 178, folios 158 and 160) —
  Iqbal appears as a poet in a list and as "a modern critic". No tomb, no date, no Lahore.
- **"Omar-kot" (p. 172, folio 154) is not mapped.** Two archive rows carry a similar place spelling
  in `location_short` — `umarkot-amarkot-shiv-mandir` ("Umerkot District, Sindh") and
  `shrine-of-makhdoom-abdul-rahim-girhori` ("Girhor Sharif, near Pithoro, Umarkot, Sindh") — and
  neither spelling matches the book's. Whether "Omar-kot" is that place is an orthographic judgement
  the chunk does not support, so it is left under "Other saints, sites and events" with both
  candidates named here. `toponym_map.txt` lists no Umarkot/Umerkot line, so its own caveat about
  truncation and split spellings applies.

**Archive mapping — place-level attachment (ruling (a)).**

- **The Multan, Lahore and Peshawar bullet heads are place-level, not site-specific.** None of the
  three places is described in the chunk at the level of an individual shrine except Madho Lal
  Husain's tomb, which additionally has its own bullet. A consolidator should weigh the Multan head
  (8 ids) and the Lahore head (31 ids) accordingly.
- **The Peshawar head (10 ids) is the thinnest attachment in this chunk and should be weighed
  hardest.** Its only evidence is the demonym "Peshawari" in a quoted poetic formula (p. 176,
  folio 158); the book says nothing about the city, and nothing whatever about any Peshawar site.
- **Regions take no id, and I checked each one.** "Sind" (pp. 171, 175, 187), "the plains along the
  Indus and its tributaries" (p. 170), "Panjabi" and "Sindhi" as language names throughout,
  "Indo-Pakistan" (pp. 166, 176), "Bengal" (pp. 179, 183, 184), "Anatolia" (pp. 182, 187),
  "the Subcontinent" (pp. 168, 189) and "India" are regions or languages, not sites in
  `location_short`, and take no archive id.
- **Bhit (p. 171, folio 153) and Jhok (p. 175, folio 157) are single-row places** and are handled
  through their persons' bullets rather than a separate toponym head, since the id would be the same
  one either way.
- **Places named in the chunk that are outside the archive's scope:** Burhanpur, Gulbarga, Delhi,
  Istanbul, Mecca, Medina, Kerbela, Badr, the Trench, North Africa, West Africa, Turkey, Indonesia,
  Anatolia, Bengal.

**Other gaps and internal points.**

- **No death year, no silsila and no tomb is stated for Shah c Abdul Latif, Sachal Sarmast, Bullhe
  Shah, Warith Shah, Shah c Abdul Karim or Qadi Qadan in this chunk.** For each, the book gives a
  century at most ("the eighteenth century", p. 171, folio 153; "the first half of the sixteenth
  century", p. 171, folio 153; "as early as the sixteenth century" for Madho Lal Husain, p. 170,
  folio 152). Everything beyond that is `(not stated)` and nothing has been supplied.
- **"King Dhyaj" (p. 169, folio 151) may be a damaged name.** The form is unusual and the chunk
  gives no variant to check it against; I have flagged it inline and quoted it as printed.
- **The chunk's one internal tension.** At p. 176 (folio 158) the book calls the wahdat al-wujud
  reading of Hallaj "historically wrong", yet at p. 169 (folio 151) it treats "The feeling of the
  essential Unity of Being" as permeating this whole body of poetry "equally strongly". These are
  claims at different levels — reception versus doctrine — but they should not be quoted together
  without the distinction.
- **Notes 59–141 and the chapter-five notes are referenced throughout but their text is not in this
  chunk**, so several attributions above (Walsh, Ritter, Vaudeville, Corbin, Suzuki, Cantwell Smith)
  cannot be checked to a source here.
