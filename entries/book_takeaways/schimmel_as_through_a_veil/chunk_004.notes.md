## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Teaching and succession in a madrasa chair.** Baha'uddin Walad "was called to teach in one of
  the numerous madrasas of Konya" in 1228; on his death "his son succeeded him in the chair"
  (p. 94, folio 84).
- **A mosque with a building date, still in use.** "the mosque which he erected in 1220 close to the
  fortress is still in use" — Sultan `c Ala'uddin`[text-layer?: ʿAlāʾuddīn] Kaikobad's mosque in
  Konya (p. 94, folio 84).
- **Tomb visitation.** Burhanuddin Muhaqqiq's "modest tomb beneath the majestic Mt. Erjiyas is
  still visited by the pious" (p. 94, folio 84).
- **Music at a tomb, requested by its occupant.** Rumi "asked his friends not to visit his tomb
  without drums and instruments, for his intoxication would last beyond the grave" (p. 104,
  folio 94).
- **Funeral as ecstatic rite.** "all inhabitants of Konya participated in his funeral, which ended
  in an ecstatic dance" (p. 104, folio 94).
- **Foundation of an order.** Sultan Walad "took over to organize his disciples into a proper
  order, known in the West as the Whirling Dervishes"; "With this organization and with his poetry
  about his father's life, Sultan Walad secured immortality for himself" (p. 104, folio 94).
- **Formal succession by `khalifa`.** "When the dictation was taken up again in 1262, Husamuddin
  had fully matured and was now officially installed as Rumi's `khalifa`" (p. 103, folio 93);
  earlier, `ifa`[text-layer?: reading uncertain — see Doubts], "a ray of light from the sun", "was,
  however, officially given to Husamuddin Chelebi a few years later" (p. 101, folio 91).
- **Marriage used to bind discipleship.** Shams "was married to a girl from the household" (p. 96,
  folio 86); "To cement the relationship with the friend, he married his eldest son Sultan Walad to
  Salahuddin's daughter, and his letters to his beloved daughter-in-law are touching proofs of his
  humanity" (p. 102, folio 92). A later poem ending in `khiimush`[text-layer?: khāmūsh] is "the
  wedding song for Salahuddin's second daughter" (p. 107, folio 97).
- **`samac`**[text-layer?: samāʿ] **and whirling in public space.** "the rhythmical, melodious sound
  of the goldsmiths' hammers in the bazaar of Konya so enraptured Maulana that he took Salahuddin
  with him to whirl through the streets" (p. 100, folio 90). Rumi "turns to the rebeck player who
  accompanied his song, admonishing him not to be so sleepy but rather to listen to his words"
  (p. 105, folio 95). "In whirling dance he poured out his longing and love" (p. 95, folio 85).
- **Patronage and relief of the poor.** "His good relations with the ministers, who for all
  practical purposes ruled the remains of the Seljuk empire after several Mongol attacks on
  Anatolia, enabled him to extend help to many poor and needy" (p. 103, folio 93).
- **Letters and pastoral care.** He "wrote a great number of letters which show him as a man deeply
  concerned with the welfare of those who were close to him" (p. 103, folio 93).
- **Pir and novice.** "The training of the falcon at the hand of the falconer is a fine image of the
  education of the novice by the Pir" (p. 86, folio 76).
- **Pilgrimage and feast.** "to kiss the black stone during the pilgrimage would remind the lover of
  kissing the mole on his beloved's cheek"; "`c1d`[text-layer?: ʿĪd], the great Feast, is meeting
  the beloved", "We celebrate the ' Id but once a year" (p. 83, folio 73).
- **A dated threat to the city.** A poem naming Husamuddin "was written at the time that the Mongols
  intended a new attack on Konya; it even bears the date of this event, 5 `Dhu'l-qac da`[text-layer?:
  Dhū'l-qaʿda] 654 (25 November 1256)" (p. 102, folio 92).


## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- The blind men who "touched an elephant in a dark stable and could not describe anything but the
  part their hands had touched" — an "Indian story, which was first used by Sana'i" and retold by
  Rumi (p. 82, folio 72).
- Abraham "was cast into the pyre whose fire became 'cool and pleasant' for him (Sura 21/69)"
  (p. 83, folio 73).
- Joseph's appearance "confused Zulaikha and the Egyptian women (they cut their hands but did not
  feel any pain while gazing at him)" (p. 84, folio 74); "the scent of his shirt … cured Jacob's
  blindness" (p. 84, folio 74), restated at p. 91, folio 81 as the scent "which brought his father
  glad tidings from his faraway son and cured his eyes, which were blind from weeping".
- Moses hears "'You will never behold Me!' (Sura 7/139)" (p. 84, folio 74).
- Jesus "is endowed with life-giving breath so that he becomes the ideal beloved whose kiss quickens
  the near-dead lover" (p. 84, folio 74).
- The hoopoe, `hudhud`, "who once was the go-between between Solomon and the Queen of Sheba", guides
  thirty birds to Mount Qaf, where they discover "the Divine bird,
  `Sfmurgh`[text-layer?: Sīmurgh], is nothing but themselves, being `sf murgh`[text-layer?: sī
  murgh], 'thirty birds'" (pp. 85–86, folios 75–76).
- The falcon is recalled "with the Koranic word `irjicf`[text-layer?: irjiʿī], 'Come back, 0 soul at
  peace!' (Sura `8cj27`[text-layer?: Sura number unreadable])" (p. 86, folio 76).
- The rose was "created from the Prophet's perspiration during his ascension, it carries his
  fragrance"; "the Prophet put the red rose on his eyes and recognized in it something of the
  divine beauty and majesty" (p. 86, folio 76).
- Shams "claimed to have reached the station of the Beloved,
  `macshuq`[text-layer?: maʿshūq], a claim never made, as far as we can see, by any mystic before
  him" (p. 95, folio 85).
- At the father-and-son reunion "they fell at each other's feet, 'and nobody knew who was the lover
  and who was the beloved'" (pp. 95–96, folios 85–86).
- After Shams' death Rumi "did not want to believe that 'the sun has died'", searched Syria, and
  "finally he discovered that the friend was living in him-Shams was he himself, and what he sang
  was in reality Shams' words" (p. 96, folio 86).
- In the poem of the destitute heart, "a voice from the Unseen exclaims at sunrise, 'Tabriz!'"; the
  heart "falls down in a swoon, overwhelmed by the appearance of the Sun", and the name
  "Shamsuddin" becomes "engraved" on its face (pp. 97–98, folios 87–88).
- "The Sun came, and the moon was split" — "a typical pun pointing to Shams' connection with the
  Prophet" (p. 100, folio 90); and the `yar-i ghar`, "'the friend in the cave,' referring to the
  extremely close relationship between Muhammad and Abu Bakr during the night in the cave on their
  way to Medina" (p. 100, folio 90).
- Rumi's inspiration is attributed to "'that one, that Turk' who breathed into him, addressing him
  (in Turkish!) `Sen kimsin`, 'Who are you?,' and forced him to answer in poetry" (p. 105,
  folio 95).
- "My word is the food of angels-when I don't say words / The hungry angel says: 'Speak! Why are
  you silent?'" (p. 106, folio 96).


## Arguments and interpretations

- **Concealment is a poetic doctrine, not reticence.** "the mystical poets knew that it is both
  impossible and illicit to express in plain words their experience" (p. 82, folio 72); "the
  greatest sin of the lover is `ifshii' as-sirr`[text-layer?: ifshāʾ as-sirr],
  `d.ivulgence`[text-layer?: divulgence] of the secret. This was at least according to the poetical
  tradition, the reason for Hallaj's execution. Persian poets have therefore woven a veil of symbols
  in order to point to and at the same time hide the secret" (p. 83, folio 73). Schimmel marks the
  Hallaj explanation as the tradition's, not her own.
- **Rhyme drives doctrine.** The rhyme of `mard` ("man") with `dard` ("pain") "was helpful" to the
  assertion "that love is only for the strong" (p. 82, folio 72); "It may be that the easy rhyme
  `gul`, ('rose') with `bulbul` ('nightingale') has contributed to the frequent use of this image"
  (p. 86, folio 76).
- **The symbol system is exhaustive by design.** "everything in the world is in some mysterious way
  connected with Love" (pp. 87–88, folios 77–78); the rhetorical figure
  `fiusn-i taclfl`[text-layer?: ḥusn-i taʿlīl] "was an ideal vehicle to make this poetical cosmos
  come alive" (p. 88, folio 78).
- **Comparison to miniature painting.** "This poetry is like Persian miniature painting, which
  combines an inspirational quality with the minutest detail work" (p. 88, folio 78).
- **Against sorting mystics into types.** "A study of their imagery proves that the vocabulary of
  both personal and impersonal mysticism is used almost indiscriminately by them, and images from
  the three major spheres outlined by Evelyn Underhill can occur even in the same verse" (p. 89,
  folio 79); `via eminentiae` and `via negationis` appear in the same poet a few lines apart
  (p. 89, folio 79).
- **Double entendre is intentional.** "as I mentioned earlier, this double entendre is intended,
  and in the case of Hafiz one may even find a third level, that of panegyrics in the guise of love
  poetry" (p. 91, folio 81); a poem "that enthusiastically sings of the beauty and kindness of the
  beloved may be meant to be a eulogy on the Prophet" (p. 91, folio 81). Without disentangling the
  designs "one neither really understands nor enjoys verses which look … often quite repetitious"
  (p. 91, folio 81).
- **Rumi is misread by his admirers.** "Rumi has also been misunderstood by his most ardent
  admirers, for nothing could be more alien to him than a systematization of his thought,
  particularly when this systematization is undertaken, as in hundreds of commentaries up to our
  day, in the light of Ibn `c Arabi`[text-layer?: ʿArabī]'s theosophy" (p. 93, folio not stated).
  Rumi "was not interested in the theosophical aspect of mysticism which developed under
  lbn[text-layer?: Ibn] `c Arabi`[text-layer?: ʿArabī]'s influence" (p. 103, folio 93).
- **Biography is the key to the lyrics.** "To understand properly Rumi's poetry, and particularly
  his lyrics, one has to know the major events of his life out of which his verse grew" (pp. 93–94,
  folios not stated / 84).
- **Dating the Diwan: a stated limit and a proposed method.** "At present it seems impossible to
  attempt an exact dating of Rumi's early verse. However, one can observe a certain change of
  emphasis in his lyrics, and a careful metrical analysis of the
  `Dfwiin-i Shams`[text-layer?: Dīwān-i Shams] is also helpful in placing at least some poems in
  their proper sequence" (p. 96, folio 86).
- **Schimmel's periodisation, marked as her own judgement.** Early markers are the
  `liilfs`[text-layer?: lūlīs] ("The `liilfs`, the charming, playful, and dangerous Gypsies, appear
  to me as part of the early vocabulary"), `�a]Jrii`[text-layer?: ṣaḥrā] "the wide desert",
  and address of the beloved as `khidfw`[text-layer?: khidīw] or with "the Greek terms
  `efendf`[text-layer?: efendī] or `aghap6s`[text-layer?: agapós]", which "are certainly early"
  (p. 97, folio 87). The three-stage use of Shams' name — absent, then mid-poem or second-to-last
  line, then as `takhallufi`[text-layer?: takhalluṣ] — structures the argument (pp. 96–99,
  folios 86–89).
- **An old Sufi rule explains the anonymity.** "It is an old Sufi rule not to reveal the name of the
  Beloved" (p. 96, folio 86).
- **The Mathnawi's character.** "The Mathnawi, with its nearly 26,000 verses, which grew out of
  Maulana's loving relationship with his disciple, is a veritable encyclopedia of mystical lore.
  But it lacks a systematic structure, and some of Maulana's more philosophically minded compatriots
  blamed him because he did not enter into any theoretical discussions or use the technical
  vocabulary of the Sufis" (p. 103, folio 93). "Maulana Rumi could not finish the sixth book of the
  `Mathnawf`[text-layer?: Mathnawī]; its last story is open-ended" (p. 104, folio 94).
- **Rumi's life read as a Sufi paradigm.** "Maulana Rumi's life is a paradigm of the ideal mystical
  biography as drawn up by later Sufis, i.e., the semicircle": annihilation in Shams, then
  `baqii`[text-layer?: baqā], "of stabilization in and through Salahuddin", then the
  `sair ilii', 1-ashyii'`[text-layer?: sair ilā'l-ashyāʾ], "the return to the created things as
  manifested in his friendship with his disciple Husamuddin" (pp. 104–105, folios 94–95).
- **The lyrics are outcries, not doctrine.** "Rumi was well aware that his lyrics were born
  spontaneously out of the agitation of his heart and that they should therefore be taken as sudden
  outcries, not as theological statements which could be ruminated upon by generations of readers or
  commentators" (p. 106, folio 96). He is "one of the few poets in the Islamic world who have spoken
  often and openly about the secret of poetical inspiration" (p. 105, folio 95), and in
  `Fihi ma fihf`[text-layer?: Fīhi mā fīhi] said "he was basically not at all interested in poetry"
  (p. 105, folio 95).
- **`khiimush`**[text-layer?: khāmūsh] **as a supposed pen name, rejected.** The word "is used so
  frequently that some scholars have been inclined to regard it as Rumi's original nom de plume.
  However", it often stands in the second-to-last line with Shams' name in the last, and a number of
  such poems "can be dated to a later time" (p. 107, folio 97).
- **The Mathnawi's reception.** "Translations of and commentaries on this work exist in almost every
  Islamic language and are available, at least in part, in Western languages as well" (p. 93,
  folio not stated).

**Internal contradictions and gaps.**

- **The Mathnawi's break does not add up.** The first book is placed "in this period (late 1256)"
  (p. 102, folio 92); "After the completion of the first book of the
  `Mathnawf`[text-layer?: Mathnawī] there was a break of some four years" (p. 102, folio 92); but
  "the dictation was taken up again in 1262" (p. 103, folio 93). Late 1256 plus a first book plus
  four years does not reach 1262. **"some four years" vs. 1256→1262 must be checked against the
  book's own chronology before either figure enters an entry.**
- **The date Shams left Konya is never stated, only inferred.** Rumi met him "in October 1244"
  (p. 94, folio 84); "after some eighteen months Shams left Konya secretly" (p. 95, folio 85);
  "After another eighteen or twenty months Shams was seen in Syria" (p. 95, folio 85); the return
  is dated "from Syria in January 1248" (p. 99, folio 89). No absolute date is printed for the
  departure — write "(not stated)" rather than computing one.
- **Shams' age is given only relatively**: "a man of at least his age" (p. 94, folio 84). His birth
  year is (not stated).
- **Salahuddin's death year and the dirge.** "he died after prolonged suffering in 1258 and Rumi
  wrote a beautiful dirge on him" (p. 102, folio 92) sits in a parenthesis about a poem dated
  25 November 1256, so the 1258 date is prospective in the narrative, not a sequencing error — but
  the surrounding sentence also says "Salahuddin's terminal illness had probably already set in",
  which is the author's hedge, not a fact.
- **Husamuddin's own death** is given as "after his death in 1284" (p. 104, folio 94) with no month
  or day; his date of birth is (not stated).
- **The chunk opens mid-sentence**, continuing a clause from chunk 003 — "mystics to the assertion
  that love is only for the strong" (p. 82, folio 72). The subject of that clause cannot be
  established from this chunk alone.
- **The Panjabi commentary is quoted but not identified.** "WHO HAS NOT heard the name of Jalaluddin
  Rumi? And who is not familiar with the `Mathnaw17`[text-layer?: Mathnawī?]" is attributed only to
  "the introduction of a Panjabi translation-cum-commentary" (p. 93, folio not stated); its author,
  title and date are (not stated).
