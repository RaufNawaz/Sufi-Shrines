# schimmel_as_through_a_veil — chunk 005 — pp. 108–139

**This chunk is the central catalogue of the chapter "Rumi and the Metaphors of Love"** — that is
the running head on every paginated page of the chunk, verso-leading (`98 Rumi and the Metaphors
of Love`, p. 108) and recto-trailing (`Rumi and the Metaphors of Love 12 1`, p. 139). It is a
single continuous sweep through Rumi's images for Love: fire, water, garden, tree, town and
kingdom, bird and beast, king, soldier, police chief, tax-collector, thief, cook, tailor,
physician, and finally the Koranic prophets and Love-as-mother. Its named human subjects are
**Maulana Rumi**, his friend **Shamsuddin** ("Shams, the Sun of Tabriz"), **Salahuddin** and
**Husamuddin**; everything else is imagery and comparison.

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Maulana Rumi** — the chunk's subject; his "Diwan" is said to run to "more than 35,000 verses"
  (p. 111, folio 101), and he is described writing "numerous Arabic or mixed verses in praise of
  Love" (p. 114, folio 104).
- **Shamsuddin / "Shams, the Sun of Tabriz"** — Rumi's friend and beloved; the sun imagery is
  "always connected with Shams, the Sun of Tabriz" (p. 118, folio 108), his name was "written from
  pre-eternity in the notebooks of Love" (p. 114, folio 104), and "the subconscious memory of
  Shamsuddin's blood near his door" is said to be palpable in Rumi's images (p. 122, folio 112).
- **Salahuddin** — named once, as the friend whose rosegarden's thorns Love's fire removes
  (p. 116, folio 106).
- **Husamuddin** — "young Husamuddin", addressed by Rumi "in the initiatory scene of the Mathnawi"
  (p. 118, folio 108).
- **al-Hallaj** — "the martyr-mystic al-Hallaj, whose name means 'cotton carder'", alluded to by
  Rumi's "Is not the cotton set aflame by Love?" (p. 117, folio 107); separately, Love is
  "`coetemal` [text-layer?: coeternal] with God or, to use Hallaj's expression, is the innermost
  dynamic essence of God" (p. 114, folio 104).
- **Ruzbihan-i Baqli** — named as the Sufi who particularly "had spoken of the educational value of
  human love, which prepares man for Divine Love" (p. 115, folio 105).
- **Sana'i** — "the combination of Love with heroism occurs as often in Rumi as in other Persian
  poets from Sana'i onward" (p. 123, folio 113).
- **Nizami** and **Avicenna** — the magnet image for Love is "a common image for Love even in
  philosophical writings such as Avicenna's", and "common also among nonmystical poets,
  particularly Nizami" (p. 115, folio 105).
- **Konya** — "the palace in Konya, where Rumi must have passed frequently" carries a stone carving
  of the unicorn killing the elephant (p. 124, folio 114).
- **Damascus** — "the place where he discovered the lost Shamsuddin and which therefore is praised
  as `dimashq-i c ishq` [text-layer?: dimashq-i ʿishq]" (p. 121, folio 111).
- **Baghdad** — "a secret Baghdad where the residence of Love is situated", with the author's aside
  "during those days, Baghdad was still the capital of the Abbasid Empire" (p. 121, folio 111).
- **Egypt** — "the place where Yusuf, the paragon of beauty, dwells and whence caravans with loads
  of sugar arrive to make the lovers happy" (p. 121, folio 111).
- **Medina** — named only as the destination of "their emigration to Medina", of the Prophet and
  Abu Bakr (p. 122, folio 112).
- **Mount Qaf** — the Simurgh's "residence, Mount Qaf at the end of the world" (p. 122, folio 112).
- **Hindustan** — "as often in medieval Persian literature, here stands for the eternal home which
  the soul suddenly remembers in a happy dream" (p. 113, folio 103).


## Practices, institutions, economy

- **`samac` [text-layer?: samāʿ]** — "in an unexpected turn the `samac` [text-layer?: samāʿ] is
  once called his army", i.e. Solomon-Love's army (p. 139, folio 121).
- **The mystical dance** — a ghazal is quoted on "the garden of Love, longing for the mystical
  dance / Those with cypress-statures come hand-clapping like plane-trees", described by the author
  as "an early, playful ghazal" (p. 121, folio 111).
- **`c rd al-a4hii` [text-layer?: ʿīd al-aḍḥā]** — "love of Shams is the great Feast of Offerings,
  `c rd al-a4hii` [text-layer?: ʿīd al-aḍḥā]"; the author adds that Rumi's idea "that growth is
  possible only through constant self-sacrifice, is conveniently expressed in such comparisons to
  Islamic ritual", and that Love "not only slaughters the lover at `c Id` [text-layer?: ʿĪd] day"
  (p. 123, folio 113).
- **Mosques** — "Love is also the Imam, and when this Imam appears, a thousand mosques are filled"
  (p. 137, folio 119). No mosque is named or located.
- **`fu tuwwa` [text-layer?: futuwwa] circles** — the epithet `cayyiir` [text-layer?: ʿayyār]
  ("wild," "roguish") is said to suggest "the vocabulary of the `fu tuwwa` [text-layer?: futuwwa]
  circles with whom Rumi may have had some contact"; the related terms named are "rind and
  qalandar" (p. 125, folio 115).
- **Confiscation, taxes and branding as period practice** — the author states of the police-chief
  image that "confiscation in the Middle Ages, as is well known, was connected with all kinds of
  humiliation if not torture" (p. 126, folio 116); Love brands man "like an animal is branded by
  its owner", and "those who have received such a brand of Love will be exempt of all taxes"
  (p. 126, folio 116); Love appears as "the judge who extorts taxes", against which "the village of
  the heart is completely in ruins, how could the poor lover pay taxes?" (pp. 126–127, folios
  116–117).
- **Plunder** — "as a true Turkish hero Love is used to `yaghmii` [text-layer?: yaghmā],
  plundering" (p. 126, folio 116).
- **A Konya palace carving** — the unicorn-and-elephant legend "is represented in a stone carving
  on the walls of the palace in Konya" (p. 124, folio 114). This is the chunk's only reference to a
  standing building or its ornament.
- **Trades named as Love's professions**, recorded because the vocabulary is occupational: cupper
  and physician who bleeds with needles (p. 123, folio 113), shield-maker (p. 125, folio 115),
  bleacher/washerman who "hits the laundry repeatedly on stones", bottle-maker, carpenter
  (p. 127, folio 117), ragman who "calls in vain (and Maulana has him speak Turkish): 'Who has got
  old shoes?'" (p. 127, folio 117), baker and cook (p. 128, folio 118), weaver and "tailor of the
  lovers" (p. 137, folio 119), sorcerer (p. 137, folio 119).
- **`dhauq` ("tasting")** — "The Sufis' use of the term `dhauq`, 'tasting,' for the immediate
  mystical experience has possibly contributed to this imagery" of kitchen and food
  (p. 128, folio 118).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Scent is Rumi's medium of reminiscence.** "Rumi often compares words to carriers of scent.
  Scent is the medium of reminiscence, and the remarkably frequent use of the word `ba`
  [text-layer?: transliteration destroyed; the Persian word for "fragrance," as glossed in the
  text], 'fragrance,' 'odor,' is typical of Rumi's way of interpreting the world"
  (p. 108, folio 98).
- **The protean image is an interpretive obstacle, and that is the chapter's methodological
  premise.** "The protean character of his images poses an additional difficulty to the
  interpreter", his verses being "often surprising in their frankness and sometimes coarseness"
  (p. 108, folio 98); the reader of his `Dfwtin` [text-layer?: Dīwān] "is always amazed at the ease
  with which he uses images and metaphors from every walk of life" (p. 108, folio 98).
- **Out of realism grows symbolism.** "Seemingly banal sensual affects trigger off the highest
  inspiration, and out of elemental realism grows the most sublime symbolism"; the listed sources
  are "the kitchen, the market place, hunting, medicine, children, the greengrocer with his
  sourmilk who toils for his daily bread, as well as obscene anecdotes and the stories of the
  Prophets, family quarrels" (p. 108, folio 98).
- **Love and the Beloved are not reliably separable.** "the center of his thoughts is Love, which
  is sometimes interchangeable with the Beloved, and one often wonders whether it is Love or the
  Beloved that is intended by his words" (p. 111, folio 101).
- **A universality claim.** "There is such variety in his poetical descriptions that they seem to
  include all the symbols ever used by mystics in East and West" (p. 111, folio 101).
- **A dated developmental claim about the philosophical verses.** "at times, mainly in the
  mid-sixties, he tries to discuss philosophical topics which soon dissolve again into poetical
  musings" (p. 111, folio 101). What "the mid-sixties" counts — see `## Doubts`.
- **Love as `coinddentia` [text-layer?: coincidentia] oppositorum**, argued from the ode beginning
  "0 Love, are you more harmoniously shaped, or your garden and apple-orchard?", which "seems to be
  one of his earlier verses" (p. 113, folio 103).
- **The kūh/kāh pun is inherited, not invented.** "Rumi also shared with other poets the frequent
  comparison of Love with amber, which attracts the tiny straw (it is so powerful that the mighty
  mountain, `kuh` [text-layer?: kūh], is transformed into straw, `kiih` [text-layer?: kāh], before
  it)" (p. 114, folio 104).
- **The school-of-love image has Sufi precedent.** "Here he is not unprecedented, for the Sufis
  (and particularly Ruzbihan-i Baqli) had spoken of the educational value of human love"
  (p. 115, folio 105); the school's teacher is "God Most Majestic (`dhu'l-jalal` [text-layer?:
  dhū'l-jalāl])" and it is "called, with an allusion to Sura 13/20: 'They are faithful in their
  promises'" (p. 115, folio 105).
- **A cross-cultural comparison to Spanish mysticism.** "It is indeed, as with Juan de la Cruz, the
  Llama de amor viva, which inspires Rumi. Here the imagery is, as it so often is with him,
  ambivalent" (p. 116, folio 106).
- **Fire imagery has ritual and alchemical ancestry.** "Such images go back to ancient rites of
  purification through fire, and lead, `in tum` [text-layer?: in turn] to the vocabulary of
  alchemy" (p. 116, folio 106).
- **A transmission claim about the reed.** The fiery-reed lines at the opening of the `Mathnawf`
  [text-layer?: Mathnawī] are "an image that was taken over by numerous later Persian poets,
  particularly in the Indo-Persian tradition" (p. 116, folio 106).
- **The sun unites `jamal` and `jaltil` [text-layer?: jalāl].** The sun is "a perfect manifestation
  of God's `jamal` [text-layer?: jamāl] and `jaltil` [text-layer?: jalāl], the mysterium fascinans
  and the mysterium tremendum" (p. 118, folio 108).
- **A comparison to ancient and Christian hymnody.** Rumi's poems "sound almost like the powerful
  odes which were sung in ancient Egypt and Assur to praise this central light of life, and they
  are reminiscent of medieval hymns to Christ, the sol verus et dies verus" (p. 118, folio 108).
- **A comparison to Quietist devotion.** Love appears "(like Mme. Guyon's Torrents spirituels) as a
  powerful torrent which washes away everything and destroys man's external being completely"
  (p. 119, folio 109).
- **A measured asymmetry between the two elemental spheres.** "Understandably, the positive,
  wholesome aspects of Love are more prominent in the watery than in the fiery sphere"
  (p. 119, folio 109).
- **Rumi as anticipator of later Urdu and Persian verse.** "Rumi, prefiguring scores of later
  Persian and Urdu poets, can claim that 'Love has rosegardens in the midst of veils of blood'"
  (p. 120, folio 110).
- **"Turk" and the contrasting pairs are conventions, not ethnography.** "`riimi` [text-layer?:
  rūmī], Byzantine, and `zanji` [text-layer?: zanjī], Ethiopian, form a traditional contrasting
  pair as do turk and `h indii` [text-layer?: hindū]" (p. 124, folio 114); and "Turk is here, as
  elsewhere, the designation of the powerful, radiant, and cruel beloved, the princely rider"
  (p. 126, folio 116).
- **The `Iii` [text-layer?: lā] of the shahada read as a sword.** Love's sword cutting off every
  head beside it is "the traditional image of the negation, `Iii` [text-layer?: lā], with which the
  profession of faith begins and which in its graphic form is interpreted as a double-edged sword.
  It is comparable to `c Ali`'s [text-layer?: ʿAlī's] famous Dhu'l-fiqar" (p. 125, folio 115).
- **The grotesque personifications are held to be without precedent.** "other images in Rumi's work
  are uncommon and verge on the grotesque. Who else would see Love as the police chief, `shi]Jna`
  [text-layer?: shiḥna] …?" (p. 126, folio 116); the unicorn image is likewise called "Unusual in
  the context of Persian poetry" (p. 124, folio 114).
- **The moth-and-candle image is comparatively rare in Rumi.** "Maulana does not frequently use the
  favorite image of most Persian poets, that of 'moth and candle,' but when it occurs is has a
  special depth" (p. 117, folio 107).
- **An explicit gap in scholarship, flagged by the author.** "A full study of this aspect of Rumi's
  prophetology, which again leads back to Shamsuddin's connection with the Prophet, is yet to be
  done (see `alst` [text-layer?: also] chapter 5)" (p. 139, folio 121).
- **A reading of fear and hope as beginners' stations.** "This means that fear and hope are stages
  of the beginners; when they are overcome man will reach annihilation in love-intoxication"
  (p. 137, folio 119).
- **A Koranic anchor for Love-as-robe.** Love is "the robe of honor for the soul and the necklace of
  `karramnd` [text-layer?: karramnā] (`Sura 17170` [text-layer?: Sura 17/70]): 'We have honored the
  children of Adam'" (p. 137, folio 119).
- **Love as a feminine concept at the chunk's close.** "Love, cruel and overpowering as it generally
  is, can at times be a feminine concept, for it is the mother who gives birth to man and to the
  four elements"; Rumi "goes so far as to speak of Love as being the pre-eternal Mary", with the
  harlot World as her contrast, "who devours her children!" (p. 139, folio 121).
