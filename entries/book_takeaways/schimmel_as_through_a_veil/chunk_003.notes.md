## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Sufi orders and language.** "The `literahtre`[text-layer?: literature] of the Sufi urban orders
  was generally composed in Persian, the standard language of
  `literahtre`[text-layer?: literature] and, from the late fifteenth century, of administration"
  (p. 65, folio 55).
- **Dhikr.** The "bipartite profession of faith, `lti iltiha illti Allah`[text-layer?: lā ilāha illā
  Allāh]" — "This formula is used by many Sufi orders as dhikr" (p. 69, folio 59). No order is
  named there (not stated).
- **Poetry in musical performance.** Of the `tarjfband`[text-layer?: tarjīʿband] form: "This form is
  also very fitting for musical performance, where the repeated verse can be taken up by the chorus
  to form a kind of continua" (p. 67, folio 57).
- **Singing at great age.** Gesudaraz "even at the age of `9<J`[text-layer?: probably 90] still
  enjoyed singing of his love" (p. 77, folio 67).
- **Contemplation of a human witness.** The chunk describes "an aspect of Sufism that had become
  more and more popular: to see the Divine beauty reflected in or manifested through a human being,
  ideally a boy of fourteen", and the regarding of him as "the true witness,
  `shiihid`[text-layer?: shāhid], of Divine beauty" (p. 77, folio 67).
- **Discipline and dispute over that practice.** "The veneration of young boys-branded by the
  orthodox as a kind of manicheism-developed into a highly refined spiritual art, but could also
  degenerate into a more or less crude homosexuality"; "The sober Sufis were always aware of the
  danger of 'looking at the unbearded'"; Sana'i "accused the Sufis of his time of having no other
  qibla but shahid, shame, and shikam (a handsome boy, candles at parties, and their stomachs)";
  "The lovers, however, maintained that such a love was licit, provided that the chastity of the
  glance was preserved" (p. 78, folio 68).
- **Pilgrimage.** The caravan bell "guides them to the sanctuary, Mecca, where the Beloved dwells",
  and the book notes the pilgrimage "is understood, as it is by many, as a metaphor for the journey
  into one's own soul" (p. 75, folio 65).
- **Hagiographical collections as a working resource for poets.** ʿAttar's prose
  `Tadhkirat al-auliyti`[text-layer?: Tadhkirat al-auliyā] "furnished innumerable poets in Iran,
  Turkey, and India with stories about the lives of earlier Sufis, particularly Hallaj" (p. 63,
  folio 53); Jami "reworked Ansari's hagiographical work, `reti-` / `tled`
  `Nafa]Jat al-uns`[text-layer?: Nafaḥāt al-uns]" (p. 62, folio 52).
- **Devotional texts in continuing use.** "Theref ore the `Muniijiit`[text-layer?: Munājāt] are used,
  though in different redactions, to our day, and its form became a model for many later writings"
  (p. 61, folio 51).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Arabic religious poetry in classical forms was an Indian as much as an Arab activity.** Such
  poetry "was not restricted to the Arab countries proper; Muslim India produced an enormous output
  of religious poetry in classical forms, faithful to the traditional poetical rules", and the
  competition in Arabic forms continued "even in the eighteenth century" (p. 57, folio 47).
- **Post-1945 Arabic mysticism is a political recourse, and the author rates it highly.** The poets
  "by no means stand in the current of orthodox Islam but have tried to find a new religious base in
  the mystical tradition of their religion"; "The recourse of these poets, some of whom politically
  are leftists, to the Sufi tradition seems to me an important development even though it may not be
  more than a passing phase in contemporary Arabic poetry"; "We have here a first attempt to create
  a truly mythical poetry in Arabic, but also an example of sociopolitical criticism" (p. 57,
  folio 47).
- **Her periodisation of decline, stated as her own view.** Such modern verse "shows that there is
  still a possibility of rescuing mystical poetry from the fetters in which it was kept more or less
  from the `tum`[text-layer?: turn] of the fourteenth century, and it also shows, as it appears to
  me, that the message of love and sacrifice, of metamorphosis through death... is alive and
  meaningful even today" (p. 58, folio 48).
- **The Western image of Persian poetry is an artefact of bad translation.** Hafiz's poetry
  "fascinated Europe from the late eighteenth century onward and was instrumental in shaping the
  general notion in the West of Persian poetry as something that expressed uninhibited sensual love,
  religious freedom if not outright heresy, and that sang mainly of wine, roses, and nightingales";
  Goethe's verse shows "a deeper understanding of the character of Persian poetry than do the very
  free and often faulty 'translations' and imitations by most other Western writers on Hafiz"
  (p. 59, folio not stated).
- **Form does interpretive work.** The monorhyme "like arabesque decorations or the pattern of a
  carpet, seems to be able to continue beyond the physical boundaries of the design", and "the rhyme
  determines, to a certain extent, the poet's choice of words and figures of speech and thus
  provides the poem with a basic unity" (p. 60, folio 50).
- **Persian mystical poetry rose as Arabic declined**, and "Mystical ideas and feelings had been
  alive in Iran from the early days of Islam; eastern Iran, Khorasan, and present-day Afghanistan
  were the homes of the first ascetic movements in Islam, which perhaps were influenced in some way
  by Buddhism, whose old center was Bactria" (p. 61, folio 51). The Buddhist influence is given as
  "perhaps".
- **She rejects a standard attribution.** "even though we can no longer accept the traditional view
  that the first Persian mystical quatrains were composed by
  `Abu Sac id-i Abu'l-Khair`[text-layer?: Abū Saʿīd-i Abu'l-Khair], the saint of Maihana (d. 1049)
  it is a fact that mystical literature in Persian developed first in the Eastern Lands of the
  Caliphate" (p. 61, folio 51).
- **A measured shift in Sufi thought, argued from two books written in one city.** "The change in
  emphasis in Sufi thought between the late eleventh and the late fifteenth centuries can be
  understood best by comparing Ansari's Munajat with a booklet in a similar style, written in the
  same city of Herat and by the scholar-poet Molla Jami"; "the difference between the heartbeat of
  Ansari's warm orisons and Jami's more cerebral `Lawa'ih` is very telling" (p. 62, folio 52).
- **The mathnawi is a Persian, not an Arabic, resource.** "The form of
  `mathnawf`[text-layer?: mathnawī], rhyming couplets, was unknown in Arabic writing circles", and
  "The form allows the poet to extend his thoughts and tales as long as he deems it necessary-from a
  couple of hundred verses to tens of thousands of lines" (p. 62, folio 52).
- **A frank negative judgement on a canonical text.** Sana'i's
  `ljadfqat al-haqfqat`[text-layer?: Ḥadīqat al-ḥaqīqat], "The Garden of Truth", "has been
  considered by orientalists and even by modern Orientals a rather boring piece of literature
  because its meter is somewhat halting, and its diction is quite straight in spite of the author's
  amazing rhetorical skill"; its language "is not exactly 'poetic,' but rather earthbound and at
  times outright vulgar" (p. 62, folio 52).
- **Sana'i as the inventor of the Persian naʿt.** "Sana'i's poetic skills are much more conspicuous
  in his lyrics and his panegyrics on the Prophet, a genre which he seems to have introduced into
  Persian literature (see Chapter 5)" (p. 63, folio 53).
- **Metre as transmission.** The metre of ʿAttar's most famous epics "was taken over by Rumi for his
  great Mathnawi and subsequently became used in most mystical and didactic poems in the Persian and
  Persianate tradition" (p. 63, folio 53). She also reads decline in ʿAttar's late work:
  `Ushturntima`[text-layer?: Ushturnāma] "shows already some disintegration of style; ecstasy, it
  seems, overwhelms him often in his later compositions" (p. 63, folio 53).
- **Ruzbihan-i Baqli is the key to reading the whole tradition.** "his theories form the basis for
  our understanding of most Persian mystical poetry" (pp. 64–65, folios 54–55); Ahmad Ghazzali
  refined the Persian language "to almost silken smoothness and transparency" (p. 64, folio 54); and
  ʿAinulqudat's work "became, from the early fourteenth century onward, one of the important
  stimulants of Indo-Muslim mystical literature" (p. 64, folio 54).
- **A rhetorical skill that obscures.** "in Jami's verse a stupendous rhetorical skill often hides
  the poet's real concern" (p. 65, folio 55).
- **India outproduced Iran in Persian.** "India too contributed to this
  `literatur�in`[text-layer?: literature—in] fact, the Subcontinent produced more Persian works than
  Iran proper" (p. 65, folio 55); and the Indian Style, `sabk-i hindf`[text-layer?: sabk-i hindī],
  developed by poets "many of whom were immigrants from Safawid Iran", is "complicated and in the
  end almost incomprehensible" yet "has moments of extreme beauty" (p. 65, folio 55).
- **Urdu mystical poetry is derivative of Persian, and almost non-existent.** "Poetry in
  Urdu-in[text-layer?: Urdu—in] which only `Mir Oard`[text-layer?: Mir Dard] in eighteenth
  `cenhtry`[text-layer?: century] Delhi composed truly mystical
  `vers�is`[text-layer?: verse—is] unthinkable without the Persian literary tradition" (p. 65,
  folio 55).
- **Turkish Divan poetry was dismissed as imitation, and she extends the charge to Persian itself.**
  "Turkish 'Divan Poetry' was often regarded as a mere imitation of Persian models, and E. H. W.
  Gibb's introduction to his useful History of Ottoman Poetry is valid for most of Persian poetry as
  well" (p. 65, folio 55).
- **Genre is matched to theology.** In a heroic epic God "would be called predominantly by names of
  wrath, like `al-qahhar` (The Wrathful), or `al-jabbiir`[text-layer?: al-jabbār] (The
  Overpowering), while in romantic epics He would appear as the one who kindles the flame of love,
  as `al-latif`[text-layer?: al-laṭīf] (The Tender), or `al-wadud`[text-layer?: al-wadūd] (The
  Loving)" (p. 66, folio 56).
- **The qasida is a borrowed Arabic form, the ghazal a derived one.** The
  `qatiida`[text-layer?: qaṣīda] "was taken over without major change from the Arabs" (p. 66,
  folio 56); the ghazal "probably developed out of the erotic introduction of the
  `qa�ida`[text-layer?: qaṣīda]" (p. 67, folio 57). The `radif` lets the poet "easily insert many
  repetitions of the same exclamation or question" (p. 66, folio 56), and such qasidas "circumscribe
  with constantly changing metaphors the Numen without ever plumbing Its abysses" (p. 66, folio 56).
- **Two rhetorical figures carry the mystical content.** `muriicat an-` /
  `n�fr`[text-layer?: murāʿāt an-naẓīr] "requires that the images in a verse stand in a well-defined
  relation to each other. When the rose is mentioned, we can definitely expect a nightingale"; the
  other, `husn-i taclfl`[text-layer?: ḥusn-i taʿlīl], "the fantastic etiology, allows the poet to
  make everything alive" (pp. 68–69, folios 58–59). She reads the result as a visual analogue: "as
  in a perfect classical Persian miniature, everything has its place so that, as in miniature
  painting, one may speak of a two-dimensional system of signs that have equal, or near-equal,
  value" (p. 68, folio 58).
- **Unity-language predates its own theory.** "The formula `hama ast`, 'Everything is He,' was
  already in use before Ibn `c Arabi`[text-layer?: ʿArabī] laid down the foundations of the theory of
  `wahdat al-wujud`[text-layer?: waḥdat al-wujūd]; it is found more than once in ʿAttar's lyrics,
  while in later mystical poetry it was to become pivotal" (p. 71, folio 61). She distinguishes this
  from doctrine: "in most cases this was rather the expression of an overwhelming feeling of God's
  omnipresence, not the kind of theoretical pantheism that became so prevalent in post-Ibn
  `c Arabi`[text-layer?: ʿArabī] poetry" (p. 70, folio 60).
- **The decline thesis, stated twice more.** Poets like `Maghribi (d. 14o6)`[text-layer?: d. 1406]
  expressed Unity of Being "in thousands of lines whose imagery soon became stereotyped", and
  Ibn ʿArabi's method in the `Tarjumtin al-ashwaq`[text-layer?: Tarjumān al-ashwāq] "made this kind
  of poetry easy to handle, and as the conventional topoi became more shallow and outworn, only very
  few poets were able to fill them with new life" (p. 73, folio 63). Later, ʿIraqi's lines "form the
  model for thousands of verses, often by mediocre writers who were no longer aware of the secret of
  longing as the source of true poetry" (p. 80, folio 70).
- **Why Muslim modernists reject this poetry.** In this tradition "the inherited values of Islam
  were considered only as a support for the poor plebeians who had not yet reached the lofty heights
  of all-embracing unity"; hence "the aversion of Muslim modernists to this poetry, which seemed to
  lull their compatriots into sweet slumber instead of inciting them to new activity on the Path of
  God. For the dynamic love-relation between God and man had been replaced, in most cases, by a
  theosophic `tauhid`[text-layer?: tauḥīd] expressed in sweeping statements" (p. 73, folio 63).
- **A comparative frame, borrowed and named.** "Evelyn Underhill has shown that in every religious
  tradition mystics try to explain their experiences in three major categories of symbols" —
  pilgrimage, alchemy, and human love — and the author adds that Persian poets "use and interweave
  them often in a single verse even though the center of their thoughts is always Love" (p. 74,
  folio 64). She reaches outside Islam repeatedly: to "the Buddha, Ignatius of Loyola" on running
  water (p. 71, folio 61), to "Meister Eckhart" on sinking "from nothing to nothing" (p. 75,
  folio 65), to "yang and yin" (p. 69, folio 59), and to Rudolf Otto's terms "the fascinans and the
  tremendum" for `jamlil and jaltil`[text-layer?: jamāl and jalāl] (p. 69, folio 59), though Otto is
  not named.
- **Anti-intellectualism as a deliberate posture.** "the mystics, anti-intellectual despite their
  great learning, repeated time and again that reason, or intellect, leads to talk while Love
  silently reveals the inside of the mysteries"; Sana'i, "probably following Ghazzali's attack on
  Avicenna in the `Ta -` / `hafut al-falasifa`[text-layer?: Tahāfut al-falāsifa]-was the first poet
  to condemn his compatriot Avicenna, the philosopher-physician, as the paragon of love-less
  intellectualism" (p. 77, folio 67).
- **Ambiguity is the defining virtue of the Persian ghazal.** "The constant oscillation between the
  two levels of experience often makes it next to impossible to translate or even to understand a
  poem correctly. It is this ambiguity between the human and the superhuman levels which makes the
  Persian ghazal so delightful, like a two-faced brocade" — and she refuses the alternative,
  "Without returning to the heavy and usually quite tasteless theological interpretation of the
  various metaphors" (pp. 78–79, folios 68–69).
- **Pain is constructed, not merely suffered.** "The Persian poets invented an ingenious way to
  prove the value of pain and affliction by pointing to the central metahistorical event, the
  `rii.z-i alast`[text-layer?: rūz-i alast]" (p. 80, folio 70); "Destruction is necessary for
  building up: kenosis, emptying oneself, complete surrender, or ''being broken' is the prerequisite
  for a higher spiritual life" (p. 81, folio 71); and the imagery's "cruelty... shocked Goethe when
  he tried to give an impartial judgment of Persian poetry" (p. 81, folio 71).

## Doubts

**Archive mapping.**

- **The Lahore attachment is place-level, not site-specific.** Under ruling (a) the clause "who
  spent his last years in Lahore" (p. 61, folio 51) is headed with all 31 Lahore rows. The book
  names no shrine, tomb, saint, ʿurs or custodian in Lahore anywhere in this chunk, so the only
  thing the 31 rows share is the city name. A consolidator should weigh this accordingly.
- **`data-darbar` rests on an author mention plus one biographical clause.** The book writes
  "Hujwiri" and nothing more — no "Ali ibn Usman", no "Data Ganj Bakhsh", no death date, no tomb, no
  order (all not stated). Under the 18 September 2026 ruling this is still legitimate material for
  the row, but it is a bare-name case and is flagged as such.
- **`mazar-e-iqbal` rests on four words inside a parenthesis** — "(e.g., in Iqbal's work)" (p. 77,
  folio 67). The mention is real and, per the ruling, belongs to the row; it carries no fact about
  Iqbal beyond his participation in a Persian topos about Avicenna.
- **Mahmud of Ghazna is refused as a mapping.** "the conquest by Mahmud of Ghazna of its north
  western part (now Pakistan)" (p. 65, folio 55) touches the archive only through
  `darbar-malik-ahmad-ayaz`, whose principal figure is described in the index as "slave of Mahmud
  Ghaznavi, minister, and governor of Lahore". Mahmud himself is the principal figure of no row, and
  the chunk names neither Malik Ayaz nor any shrine, so no id is claimed. Flagged only so the
  descriptor is not later mistaken for evidence.
- **Silsila names are refused as mappings.** "Gesudaraz, the Chishti saint" (p. 77, folio 67)
  matches the silsila field of several rows (`shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar`, `golra-sharif`,
  `sial-sharif`, `mausoleum-of-waris-shah`, `eidgah-sharif` and others); "many Sufi orders" (p. 69,
  folio 59) and "the mystical silsila of Ibn-i Khafif" (p. 64, folio 54) name no archive order at
  all. Gesudaraz is the principal figure of no row and is recorded under "Other saints, sites and
  events".
- **No other archive row appears in these twenty-five pages, and I checked for them.** Every named
  figure was tested against the 169 rows' `principal_figure` column and every place name against
  `location_short` / `toponym_map.txt`. The chunk's saints and poets are overwhelmingly Iranian,
  Iraqi, Ottoman and Delhi-based; no Sindhi, Panjabi, Pashto or Balochi shrine figure is named, and
  "Lahore" is the only Pakistani place name in the chunk.
