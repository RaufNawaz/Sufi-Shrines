## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Samaʿ.** The "popular style" verses "immediately sound singable and must have been used in samac
  [text-layer?: samāʿ] meetings; they usually consist of rather short lines in light meter" (p. 33,
  folio 23).
- **Music and orthodox reaction.** Love poems read mystically "inspired them to ecstasy and more than
  once caused the death of a lover, be he Sufi or layman"; orthodoxy recognised the danger of
  "breaking out of the well-ordered world of the nomos, the Divinely inspired law, into the vast
  fields of eros in all its shades; hence the Muslim orthodoxy's fierce reactions to the introduction
  of music in Sufi circles" (p. 34, folio 24).
- **Qawwali form, described as living practice.** One short poem of Dhu'n-Nun "has a form which is
  still used today by the qawwiili [text-layer?: qawwālī]. This form includes a first line which can
  be repeated by the chorus and different second hemistichs" (p. 35, folio 25).
- **Dhikr.** In *dhikr*, "the recollection of God", "not only the tongue and then the heart are filled
  with the name of God, but the whole body of the meditating Sufi is permeated so that his blood and
  each of his limbs is replete with the name of God and practices, as it were, its own dhikr" (p. 38,
  folio 28). Hallaj holds that it is God as remembered who confuses the mystic, not the act:
  "Dhikr is a means [or 'a central pearl'] which hides You from my view" (p. 44, folio 34).
- **Initiation and secret names.** "to know the name of something means to be able to control it: how
  then could anyone be allowed to use the sacred name without proper initiation?"; and "the Sufis
  assume unusual or 'heavenly' names known only to the innermost circles of the initiated" (p. 38,
  folio 28).
- **Martyrdom redefined.** The Sufis "too claimed to die ff sabfl Allah [text-layer?: fī sabīl Allāh],
  on the path of G od, as martyrs; but that meant not, as in orthodox Islam, during the Holy War
  against the infidels, but rather in the 'major holy war,' the struggle against their lower selves,
  in mortifications and by willingly taking upon themselves all afflictions" (p. 40, folio 30).
- **Institutional expansion of the orders.** In the thirteenth century "the Sufi orders, which were
  just beginning to crystallize, carried the message of Divine love into the various lands of Islam
  so that from Morocco to Transoxania, from Central Anatolia to Bengal an almost uninterrupted chain
  of mystical centers was established, on the framework of which all later mystical movements as well
  as literary activities developed" (pp. 45–46, folios 35–36).
- **Hierarchy claimed by founders.** "the founder of a fraternity … claims to be the qutb or ghauth,
  the highest member of the mystical hierarchy" (p. 47, folio 37).
- **The Shadhiliyya.** Identified with "the sober trend grounded in the teachings of the school of
  Baghdad, particularly those of Junaid", and remembered for prose rather than poetry (p. 46,
  folio 36); Busiri "was an early member of the Shadhiliyya in Egypt" (p. 47, folio 37).
- **State-funded shrine cloth.** "just as the Egyptian government used to send every year the black,
  gold-embroidered veil for the Kacba [text-layer?: Kaʿba] to Mecca, thus lbn al-Farid has woven his
  poetical kiswa for the pilgrimage of the soul" (p. 52, folio 42).
- **Censorship by a religious institution, with a date.** Ibn ʿArabi's work was "often regarded … as
  dangerous, if not outright heretic, and its perusal was prohibited once more in February 1979 in
  Egypt by the authorities of al-Azhar" (p. 49, folio 39).
- **Scholarly dispute.** "in the late fifteenth century a fierce fight raged in Cairo between those
  religious scholars who supported his views and those who regarded him as a heretic" (p. 52,
  folio 42).
- **Political geography of the revival.** "Egypt being the only major country that was spared a
  Mongol invasion and remained therefore the leading cultural center of the Arab world" (p. 46,
  folio 36).
- **Transmission and translation.** The *Fuṣūṣ* was "commented upon and translated until our day (the
  Fu�ii� [text-layer?: Fuṣūṣ] even into Urdu verse during the eighteenth century !)" (p. 49,
  folio 39); the first German rhymed *Ta'iyya* was Joseph von Hammer-Purgstall's, "despite the
  beautiful print in oriental style (Vienna, 1854)" (p. 54, folio 44).
- **Popular verse forms taken into Sufi use.** "didactic verse in the simple meter Rajaz", "the
  strophic muwashsha]J [text-layer?: muwashshaḥ] and zajal, and 4-ot [text-layer?: 4- or] 5-lined
  forms such as billfq [text-layer?: reading uncertain], dabait, and mawiiliyii [text-layer?:
  mawāliyā]" (p. 55, folio 45); and the arts of "tash tir and takhmis", "adding two verses of one's
  own to a hemistich of a well-known poem, or filling up each of its distichs with three new lines"
  (p. 56, folio 46).

- Dhu'n-Nun's long-rhymed complaint is "a poem which he allegedly heard from some hermit in Lebanon"
  (p. 35, folio 25).
- Love poetry "more than once caused the death of a lover, be he Sufi or layman" (p. 34, folio 24).
- Shibli "threw a rose at him when he went, dancing, to the gallows" — told of Hallaj's execution
  without qualification (p. 45, folio 35).
- A fraternity's founder, "United with the Light of Muhammad", "claims to have existed well before
  Adam, to have known Idris on his way to heaven and Noah during the flood" (p. 47, folio 37).
- Ibn ʿArabi "admits 'that in some passages of his poems the mystical import was not clear to
  himself, and that various explanations were suggested to him in moments of ecstasy'" (p. 50,
  folio 40).
- Of Ibn al-Farid: "we are told and have every reason to believe that most of his poetry was recited
  in a state of trance as a result of inspiration" (p. 52, folio 42).
- Of the primordial wine of the *Khamriyya*: "its veiled bottle makes the blind see; the sound of its
  libation makes the deaf hear, and its radiant color shows the way like the polar star" (pp. 53–54,
  folios 43–44).


## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Concealment as a historical cause of style.** Junaid's cryptic manner is explained by government
  suspicion of the Sufis "around 900" (p. 32, folio 22); "The Sufi poets knew that they had to hide
  their feelings, but that is impossible" (p. 37, folio 27); the unnamed beloved becomes "the model
  for later love lyrics in which the name of the beloved is never revealed" (p. 37, folio 27).
- **Schimmel's own caution about reading the poetry.** She endorses Temple Gairdner's question "Do we
  not take their language too seriously?" and notes that "'wilful paradox' plays an important role in
  this type of Arabic mystical poetry as it does in later Sufi writing" (p. 32, folio 22). She also
  judges freely: "This verse is certainly not great poetry" of the Cameldriver's Song (p. 33,
  folio 23).
- **Modern readings she reports, not endorses.** Adonis calls early Arabic mystical poetry
  "surrealism before surrealism"; Massignon speaks of the "sapientele plus que philosophique
  [text-layer?: sapientiale plus que philosophique] quality of the Arabic language" and of "Taqmin,
  'inclusion,'" the "germination of the spiritual roots of a word into different branches"; Paul
  Nwyia "has shown how Massignon himself translated Hallaj's line ya sirra sirri [text-layer?: yā
  sirra sirrī] in the three editions of Hallaj's Diwan in three different ways, each of which can be
  defended and makes perfect sense" (pp. 32–33, folios 22–23).
- **Her own linguistic argument.** "every word has a �iihir [text-layer?: ẓāhir] and a batin
  [text-layer?: bāṭin], an external and an interior sense, this holds true for mystical poetry in the
  whole Islamic world"; Arnold Steiger's simile of "a lyre in which the movement of one string makes
  all the others vibrate"; hence the vocabulary "can therefore barely be adequately translated into
  any other language" (p. 33, folio 23).
- **A three-genre scheme, her own.** "A third genre of early Sufi poetry is more easily accessible;
  that is what I would call the popular style" (p. 33, folio 23) — the first two genres are set out
  before this chunk.
- **Hallaj's execution.** "Massignon has proved that it was not so much his famous sentence anii'l-l]aqq
  [text-layer?: anā'l-ḥaqq], 'I am the Absolute Truth,' which brought about his execution, but rather
  some of his religious theories" (p. 40, folio 30).
- **Her strong claims for Hallaj.** "Indeed, Hallaj was the first to discover that the mystery of
  creation lies in the dynamic love which is the very essence of God. He was by no means a pantheist,
  as his teaching was interpreted later; rather, he maintained the Divine transcendence" (pp. 40–41,
  folios 30–31). Massignon is quoted at length: Hallaj's poetical works are "conversations of his
  spirit with the Divine spirit about their common love", and "There is no mystical poetry which is at
  the same time more ardent and more radically dematerialized than that of Hallaj" (p. 41, folio 31).
- **Periodisation.** "With Hallaj, Sufi history and, in a certain way, Sufi poetry reached its first
  climax"; "in the following centuries we rarely find any great Sufi poetry in Arabic; rather,
  handbooks of Sufism were composed which constitute our main sources for early poetry"; "Poetry on a
  higher level appears again in the thirteenth century" (p. 45, folio 35). Gustave E. von Grunebaum
  is cited seeing in it "a growing form of self-expression when the creative impulse in the other
  branches of Arabic literature had long been fading" (p. 46, folio 36).
- **ʿAli Safi Husain's classification, reported.** He distinguishes "practical Sufis," who wrote
  "shier a4-4amfr wa'l-wijdiin [text-layer?: shiʿr aḍ-ḍamīr wa'l-wijdān], poetry born out of
  religious sentiments", from "theoretical Sufis," who composed "shier al-kashf wa'l-ilhiim
  [text-layer?: shiʿr al-kashf wa'l-ilhām]"; and further by *ghazal* style, by "the theories of the
  IJaqiqa mulJammadiyya [text-layer?: ḥaqīqa muḥammadiyya] and the Muhammadan Light (nur Muhammad)",
  and by strict Sunnite sobriety (pp. 46–47, folios 36–37).
- **Schimmel's generalisation from Egypt.** The *ḥaqīqa muḥammadiyya* poetry "is, in fact, typical not
  only of Egyptian or Arabic poetry but of virtually all poetry composed in the Sufi orders" (p. 47,
  folio 37), and "not only the contents but even formal characteristics of mystical folk poetry
  outside the Arabic world are preformed in thirteenth-century Arabic verse" (p. 47, folio 37) — the
  hinge on which the book's later chapters on non-Arabic poetry turn.
- **Against reading Ibn ʿArabi as a pantheist.** "Ibn c Arabi's system-generally called wahdat
  al-wujud, 'Unity of Being' -is much more complicated and should not be interpreted as pure
  pantheism"; "the creatures in their actual existence are not identical with God but are solely the
  reflection of His attributes" (p. 48, folio 38). Corbin and Nasr are reported as having
  "interpreted Ibn c Arabi's theories of 'the Pathetic God Who yearned to be known'" and "the mystery
  of the unio sympathetica between the Divine Names and the named things" (p. 48, folio 38).
- **Her central contrast of the chunk.** "there is a world of difference between his approach to
  religion and the dynamic, personal religion of Hallaj. With Ibn c Arabi, Islamic mysticism comes
  close to the mysticism of infinity, and his approach is theosophical or gnostic rather than
  voluntaristic … while in early Sufism the element of personal love between man and God was
  predominant" (p. 48, folio 38).
- **The "tolerance" poem re-read.** The famous "My heart is capable of every form" poem "seems to
  express the 'tolerance' of the mystic in the most poetical words", but "The poem is, however, a
  glowing tribute to Islam", and Nicholson is quoted: the station of perfect love is "a peculiar
  prerogative of Moslems" (pp. 48–49, folios 38–39).
- **Nicholson contradicted by Lings.** Nicholson "feels that 'the book is too abstract and remote from
  common experience to give pleasure to others'"; this "is gently but firmly contradicted by Martin
  Lings in his brief introduction to the new edition of the translation" (pp. 49–50, folios 39–40).
  Schimmel herself prefers Rumi: comparing the introductory poem with Zulaikha's monologue makes one
  "feel the difference in poetical depth and warmth" (p. 50, folio 40).
- **Allegorical commentary as a survival strategy.** The Ibn ʿArabi method of interpretation "became a
  vehicle that carried the teachings of the Ibn c Arabi-school into every corner of the Muslim world,
  because it enabled poets to give even the most worldly poem a 'spiritual' interpretation, and thus
  avoid the wrath of the orthodox" (p. 51, folio 41). She also floats a genealogy for a poetic motif:
  "one wonders whether the constant interpretation of barq, 'lightning,' … has not caused the
  extremely frequent use of the motif of lightning in Indo-Persian poetry" (p. 51, folio 41).
- **On Ibn al-Farid's Ta'iyya.** Massignon saw "a lourde tapisserie de brocade mordante, a kiswa for
  the spiritual journey" reaching "transcendental esthetics"; Nicholson that "His style and diction
  resemble the choicest and finest jewel work of a fastidious artist rather than the first fruit of
  divine inspiration" (p. 52, folio 42); Nicholson also "rightly points out that the pantheistic
  trends which aroused the anger of the theologians are expressions of a state of feeling rather than
  a system of thought" (p. 54, folio 44). Her own verdict: his odes are, "along with the poems of
  Hallaj, the only truly great mystical poetry in Arabic" (p. 55, folio 45).
- **Decline thesis.** "While true inspiration seems to be lacking in most of the post-thirteenth-century
  mystical poetry, the Sufis indulged in writing extensions of and variations on classical poems"
  (p. 56, folio 46); ʿAbdul Ghani's verse "does not breathe the bittersweet happiness of the
  immediate, loving experience as found in the simpler lines of some early Sufis" (p. 56, folio 46).
- **Comparative excursions into European material** (reported here because they are the author's own
  argumentative moves, not because the chunk documents them): "Doppelgliick der Tone und der Liebe-
  [text-layer?: Doppelglück der Töne und der Liebe]" (p. 34, folio 24); "Suso's remark that: 'To love
  belongs according to the old custom, suffering'" (p. 40, folio 30); "the secret of Stirb und werde"
  (p. 42, folio 32); the moth-and-candle allegory reaching "Europe around 1800 to find there its most
  famous expression in Goethe's poem Selige Sehnsuch t [text-layer?: Selige Sehnsucht]" (p. 44,
  folio 34); "true Erlebnislyrik" (p. 53, folio 43).

**Archive mapping.**

- **The single mapping rests on a bare name in a parenthesis.** "Shah c Abdul Latif in Sind" (p. 43,
  folio 33) is the whole of the evidence for **bhit-bhit-shah**: no date, no work, no site, no
  silsila. The 18 September ruling makes this a legitimate bullet, but the consolidator should weigh
  it as author-only, single-mention evidence.
- **Name collision on "Abdul Latif", flagged rather than resolved in silence.** Two archive rows
  carry that element: `bhit-bhit-shah` (principal figure "Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai", location "Bhit
  (Bhit Shah), Bhit, Sindh") and `bari-imam` (principal figure "Sayyid Abdul Latif Kazmi (Bari
  Imam)", location "Bari Imam, Islamabad"). I mapped to `bhit-bhit-shah` because the book supplies
  both the title "Shah" and the location "in Sind", which match that row and not the Islamabad row;
  `shrine-of-shah-abdul-karim-bulri` shares "Shah Abdul" but the given name differs (Karim, not
  Latif). Recorded here so a human can confirm the choice.
- **Four string-similar names deliberately refused; no id claimed for any of them.** "lbn al-Farid"
  (pp. 51–56, folios 41–46) is an Egyptian Arabic poet and is **not** Baba Fariduddin Masʿud
  Ganjshakar (`shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar`) or Khwaja Ghulam Farid (`mithankot-kot-mithan`).
  "Sarmad in Delhi" (p. 43, folio 33) is **not** Sachal Sarmast (`shrine-of-sachal-sarmast`) — a
  different name and a different place. "Hafiz's poetry" (p. 51, folio 41) is the Persian poet and
  **not** Hafiz Muhammad Jamal Multani (`shrine-of-hafiz-muhammad-jamal-multani`) or "Hazrat Hafiz
  Muhammad Abdul Karim" (`eidgah-sharif`). "Shibli" (pp. 37–54) matches no archive row. Flagged so
  the consolidator does not later mistake the resemblance for evidence.
- **Ruling (a) is not triggered in this chunk, and the negative is deliberate.** The only place named
  that lies inside the archive's Pakistan scope is "Sind" (p. 43, folio 33). It is a province-level
  name: no row's `location_short` is "Sind" or "Sindh" alone, and `toponym_map.txt` — which is the
  join already done for every place with 2+ rows — has no "Sind"/"Sindh" entry. I have therefore
  treated it as a region rather than a site, consistent with the calibration file's treatment of
  "the Panjab". **If Rauf rules that a province-level name does trigger (a)**, the candidate set from
  this one word would be every Sindh row in the index (`bhit-bhit-shah`, `lal-shahbaz-qalandar`,
  `shrine-of-sachal-sarmast`, `dargah-roza-sufi-shah-inayat-shaheed`,
  `shrine-of-shah-abdul-karim-bulri`, `shrine-of-makhdoom-nooh-hala` and the rest of the Sindh
  rows) — recorded this way so the decision stays reversible. Every other place in the chunk
  (Baghdad, Lebanon, Delhi, Mecca, Egypt, Cairo, the Nile, Morocco, Transoxania, Central Anatolia,
  Bengal, Tlemcen, Vienna, Europe) is outside the archive's scope entirely.
- **No silsila-based mapping is claimed.** The chunk names the "Shadhiliyya order" (pp. 46–47, folios
  36–37) and fraternities founded by "Ahmad al-Badawi and Ibrahim ad-Dasuqi in Egypt" (p. 47,
  folio 37). No archive row records a Shadhili silsila, and the chunk connects none of these orders
  to any site in the archive.
