## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The orthodox objection to abrogating ritual.** The doctrine at issue in the opening note is
  "isqat al-wasa'""it"[text-layer?: isqāṭ al-wasāʾiṭ], "the abrogation of intermediaries: ritual
  practices are optional for those who have reached union with the Di­vine-a theory which could lead
  to dangerous consequences, particularly in social ethics, and was therefore objected to by the
  orthodox" (p. 241, folio 223).
- **A dated juridical trial of a mystical poet.** "That happened in Muharram 875'July 1470"
  [text-layer?: Muharram 875/July 1470]: "the Hanafite qiicli al-qucliit"[text-layer?: qāḍī
  al-quḍāt], "Muhibbuddin ibn ash-Shihna, was the leader of the faction which claimed that lbn
  al-Farid was a heretic or infidel, while the strongest defense of the mystical poet came from
  Jalaluddin as-Suyuti, the noted traditionist and po­lyhistor, who was a member of the Shadhiliyya
  order. The ruler, Sultan Qait­bai, who himself had some religious inclinations, sided with the
  latter group" (p. 243, folio 225). The source given is "Ibn lyiis"[text-layer?: Ibn Iyās],
  "Bada'i' az-zuhar fi waqa'i' ad-duhar".
- **Naming as an initiatic act.** "One has to remember that a new name is usually given at an
  initia­tion, be it into an order, or a conversion or baptism; Maulana, by assuming Shamsuddin's
  name for his poetical activity, has as it were died to himself and found a new life in the mystical
  beloved" (p. 255, folio 237).
- **Initiation without a living master.** The *uwaisī* mystic "enters the Path without formal
  initiation through a living master" (p. 250, folio 232).
- **Appointment of a khalifa.** "According to Aflaki, Sultan Walad mentioned that this poem was
  recited when Maulana chose Husamuddin as his khalifa" (p. 257, folio 239).
- **Dhikr and the power of the name.** The note speaks of "this 'magic of the name,' of which the
  Sufis in their dhikr were very well aware", and pairs it with Gertrude Stein's remark on repeating
  a name (p. 256, folio 238).
- **Inscription and tomb epigraphy — two instances.** Gesudaraz's poem "is inscribed around the
  interior of the grand dome over his tomb in Gulbarga" (p. 254, folio 236); and of a poem with the
  *takhalluṣ* Shamsuddin, "these verses are inscribed on his sarcophagus" (p. 257, folio 239) —
  whose sarcophagus is left ambiguous, see `## Doubts`.
- **Execution for religious claims in verse.** Nesimi's "religious claims, expressed in daring verse
  in classical style, led to his execution in Aleppo in 1417" (p. 247, folio 229).
- **Popular saying attached to a saint's death.** "still today the Turkish proverb says doshm attfgf
  gal onar"[text-layer?: dost attığı gül] "'The rose cast by a friend, hurts.'" (p. 241, folio 223).
- **Burial ground as a scholarly subject.** "Louis Massignon, La Cite des Morts au Caire" is cited
  bare (p. 243, folio 225).
- **Dispute over gazing at youths.** Beside the *Kashf al-maḥjūb* prohibition, "The Hanbalite Ibn
  Qayyim al-Jauziyya goes even farther. He writes: 'The immoral among mystic lovers commit a more
  serious sin than either idolators or sodomites . They combine the transgression of the two
  groups-worshipping their minions and copulating with their idols'" (pp. 250–251, folios 232–233).
- **Paradise dress and popular symbolism.** "The green color of the parrot reminded the Sufi poets of
  the green robes in Paradise, and his gift for languages earned him the constant epithet 'sugar
  chewing'" (p. 253, folio 235).
- **A living informant's practice, reported first-hand.** "In Turkey, the fondness of poets and
  painters for the tulip was explained to me by mystically minded friends with the re­mark that
  Iii/a"[text-layer?: lāla], "hi/iii"[text-layer?: hilāl] "('crescent'), and Al/ii/I"[text-layer?:
  Allāh] "all consisted of the same four letters and had the same numerical value, namely 66, hence
  the tulip became a kind of religious cipher" (p. 254, folio 236).
- **Manuscripts and collections named as holdings** (useful for provenance): "Berlin,
  Staatsbibliothek Preussischer Kulturbesitz" and "a manuscript in the Pierpont Morgan Library, New

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Against reading classical Persian lyric as autobiography.** Of Hafiz's *turk-i shīrāzī* line:
  it "is not primarily important because of its contents but rather because of the de­lightful
  rhetorical plays concealed in it" (p. 247, folio 229). The analysis names "the contrast
  Turk-Hindu, extremely widespread in Persian and related poetry", "three geo­graphical names:
  Shiraz, Bukhara, and Samarqand", and "three parts of the body: mole, heart, and hand, so that a
  multilevel murli°dt an-111l4ir"[text-layer?: murāʿāt an-naẓīr] "is achieved which is immediately
  destroyed when the Shirazi Turk is trans­formed into a 'friend' or a 'barmaid'" (p. 248,
  folio 230). The conclusion: "we should not look for Erlebnislyrik, in the postenlightenment sense,
  in this poetry", with the miniature-painting analogy and the Schaeder-versus-Stolz exchange
  (p. 248, folio 230).
- **Unity of Being read down to psychology.** On Rumi's Zulaikha passage: "Zulaikha's song is not an
  expression of a the­oretical Unity of Being but rather translates into verse the psychological
  truth which the Sufis condensed in the sentence: 'Who loves something mentions it often.'"
  (p. 242, folio 224).
- **Why the West mistook Sufism for pantheism.** "It is due to this kind of poetry, which became
  known in the West prior to other Islamic works, that most Western scholars regarded Sufism as
  sheer pantheism, as is evident from the very title of Tholuck's pi­oneering thesis of 1821,
  Ssufismus sive theosophia persarum pantheistica" (p. 249, folio 231).
- **A suppressed early corrective.** Hammer-Purgstall "has clearly stated the nonpantheistic
  character of the Matlmawi"[text-layer?: Mathnawī]; "Unfortunately, this interesting study has never
  been mentioned by any later student of Rumi in Europe" (p. 254, folio 236). The same scholar's
  *Das arabische Hohelied der Liebe* "was dismissed at"[text-layer?: by] "R. A. Nicholson with the
  remark that 'the ta'iyya had the misfortune of being translated by Hammer'" (p. 243, folio 225).
- **A field-level complaint about coverage.** "The wealth of Arabic literature in classical forms
  that existed in the Indo-Pakistan Subcontinent is often overlooked in histories of Arabic
  litera­ture" (pp. 244–245, folios 226–227).
