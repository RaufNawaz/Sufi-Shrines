## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **gurudwara-janam-asthan-nankana-sahib**, **gurdwara-babay-de-ber**, **gurdwara-balila-sahib-bal-lila-sahib**, **gurdwara-chakki-sahib**, **gurdwara-choa-sahib**, **gurdwara-darbar-sahib-kartarpur**, **gurdwara-khoohi-bhai-lalo-bhai-lalo-di-khooi**, **gurdwara-malji-sahib**, **gurdwara-panja-sahib**, **gurdwara-patti-sahib**, **gurdwara-pehli-patshahi-jind-pir-sukkur**, **gurdwara-rori-sahib**, **gurdwara-sach-khand-sahib**, **gurdwara-sacha-sauda**, **gurdwara-sahib-saidpur-guru-nanak-dev-ji**, **gurdwara-sri-kiara-sahib**, **gurdwara-sri-tilganji-sahib**, **gurdwara-tambo-sahib** (the book's "Nanak", "the Guru"; all material below is Dhillon's retelling of janamsakhi stories, not history):
  - In Dhillon's retelling (end of the Kumbh Mela story), Nanak's teachings were at first "considered strange because many of them did not subscribe ro [text-layer?] [to] the traditional beliefs about religion held by the Hindus"; the precepts he preached are listed as earning an honest living, sharing with the less fortunate, never consciously harming another, performing "seva (service)", and always keeping God in mind (p. 103, folio 114).
  - What was different, the book says, was "the complete absence of any emphasis on rites and rituals", yet "he never criticized the beliefs of those who did lay a great deal of emphasis on rituals" (p. 103, folio 114).
  - Leaders of neighbouring "akharas" were jealous and ran "a vilification campaign against him, accusing him of being an apostate" (p. 103, folio 114; p. 104, folio 115).
  - In the retelling, on "the day of the Mauni Amavasya, the most auspicious day of the Kwnbh Mela [text-layer?] [Kumbh Mela]" at "Brahma muhurta (early morning)", Nanak stayed in meditation and did not bathe (p. 104, folio 115); the envious "mahant (or head)" of the neighbouring akhara denounced him publicly: "He is in Prayag on the banks of the holy Ganga … but he has not bothered to go down to the river" (p. 105, folio 116).
  - Nanak's reply in the retelling: the body "merely obeys the commands of our souls", so it is the soul that must be washed; he compares the ritual-only bather to "a brass utensil" polished outside but "filled wirh [text-layer?] [with] poison" inside, and says the holy bath "should be a precursor to a life well lived" (p. 106, folio 117).
  - "As it had been in Haridwar, so it was at Allahabad": the bathing practice did not stop, but "a small minority amongst that vast throng" was convinced and would spread his teaching (p. 106, folio 117; p. 107, folio 118).
  - A verse attributed "Says Nanak, it is best to remain quiet" is printed as an epigraph, sourced "Raag Malhar, Chau Padtts27 [text-layer?] [Chau Padas, note 27?]" (p. 108, folio not stated).
  - Ch. 8 "Bad Village, Good Village" opens: Nanak and Mardana "further travelled eastwards, all the way to Assam"; the book says "There are numerous stories in the Janamsakhis" of their "udasis" and the followers they gained (p. 109, folio not stated).
  - The retelling describes their travel: satchels holding two changes of clothing, emergency rations and rosaries; dependence on hosts and alms; occasional hunger (p. 109, folio not stated; p. 110, folio 122).
  - Parable (as the book retells it): a village that had "forgotten God altogether", whose "one temple … has been allowed to fall into ruin", refused them food and shelter; Nanak blessed it: "May your village prosper! … never feel the need co [text-layer?] [to] go beyond its confines" (pp. 112–116, folios 124–128).
  - A poor cart owner's hesitant offer of hospitality is read by Nanak as "the caste system at play again" (p. 112, folio 124).
  - A second, welcoming village hosted them for seven days (satsang, Nanak reciting "his Bani"); Nanak blessed it that its people "scatter in all directions" (pp. 117–120; p. 117 folio not stated, pp. 118–120 folios 130–132).
  - Nanak's explanation: the rude should stay confined "so that they do not colour the rest of the world", the good should "scatter far and wide so chat [text-layer?] [that] their goodness illumines as many corners of the world as possible" (p. 121, folio 133).
  - Epigraph verse "You bury your treasure underground, / But seek not Him whose treasury is boundless", sourced "R.aag Gauri Bairagan28 [text-layer?] [Raag Gauri Bairagan, note 28]" (p. 122, folio not stated).
  - Ch. 9 "The Story of the Needle": "Nanak and Mardana finally returned to Talwandi in I 506 [text-layer?] [1506]" (p. 123, folio 137); "Mata Tripta felt great joy on seeing her son again" (p. 123, folio 137).
  - In Talwandi Nanak spent time daily with "Rai Bhoe", who, "though the Rai was older", looked upon Nanak "as his Guru" (p. 123, folio 137); "Mehta Kalu" finally recognized "the greatness that Pandit Hardyal had predicted" and was "a little in awe of his son" (p. 124, folio 138).
  - From Talwandi he moved to "Sulcanpur [text-layer?] [Sultanpur]", where "his wife Su.lakhani [text-layer?] [Sulakhani] and their rwo [text-layer?] [two] sons, Sri Chand and La.kb.mi Das [text-layer?] [Lakhmi Das]" joined him (p. 124, folio 138).
  - He then "set out southwards, and as an early step in this journey, travelled to Lahore" (p. 124, folio 138) — the Lahore sojourn is recorded under the Lahore bullet below.

- **gurdwara-rori-sahib** (the book's "Mardana", "Bhai Mardana" not used in this chunk; Nanak's companion):
  - In Dhillon's retelling, Mardana travelled with Nanak eastwards "all the way to Assam" (p. 109, folio not stated).
  - Homesickness: "Often he would sob into his pillow"; Nanak would sit beside him and caress his head (p. 110, folio 122).
  - Mardana went house to house for food in the inhospitable village and was turned away everywhere; "Rudeness and hostility seemed to be the special hallmark of this village" (pp. 113–114, folios 125–126).
  - "Mardana rook [text-layer?] [took] up his rabaab" while Nanak sang "a shabad (hymn)" (p. 115, folio 127).
  - Mardana was troubled by the two blessings and asked Nanak why (pp. 120–121, folios 132–133).
  - Mardana accompanied Nanak to Duni Chand's haveli in Lahore (p. 126, folio 140).

- **gurdwara-babay-nanki** (the book's "Nanki"):
  - At Sultanpur, "Nanki and Jai Ram were ecstatic to have their beloved Nanak back" (p. 124, folio 138).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- Kumbh Mela: "akharas" with a "mahant (or head)"; bathing on "the day of the Mauni Amavasya" at "Brahma muhurta" held necessary "if they hoped co [text-layer?] [to] attain salvation" (p. 104, folio 115).
- Travelling mendicants depending on hosts and alms for food and shelter (p. 110, folio 122); village "dharamshala" invoked as a place of free hospitality (p. 113, folio 125).
- Satsang arising naturally from a host's welcome; villagers seeking a holy man's blessing (p. 119, folio 131).
- "amil (revenue collector)" of Lahore as an official of "the governor's court" (p. 125, folio 139); customary "feeding of the poor and the holy ones on the prescribed days" (p. 127, folio 141).
- Wealth display: red pennants, each "a lakh of rupees" (p. 128, folio not stated).
