  - Ch. 11 opening: "On his journey southwards, Nanak crossed the river Sutlej at Bhatinda and spent four months with Sufi saints of the town"; "The Janamsakhis tell us" he preached there what he had taught Hamza Ghaus (p. 161, folio 177).   - "Sialkot became a part of the Muslim Sultanate when Shahabuddin Muhammad Ghori captured Punjab in 1192. He was not able to conquer Lahore, but left a garrison in Sialkot" (p. 136, folio 151).
  - "At this time, many Sufi missionaries settled here thus making the Sialkoc [text-layer?] [Sialkot] region a predominantly Muslim one and giving it a rich Sufi tradition" (p. 137, folio 152).
  - "an important post on the trade route between West Asia and Ddhi [text-layer?] [Delhi]" (p. 137, folio 152).
  - In the story: the city "inhabited predominantly by Muslims" with "a fairly large and powerful presence of Hindus, comprising mainly of rich businessmen and traders" (p. 137, folio 152); the faqir's curse on "the citizens of Sialkoc [text-layer?] [Sialkot]" in "the centre of the market square" (p. 150, folio 165).

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)
- Faqir Hamza Ghaus, Sialkot: "the wondrous Sufi faqir" said to have "direct access to God", more disciples "than any other teacher of the Sufi philosophy", with "a small vaulted room" for retreat (p. 138, folio 153); his "unpretentious abode on the outskirts of the city" (p. 140, folio 155); curses the town, then retracts at Nanak's lesson (pp. 150–159).
- Ganga: Hindu trader of Sialkot "well loved and respected by both the Muslim and the Hindu communities", childless (pp. 137–138, folios 152–153); vows his firstborn to the faqir and breaks the vow (pp. 142–147).
- Hamida: the guest's cousin, blessed with a son through the faqir (pp. 138–139, folios 153–154).
- Moola (also printed "Meola", "Moala" [text-layer?]): Sialkot shopkeeper "well known for his wisdom and sagacity" who became "an ardent devotee" (p. 157, folio 172; p. 159, folio 174).
- Duni Chand, the amil: distributed his "seven lakh rupees" to the needy; contributor to the Kartarpur dera and township (p. 134, folio 148).
- Kartarpur: the dera and township, "details given later" (p. 134, folio 148) — not placed by this chunk (see Doubts).
- Bhatinda (Sutlej crossing, four months with Sufi saints), Bikaner (Jain temple of the Dhundia sect), Ajmer (dargah of "Khwaja Moiuddin Chishti [text-layer?]"), Pushkar (mela on Baisakhi, "14 April", note 32) (p. 161, folio 177) — all Indian, not Pakistani rows.

## Practices, institutions, economy
- Childless couples' vow: they "promised to give up their firstborn child to his ashram or dertt.. [text-layer?] [dera]" (p. 142, folio 157); the author adds "Historically, over 90 per cent of the parents who gave up their firstborn to God soon went on to have a second child" (p. 142, folio 157).
- "the mandatory forty days of 'quarantine' of the mother and child", followed by a feast to which "all the holy men of the city" were invited (p. 143, folio 158).
- "the chaliha, the forty-day period of fasting and self-mortification that was necessary for the fulfilment of the curse", undergone in the faqir's cellar (p. 151, folio 166).
- Camping outside a holy man's house in silent prayer and fasting as supplication (pp. 138–140, folios 153–155).
- Duni Chand's seven red pennants, one per lakh held, taken down (pp. 133–134, folios 147–148).
- "A paisa was no mean amount of money in those days" (p. 156, folio 171).
- Mela at Pushkar on Baisakhi (p. 161, folio 177).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)
- In Dhillon's retelling, Hamida bears a son "two years later" after the faqir's prayers (p. 139, folio 154), and Ganga's wife likewise "two years later" (p. 143, folio 158).
- The faqir's curse: "May the most terrible of plagues fall upon this town … till not a stone is left standing upon stone" (p. 150, folio 165).
- Ganga's dream of his three sons dead in shrouds (p. 147, folio 162); his wasting illness, beyond "vaids and hakfrns [text-layer?] [hakims]" (p. 149, folio 164).
- The slips: on the reverse Moola writes "Life is false" and "Death is the truth" (p. 158, folio 173); the faqir retracts his curse and, with Nanak, restores Ganga's health (p. 159, folio 174).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)
- The author: "deep in the subconscious of almost all Indians, there lurks the certainty that a holy man may not always have the power to help, but he certainly has the power to cause harm" (p. 151, folio 166).
- The author, on the faqir: "the spiritual powers that he had come to possess had, on occasion, made the faqir forget his humility" (p. 154, folio not stated).
- The faqir's recantation frames compassion toward the fallen as what "followers of all Sufi traditions practise" (p. 159, folio 174).
- Moral of the episode as the book restates it: "compassion and piety were more becoming to men of God than the exercising of occult powers" (p. 161, folio 177).

## Doubts
- Damage inventory (text_layer): `[source stamp: SikhBookClub.com watermark]` at the foot of nearly every page (variants `Sikh8oohCL11b. ~`, `rsikhBookCL~`, `lsi khBook_f_Lub.com`) — not content. Systematic `t`→`c` (co, chat, chis, cicy, Sialkoc), `t`→`r` (bur, rwelve), `th`→`m` (mought, mat, me next world), dropped spaces (thewhole, ofleading), garbled running heads. Quoted prose above silently reads the ordinary `t`→`c` letters where no name/place/date is involved; flags are carried on every name/place I carry.
- Folios: read at page foot and in sequence for pp. 133–134 (147–148), 136–152 (151–167), 155–159 (170–174), 161 (177). p. 153 foot reads "Hill" and p. 154 "16'>" — misread, so `folio not stated`. pp. 135 and 160 are unnumbered epigraph pages. The foot sequence jumps 148 (p. 134) → 151 (p. 136) and 174 (p. 159) → 177 (p. 161); both jumps fall at chapter boundaries/epigraph pages, and no story breaks off mid-sentence in this chunk.
- Guru Nanak 18-row bullet and Mardana → `gurdwara-rori-sahib`: person-level, not site-specific.
- `gurdwara-babay-de-ber` single-site bullet: the book never names a gurdwara; the attachment rests on Sialkot + "an old beri (jujube) tree" matching the row's name and place. Weigh accordingly.
- Sialkot (5 rows) and Lahore (35 rows) bullets are place-level. The Lahore material is thin: a conquest note and a point of departure.
- PROVINCE punjab: province-level, weigh accordingly. The book's "Punjab" here is the 1192 unit Ghori captured; the book gives no boundary.
- Kartarpur (p. 134): the book does not place it on the Ravi or in Pakistan in this chunk ("details given later"), so not mapped to `gurdwara-darbar-sahib-kartarpur`.
- "Khwaja Moiuddin Chishti" printed as such (p. 161); I cannot tell whether the book or the text layer dropped a letter. No archive row.
- The shopkeeper's name is printed "Meola", "Moala" and then "Moola" (p. 157, folio 172; p. 158, folio 173; p. 159, folio 174) — "Moola" is the settled form in the text, but the variants may be text-layer damage or the book's own.
- "Suhi l Chhant" (p. 160): the "l" may be a misread "1" or a stray mark.
- Note markers carried: 30 (Raja Sela, p. 136), 31 (Suhi Chhant epigraph, p. 160), 32 (Baisakhi day, p. 161); the notes themselves are not in this chunk.
- No Talwandi, Saidpur, Eminabad or Nankana in this chunk.
