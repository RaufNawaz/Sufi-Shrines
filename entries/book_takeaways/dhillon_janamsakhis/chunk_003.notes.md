  - End of ch. 4 "Bhai Lalo": in Dhillon's retelling, Nanak tells Malik Bhago he has "tasted much richer and more delicious food" than Bhago's feast, and has Mardana fetch a "chick roti [text-layer?] [thick roti] made of coarse bajra" from "Lalo's hue [text-layer?] [hut]" (p. 70, folio 76; p. 71, folio 77).
  - Nanak tells Bhago his twenty-one dishes are made of "the sweat and blood and tears of the suffering poor", and that he has "hired priests to pray for you" but does not remember God himself (p. 71, folio 77).
  - Bhago falls at Nanak's feet; Nanak answers: "It is not I who muse [text-layer?] [must] forgive you. It is the people who have suffered at your hands" (p. 72, folio 78).
  - "Nanak moved on from Saidpur" and Malik Bhago "lived up co [text-layer?] [to] his promise" (p. 73, folio 79).
  - Ch. 5 "Haridwar and Ganga": "Nanak, having set out on the first of his four udasis, found himself in Haridwar the day before Baisakhi ... in 1504" (p. 77, folio 85).
  - A "kindly swami" gives Nanak and Mardana room in his ashram; next morning at Har ki Pauri Nanak takes "three dips", turns "westwards, his back turned resolutely cowards [text-layer?] [towards] the rising Sun" and offers water (p. 78, folio 86; p. 79, folio 87).
  - Nanak says he is offering water "to my land back home and that is why I am facing west, in the direction of Punjab ... to my newly ploughed fields there" (p. 79, folio 87).
  - Nanak's retort: if water reaches heaven "forcy-nine-and-a-half-crore kos [text-layer?] [forty-nine-and-a-half-crore kos]" away, his offering will reach "my fields near Lahore, which are only a few hundred kos away" (p. 80, folio 88).
  - Nanak holds "his usual satsang" in the ashram; the swami's "sermons from then on were peppered with Nanak's thoughts"; after a few days Nanak resumes "his journey eastwards" (p. 81, folio not stated).
  - Ch. 6 "The Two Shopkeepers": opens with a quotation "According to a scholar on Sikhism" (scholar not named in this chunk; note "18"): "He attended fairs and festivals, and visited temples and mosques, hermitages and khanaqahs [meeting places of Sufis]" (p. 84, folio 93).
  - In the retelling, Nanak and Mardana, worn out by monsoon travel, stop for some weeks at an unnamed prosperous town ("not big enough ro [text-layer?] [to] be called a city"); Nanak sings "his Bani every morning and evening"; people of "neighbouring towns and villages" come to the kirtans (p. 85, folio 94; p. 86, folio 95).
  - A small trader becomes a devout follower; his hedonist neighbour mocks him; the neighbour finds one gold coin in an urn of "dust and ash", the disciple gets a sliver of wood in his foot (pp. 86–93, folios 95–102 as read, folio of p. 92 not stated).
  - Nanak's explanation: the neighbour was "destined to find an urn full of gold coins" but they "were turned to ash and dire [text-layer?] [dirt]"; the disciple "was destined to be impaled on a sharp stake" but it "was reduced co [text-layer?] [to] a sliver of wood"; "Man's life is determined by che [text-layer?] [the] actions he performs" (p. 95, folio 104).
  - The second shopkeeper "admitted in his heart that he had come face co face with a crue [text-layer?] [true] Guru" (p. 96, folio 105).
  - Ch. 7 "The Kumbh Mela": "On his udasi towards the east, Nanak found himself at Prayag at the time of the Kumbh Mela"; he and Mardana camp "on a vacant piece of land between two akharas" (p. 101, folio 112).
  - At the mela Nanak invites leaders of other akharas to sing their bhajans and takes part in religious debates; devotees ask about the man "dressed neither like a Hindu nor like a Mussalman", and are told "He is Nanak, the great Guru from Punjab" (p. 102, folio 113).
  - Gurbani epigraphs attributed to Nanak close/open chapters: "Raag Basant1 5 [text-layer?] [Raag Basant, note 15]" (p. 74, folio not stated); "RaagSri17 [text-layer?] [Raag Sri, note 17]" (p. 82, folio not stated); "Japji, Pauri 20 19 [text-layer?] [Japji, Pauri 20, note 19]" (p. 97, folio not stated).

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- Malik Bhago — the rich man of Saidpur whose feast Nanak refuses; repents and "lived up co his promise"; "A fresh and happy chapter ... opened up in the lives of the residents of Saidpur" (pp. 70–73, folios 76–79).
- Saidpur — place of the Bhai Lalo / Malik Bhago story in this chunk; no modern location given here (p. 73, folio 79).
- Haridwar (India) — "arguably the holiest of the many holy cities" on the Ganga; the char dham yatra begins there; one of four spots where amrit fell; Kumbh Mela there "every twelve years", attended "by around 100 million people"; ashes of the dead cast into the Ganga; Brahma Kund on "Har k.i Pauri [text-layer?] [Har ki Pauri] (steps of God)" (pp. 76–77, folios 84–85).
- The Ganga's course — Gomukh (note "16"), "Gangoai [text-layer?] [Gangotri]", Bhagirathi, Devprayag "in Uttarakhand", Alakananda, Rishikesh, Doon Valley, Shivaliks (p. 75, folio not stated; p. 76, folio 84).
- The Haridwar swami (unnamed) — hosts Nanak, is "profoundly influenced", touches Nanak's feet at parting (pp. 78–81).
- The two shopkeepers of an unnamed town (pp. 86–96).
- Kumbh Mela at Haridwar, "Allahabad {Prayag}", Nasik, Ujjain — "held every third year at one of the four places by rotation"; Ardha Kumbh "every sixth year" at Haridwar and Allahabad; Maha Kumbh at Allahabad/Prayag; "approximately 80 million people, it is reported, attended the last Kurnbh [text-layer?] [Kumbh] Mela at Allahabad on 14 February 2013" (note "20") (pp. 98–101, folios 109–112 as read).
- Baisakhi — "celebrated annually on 13 April every year"; for Buddhists it commemorates the Buddha's birth, enlightenment and death; for Hindus the descent of Ganga (p. 77, folio 85).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- Malik Bhago's feast: "twenty-one dishes" laid out for Nanak and "all the other holy men"; feasts held "only to earn merit in the eyes of God" (pp. 70–71, folios 76–77).
- Haridwar rite: pilgrims bathe and offer "palmfuls of water to the sun" for ancestors in "Suryalok or heaven" (p. 78, folio 86); heaven "forty-nine and-a-half-crore kos [one kos is equivalent co about 3 km] away" (p. 80, folio 88).
- Nanak's practice as retold: satsang in the evening (p. 81), "his Bani every morning and evening", kirtans (p. 85, folio 94; p. 86, folio 95); Mardana's rabaab (p. 102, folio 113).
- Kumbh Mela: akharas set up camps "which together take on the ambience of a tented city"; Mauni "Amasvaya" (note "26") the most auspicious bathing day; "mass feeding of holy men and women and of the poor" and religious debates (p. 101, folio 112).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- Nanak's watering of his Punjab fields from Haridwar, westward, exposing "an empty ritual" — Dhillon's retelling (pp. 79–80, folios 87–88).
- The gold coin / urn of ash and the stake reduced to a sliver, explained by Nanak as destiny altered by actions — Dhillon's retelling (pp. 92–95).
- Legend of Bhagirath bringing the river down, the sage Kapil's curse (p. 75); churning of the ocean, Garuda and the amrit (p. 76; pp. 99–100) — told by the author as Hindu legend.

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- The author's comment on the Haridwar rite: "People, however, did not stop performing chis ritual; in fact, more people perform it today than ever before" (p. 80, folio 88).
- The author frames the Haridwar episode as a beginning: "Nanak had couched [text-layer?] [touched] the lives of many and the dissemination of the seeds of a new faith had begun" (p. 81, folio not stated); the teaching summed as "authenticity in behaviour and love towards another human being" (p. 81).
- The author on bathing: it "stems perhaps from the desire co cleanse oneself from the dirt of sins" (p. 101, folio 112).
