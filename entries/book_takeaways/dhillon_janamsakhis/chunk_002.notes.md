# dhillon_janamsakhis — chunk 002 — pp. 38–69

*All story material below is Dhillon's retelling of janamsakhi tradition (end of ch. 2 "Sacha Sauda", all of ch. 3 "The Guru and the Qazi", first part of ch. 4 "Bhai Lalo"), not history. The udasi lists (pp. 54–55) and the author's "Personally, I believe…" (p. 56) are his own expository voice.*

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **gurudwara-janam-asthan-nankana-sahib**, **gurdwara-babay-de-ber**, **gurdwara-balila-sahib-bal-lila-sahib**, **gurdwara-chakki-sahib**, **gurdwara-choa-sahib**, **gurdwara-darbar-sahib-kartarpur**, **gurdwara-khoohi-bhai-lalo-bhai-lalo-di-khooi**, **gurdwara-malji-sahib**, **gurdwara-panja-sahib**, **gurdwara-patti-sahib**, **gurdwara-pehli-patshahi-jind-pir-sukkur**, **gurdwara-rori-sahib**, **gurdwara-sach-khand-sahib**, **gurdwara-sacha-sauda**, **gurdwara-sahib-saidpur-guru-nanak-dev-ji**, **gurdwara-sri-kiara-sahib**, **gurdwara-sri-tilganji-sahib**, **gurdwara-tambo-sahib** (the book's "Nanak", "Guru Nanak"; person-level):
  - Close of the Sacha Sauda story in Dhillon's retelling: Mehta Kalu calls his son a failure; Rai Bular defends him — Nanak "used the money to feed a whole akhara of famished sadhus", a bargain "true in the eyes of God" (p. 38, folio 42; p. 39, folio 43). The chunk names no place for the bargain beyond "the forest" and "Talwandi" (p. 38, folio 42).
  - Pandit Hardyal, who "first cast Nanak's horoscope", asks: "Do you know of any ocher [text-layer?] [other] sixteen-year-old who has shown such maturity…" — the book's age for Nanak at the Sacha Sauda episode (p. 40, folio 44).
  - Mehta Kalu's lingering hostility; Nanak's sister proposes he come to Sultanpur (p. 42, folio 47; p. 43, folio 48).
  - "Nanak moved to Sultanpur (around 60 km from Talwandi)", employed by Nawab Daulat Khan, who "appointed him as the keeper of his modikhana (score [text-layer?] [store])" (p. 44, folio 49).
  - Daily round: bathing in "the Bein rivulet", prayer and meditation on its bank, the modikhana by day, and "a small group of people would assemble in his house to listen to the hymns he had composed" in the evenings (p. 44, folio 49).
  - Marriage: "He got married on 24 September 1487 to the gencle [gentle], kind-hearted Sulakhini, daughter of Mul Chand and Chando Rani of the town of Batala"; Mul Chand "belonged to the Chona subcaste of the Kshatriyas" (note 10) and "looked after the lands of the Randhawa Jats in the village of Pakhoke" (p. 44, folio 49).
  - Sons: "Sri Chand (8 September 1494) and Lakhmi Das (12 February 1497)" (p. 44, folio 49).
  - Jealous senior officials spread rumours about his integrity; Nanak insisted on an audit; "Everything was found in order", but he "got mentally and emotionally disturbed" (p. 45, folio 50).
  - Disappearance in the stream: clothes found on the bank, a week's absence; "Daulat Khan's men found him wandering on che [the] left bank of the river"; he declares "There is only one God. There is no Hindu, no Mussalman." (p. 45, folio 50; p. 46, folio 51).
  - The qazi of "Sulranpur [text-layer?] [Sultanpur]" is petitioned by a delegation; Daulat Khan, "a genrle [gentle], secular human being at heart", summons Nanak; Nanak: "I am neither a Hindu nor a Mussalman… I believe in che [the] teachings ofall religions" (p. 46, folio 51; p. 47, folio 52; p. 48, folio 53).
  - At the Friday namaz Nanak remains standing; he asks the qazi what was on his mind; the qazi admits thinking of his mare and newborn foal near an open well, and concedes "My prayers were not true prayers" (p. 48, folio 53; p. 49, folio 54; p. 50, folio 55).
  - Setting out: Nanak "dressed himself in a strange garb - an approximate combination of what the holy men of Hinduism and of Islam would wear" and set out with Mardana "to all the four corners of the country and to places rhat [that] lay beyond India's then-known boundaries" (p. 53, folio 59; p. 54, folio 60).
  - Two udasi schemes (author's exposition): "According to the Puratan Janamsakhi… he undertook five journeys (also known as udasis)": first "(1499-1506)", second "(1506-1513)", "Third ,ul.asi [text-layer?] [udasi] (1514-1518)", "Fourth tulasi [text-layer?] [udasi] (1519-1521)", "Fifth udasi (1523-1524)" (p. 54, folio 60; p. 55, folio 61). "More modern accounts" give four, "in the four cardinal directions… which spanned rwency-three [text-layer?] [twenty-three] years": "(1499-1505)" central and eastern India; "(1506-1509)" southern India and Sri Lanka; "(1514-1516)" Gangetic plains, Bihar, Nepal, Lhasa, Leh, Tashkent, "back to Punjab via the Kashmir Valley"; "(1518-1521)" Arab countries (p. 55, folio 61).
  - In Dhillon's retelling the first udasi goes "From Sultanpur… to Talwandi… from where he turned north-west cowards [towards] the town of Saidpur" (p. 56, folio 62).
  - At Lalo's hut he sings "one of his early compositions… Ik Onkar Satnam ... (there is only one God Satna.m [text-layer?] [Satnam] ...)" with Mardana on the rabaab; villagers gather (p. 64, folio 70).
  - He is invited twice to Malik Bhago's Navratras feast and declines; on a third message he goes, knowing that otherwise "poor Lalo would have to bear the brunt of Malik Bhago's ire" (p. 68, folio 74; p. 69, folio not stated).

- **gurdwara-khoohi-bhai-lalo-bhai-lalo-di-khooi** (the book's "Lalo", "Bhai Lalo", "La.lo [text-layer?] [Lalo]"):

  - He warns Nanak that staying with him risks excommunication "from your biradari"; Nanak replies that "in God's eyes a man's worth is never measured in terms of his caste or social status or his wealth" (p. 59, folio 65).
  - His poverty: one oil lamp, a loosely strung charpoy, "a single, thick bajre ki roti (millet roci [roti]) and a single, small onion" (p. 60, folio 66).
  - He sings "a Kabir (note 14) bhajan" while sweeping; asks Nanak to stay some days (p. 62, folio 68; p. 63, folio 69).
  - He works "on a daily wage" for "Ramdev, the master carpenter", and "could nor [not] afford to miss a single day of work" (p. 63, folio 69).
  - Nanak asks him to lead "one of Bhagat Kabir's hymns" (p. 64, folio 70; p. 65, folio 71).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- Village layout at Saidpur: the haveli ringed "in concentric circles" by houses in order of caste, the untouchables on "the very outskirts"; house construction "displayed the social status of their owners" (p. 57, folio 63).
- A "dharamshala - an inn for travellers" at Saidpur (p. 58, folio 64).
- Malik Bhago's economy: a "lagaan (land cax [tax])" fixed by the landlord with his own rising commission; arrears carried as debits; his munshi "functioned as a proxy moneylender" at "usurious rates", making tenants and children "bonded slaves" (p. 65, folio 71; p. 66, folio not stated).
- His piety: a temple in his haveli courtyard with priests praying "in a relay"; feasts for the poor twice a year; on "the seventh day of the Navracras [text-layer?] [Navratras]" a feast for swamis, sadhus and bairagis, with only Brahmin cooks and servers and lower castes barred from the haveli (p. 66, folio not stated; p. 67, folio 73).
- Nanak's evening gatherings to hear his Bani (note 12), growing into "a sizeable gathering" of disciples (p. 44, folio 49; p. 53, folio 59).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- In Dhillon's retelling, Nanak vanishes in the river for a week and returns with the refrain "There is no Hindu, there is no Mussalman" (p. 45, folio 50; p. 46, folio 51).
- The qazi, questioned by Nanak, confesses he was thinking of his mare and foal during namaz (p. 49, folio 54).
- Lalo marvels "at the prescience displayed by Nanak", who sends him to work when he cannot afford to miss a day (p. 63, folio 69).

## Arguments and interpretations

- The author sets the Puratan Janamsakhi's five udasis against "more modern accounts" of four, and sides with four: "Personally, I believe chat [that] he undertook four udasis and not five" (p. 54, folio 60; p. 55, folio 61; p. 56, folio 62).
- Rai Bular's argument (within the story) that Nanak's "true bargain" was "true in the eyes of God" (p. 39, folio 43).
