## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **gurdwara-darbar-sahib-kartarpur** (the book's "Kartarpur", also garbled "Karrarpur", "Kanarpur", "Karcarpur", "Karcarpur" [text-layer?] [Kartarpur]): the book places Kartarpur on the Ravi — Nanak lay dying "under a uee [text-layer?] [tree] on the bank of the river Ravi, just outside Kartarpur" (p. 257, folio 285). Facts, all in Dhillon's retelling / his own framing:
  - In "his community in Karrarpur [text-layer?] [Kartarpur]" the ideal of religious discipline lived "in the world of ordinary human beings" "was given practical shape" (p. 228, folio 252).
  - "The three great traditions chat [text-layer?] [that] were established in Karrarpur [text-layer?] [Kartarpur] were the traditions of seva or sel.Bess [text-layer?] [selfless] service, kirtan or religious singing and langar or free kitchen" (p. 228, folio 252); "In Kanarpur [text-layer?] [Kartarpur], these traditions became firmly entrenched in the daily life of the community" (p. 229, folio 253).
  - The Guru's compositions "were sung at the appropriate times ofthe day to the accompaniment ofMardana's rabaab" (p. 229, folio 253).
  - Langar at Kartarpur: everyone "whoever could do so, involved himself or herself in the running of the langar" — drawing water from the wells, grinding wheat, collecting wood, cooking, serving, scrubbing utensils; it "came co [text-layer?] [to] foster the spirit of equality, humility and brotherhood" (p. 229, folio 253).
  - Settlement: "the original inhabitants of Kartarpur, who had come and settled here, when the Guru had first earmarked the site as his permanent home", plus "new settlers" — groups from communities of followers "all along the routes that he had followed on his udasis", some of whom "abandoned their homes and made new homes here" (p. 230, folio 254).
  - Retelling of Moola Keer, "One of the earliest settlers in Kartarpur", a wealthy, honest trader who housed strangers; a traveller-guest stole his wife's jewellery, Moola gave it to him and covered for him; the thief later confessed before Nanak after the kirtan and Nanak praised Moola (pp. 230–237, folios 254–261; p. 236 folio not stated). The "whole of Kartarpur came to believe Moolas [text-layer?] [Moola's] version" (p. 236, folio not stated).
  - Many disciples "flocked to Karrarpur [text-layer?] [Kartarpur] and made their homes in che [text-layer?] [the] dera" (p. 240, folio 265).
  - Lehna, on pilgrimage to Jwalamukhi, "reached Kartarpur" and stayed; worked in the fields, at "a construction site", in "the community kitchen" / "l.angar" [text-layer?] [langar] (pp. 248–249, folio not stated / 276).
  - A "newly built wing of the dharamshala" at the Guru's settlement nearly collapsed in the monsoon; Lehna led an overnight repair (pp. 253–254, folio not stated / 281).
  - Nanak's last days and death "just outside Kartarpur" on the Ravi bank; Muslim followers claimed his body for burial ("He is ours, our peer"), Hindus for cremation; the flowers legend (see Legends); "(The date: 22 September 1539.)" (pp. 257–260, folios 285–288). The printed year matches the row's own `figure_died` 1539.

  - Author's framing: true religious discipline "could only be achieved while living in the world of ordinary human beings"; this "had been the refrain of his discussions" with "ascetics, yogis and siddhas" all through the udasis (p. 228, folio 252).
  - Nanak composed "spontaneously inspired verse"; "Nanak's sermon was his Bani"; he would tell Mardana "'Mardana, Bani aaye hai - I have inspiration for a Bani'"; only among strangers did he offer an exposition (pp. 228–229, folios 252–253).
  - During the udasis he sought "to gee [text-layer?] [get] the sangat ... co [text-layer?] [to] organize a langar" (p. 229, folio 253).
  - Nanak's father "Mehta Kalu" / "MEHTA KALu" [text-layer?] saw "Pandit Hardyal's prophecy" fulfilled (p. 240, folio 265); in the Hindu followers' speech the father is "Kalyan" and the mother "Tripca" [text-layer?] [Tripta] (p. 258, folio 286); elsewhere "mother Trip ta" [text-layer?] [Tripta] (p. 257, folio 285).

  - Story "The Impostor": on the way back Nanak found a village gathered round a sadhu, "a yogi from Benaras who comes here once a year at Shivratri", reputed for his "third eye"; when a villager quietly moved his coin vessel the sadhu could not see where it was, and Nanak asked why the third eye could not show him that; "'Do not credit anyone with occult powers'" (pp. 241–244, folios not stated / 267–269).
  - Wife "Sulakhini" (pp. 250, 252–253, folios 277 / not stated); sons "Lakhmi Das, Nanak's younger son" (p. 252, folio not stated) and "His older son Sri Chand", "not an ambitious man", "spiritually inclined" (p. 254, folio 281).

  - Death: loved ones already gone — "His mentor and friend Rai Bular, his father Mehta Kalu, mother Trip ta [text-layer?], his sister Nank.i [text-layer?] [Nanki] and her husband Jai Ram, and even Mardana" (p. 257, folio 285); date "(The date: 22 September 1539.)" (p. 259, folio 287).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- Seva, kirtan, langar as the three Kartarpur traditions; langar against "the distinctions of caste, religion and social standing" (pp. 228–229, folios 252–253).
- Seva defined: "voluntary service, physical or otherwise, undertaken for the welfare ofthe [text-layer?] [of the] community with no regard for reward"; "the more menial the task, the more it was prized" (p. 246, folio 273).
- Kartarpur had fields worked by the Guru and disciples, "sevadars" with "bullock-carts", and a "dharamshala" with a "newly built wing" (pp. 252–253, folios not stated).
- The sadhu's copper vessel of silver coins; his prescription of the "Gayacri Manua" [text-layer?] [Gayatri Mantra] "ten thousand times" and "agodaan" [text-layer?] [a godaan] "(the gift of a cow) to a Brahmin" (p. 242, folio 267).
- Contesting of Nanak's body: Muslims wanted "holy burial", Hindus "cremation rites" (p. 258, folio 286).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- In Dhillon's retelling, Nanak says mud from selfless service "becomes saffron", and Sulakhini "thought chat [text-layer?] [that] she saw a circle of light around his [Lehna's] head" (pp. 252–253, folios not stated).
- In Dhillon's retelling, the flowers laid by Muslims (left) and Hindus (right) beside the dying Nanak were both found unwithered next day (pp. 258–260, folios 286–288).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- Author's moral: "Even in death, Nanak had underscored the lesson that he had taught all his life: All human beings are equal" (p. 260, folio 288).
- Author on the Impostor story: "As always, Nanak's logic was simple but irrefutable" (p. 243, folio 268).
- Author contrasts Hindu bhajans, "an accompaniment to their sermons", with Nanak, whose "sermon was his Bani" (p. 228, folio 252).
