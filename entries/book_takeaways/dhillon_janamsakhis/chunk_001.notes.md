## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **gurudwara-janam-asthan-nankana-sahib**, **gurdwara-babay-de-ber**, **gurdwara-balila-sahib-bal-lila-sahib**, **gurdwara-chakki-sahib**, **gurdwara-choa-sahib**, **gurdwara-darbar-sahib-kartarpur**, **gurdwara-khoohi-bhai-lalo-bhai-lalo-di-khooi**, **gurdwara-malji-sahib**, **gurdwara-panja-sahib**, **gurdwara-patti-sahib**, **gurdwara-pehli-patshahi-jind-pir-sukkur**, **gurdwara-rori-sahib**, **gurdwara-sach-khand-sahib**, **gurdwara-sacha-sauda**, **gurdwara-sahib-saidpur-guru-nanak-dev-ji**, **gurdwara-sri-kiara-sahib**, **gurdwara-sri-tilganji-sahib**, **gurdwara-tambo-sahib** (the book's "Guru Nanak", "Nanak"):
  - Introduction (author's own prose): the Janamsakhis are "cycles of tales purportedly drawn from the life of the first Guru of the Sikhs, Guru Nanak (f469-1539) [text-layer?] [1469-1539]" (p. 9, folio 9).
  - The author calls Nanak "no unworldly mystic" but "a householder in every sense of the word" who "brought a message of peace and harmony to believers of the mutually hostile religions of Islam and Hinduism" (p. 12, folio not stated).
  - The author states Nanak taught that "all men and women were equal, irrespective of caste, creed, wealth, social standing and gender", and that the basics of a good life were honest earning through one's labour, compassion for the less fortunate, selfless service to the community, and constant renewal of faith in God through prayer (pp. 12–13, folio not stated / folio 13).
  - The author notes "Guru Nanak's clear and emphatic disavowal of the miraculous" (printed "dear" [text-layer?] [clear]) against the Janamsakhis' reliance on miracles (p. 11, folio 11).
  - Author states he placed the stories "against the background of Guru Nanak's udasis (travels)" to give them a setting in time and place (p. 13, folio 13).
  - In Dhillon's retelling (ch. 1): "On 14 April 1469" Kalyan Chand Das Bedi, "or Mehta Kalu as he was affectionately called", accountant of the Bhatti family under Rai Bular, "was blessed with a son who was named Nanak" in Talwandi (pp. 17–18, folios 19–20).
  - In Dhillon's retelling: at Nanak's birth "Pandit Ha.rdyal [text-layer?] [Hardyal], the village astrologer" predicted he would be a great man (p. 18, folio 20).
  - In Dhillon's retelling: at school he showed "a brilliance that had never before been seen in any child in Talwandi", composing "long poetic works"; three teachers taught him; Mehta Kalu was disappointed, wanting him in service with officials of the ruling class (p. 18, folio 20).
  - In Dhillon's retelling: Nanak developed a meditative nature and looked for the reasons for "the schism between Hinduism and Islam", one being the insistence on opposed rituals (p. 18, folio 20).
  - In Dhillon's retelling (The Sacred Thread): at thirteen Nanak refused the janeu at the ceremony in "Mehta Kahis [text-layer?] [Mehta Kalu's]" courtyard, performed by Pandit Hardyal, saying "it is our actions and our actions alone" that give position among men (pp. 19–20, folios 21–22); Mehta Kalu was "mortified beyond words"; "For Nanak, it was the beginning of a lifelong crusade against the performing of empty rites and rituals" (p. 21, folio 23).
  - Epigraph attributed to Nanak on the true sacred thread, cited "Raga Asa 471" (p. 14, folio not stated); epigraph "Make this frail body thy shop…", cited "Sorath" (p. 22, folio not stated).
  - In Dhillon's retelling (ch. 2): at sixteen Nanak herded cattle; the author presumes "he gave himself up to prayer and meditation" (p. 23, folio 27).
  - In Dhillon's retelling: holy men camped "in a grove of trees on the outskirts of the village" and Nanak interacted with many of them (p. 24, folio 28).
  - In Dhillon's retelling: in these years "he became well acquainted with the dervish Sayyad Hussain and gained an intimate knowledge of the Sufi line of thought", was "profoundly influenced by this line of thought and admired its philosophy throughout his life" (p. 24, folio 28).
  - In Dhillon's retelling: his father had "beaten him black and blue" for past disobedience (p. 26, folio 30).

- **gurdwara-sacha-sauda** (the book's chapter "Sacha Sauda", glossed "*The true bargain."; market town "Chuharkhana"):
  - In Dhillon's retelling, Mehta Kalu gave Nanak "twenty silver coins" and a list of goods to buy "from the nearby market town of Chuharkhana at wholesale prices" and sell in Talwandi (p. 25, folio 29).
  - Nanak and Bala took "the path through the woods" instead of "the thoroughfare to Chuharkhana" and met "dozens of sadhus in the forest", the Nagas, who had not eaten "in a week" (pp. 26–32, folios 30–31, 32; pp. 31–32 folio not stated).
  - In Chuharkhana Nanak went to "the biggest wholesale grocer" and bought "enough food to feed fifty starving sadhus", plus vegetables, fruit, firewood, with two cooks and two porters, and fed them at "the grove of trees" (pp. 33–35, folios 37–39).
  - Nanak's words: "Any dilution of my bargain will be a dilution of the truth" (p. 34, folio 38); feeding the holy men is "a truly selfless service" and the more profitable bargain (p. 35, folio 39).
  - Bala fled home; Mehta Kalu, told by Bala, "strode from his house towards the forest"; Nanak, returning with the change, "had come face to face with his father" (pp. 36–37, folios 40–41). The story continues beyond this chunk.

- **gorakhnath-goraknath-temple**, **tilla-jogian** (the book's "Gorakhnath, the founder of the Nath sect of yogis"):

  - Bhai Mani Singh, "an eighteenth-century Sikh scholar and martyr", to whom one set of janamsakhis is supposedly attributed, "had been the childhood companion of Gobind Singh" (p. 9, folio 9).

- **amb-temples-amb-sharif**, **bhagnari-mandir**, **chandragup-baba-chandragup**, **katas-raj-temples**, **shahwala-teja-singh-mandir**, **shiv-mandir-chiti-ghati**, **shree-ratneshwar-mahadev-temple-karachi**, **umarkot-amarkot-shiv-mandir** (the book's "Lord Shiva"):

## Province-level material

- PROVINCE punjab (book's word: "Punjab" / "the Punjab region"; refers to: the period of the invasions and Muslim rule before Nanak's birth, as the book frames it — the Ghaznavis, Ghoris and Tughlaqs "after the beginning of the eleventh century", through Taimur's invasion "in 1398"):
  - "Since Punjab in the north was the gateway to India, it bore the brunt of all these invasions" (p. 15, folio 17).
  - The Muslim conquerors, "once they had settled down in the Punjab region", directed their energies towards "either converting [printed "convening" [text-layer?]] the kafirs or non-believers or destroying them" (p. 16, folio 18).
  - "For over three hundred years, Islam and Hinduism existed side by side in the Punjab region in a state of constant conflict" (p. 16, folio 18).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- Sufis (general): "for the fuse [text-layer?] [first] time, a Muslim sect called the Sufis introduced music as an integral part of the practice of Islam", welcomed non-believers into their homes and mosques, and held all had a right to their own worship (p. 16, folio 18).
- "The Sufis were revered by both Hindus and Muslims in India, and to this day the Sufi dargahs (shrines) stand testament to the fact" (p. 24, folio 28); the Sufis "took the best aspects of Hinduism and Islam in order to reach God" and taught laymen through parables (p. 24, folio 28).
- The Bhaktas ("Bhakcas" [text-layer?] [Bhaktas]) preached one formless God and human equality (p. 16, folio 18).
- Janeu ceremony as the book describes it: worn from thirteen, cord of cotton yarn blessed by a priest, platform in a courtyard plastered with mud and cow dung, lamps, incense, flour figures, mantras (p. 19, folio 21).
- Naga practices as the author describes them: nakedness, sacred ash, matted hair, bhang in a chillum later abjured by seniors, celibacy, martial-arts display in Kumbh processions, secret two-day initiation, eating only what is provided (pp. 29–31, folios 33–34; p. 31 folio not stated).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- The author states the Janamsakhis tell "numerous stories of the miraculous incidents" of Nanak's cowherd phase; he does not retell them (p. 23, folio 27).
- Author's method: he retold the stories "stripping them of all supernatural elements, including miracles" (p. 13, folio 13).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- Author: the Janamsakhis "are not designed as biographies", nor strictly fables or parables, but serve "to preach a moral or illustrate an essential part of the Guru's life and his teachings" (p. 9, folio 9).
- Author: four extant sets — "the Bala Janamsakhis, the Puratan Janamsakhis, the Meheraban Janamsakhis" and those "written supposedly by Bhai Mani Singh" (p. 9, folio 9); their two sources were Nanak's Bani and the first var of Bhai Gurdas (p. 9, folio 9).
- Author: embellishment "resulted in many versions of the same story and also led to contradictions and anachronisms" (p. 10, folio not stated); the Sakhis "do not form any part of formal Sikh religious texts, largely because many of them are apocryphal" and written long after, from oral history (p. 10, folio not stated).
- Author: "the veracity of the entire cycle of stories in the very popular Bala Janamsakhis became suspect" (p. 11, folio 11); yet "there can be no doubt of the essential veracity of the picture of Guru Nanak that emerges from the Janamsakhis" (p. 13, folio 13).
- Author: two stories, "The Joy of Giving and The Widow and Her Lost Son", have no reference he could find; he heard them in childhood sermons (p. 13, folio 13). He "used my imagination to build up details of backgrounds, settings, incidents and even characters" (p. 13, folio 13).
