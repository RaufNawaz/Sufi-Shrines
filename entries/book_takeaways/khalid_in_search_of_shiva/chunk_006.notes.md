# khalid_in_search_of_shiva — chunk 006 — pp. 13–14 (ch. 7-8)

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar** (the book's "Baba Farid", "his title Ganj Shakar (Treasure of Sugar)", "the shrine of Baba Farid", "Pakpattan", "Ajodhan"):
  - The author, with Maryam and Anam, "had driven here from Lahore, a journey of about four hours, to do a piece on the shrine of Baba Farid"; women are searched in "the small makeshift room covered by curtains" by "female police officials"; the author shows his press card to "the head of security" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - "A couple of years prior to our visit there was a blast at the entrance of the shrine killing six people" (date of the visit not stated) (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - After the attacks "Photography was strictly prohibited in all such shrines"; "elaborate security measures at the entrance"; "Earlier these shrines would remain open throughout the night providing shelter to all, but now they are shut at midnight on the order of the government" (said of "all such shrines", in the Baba Farid passage) (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). The head of security nonetheless allowed cameras inside on seeing the press card (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - "The shrine of Baba Farid is located on the top of a mound that overlooks the entire city"; "always a rush outside the shrine, where there are several shops selling religious paraphernalia"; devotees buy "chaddar, flowers and incense sticks to be presented to the caretakers of the shrine"; "It is considered inauspicious to visit the grave of a saint without a gift" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - "It seems as if the entire city of Pakpattan has developed around this shrine. All roads lead here. The name of every other shop or business entity either includes Baba Farid or his title Ganj Shakar"; "several myths as to how the saint earned this title—one of them being that he was extremely soft-spoken" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - "The city of Pakpattan was a small, insignificant one known as Ajodhan on the bank of river Sutlej when Baba Farid had decided to settle here in the thirteenth century"; he was "at that point the head of Chisti order with its centre in Delhi"; believing the order "should distance itself from the imperial authority", "he had shifted the centre from Delhi to Ajodhan"; "The madrassah of Baba Farid was located on the mound where he is buried" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - Layout: stairs up to "an open courtyard with a new mosque on one side and the shrine of Baba Farid in front"; "a huge line of men" waiting; "Women are not allowed inside the chamber where the grave is located"; for them "a verandah adjacent to the shrine, from where they can catch a glimpse of the grave"; "This was a modest building" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - "Behind the shrine is a splendid structure rising several metres higher. This is where the progeny of Baba Farid is buried. The graves of his female family members are covered with purdah. Here too women are not allowed" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - The author reads in the two buildings "the transition that occurred after the death of Baba Farid, who had lived a life of poverty and away from the politics of his time"; his progeny "received gifts and awards from different rulers"; "The current guardians of the shrine are from the family of Baba Farid and they are not only one of the richest but also one of the most powerful families of the region" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - "Facing the shrine is an ancient Karir tree"; Nizamuddin Auliya, "a disciple of Baba Farid", wrote that "the day dela, an unripe fruit of the wild Karir tree, was cooked it was regarded as a feast by the students" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - "At the shrine of Baba Farid, the most important Chisti Sufi in Pakistan, there are qawwals performing at all times of the day" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). A group of qawwals sat "under the shade of a building across from the Karir tree" — "one man on the harmonium and one on tabla while the rest of them clapped"; "Aptly the musicians were singing verses of Baba Farid"; "Behind the qawwals was the langar hall, offering free food to all" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - Observed: "a young man, wearing an off-white shalwar kameez, started whirling on his knees in a state of trance"; another devotee "threw notes of ten rupees in the air" which an assistant collected; "Here women and men sat together"; after "about half-an-hour" the qawwals "were replaced by another group" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). Devotees were of "different socioeconomic backgrounds", "several more devotees around me slip into a state of trance" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - "Perhaps the greatest contribution of Baba Farid is not in religion but in literature. He is believed to be the first poet of Punjabi" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - Guru Nanak "visited the shrine during his travels" and "through the descendants of Farid he learned about the poetry of Baba Farid"; thus "the poetry of Baba Farid found its way into the Guru Granth Sahib, making Baba Farid a saint in Sikh tradition as well" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - "For years now Sikhs have petitioned the Pakistani government to grant them visas to attend the urs of Baba Farid but in vain" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - Orthodox critics argue Sufis "like Baba Farid" condoned qawwali "because they wanted to win over Hindus into Islam"; "However today there is no need for qawwali" (attributed to the orthodoxy) (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
  - Ch. 8: the author wonders "why our heroes cannot be people like Baba Farid, Waris Shah and Bulleh Shah, who spread the message of peace and brought several religious communities together instead of shedding blood" (p. 14, ch. 8, folio not stated).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- Eid Milad-Un-Nabi: paharis (mounds) built by boys; the author first took part "only ten"; Irfan says the pahari represents "the cave of Hira" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). "a national holiday"; "an unofficial competition"; "Processions and rallies are held all over the country" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
- Scale growth: "earlier it was considered a pariah religious festival, even referred to as a fake Eid by many, now it has become a mainstream festival"; the shoe symbol on "houses and autorickshaws" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
- State: "After the creation of Pakistan, the event continued to be celebrated without the government's support. In the 1980s ... General Zia-ul-Haq ... recognized the festival and for the first time Eid Milad-Un-Nabi was celebrated at the government level" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
- Shrine security after attacks: photography prohibited, night closure "at midnight on the order of the government" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
- Gifts at shrines: chaddar, flowers, incense; caretakers; hereditary guardianship at Pakpattan (see Farid bullet) (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
- Qawwali "has its origin in the ancient devotional musical traditions of India, primarily bhajan"; "Qawwalis are now a prominent feature of all Chisti shrines"; kirtan "has uncanny similarities to qawwali"; langar "too was introduced to Sufi shrines by the Chisti order" (author's claims) (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
- Pakistan Studies "replaced History and is compulsory for all school and college students", begins with the Indus Valley, then Buddhism, then "the first Muslim invasion of Muhammad bin Qasim, leaving a vacuum of more than a millennium" (p. 14, ch. 8, folio not stated).
- "The nuclear warheads of Pakistan are named after Ahmad Shah Abdali, Mahmud Ghazni, Mohammad Ghori and Babur" (p. 14, ch. 8, folio not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- Syncretism is not only rural: two urban "schools of thought ... borne out of, and became popular primarily because of their religious syncretism" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
- The rise of Eid Milad-Un-Nabi is, "I believe, ... in reaction to the increase of religious puritanism" — people embrace "an interpretation that is tolerant and syncretistic" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
- Barelvi/Deoband: "Barelvis, an offshoot of the Deoband School"; "After separating from the Deoband in the nineteenth century, and spearheaded by Ahmed Raza Khan, the Barelvis embraced folk religious practices"; "The majority of Muslims in India and Pakistan belong to this particular school"; Barelvis "have taken a clear stance against the Talibanization of society", Deobands "have failed to denounce Islamic religious terrorism in the same manner" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
- Shrine attacks are "a new phenomenon": "Intellectual battles have been fought for several years but never before have they been physically attacked" (p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
- Two-nation theory: Pakistan "Carved out of India in 1947"; textbooks portray Hindus negatively; "all traces of the Hindu past are obliterated" (p. 14, ch. 8, folio not stated).
- Urban puritanism: "shrines which are located in developed areas ... are losing their idiosyncrasies much faster than those found in remote regions"; "Muslim fundamentalism (not defined as militancy but puritanism) is more popular in the urban centres" (p. 14, ch. 8, folio not stated).
- Islam "was understood in a language that was already available to the locals through existing religious practices"; the shrines "are extraordinary because they continue to survive in an environment constructed out of Muslim separatism" (p. 14, ch. 8, folio not stated).
- "at the moment, the biggest threat to the shrines doesn't come from militancy but rather from education and development" (p. 14, ch. 8, folio not stated).
- Scholarship-panel anecdote: told of "the shrines of phallic offerings", a panelist replied "But that's not true Islam" (p. 14, ch. 8, folio not stated).
