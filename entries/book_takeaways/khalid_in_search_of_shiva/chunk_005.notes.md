## Practices, institutions, economy

- Kallar Kahar peacock shrine: market of vendors selling "plastic toys, indigenous pottery, makeup"; parking receipt; a cable cabin between the two shrines — "extraction of monetary benefits from religious pilgrimage at its best" (author) (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Visitors: "quite a few tourists", "not serious pilgrims, but temporary devotees lured by the scenic view"; a few devotees reciting "the fateh"; no peacocks seen (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Auqaf at the peacock shrine, per the man at the threshold (a resident of the town, "employed by the Auqaf Department"): women once brought water in pots from the lake and lit lamps "as supplication"; "ever since the shrine has come under the protection of the Auqaf Department ... We have electricity. We have ten huge pumps"; devotees no longer light lamps or bring water (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- His duties: preventing "immoral activities", sending away boys bothering girls, ensuring "nothing unIslamic takes place here such as 'prostrating or offering tawaf around the grave'"; "It is our job to educate people that they should only ask from Allah and not from the saint. We have written that as well"; Urdu lines at the chamber entrance forbid these acts (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Peacocks and locals, per the same man: "For us they are sacred ... a local will not pass until the bird crosses over"; peacocks now come "only at the time of fajr [sunrise] and maghrib [sunset]"; "That changed after the construction of the motorway"; tourists chase them (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Baba Mastan shrine: "On the occasion of the celebration of his urs we also celebrate the urs of the mare" (dervish) (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Ghore Shah shrine: caretaker family — an old man "whose family had migrated from Jalandhar during Partition" "claimed to be a descendant of a family of caretakers of this shrine" and runs a stall selling the toy horses; vendor "Allah Rakha": "The deendars make them at Chinatown"; a small mosque inside the graveyard complex, another across the road (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Gujrat crow shrine: pots of offerings for crows; a girl places rice in them; kawa offered to devotees by a dervish; "a young male employee of the Auqaf Department forbade Iqbal Qaiser from taking photographs" and a police official followed them — the author links this to the high alert after the Marri blast (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Gujrat crow shrine, per Ghulam Muhammad ("seventy-five years old"): "All langar provided to devotees at the shrine is offered to the crows before anybody else"; stuttering children "drink water from the same vessel as the crows and are cured"; the ash under the pot is eaten by devotees (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Cat shrine, per Muhammad Tanveer ("forty-year-old", "an old devotee"): "we don't encourage the practice anymore. These kittens enter the mosque and spoil the holy space"; a sign: "It is forbidden to drop off kittens here"; "there is no one at the shrine to take care of the cats"; about a year later Samra Noori found "there aren't any kittens there anymore" (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- Peacock saint, per the Auqaf man: "the grandson of Ghaus Pak himself— Hazrat Abdul Qadir Gillani"; he "came to Kallar Kahar from Baghdad Sharif and brought these peacocks with him"; he assented ("Yes") that the birds descend from peacocks that came "about 900 years ago"; "The birds used to obey every message of the saint" (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Peacock saint, per the plaque: "He is a martyr ... He laid down his life helping the Muslims of this region against the Hindu Marathas. And now even the birds from heaven come to the shrine to pay homage to the holy martyr"; "The plaque did not give any dates" (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Bhera temple, per a young boy: women saw "an old man wearing a white shalwar kameez" with long white hair inside; "When they followed him inside he disappeared. He was a sage. Ever since then the locals here have lit lamps at the temple" (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Mai Rani, per the senior dervish: Baba Mastan "ordered that she should be buried and instructed all his devotees to pray at the grave of Mai Rani before coming to his grave"; "if any of your prayers are not answered at the grave of Mai Rani, then you should stop calling me Baba" (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Ghore Shah, "According to folk legend": real horses were once offered; "The saint, who died at the age of five, loved riding horses"; "Suffering from palsy, the child-saint would shake uncontrollably earning him the title of Jhole Shah"; "Due to his love for horses he became known as Ghore Shah" (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Kawan Wali Sarkar, per Ghulam Muhammad: crows have long lives — "Maybe one of these crows has seen the Holy Prophet (PBUH). In serving these crows I am serving the Prophet" (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Kawan Wali Sarkar, per Muhammad Aslam: "He would not allow humans to come too close to him"; "He was a malamati, which means he did not conform to the laws of the Shariah"; Peer Waliyat Shah told the scholars "he had seen Kawan Wali Sarkar at Kaaba" (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Milk well, per Ghulam Muhammad: a woman refused well water, the saint said "It has milk now", and the well had milk; it "finished during the governance of the British" (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Allama Iqbal's visit to Kawan Wali Sarkar (see mazar-e-iqbal) (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Bibi Kaulan and Guru Hargobind (see the Hargobind bullet) (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- "Authenticity through foreignness as opposed to indigenousness remains a pathological problem in Pakistan"; admitting indigeneity means admitting "their ancestors were Hindus", "threatening the two-nation theory"; Vaisakhi, Basant, Mehndi "are celebrated with a sense of shame" (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- The plaque at the peacock shrine "was tinged with state nationalism", reinterpreting the saint's life "to reinforce the separateness of Muslims from Hindus" (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Sacred animals and trees aid "the preservation of the ecosystem"; cites Karen Armstrong, The Case for God, on symbolic reading of myth, and Chief Seattle's letter "in 1855" (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- The Kallar Kahar peacock tradition "is likely to have drawn inspiration from ancient religions irrespective of what the plaque says" (Krishna, Sarasvati, Buddha "a golden peacock") (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- "Shrine culture has always been heterodox"; "under mounting pressure from the orthodoxy which receives state patronage, some shrines are willing to concede"; by barring devotional acts the Auqaf "conceded to the fact that orthodoxy is preferred over heterodoxy" (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Commercialization of religion ("mass production of books and CDs") produces "a uniform religion ... a major ingredient of fundamentalism" (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Folk shrines show "a sense of rootedness, which challenges the notion of nationalistic religion that wants us to believe that we are all migrants in our own land. The saint here becomes the saint of the rocks" (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Saints and travel: Sufis' knowledge of different cultures elevated them "in a caste-ridden society" (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Ghore Shah: written records "belong to the nineteenth century, written almost 300 years later by British colonialists"; the toy horses resemble those "unearthed from the Indus Valley Civilization"; the author concludes the cult "originated in the depths of antiquity"; deendars ("Muslim converts who were originally Hindu untouchables") still make the offerings; cites "Jürgen Wasim" on treatment of the mentally disturbed as divine; "Mary Kenoyer" cited as denying the Aryan invasion theory (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Kawan Wali Sarkar's crow-feeding is "a tradition that he clearly picked up from Hindu mythology" (Hindu belief that "crows are a bridge between the living and the dead") (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- The author surprised that "Naqshbandis are considered to be the most puritanical", yet a Naqshbandi led the malamati's funeral (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Cats in Islam: Abu-Hurayra, the Prophet's cat "Muizza", "the Siddi Haddi and the Mevlevis" (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Junkies in graveyards as a legacy of "General Zia-ul-Haq" and the Afghan jihad (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- Cities absorbed into "the global economy" "tend to shed their idiosyncrasies" (p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
