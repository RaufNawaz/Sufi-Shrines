## Province-level material

- PROVINCE punjab (book's word: "Punjab"; refers to: the period after "the fall of the Mughal Empire", the governorship of "Moin-ul-Mulk, popularly known as Meer Manu", Ahmad Shah Abdali's raids, and "1797" — as the book states):
  - "Ever since the fall of the Mughal Empire conditions had deteriorated in Punjab. Ahmad Shah Abali [sic], the Afghan king, had already completed two raids of Punjab and beyond" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
  - Meer Manu "was the governor of Punjab" and was "constantly pestered by the Sikh insurgent groups Misl, which had sprung up all over Punjab"; "The Sikh groups did not control much land but because of their anti-status quo politics the lower rungs of the society supported and protected them"; he "cast the battle between the state and insurgents as a war between Muslims and Sikhs, even though there were several Muslims in the Sikh groups and many non-Muslim soldiers in the army of Meer Manu" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
  - Abdali had "agreed an annual tax stipulation which the governor was responsible of paying to him, for the right of governing Punjab" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
  - "In 1797, when the grandson of Ahmad Shah Abdali, Shah Zaman Durrani, following the footsteps of his grandfather, attacked Punjab", the Sikh band "met him on the banks of the Chenab" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- PROVINCE punjab (book's word: "Punjab"; refers to: the present, and "days following the creation of Pakistan" — as the book states):
  - "Punjab, still predominantly an agricultural society, upholds agrarian culture and traditions, of which the harvest season is the ultimate source of happiness"; but "in days following the creation of Pakistan ... several cultural festivals like Vaisakhi, Lohri and Basant were given up" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- Ahmad Shah Durrani / Abdali: "AHMAD SHAH DURRANI (1747–1772), an Afghan ruler, is considered to be a national hero in Pakistan historiography"; "A national hero was a folk villain" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- Maraka village ("a few kilometres inroad from the Multan Road highway"; "a few kilometres from Lahore"): burned by Abdali's forces; Iqbal Qaiser points to blackened bricks; "several graves all over the mound", "an ancient banyan tree", a canal behind; Iqbal Qaiser took a brick "for his private collection" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). Per "the Land Revenue Records of 1866, Maraka was established in the latter half of the thirteenth century by a man called Marakana from Multan" — the author notes this "is part of folk history"; populated "for a hundred years", abandoned "due to the government's atrocities", reoccupied "Fifteen years later", "only to be devastated five centuries later" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). Bodh Singh and Jassa Singh named in "The Punjab Chiefs written by Lepel H. Griffin" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- Esaiyan da mazaar ("the shrine of the Christians"), Maraka: "a small structure with a single grave in the centre, and the mark of a cross at the entrance"; per Basheer ("forty-year-old", caretaker "for the past twenty years") it is "the shrine of Baba Gur Baksh Masih"; mud graves behind are of "followers of Baba Gur Baksh Masih" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- Shaheedan da mazaar ("the shrine of the martyred"), Maraka: per the book, raised "during the tenure of Maharaja Ranjit Singh" "to honour the martyrs killed earlier by Ahmad Shah Abdali"; now inside the house of "Hassan Deen"; host "a seventy-eight-year-old man by the name of Muhammad Ali Cambho": "two simple graves, one of which was covered with an elaborate green shawl" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- Bodh Singh, Jassa Singh (his son), Nadhan Singh (successor): after the burning they "established themselves at a place in Sialkot district"; Shah Zaman agreed to return their Maraka property (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). Also named: Jassa Singh Kalal, head of "Khalsa Dal ... based near Amritsar"; Raja Kaura Mal, "trusted Hindu minister" of the governor (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- Shrine of Sahari Mal: located "on top of a pahari far from civilization", near "the village of Hardo Sahari"; "triple-storey building with a small green dome"; "All four entrances into the shrine, hallmark of Sikh architecture"; "an orange flag"; "This abandoned shrine was once popular throughout Punjab" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- Baba Ram Thamman, village of Ram Thamman, "a few kilometres away from the railway station of Khalu Khara, in the outskirts of the city of Kasur", "named after a Hindu sadhu from the fifteenth century"; complex "which includes his samadhi, Hindu temples and other historical buildings, is spread over several acres"; "In pre-Partition days ... one of the largest festivals of Vaisakhi in Punjab" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). Temple of "Kali Mata next to the samadhi" occupied by Ghulam Hussain's family (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- Shrine of Sher Shah at "Kahna Nau": Shia black flag and "alm"; caretaker Shadi Shah, "grandson of the saint Sher Shah", whose family lives in the abandoned Gurdwara Baba Jamiat Singh across the road (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). - Gurdwara Baba Jamiat Singh (Kahna): quoted from "Historical Sikh Shrines in Pakistan": "The construction work of the shrine was completed in 1921. A fair is organized every year but lacks the luster of the past." (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). - Shrine of "Bava" (Sher Shah's cows), village of Bhambha/"Bhamba": construction work by junior malangs; senior malang "Saeen Shoukat" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- Maraka martyrs' shrine festival: per an old woman, "In the early days, every year there used to be a small festival there, in which people danced and sang"; "It was mainly a Christian affair but ... the Muslims would also participate"; now "No. But the shrine exists." Muhammad Ali Cambho: festival "used to be in the early days of Pakistan"; visitors "Not really. A few years ago a Sikh man came"; he "regularly clean[s] the area and cover[s] the graves with a shawl"; his family "razed" the rough structure "and constructed these graves" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- Maraka demography per Iqbal Qaiser: Sikh majority migrated at Partition, leaving property with "their servants who were Mazhabi Sikhs", who "converted to Christianity ... to avoid persecution"; the Muslim population "primarily comprises people who had shifted here from India following Partition" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- Sahari Mal shrine: Hindus and Sikhs left at Partition, "replaced by Muslims from Ferozepur district"; "the indigenous Muslims of the village continued visiting the shrine"; Haji Kamal "is a descendant of the indigenous Muslims"; "over the years, attendance at the shrine has reduced" (Haji Kamal) (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). Offerings: "Colourful pieces of cloth ... tied to some of the trees"; sitting-figure structure converted to a grave "a decade ago by the Muslim devotees" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). Festivals per Haji Kamal: "Earlier the festival of Lohri was celebrated here"; now "People gather here only once a year for the annual festival in the month of Had [indigenous calendar]" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- Ram Thamman: Ghulam Hussain ("eighty-five years old"), whose family migrated "from Ferozepur" in "1947", kept the samadhi locked, changing "the chaddar that covered a small pahari which represented Baba Ram Thamman's smadh"; Vaisakhi festival revived; bangle-seller Razzaq: "This is not just an economic opportunity for us, but a religious duty"; dandiya group led by "twenty-two-year-old Ghulam Ali" from Kasur; the festival "is attended by hundreds of people every year on the occasion of Vaisakhi"; "voices against the festival of Ram Thamman are being raised" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- Sher Shah's cows: per Shadi Shah, festival "on the 11th of Chaitar [month in indigenous calendar]"; "about seventy to eighty cows. Earlier they used to be in thousands"; cows sold "to raise money for the festival or for renovation of the shrine" but "no one wants to buy them ... Only Wahhabis buy the cows" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). Per Saeen Shoukat: "two herds", one from Kahna (bought) and one from Bhambha (progeny of the original gift); offerings in "little bags attached to their ears"; former practices (touching hooves, passing children under them, oiling horns) "have stopped"; dead cows given "a proper burial" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- Naulakh Hazari: "the fifty-six-year-old Iqbal, a government official" shows pilgrims the rock and asks for "token money"; a candidate's election billboard shows the shrine — the author takes him to be "from the guardian family of the shrine" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- Gur Baksh Masih, per Basheer: "Once while he was fighting, his head was severed but his body continued fighting and eventually came and rested here" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- Sahari Mal: "On his deathbed, Sahari Mal had instructed his followers to neither bury him, nor cremate him"; his body to be "placed in a sitting yogic position and then covered" (author's account) (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). Pool water, per Haji Kamal: "had magical qualities and could cure all diseases" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- Jamiat Singh the cattle thief (quoted from "Historical Sikh Shrines in Pakistan"); per Iqbal Qaiser, "according to Sikh tradition" the cattle's "patterns had changed by a miracle"; Shadi Shah first called it "a lie" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- Sher Shah's cows: a devotee gave cows to the saint at Bhambha (Shadi Shah); a thief "would have no other option but to get rid of them" (Shadi Shah); "Sometimes the cows ask for the offerings themselves. They knock on doors" (Saeen Shoukat) (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- Naulakh Hazari, per the attendant Iqbal: "There was no water up here. So the saint prayed to Allah and water appeared in this bowl ... Both the lamb and the lion had water from here together"; rock marks said to read "Muhammad" and "Allah" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- Mai Mufto: per Iqbal Qaiser, the donkey's grave "became a shrine, and the donkey became a woman" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- State vs folk narrative: the Maraka shrine commemorates Abdali's victims, "casting him as a villain"; "It highlighted how cut off the state was from the culture and history of its own people" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- The author's inference that Gur Baksh Masih's shrine "must have originally been the samadhi of Gur Baksh Singh, converted posthumously"; and on Maraka's two graves: "There was no need for a samadhi in post-Partition Pakistan, so two graves were constructed instead, I thought" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- Sahari Mal's Muslim devotees, "irrespective of the fact that the patron saint here was not a Muslim but a Hindu": "For a country formed from the Hindu and Muslim division, this is an extraordinary feat, throwing the two-nation theory topsy-turvy"; the grave conversion was "an attempt to woo conventional devotees" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- Sacred cows at a Muslim shrine contradict the Partition-era line "They worship cows and we slaughter them" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- The sacred rock: "how is this sacred rock at a Muslim shrine any different?"; the lion-and-lamb image "clearly adopted from the Biblical references"; the lotus pillar shows "cultural symbols which have been sacred for thousands of years to the people of this region are still sacred" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- Shrine politics: guardians "have amassed political and economic strength becoming a hegemonic power"; orthodox attacks "provide a platform for the locals to challenge the control of the progeny of a saint" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). This "brand of religion is now slowly fading away" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- "Pakistani civilization, in one way or the other, is a continuation of that [Indus Valley] civilization" (p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
