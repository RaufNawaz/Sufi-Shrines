- Killis (phallic offerings), per Hajra: "Women also sometimes offer killis to the shrine. ## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- Per Hanifa's brother (via Hanifa): the saint, "Baba Lal Shah", "tied his horse to the waan tree next to the shrine. While he was staying here the horse died so the saint buried it and moved on" (p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated).
- Per Hanifa: "If you come with evil intention he will emerge as a lion and devour you. I have seen it with my own eyes"; snakes "guard the shrine and bite only those who are evil" (p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated). Salma repeated the lion stories (p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated).
- Per Salma: while pregnant, "Saint Aban Shah appeared in her dream and suggested the names of the children" (p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated).
- Per Faheem: Syed Lal Esan Shah's three Syed sons refused to help with "Tahajjud" prayers and were cursed: "Fareed Gharib [Fareed, you will remain poor] / Ghaus marchade dhos [Ghaus, you would be subjected to fraud] / Dadu tenu bhok kabo [Dadu, you would be controlled by hunger]"; the Mir son was blessed to rule the Ravi — his river shrine "always remains protected" from monsoon floods ("I have seen it with my own eyes"); his progeny "bears two marks on the forehead depicting horns"; Baba Aban Shah, forgotten, asked "What do you have for me, lun?" and was told his progeny "would belong to every caste … They would spread all over" (p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated).
- Per Faheem: "Women who are too old to have children have walked out from here bearing a child" (p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- The phallic shrine is "a continuation of a tradition that evolved long before the birth of Islam"; Indus figurines and seals were votive fertility offerings ("consensus amongst archaeologists") (p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated).
- Offering toys and venerating trees continue Indus practice; "it is the same people with similar beliefs who live here today", the changes "only … on the surface" (paganism, Buddhism, Jainism, Hinduism, Islam); the banyan sacred in Hindu, Buddhist "and even Muslim traditions"; "waan trees … considered sacred in the Muslim saints' hagiography" (p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated).
- Hajra's concealment of killis reflects "concerns of modesty. Morality defined by a more urbane Islam had finally caught up with this particular practice" (p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated). Television brought "uniform nationalism" and "a pure religion purged of its past corruptions" and "played a pivotal role in inviting disdain towards the shrine" (p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated).
- The women's lion/snake stories are "a counter-narrative" to the elders' intervention; "Of course, the women of this village believed in this cult" (p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated).
- Faheem's ancestor story is "a subtle critique of the hegemony that the Syed typically have over spiritual matters", elevating indigenous castes (p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated). Mir and Kharal: "Mirs deriving their ancestry from Kashmir and Kharals from Punjab"; "The Mirs trace back to Hindu Brahmins and Kharal to Hindu Rajputs" (author) (p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated).
- Police in sectarian conflict "succumbed to the pressure of the majority Muslim group"; parallel drawn with "the minaret of an Ahmadi place of worship" (p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated).
- The author doubts "Aban Shah was his real name" and that any saint started the tradition "350 years ago. This is an ancient tradition"; "sometimes Muslim saints and such legendary stories were invented to justify devotion to a 'non-Muslim' tradition"; Aban Shah "a figure that was resurrected to fit within the framework of a newly created Islamic country" (p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated).
