## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **shergarh** (the book's "Shergarh", "the annual festival of Daud Bandagi Kirmani", "the saint Daud Bandagi"):
  - On the way to "Tibba Haji Deen", "One of the places we stopped at was Shergarh, about an hour from Lahore, where we witnessed the festivities at the annual festival of Daud Bandagi Kirmani" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
  - A malang "Halting at the threshold of the shrine, he said a silent prayer. He touched the base of the entrance and entered"; described with "eyes ... smeared in kohl", "a vertical mark on his forehead", "a glass earring on his right ear", "thin black clothes", "a thick bangle on his right ankle", shaved head and no facial hair (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
  - "Circling around the grave of the saint Daud Bandagi, the malang stopped at a spot where he kissed the footprints of the saint engraved on a small marble platform" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
  - According to Iqbal Qaiser: malangs "only drape a shawl when they are in public ... When they are with other malangs they wear nothing but a small lungi"; on the footprints: "If you go to Jain temples there are footmarks of their prominent priests which become objects of veneration. This is exactly like that"; and "There has to be some sort of an inspiration from that particular Jain sect as well" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
  - The author asks "They are Nanghe sadhus, aren't they?" and observes the malang "looks like a Digamabar Jain monk" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated). (Nanghe Sadhu history under Practices.)
  - "Driving out of Shergarh towards Tibba Haji Deen, we drove past a small group of ascetics walking barefoot on the road" — the eldest in "a black shawl", "a green turban", "a belt of bells ... around his waist" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).

- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar** (the book's "Baba Farid", "the famous Muslim mystic from the twelfth century"):
  - At Tibba Haji Deen the party walked towards "'the well of Baba Farid', the famous Muslim mystic from the twelfth century. According to tradition, he hung himself inside a well not far from the shrine, while performing religious meditation for a number of days"; "Signs on the walls pointed towards the well"; pilgrims walked "barefoot on the dusty roads of the village, out of respect for the holy village" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
  - "The well was covered by an iron cage, on which devotees had tied saffron-coloured threads"; a boy drew water "for the pilgrims to consume"; "There were coins and notes at the base of the well, thrown in by pilgrims to seek the blessings of the saint" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
  - A shrine official: "'Drinking water from this well will cure all your problems!'"; and, privately: "'Baba Farid did not hang inside the well, as it is believed ... This well belonged to his family. Baba Farid was born here and this well was part of his house'" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
  - Iqbal Qaiser: "'Wasn't he born in Kothewal?'"; the official: "'This is Kothewal. Kothewal was its old name'" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
  - "the house where Baba Farid was born": "In an open courtyard were seven graves"; an old caretaker said "the graves belonged to Baba Farid's father, grandfather, uncles, and siblings", plus "a small grave" of one of his own ancestors "who, like him, also spent her life looking after this sacred place" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
  - The caretaker: "'Our family has been serving this shrine for the past seven generations'" and "'I am soon going to put up Baba Farid's family tree here. I have given it for composing'" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
  - The author "wondered if his family once served the family of the Muslim saint before they moved to Pakpattan" and whether "seven generations was rhetorical"; the caretaker "was not educated but he knew the saint's poetry and his family history ... learned it from his elders" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
  - The author: Nanak "was fascinated by Baba Farid's poetry which was later also recorded in the Guru Granth Sahib. By visiting the city of Farid's birth, Nanak probably wanted to pay homage to the Muslim saint" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- Shrine at "Tibba Haji Deen" ("a small village" in "southern Punjab", "about five hours by road from Lahore"): "a shrine reserved for the mentally challenged"; per "Tarekh-e-Cambho, a book written by a resident of Chunian, the shrine of Diwan Chawali Mushahiq Haji Muhammad Sheikh at the village of Tibba Haji Deen was raised on the spot where Maha Chawar and his sister had been killed"; Raju calls it "the shrine of Haji Deen" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
- Maha Chawar and his sister Kangna: son of the Hindu ruler of "Chunian" ("whose caste was Cambho"), given as collateral to Muhammad bin Qasim, taken to Arabia, became Muslim; on return, on the advice of "Hindu priests", condemned; Kangna fled with him; both "killed ... near the city of Mandi Borewala"; "Years later, a mausoleum was constructed for the fallen prince and his sister, to commemorate the memory of one of the earliest Muslim martyrs in South Asia" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
- Kothewal: per the shrine official, the old name of the village (Tibba Haji Deen) and Baba Farid's birthplace; site of the well, the birth-house graves and the abandoned (renovated) gurdwara (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
- Rimsha Masih: "a Christian girl living in a small settlement within the capital city of Pakistan", "fourteen-year-old", arrested for blasphemy for "burning the pages of the Quran"; "Later evidence showed that the burned pages were planted by the Muslim prayer leader of the local mosque"; "The Supreme Court of Pakistan eventually released the girl and lodged a case against the prayer leader"; Christian families "numbering around 200" abandoned their houses; arrested on "That fateful night of August" (year not stated) (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
- Bahawal Nagar: overnight stop; the author's "earlier visit to Bahawal Nagar to document a Hindu festival"; "Akaliyan mohalla"; Raju, a Hindu, "live[s] near the civil hospital" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
- Nanghe Sadhu lineage named: Shri Chand → "Baba Gurdita who trained four disciples: Balu Hasna, Al Mast, Phool Shah and Govinda, who later formed four different sects"; "Some people believe that the first two disciples, Balu Hasna and Al Mast, were Muslims, while others deny this claim" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
- Endnote 71: "An eleventh-century Muslim saint from Baghdad, founder of the Qadriyyah sect" (saint not named in the note) (p. 19, Notes, note 71, folio not stated).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- Tibba Haji Deen shrine — custodianship and revenue: guests seated in "a special room reserved for guests of the shrine's gadi nasheen"; his number plate read "'Gadi Nasheen Tibba Haji Deen'" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
- According to the gadi nasheen: "'Last year ninety lakh rupees were collected on the occasion of the annual festival of the saint ... This is not taking into account all the silver and gold that was offered. But the Auqaf Department refuses to spend any money on the renovation of the shrine'" (year not stated) (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
- The author: the caretaker "was getting his share of the sum as stipend for being the gadi nasheen", besides "gifts they are offered for their special blessings and prayers" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
- Asked how his family became guardians (Iqbal Qaiser: "'in the history books there is no mention of Maha Chawar's children'"), the gadi nasheen: "'I don't know. We have never really taken an interest in our family history'" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
- Treatment of the mentally ill, per the shrine family: "'Those who are mentally disturbed are brought here by their families and left here for some time. Fetters are put around their ankles and enclosed with a lock'"; the guardian: "'Those who are violent are tied to trees or tied in the verandah'" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated). The author saw "people roaming around with shackles on their feet"; one chain "seemed to be symbolic in nature and did not hinder movement"; "A teenage boy tied to the gate at the entrance" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
- Muhammad Zafar, "thirty-five years old, from village Padoke, district Khanewal": "'I have been here for the past ten years. I am stuck here. My family will not take me back'" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
- Raju (Bahawal Nagar): took his nephew ("about fifteen-sixteen years old") to "the shrine of Haji Deen"; "the guardian there told us to leave him there for four days. Four days later when we returned he was cured. Now he works at the hospital" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
- Nanghe/Udasi Sadhus (per the book, citing the Encyclopaedia of Sikh Literature for origins): originally dressed "like Sikhs by wearing a turban", "a black scarf around their necks", "sang hymns on their tomba"; later "removed all their body hair", "wore nothing but lungis" — "This is when they came to be known as Nanghe Sadhu or the naked sadhus"; "scrubbing oil and ash on their naked bodies"; lived in "secluded communities known as deras" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
- Muslim followers: "instead of reciting the Dasant Granth, they stuck to the Quran and the teachings of the Sufis"; "They chant hymns of Al Mast"; travelling "from one shrine to the next, following urs celebrations. This has also earned them the name of Udasi Sadhu. Udasi in Sanskrit means pilgrimage" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
- Endnote 70: "A government organization that looks after the major shrines of the country" (the note's referent term is not in this chunk) (p. 19, Notes, note 70, folio not stated).
- Endnote 17: "Fire. A place where a saint spends some time is referred to his or her dhoan, which they used to light there during the duration of their stay" (p. 19, Notes, note 17, folio not stated).
- Endnote 45 (Frembgen, Journey to God, quoted): saints "are endowed with the power of healing and blessing (baraka)"; "they rely primarily on the tradition of curing diseases (tibb an-nabi) inherited from the Prophet Muhammad (PBUH)" (p. 19, Notes, note 45, folio not stated).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- Tibba Haji Deen: per the shrine family (speaker "he"), after "about three, four, five or nine days, the lock opens by itself and the person is cured"; "'Is everyone cured?' 'Yes, everyone.'" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
- Per the gadi nasheen's brother: "'the saint was converted by Imam Hassan [the grandson of the Prophet of Islam] himself'" and "'He, along with his sister, was martyred here'" by "'Infidels'"; the author: "Of course this wasn't true" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
- Baba Farid: "According to tradition, he hung himself inside a well ... while performing religious meditation"; denied by a shrine official, who says Farid was born there (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
- Guru Nanak's spirit beats squatters "with a stick at night", per an old man living at the gurdwara (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- The Rimsha Masih case "marks a low point in the recent religious and political history of the country"; it "reflects how dangerous the combination of religion and politics is in Pakistan" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
- Citing "Jürgen Wasim": historically Muslim societies regarded "fools and the mentally disturbed as God's favorites"; "In South Asia, people treat the truly mentally disturbed with remarkable patience and indulgence" (endnotes 94, 95: Frembgen, Journey to God, 75 and 76); the author contrasts this with the mob, concluding that "reinterpretation of religion has brought religious fundamentalism and extremism into the social fabric of Pakistan" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated; p. 19, Notes, notes 94–95, folio not stated).
- Endnote 7: "In the Islamic tradition people with psychological complexities are regarded to be closer to God compared to ordinary people because of their lack of interest in the material world" (p. 19, Notes, note 7, folio not stated).
- Malang/Jain parallel at Shergarh (author and Iqbal Qaiser, see shergarh) and malang = "Shiva, the ascetic" (see Shiva bullet) (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
- Author is skeptical of the gadi nasheen ("an air of pompous authority") and of the "seven generations" claim; he praises the oral transmission at Farid's birth-house as "truly remarkable" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
- Anam on Tibba Haji Deen: "'this is not much different from how they treat patients at mental hospitals'"; on Raju's nephew: "'What he was suffering from was teenage angst'" (p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).
- Endnote 8: "Senior archaeologists like Mark Kenoyer have rubbished the Aryan invasion theory" (p. 19, Notes, note 8, folio not stated).
- Endnote 16: "It would be illegal to call it a mosque according to the Constitution of Pakistan ... in Pakistan the law declares them [Ahmadis] to be non-Muslims" (p. 19, Notes, note 16, folio not stated).
