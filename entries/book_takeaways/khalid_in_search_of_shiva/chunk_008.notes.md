## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **madho-lal-hussain** (the book's "Shah Hussain"; person rule — named as poet and as the disciple of Behlol, no Lahore shrine site described):
  - "Around the same time that he [Damodar] lived, there was a wandering malamati Sufi in Lahore known by the name of Shah Hussain, a spectacular Punjabi poet" (p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).
  - "He for the first time, transformed the story of Heer Ranjha from a secular epic to a spiritual legend. He compared the love of Heer for Ranjha to that of a believer for his God, a theme that was subsequently picked up by Bulleh Shah and Waris Shah" (p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).
  - "Through his poetry, he introduced the concept of Wahadut-ul-Wajud, or monism, into the story which remains an essential part of Hindu philosophy and Islamic spirituality"; verse quoted: "Mahi mahi kook di mein ape Ranjhan hoyi / Ranjhan Ranjhan sab koi akho, Heer na akhon koi" (glossed in the book: "Calling the name of my beloved I myself have become Ranjhan / Call me Ranjhan only now as I am no longer Heer") (p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).
  - According to Iqbal Qaiser, Behlol (shrine at "Tiba Shah Behlol") "was the spiritual master of Shah Hussain" (p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).
  - At the shrine of Mai Heer (Jhang), "a lone musician sang Shah Hussain (Punjabi Verses) on his harmonium": "O Mother, to whom should I now narrate these tales of my pains?"; the author notes the poem "also had a reference to the love legend": "I roamed all around searching for my Ranjhan / Little that I knew my Ranjhan was always with me" (p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).

  - "According to Damodar's version, which was then supported by Waris Shah, Heer Ranjha is based on an actual story that Damodar saw unfold in front of his eyes" (p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- Mai Heer shrine (according to caretaker Amanullah): "Girls looking to get married, tie bangles here. Young couples who want to get married but cannot for some reason, tie threads here and their problem is alleviated. Barren women present cradles here and with the blessings of Mai Heer they are gifted a child" (p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).
- Mai Heer shrine: singing or reciting Heer is forbidden — Amanullah: "Let them try doing that and they will not be able to recognize themselves after a few minutes"; "We don't allow that here" (p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).
- Mai Heer shrine offerings: the author says the Siyals were "cashing in on the same love legend by keeping all the monetary offerings presented to the shrine" (p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).
- Mai Heer shrine walls "filled with love messages written by pen": "You may never be mine but I wish that wherever you live you may spend a happy life. Murad. Xox." "Zainab and Imran forever." "Salute to the love legend Mai Heer and Baba Ranjha." (p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).
- Behlol shrine, "Nauratre" (according to the oldest of the Chiniot women): "We stay up for nine nights praying to the saint to grant our wishes"; "One is never supposed to tell one's wish" (author) (p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).
- Chistian, pink-marble grave building (according to Iqbal Qaiser): "Young lovers who had certain impediments in their relationships would write their names on these walls with the oil of lamps from the neighbouring graves"; "It has stopped. This is where it used to happen"; yet a small untiled segment of the external wall had "a heart cut out of tile" with "names of young lovers ... clumsily written in black"; the author and Anam wrote their names with a used lamp (p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).
- Opening hours and security: "Historically, Sufi shrines remained open throughout the night ... But ever since these shrines have become a target for Islamic militants, devotees, at least at the major shrines, were asked to exit by a fixed time and the shrine is shut for the night" (p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- The author: the rising display of beard and hijab is "a new phenomenon that might have parallels in Islamic history but remains a product of historical contingencies—9/11 in particular" (p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).
- The author: the trend in Pakistan is towards "an Islamic tradition that is not a legacy of religious syncretism like Chistiya or Barelvi, but reactionary and exclusivist, originating out of the Deoband and Ahle-Hadith schools of thought" (p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).
- Dr Sadaf Ahmad (author of "Transforming Faith: The Story of Al-Huda and Islamic Revivalism among Urban Pakistani Women"): Al-Huda women "criticize the rituals and rites at these shrines but beyond that they have little understanding of its culture"; "women who come from backgrounds imbued in Barelvi ethos are not easily attracted to this movement"; Al-Huda's interpretation "is similar to the Ahle-Hadith strand"; local practices "like Basant or Mehndi are looked down upon" (p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).
- Sadaf on the future of shrine culture: "for shrine culture to end, things would have to become much worse ... shrine culture is so deeply embedded in our society that it would be hard to remove it"; "there is resurgence in the interest in Sufi poetry and music ... that is where hope lies" (p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).
- The author: the derogatory view of tradition "is a colonial legacy"; Heer Ranjha "raises it to metaphysical dimensions comparing the love between Heer and Ranjha to that between a believer and God"; "True to its pagan roots, folk religion in Pakistan has specialized shrines for particular needs" (p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).
- The author on the Siyals: "a strangely comical situation" — reaping benefits from the Heer tradition while forbidding its performance over their "daughter's" "honour" (p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).
- The author speculates whether Partition displacement shaped Muhammad Ahmed Ludhianvi's extremism: "Was there a connection between the haphazard partition of India and the sectarian conflict in Pakistan?" (p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).
