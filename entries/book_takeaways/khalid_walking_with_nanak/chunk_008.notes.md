## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Kanganpur**: reconstructed as a walled town of "several hundred houses", with rulers in "a fort a few metres away", a small army of its own and the role of "unofficial regional capital" (p. 177, folio 151). Today, according to Ghaffar: "During [the] British era, a railway station was built here", which led to a market town (p. 175, folio 149). "All that remains of this community [the Sikhs] are abandoned houses and shops which have now been taken over by victims of Partition and the land mafia" (p. 176, folio 150).
- **Second gurdwara in Kanganpur** (name not stated): raised "due to the demands of the local Sikh community, after the population of the city increased"; it "now serves as a residence for a Muslim family" and was locked (p. 176, folio 150). Iqbal Qaiser: earlier "this street was called Gurdwarewali Gali (lane)", and now it "is called Masjidwali Gali" (pp. 176–177, folio 150–151).
- **Manakdeke**: reconstructed as a small, regularly harassed village whose fields partly belonged to "the aristocracy of Kanganpur" (p. 177, folio 151). Ghasita Ram, "a Hindu landowner from the village", hosts Nanak and Mardana (pp. 177–178, folio 151 / folio not stated). Ghaffar says the villagers "have scattered everywhere" and are known for "petty crimes like stealing sandals" (p. 176, folio 150). Travelogue: "still a small village like Nanak hoped" (p. 178, folio not stated).
- **Gurdwara at Manakdeke** (name not stated): "Nanak's followers constructed a gurdwara at the spot" where he is believed to have stayed. It is now the home of "this Mewati family, who moved to this village after Partition" (p. 178, folio not stated). It is "a small building … made of thin bricks", "a single room with a charpoy in the centre", with a tandoor beside it and other rooms used by the family (p. 178, folio not stated). The old woman: "It is not a gurdwara anymore … We are too poor to take care of this building. Please write to the authorities … It is now in poor condition and might fall down anytime" (pp. 178–179, folio not stated / folio 153).
- **Ahle-Hadith mosque, Kanganpur**: being extended by labourers. The guide: "This used to be a small mosque only a few years ago" (p. 176, folio 150).
- **Temple and well of Puran Bhagat, near Sialkot**: devotees built a temple around the well; the evening prayer there included "Punjabi songs" (p. 184, folio 158). "Hundreds of devotees gathered at the temple" [OCR?] (p. 185, folio 159).
- **Puran Bhagat / Raja Salivahan / Raja Rasalu**: legend summarised on p. 184 (folio 158): Puran was thrown into a well on his father's orders, rescued by ascetics, and became one himself; through his blessing the king had another son, "Raja Rasalu".
- **Maula Karar**: Sialkot dry-fruit vendor, reconstructed scenes pp. 187–190. His business later "thrived" through wholesalers (p. 189, folio not stated). Iqbal Qaiser compares him with people who promise to travel and back out (p. 193, folio 167).
- **Shrines around Sialkot visited in the reconstruction**: the OCR is badly broken. It keeps "one of the most popular saints of … shrine … where they were seated", "A few kilometres … another shrine belonging to Mian Mitha, another … dervish" (p. 189, folio not stated), and a fragment "of Hamza … ot, which was not far from Sialkot there was … another important Muslim" [OCR?] (p. 189, folio not stated). No site can be identified.
- **Pir Bairi**: the grave under the Baba Bair berry tree (p. 195, folio 169). See gurdwara-babay-de-ber above.
- **Guru Har Rai**: "about fourteen days before his death" he appointed "his six-year-old son" Harkrishan (p. 180, folio 154). He had backed "Dara Shikoh" against Aurangzeb and later disowned Ram Rai (p. 180, folio 154).
- **Guru Harkrishan**: "the youngest leader of the Sikh community" (p. 180, folio 154). He marched "from Kartarpur" to Delhi, where Mirza Raja Jai Singh, "the Raja of Jaipur", protected and hosted him (p. 182, folio 156). His mother, "Mata Sulakhni", went with him (p. 182, folio 156). In Delhi he worked with people afflicted by cholera (p. 182, folio 156), then caught "chicken-pox". Before dying he said the next Guru would "be found in Bakala", "appointing his great-uncle, Tegh Bahadur" (p. 183, folio 157).
- **Ram Rai**: Aurangzeb dismissed his petition "by giving him a jagir … where the city of Dehradun was later established" [OCR?] (p. 183, folio 157). Cunningham records him as "the son of a handmaiden" (p. 181, folio 155).
- **Sikh expansion**: by Harkrishan's time the community "had expanded beyond the confines of the Punjab into places as far as Kabul and Ghaznvi [OCR?]" (p. 181, folio 155).
- **Iqbal Qaiser's book**: a Sikh woman and her son "from England" used "my book as their guide" to visit Pakistan's gurdwaras, and Iqbal Qaiser brought them to Manakdeke (p. 179, folio 153).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- Auqaf Department (printed "Augaf" [OCR?]), Kanganpur: Iqbal Qaiser suggests Ghaffar send a letter; members of the department "will come to you directly" about renovation and "ask you to look out for its maintenance" (p. 175, folio 149).
- Auqaf renovation board dated 2012 at Baba Bair, and the unfinished work found there (p. 194, folio 168).
- Iqbal Qaiser: "The government of Pakistan has only recently realised the potential of the revenue they can earn through Sikh pilgrimage", and it now welcomes "the funds of foreign investors to renova[te]"; "Initially, they were reluctant … Now they have gotten over their paranoia" (p. 194, folio 168).
- Gurdwaras taken over as housing: at Kanganpur by a Muslim family (p. 176, folio 150), at Manakdeke by a Mewati family (p. 178, folio not stated).
- Folk practice at the tree (reconstructed): cloth tied to the branch, circling the tree, praying for a son (pp. 185–186).
- The Masand system: Grewal's article "'Nanak's Doctrine and the Feudalization of the Sikh Gurudom'" reports "corruption by Masands" and says the Guruship had become "feudal in nature, with large tracts of land under its control" (p. 181, folio 155). By Harkrishan's time "the institution of Guru had become a Sodhi family dominion" (p. 181, folio 155).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- Nanak's blessing of hostile Kanganpur and "curse" of hospitable Manakdeke, told in the author's reconstruction (pp. 175–179). The author notes Manakdeke is "still a small village like Nanak hoped" (p. 178, folio not stated).
- Truth and untruth bought for four paisas; Karar's chits read "Life is false" and "Death is truth" (p. 188, folio 162). Author's reconstruction.
- Maula Karar dies of snakebite while hiding from Nanak. The book says Nanak "could not perform any miracles" (p. 190, folio 164).
- Local claim at Baba Bair that "Guru Nanak also came to this saint [Pir Bairi] and stayed with him" (p. 195, folio 169). The author calls it "an apocryphal story".

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- The author doubts Ghaffar's criticism of Manakdeke's people and asks whether "my own biases" had led him to validate "Guru Nanak's analysis" (p. 176, folio 150).
- The author links the growth of Ahle-Hadith mosques to Saudi money sent "since the Iranian revolution" to "counter Iranian Shia influence" (p. 176, folio 150).
- On Ram Rai: "The Sikh version of the story" blames Ram Rai's own actions. Another view, from Cunningham, "A History of the Sikhs, page 108", holds that he was the son of a handmaiden, and raising him "could have tarnished the entire institution" (p. 181, folio 155).
- The author calls it ironic that the Guruship became feudal, "because Sikhism is seen as a reaction against the feudal land [grant system?] [OCR?] of Punjab by a lot of historians" (p. 182, folio 156).
- On the Pir Bairi story: "This is an apocryphal story. I have seen quite a few shrines around the country to know how to tell a real story from a fabricated one" [OCR?]. The author offers "one of two possibilities": the first is that "this tree was already considered sacred in the local folk religion of [the] region when Nanak came here", and that "Besides berry, … peepal are some of the trees that have historically been worshipped and are still revered in the Islamic folk religion" [OCR?]. The second possibility is cut off at the end of the chunk (p. 195, folio 169).
- Travel writing: an article cuts "hours" of travel down to "a thousand words or so", so readers "associate romanticism with travel" (p. 192, folio 166).

**Mapping doubts.**
1. The 18-id Guru Nanak bullet, the Hargobind bullet (6 ids) and the Gobind Singh and Mardana bullets are person-level attachments, not site-specific.
2. The Sialkot (5), Eminabad (3), Gujranwala (2), Nankana Sahib (7), Lahore (35), Kasur (1) and Multan (8) bullets are place-level under ruling (a). Several rest on a single passing mention (Lahore, Kasur, Multan, Gujranwala), so a consolidator should weigh them as very thin.
3. "Baba Bair" mapped to `gurdwara-babay-de-ber`: the place matches (Sialkot) and the site is a gurdwara at Nanak's berry tree. However, the book never prints the name "Babay de Ber", only "Baba Bair". The identification is my judgement on name plus place.
4. The woman's prayer and the Karar conversation "under the tree" are placed on that row because the book links the tree to the later gurdwara ("Under the berry tree where Nanak and Mardana sat", p. 195).
5. "Kartarpur" (p. 182, Harkrishan's march to Delhi): the book does not say which Kartarpur, and nothing ties it to Narowal. I have not mapped it to `gurdwara-darbar-sahib-kartarpur`.
6. "Amritsar" and "Delhi", "Dehradun", "Jaipur", "Bakala", "Kabul" are not archive places.
7. "Mian Mitha" is not an archive row, and "Hamza…" is too fragmentary to identify.
8. Bhai Lalo appears only as someone Nanak met at Eminabad, carried on the Eminabad bullet, which includes his row.
9. No "Saidpur" or "Talwandi" occurs in this chunk.

**Registers.** Everything in Nanak's, Mardana's, Karar's and Harkrishan's scenes is the author's reconstruction; the Harkrishan chapter is historical narrative with footnotes to Cunningham and Grewal. The travelogue observations are: Kanganpur, Manakdeke, the Sialkot road and Baba Bair (pp. 175–179, 191–195). The visit dates are not stated, apart from the Auqaf board's "2012".
