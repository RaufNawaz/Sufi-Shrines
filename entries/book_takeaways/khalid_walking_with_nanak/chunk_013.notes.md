  - The author says that "In his poetry Nanak spoke vehemently against religious exploitation", and quotes a hymn ("His goodness cannot be priced or traded …"), footnoted "Singh, trans. Hymns of the Gurus, page 20" (p. 280, folio 254).
  - The author's reading: "We find in his poems a disdain for [printed 'ior'] priests and Sufi pirs who manipulate the people for their own economic benefit"; Nanak's philosophy has "a strong concept of individualism"; "Nanak talks about removing intermediaries and establishing a direct connect with God" (p. 280, folio 254).

  - In the author's reconstruction, Nanak's sons disappointed him: "Lachman … had become a businessman"; Sri Chand "was highly inspired by the yogis and would soon take to the path of the ascetic" (p. 297, folio 271); Nanak "blamed himself for the extremes of his son's behaviour" (p. 298, folio 272).
  - In the author's reconstructed dialogue, Sri Chand objects to Lehna ("he is still a practising Hindu. He still worships Durga in his house") and to his appointment as "spiritual successor"; Sulakhni says "You should listen to your blood" (p. 300, folio 274).

- **gurdwara-darbar-sahib-kartarpur** (the book's "Kartarpur … on the banks of the Ravi", also "Kartarpur Sahib"):
  - Nanak "had finally settled down at a place called Kartarpur on the banks of the Ravi. Here he had started working on a piece of land that was given to him by one of his devotees" (split by OCR across lines); he was joined by "his parents and also his wife and children"; devotees moved there; "Using Kartarpur as his base, Nanak now started travelling to nearby regions" (p. 288, folio 262).
  - In the author's reconstruction, Nanak lived "in a simple hut that was built of wood and hay"; walked each dawn "towards the Ravi that flowed close to his hut", bathing there "for the past seventeen years" (p. 293, folio 267).
  - In the author's reconstruction, Nanak wished he could have buried Mardana at "Kartarpur, his final abode" and visited his grave there (damaged passage) (p. 293, folio 267).
  - "He sent for his family from Talwindi [OCR?] [Talwandi] and started living at this small village called Kartarpur" (p. 296, folio 270).
  - In the author's reconstruction, the evening routine at Kartarpur: devotees came "to listen to the songs of their spiritual master"; Sulakhni and other women "prepared for the langar which concluded the evening"; Nanak told her "that Muslims and even untouchables would be welcome to the langar", which at first "repulsed her" (p. 296, folio 270).
  - "It had now been seventeen years that Nanak had been leading a sedentary life. Hundreds of devotees from neighbouring villages and beyond came to visit him every day"; kirtan every evening; "eventually a new hall was constructed which could host the huge gathering. This place was reserved for the evening prayers and was referred to as a Gurdwara by Nanak, where the Ultimate Guru resided" (p. 298, folio 272).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)
- The author, formerly sympathetic to "shrine culture" as "religious syncretism and a greater tolerance", now "felt derision towards the shrine culture like I had never felt before" at seeing people "robbed of their hard-earned money" (p. 280, folio 254); yet "as an anthropologist" he holds that shrines give "a source of identity to a community" (pp. 280–281).
- The author finds the banner of four saints misleading: "all of them belonged to different schools of thought" — Zakariya elite/court-linked, Farid anti-court and poor, Lal Shahbaz Malamti (p. 282, folio 256).
- Ayesha Siddiqa (reported): Jamat-ud-Dawa and "Laskhar-e-Jhangvi" [OCR?], of the Ahl-e-Hadith school, "have seeped into the social fabric of southern Punjab", historically syncretistic; "Such Sufi shrines are a symbol of that strand of Islam" (p. 284, folio 258).
- Tahir Kamran (reported): extremist organisations appeal to youth and first "target such shrines and their guardians, who have become immensely rich feeding off the oppressed classes" (p. 284, folio 258).
- The author traces "similar[ities] between [Nanak's] revulsion for such practices and those of these proscribed" organisations (damaged) (pp. 284–285).

- **Mapping — person-level:** the 18-row Guru Nanak bullet is a person-level attachment, not site-specific. Mardana → `gurdwara-rori-sahib`, Bebe Nanki → `gurdwara-babay-nanki`, Ram Das → `gurdwara-guru-ram-das-ji`, Farid, Zakariya, Lal Shahbaz Qalandar → their rows: all person-level; the Zakariya, Farid and Lal Shahbaz material is about the saints and their depictions at Uch, not about their own shrines.
- **Mapping — place-level:** Uch Sharif (5 ids), Bahawalpur (4), Lahore (35), Islamabad (4), Kasur (1) are place-level. Lahore and Islamabad mentions are incidental (the author's home), as is Bahawalpur (Ayesha Siddiqa's home). The travel passages at pp. 281–286 never name Uch Sharif; the location is inferred from the shrines named (Jalaludin Surkh Posh Bokhari, Bibi Jiwindi, Baha-ul-Halim, Nauria) and from the reconstruction at p. 288 ("the shrine of Jalaludin Bokhari at Uch Sharif"). Which shrine the author sits at on p. 280 ("sitting at this shrine") is not stated in this chunk.
- **Toponyms deliberately not given their own bullet:** "Multan" (appears only as Zakariya's seat, and in "Multani tiles"), "Pakpattan" (only in the Farid bullet, same row), "Sehwan Sharif" (only as Lal Shahbaz's epithet; toponym would also take `shrine-of-lakhi-shah-saddar`) — flagged for the consolidator in case ruling (a) is to be applied to these epithet-style mentions.
- **Kartarpur:** "Kartarpur on the banks of the Ravi" / "Kartarpur Sahib" is mapped to `gurdwara-darbar-sahib-kartarpur` (Narowal, on the Ravi), per the book's own Ravi description.
- **Talwandi:** "Talwindi" [OCR?] is where Nanak's family lived before Kartarpur (p. 296); the chunk does not identify it as Nankana Sahib or as his birthplace, so it is not mapped to the Nankana Sahib rows.
- **Nauria vs. Ustad Nuriya:** mapped to `tomb-of-ustad-nuriya` on name and site (Uch mound); the book says only "Nauria".
- **Bibi Jiwindi vs. Javindi Bibi:** mapped to `tomb-of-javindi-bibi` on name and site.
- **Internal:** the book says Nanak visited Uch "sometime in the late fifteenth century" (p. 285) yet the reconstruction places the visit after he settled at Kartarpur following twenty-four years of travel (p. 288); no year is given for either.
- The chunk ends mid-dialogue (p. 300); the text between p. 299 (Shahmukhi) and p. 300 (a family conversation) does not join.
