# khalid_walking_with_nanak — chunk 005 — pp. 102–122

*Covers the end of chapter 5, "THE JOURNEY BEGINS" (p. 102, folio 84), including the author's reconstructed Saidpur/Lalo/Malik Bhago scenes and a present-day Eminabad travelogue, then the opening of chapter 6, "SOUTHWARDS" (p. 119, folio 101), which starts the reconstructed Sajjan–Gajjan caravanserai scene and the approach to Multan. Folios are computed as PDF − 18 and checked against legible headers (see Doubts for p. 109).*

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **gurdwara-khoohi-bhai-lalo-bhai-lalo-di-khooi** (the book's "Bhai Lalo di Khoi"; also the person Bhai Lalo):
  - Travelogue: the author and Iqbal Qaiser asked a group of old men in a market where "Bhai Lalo di Khoi" was, and were guided into an alley. "There was a lock at the entrance of the gurdwara. This was the site of the house of Bhai Lalo." (p. 115, folio 97)
  - Travelogue: "Far from being a worn down place that Nanak had visited, it was now in good shape. This is one of the most popular Sikh shrines in Pakistan and is visited by hundreds of devotees of Nanak." They photographed the locked gurdwara while passers-by wondered whether they were Sikh pilgrims (p. 115, folio 97).
  - Iqbal Qaiser: "This is how the gurdwara is at all times", meaning locked. The author guesses the keys were "probably with members of the Sikh comm[unity]" at the nearby Gurdwara Rori Sahib (p. 116, folio 98).
  - Citing Iqbal Qaiser's book *Historical Sikh Shrines of Pais [OCR?] [Pakistan]*: "a Muslim migrant family" was living inside "the Gurdwar[a] Bhai Lalo di Khoi". "In 1989, a Sikh by the name of Sardar Surjit Singh Panesar bought the property of the shrine with money collected by the expat Sikh community and renovated the shrine." It was then opened for worship, and at pilgrimage time several Sikhs come to "pay tribute to the memory of Guru Nanak and Bhai Lal[o]" (p. 116, folio 98).
  - In the author's reconstructed scene, Lalo is a carpenter in Saidpur, older than Nanak, living in the poorest house in the city. The house has no window, only a charpoy, a rolled-up mattress and a few clay utensils (pp. 102, 104; folios 84, 86).
  - In the reconstruction, Lalo "could not read or write but was eager for education". As a child he loitered outside the havelis of rich teachers, hoping to overhear lessons in Persian and Arabic, while his father trained him as a carpenter (p. 105, folio 87).
  - In the reconstruction, Lalo serves Nanak and Mardana two dried-up rotis with water instead of daal. He asks why Nanak did not go to Malik Bhago's feast, and Nanak explains his rejection of caste (pp. 109–111; folio not stated for p. 109, folios 92–93).
  - In the reconstruction, Mardana plays the rubab at night while Nanak sings. "throngs of people headed towards Lalo's house to hear the songs of Guru Nanak and Bhai Mardana." Nanak stays at Saidpur "for a few days" (p. 111, folio 93).
  - The book says that in the Janamsakhis the encounter between Nanak and Bhai Lalo "is one of the most important" (line damaged) (p. 112, folio 94).
  - Babur Bani, as the book quotes it, repeatedly addresses "O Lalo" (pp. 113–114, folios 95–96).
  - Iqbal Qaiser compares Bhai Lalo with Bhai Ram Singh: "Bhai Ram Singh is of the carpenter caste and Bhai Lalo was also a carpenter." He denies that Ram Singh was Lalo's descendant: "They shared the same caste. That's all." (p. 117, folio 99)

- **gurdwara-chakki-sahib** (the book's "Gurdwara Chakki Sahib"):
  - Travelogue: Iqbal Qaiser stopped in front of "a small house. It was an ordinary structure with a small door and a few steps leading to it." A pole covered in saffron cloth rose from behind its walls, with "a Khalsa flag" on top. "This too was locked." (p. 116, folio 98)
  - Iqbal Qaiser: "This place was used as a prison at the time of Babur. Guru Nanak was imprisoned here and was forced to work on a stone grinder." (p. 116, folio 98)
  - The book attributes the related story to the Janamsakhis: Nanak was in Saidpur when Mughal forces took the city. He was jailed with the other citizens and set to a stone grinder, which moved by itself "using his miraculous powers". The guards told Babur, who sent for him (p. 114, folio 96). (The book does not name the Janamsakhi prison as Chakki Sahib; only Iqbal Qaiser's remark at p. 116 makes that link.)
  - Asked whether Nanak may never have met Babur, Iqbal Qaiser replies "Yes. Very much so." He calls the stories of Nanak meeting a Muslim saint, Malik Bhago and Babur "later constructions by his devotees" (p. 116, folio 98).

- **gurdwara-rori-sahib** (the book's "Gurdwara Rori Sahib"; also the person Bhai Mardana):
  - Named as nearby, with "members of the Sikh comm[unity]" residing there (p. 116, folio 98).
  - Travelogue: the gatekeeper knew Iqbal Qaiser and let them in. There were empty grounds on both sides of the path, and on one side "a few police officials lazed on a charpoy" (pp. 116–117, folios 98–99).
  - The doorway is "a splendid construction". "A verse from the Guru Granth Sahib was carved on top of it with an artistic effect of bricks." (p. 117, folio 99)
  - Iqbal Qaiser says the designer was "Bhai Ram Singh", Principal of "Mayo School of Arts in Lahore", who designed "the Indian gallery in Buckingham Palace" and came from a family of carpenters. Qaiser says Ram Singh created "the effect of woodcutting from the use of bricks" (p. 117, folio 99).
  - Dating: "It was constructed around 1922-23. Before that, it was a small shrine. Only this gateway was constructed by Bhai Ram Singh." (p. 117, folio 99)
  - Next to the gateway is a small courtyard with several rooms, where "A few Pathan Sikh children were playing". Iqbal Qaiser: "They are the caretakers of this Sikh gurdwara. They have moved here from Nankana Sahib." (p. 117, folio 99)
  - They took their shoes off at the portal. The main shrine "was an ordinary room which too was locked" (p. 117, folio 99).
  - Iqbal Qaiser explains the name: this is not where Nanak worked the grindstone but "the place where he was picked up by the guards". It "was a small mound outside Eminabad where there Were Baby, [OCR?] pebbles, hence the name Rori Sahib". "Nanak was sitting here with Mardana at the time of his arrest." (p. 118, folio 100)
  - A structure stands in the middle of the ground next to the gurdwara, with buffaloes wallowing around it in pools of water. Qaiser: "That was also a part of the gurdwara. It must have been the [OCR?] hall." The protective wall around the gurdwara is recent (line damaged: "It was onl[y] … a few years ago") (p. 118, folio 100).
  - Former condition, per Qaiser: "Oh it was horrible. The structure was standing as it is but there [was] no protection. A bazaar for the sale of buffaloes used to be set up [at] the premises of the gurdwara." (p. 118, folio 100)
  - Present condition, per Qaiser: "It was after my book was published that this gurdwara came into the limelight. Now it is fully restored. There are security officials here at all times and Sikh pilgrims [come] here every year." (p. 118, folio 100)
  - Mardana, in the author's reconstruction: "a Muslim Mirasi (a community of genealogists and bards)" and Nanak's best friend from Talwindi. He joined Nanak at Sultanpur and "had ignited within Nanak a passion fo[r] music". Nanak's family "regarded Mardana as an untouchable Mirasi" (p. 106, folio 88).
  - In the reconstruction, Mardana grew up travelling with his clan, singing the bravery of local chieftains for gifts from landlords, and knew "folk stories of Hindu deities and Muslim saints" (pp. 120–121, folios 102–103).
  - In the reconstruction, Mardana worried that "music was un-Islamic, a criticism that came from the more puritanical sects of Islam", and Nanak reassured him. Mardana followed Nanak to Sultanpur and then on the journey, often regretting the hardship (pp. 122, folio 104).

  - Nanak "had been sent to Sultanpur by his father on the request of Bey [OCR?] [Bebe] Nanaki and her husband" (p. 106, folio 88).

  - In the author's reconstruction, Nanak tells Lalo he "used to be in charge of the food store at the court of the Diwa[n] [OCR?] of Sultanpur". He quit that job to "take up this garb and travel the world in search of Truth" (pp. 104–105, folios 86–87).

  - In the reconstruction, he was storekeeper at the court of "the Governor of Sultanpur, Daulat Lodh[i] Khan".   - In the reconstruction, at Sultanpur Nanak and Mardana began a ritual: every night Mardana played the rubab while Nanak sang his poetry. The book calls this a daily feature "for the rest of their [lives] and also for the Sikh community" (p. 106, folio 88). Nanak's audience "included both Hindus and Muslims" (p. 107, folio 89).

  - In the reconstruction, the leave-taking: "Sulakhni and her two children", "Mehta Kalu and Mata Tr; Ch [OCR?] [Mata Tripta, as printed clean on the same page]", the children "Sri Chand and Lakitor . Das [OCR?] [second name not recoverable]" (p. 108, folio 90).
  - The book states that Nanak and Mardana "would not return to a sedentary life for twenty-four years" and "would travel thousands of kilometres, that too on foot" (p. 108, folio 90).
  - In the reconstruction, the journey's first goal is Saidpur, the city "with the grandest Hindu temples" (p. 108, folio 90).
  - The author's view: Nanak "was repulsed by the concept of making a larger-than-life figure out of a religious leader. He spoke vehemently against miracles being associated with them."   - "Babur Bani was the first poem of Nanak that I read." The book says it is believed to have been written when "the first Mughal Emperor from Kabul crossed the Indus and attacked India". The quoted translation includes "Coming in seventy-eight (1521 C.E), they will depart in Ninety sear, [OCR?] (1540 C_E.) [OCR?]".   - The Janamsakhis record a meeting between Nanak and Babur at Saidpur. Babur asks for a blessing to conquer India, and Nanak rebukes him (p. 114, folio 96).
  - In the reconstruction (ch. 6), Nanak prefers sleeping under trees to caravanserais and does not seek hospitality in cities or villages. He reasons that Hindus and Muslims are such only by birth (p. 120, folio 102).

- **gurdwara-chakki-sahib**, **gurdwara-khoohi-bhai-lalo-bhai-lalo-di-khooi**, **gurdwara-rori-sahib** (the book's "Saidpur" = "Eminabad"):

  - In the reconstruction: "Saidpur was an ancient city." Legends held that its temples had been built "thousands of years ago, long before the birth of Islam". The temples had grown into complexes with rooms for pilgrims, shops leased to traders, stables and "an army of servants" (p. 103, folio 85).
  - In the reconstruction, temple economy: long lines of devotees, the "Brahmin pandits were the bosses", and vast offerings of clothes, food and jewellery were kept, distributed to temple workers, or sold to cover running costs. Guards kept "naked and starving beggars" out of the temple premises (pp. 103–104, folios 85–86).
  - In the reconstruction: "This was a Hindu-majority city with a few Muslim household[s]." The Hindus were mostly traders and the Muslims aristocrats. It was "one of the most important trading centres between the Chenab and the Ravi.   - In the reconstruction, Malik Bhago's "four-storey haveli known as the Shahi Qilla at Saidpur": the largest structure in the city, with frescoes on its external walls. Malik Bhago was "a minister of the Rai of Saidpur", rumoured to steal from the King's treasury, and was holding a wedding feast for his son to which "all Brahmins and members of the high castes" were invited (p. 109, folio not stated).
  - Travelogue, present-day Eminabad: "The cone like spires of ancient Hindu temples" rise among the houses, "beautiful, in contrast to the monolithic Muslim architecture that now dominates the city of Eminabad".   - Travelogue: "Once, it was an important trading centre on the Grand Trunk Road that connected with Lahore and then Delhi". The highway had since shifted southwards (p. 115, folio 97).
  - Travelogue, one temple: "Despite being abandoned in 1947, the temple still looked sturdy." Its paint had worn off and "a corrosive layer had developed". These temples "had been taken over by migrants from the other side of the border following the creation of Pakistan", and descendants of those refugees stared back at the author (p. 115, folio 97).
  - Travelogue: the author wondered whether the temples had been attacked "in 1992, after the destruction of the Babri mosque in India", and "searched for signs of attack but found none" (p. 115, folio 97).
  - Iqbal Qaiser walked the narrow alleys. The author "marvelled at how much of the historical architecture had stil[l] been preserved" (p. 116, folio 98).

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

He summons Nanak ("Bring that infidel to me"), and Nanak says Bhago's food "is prepared out of the blood of [y]our subjec[ts], while Bhai Lalo's food is cooked in the milk of honesty" (pp. 111–112, folios 93–94).

- Bhai Ram Singh: Principal of Mayo School of Arts, Lahore, and designer of the Indian gallery in Buckingham Palace, according to Iqbal Qaiser (p. 117, folio 99).

- Iqbal Qaiser, *Historical Sikh Shrines of Pais [OCR?] [Pakistan]* (p. 116, folio 98).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- The keys to Bhai Lalo di Khoi are said to be held at Rori Sahib; both Khoi and Chakki Sahib were locked on the visit (pp. 115–116, folios 97–98).
- Custody and security at Rori Sahib: a gatekeeper, police officials on the grounds, Pathan Sikh caretakers from Nankana Sahib, security officials "at all times" and yearly Sikh pilgrims (pp. 116–118, folios 98–100).
- The Bhai Lalo di Khoi property was bought in 1989 with expatriate Sikh money and reopened for worship, after being occupied by a Muslim migrant family (p. 116, folio 98).
- Hindu temples in Eminabad were abandoned in 1947 and occupied by migrants from across the border (p. 115, folio 97).
- In the author's reconstruction, the temple economy of Saidpur: offerings, leased shops, pilgrim rooms, stables and servants (p. 103, folio 85).
- In the reconstruction, the evening rubab-and-song gathering began at Sultanpur and continued at Lalo's house (pp. 106, 111; folios 88, 93).
- Shoes are removed at the portal of Rori Sahib (p. 117, folio 99).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- Attributed to the Janamsakhis: when Nanak squeezed Malik Bhago's bread, "blood started to ooze out of it"; Bhai Lalo's bread gave milk (p. 112, folio 94). The author thinks the miracles were "inserted into the Janamsakhis to give Nanak a larger than-life personality" (p. 112, folio 94).
- Attributed to the Janamsakhis: in Babur's jail at Saidpur, Nanak's stone grinder moved by itself (p. 114, folio 96). Iqbal Qaiser places the prison at Gurdwara Chakki Sahib (p. 116, folio 98).
- Attributed to the legend: Malik Bhago was humbled and "realised Nanak's spiritual depth" (p. 112, folio 94).
- Name legend: Eminabad was named after a woman, "Eni [OCR?]", who fed Babur roasted grain (p. 116, folio 98).
- Name origin, per Iqbal Qaiser: Rori Sahib is named for the pebbles ("rori") on the mound where Nanak was arrested (p. 118, folio 100).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- On the Janamsakhis: they were collected "[s]ixty-five [OCR?] years after Nanak's death" by devotees (line damaged), and "several miracles" were "inserted" into them (p. 112, folio 94).
- The author doubts Nanak met Malik Bhago: Bhago is "too tyrannical to allow Nanak to leave unharmed" (p. 112, folio 94).
- The author doubts the meeting with Babur because of the Baburnama's silence and because "That was not the way of kings" (p. 114, folio 96). He notes that in Sikh tradition the interaction is seen "as the beginning" of the history of hostility between the Mughal emperors and the later Sikh Gurus (p. 115, folio 97; badly damaged).
- The author calls Nanak "a rationalist" (p. 112, folio 94).
- Iqbal Qaiser thinks Nanak "must have been an ordinary person" and that the meeting stories are "later constructions by his devotees" (p. 116, folio 98).
- Iqbal Qaiser credits his own book with bringing Rori Sahib "into the limelight" (p. 118, folio 100).

## Doubts

- **Damage inventory (tesseract):** [source stamp: www.sikhbookclub.com watermark] on almost every page. The running headers and stray symbols are garbled. There are letter confusions and margin truncations throughout (e.g. "hungr,", "Brahming", "thy,", "] would"). The top of p. 115 (lines before "immense historical inportance") is almost entirely destroyed and was used only for the phrases quoted. Parts of pp. 104, 106, 112, 118 and 122 are damaged at the margins, and the phrases quoted from them are marked.
- **Folio:** p. 109's header reads "THE JOURNEY BEGINS 7", which is not consistent with the computed 91, so I cited it as folio not stated. p. 114's header is illegible ("%"), and I cited the computed 96. p. 115's header is garbled, and I cited the computed 97, which matches the brief. The chapter-opening pages (p. 102 and p. 119) print no folio; I cited the computed 84 and 101. Footnote markers ("?3", "4", "25") and the footnote's "page 75" / "page vii" / "pages 722-723" are not this book's pages.

It is not mapped to the Islamabad Saidpur rows, because the chunk never mentions Islamabad, Margalla or Rawalpindi.
