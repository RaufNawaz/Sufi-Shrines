## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **gurudwara-janam-asthan-nankana-sahib**, **gurdwara-babay-de-ber**, **gurdwara-balila-sahib-bal-lila-sahib**, **gurdwara-chakki-sahib**, **gurdwara-choa-sahib**, **gurdwara-darbar-sahib-kartarpur**, **gurdwara-khoohi-bhai-lalo-bhai-lalo-di-khooi**, **gurdwara-malji-sahib**, **gurdwara-panja-sahib**, **gurdwara-patti-sahib**, **gurdwara-pehli-patshahi-jind-pir-sukkur**, **gurdwara-rori-sahib**, **gurdwara-sach-khand-sahib**, **gurdwara-sacha-sauda**, **gurdwara-sahib-saidpur-guru-nanak-dev-ji**, **gurdwara-sri-kiara-sahib**, **gurdwara-sri-tilganji-sahib**, **gurdwara-tambo-sahib** (Guru Nanak, person-level; no archive site named):
  - In the author's reconstructed scene, Sheikh Ibrahim (a descendant of Baba Farid, holding the position of Diwan) questions Nanak about his religious identity; Nanak is seated "on a small mound, a few kilometres away from the city and the shrine" (p. 146, folio 128).
  - In the reconstructed scene Nanak says: "If I say I am a Hindu, I would be telling a lie. I am also not a Muslim. I don't accept the differences between Hindus and Muslims" and calls himself "a servant of God and a brother of true devotees" (p. 146, folio 128).
  - In the reconstructed scene Nanak praises "the use of simple vernacular" in Baba Farid's poetry and says he has never met "any Muslim alive or dead" who understood the essence of Islam as much as Baba Farid, "the true follower of the Prophet Muhammad (PBUH)" (p. 147, folio 129); the scene is footnoted "Singh, Guru Nanak: Founder of Sikhism, page 109" (p. 147, folio 129), "page 110" (p. 148, folio 130), "page 111" (p. 149, folio 131).
  - In the reconstructed scene Nanak promises to answer Ibrahim's question with a poem, and before leaving recites it ("Launch ye the boat of austere contemplation …") (pp. 148–149, folios 130–131).
  - The author states Ibrahim's scribe compiled "all the poems of Baba Farid" on parchment and handed them to Nanak; "It was through this channel that the poems of Baba Farid found their place in the Guru Granth Sahib"; before that Farid's poetry passed orally, and "it was through Guru Nanak that they were preserved for the first time in written form" (p. 149, folio 131).
  - The author: Nanak "must have visited" Tibba Haji Deen (then called Kothewal) "around the end of the fifteenth century" (p. 150, folio 132).
  - The author: in Benaras, "where Guru Nanak visited, there are legends that Nanak met Kabir but that too is historically not proven" (p. 153, folio 135).
  - The author: Nanak "like Farid, also chose Punjabi as his medium of expression" and was "impressed by the fact that Baba Farid lived a life of simplicity and shunned violence" (p. 153, folio 135).
  - A hymn of Nanak ("As a beggar goes a-begging …") is quoted, footnoted "Singh, trans. Hymns of the Gurus, pages 23-24" (p. 158, folio 140).
  - In the reconstructed Kanganpur scene: after "their journey east, which went all the way to Bengal", Nanak and Mardana "headed south where they had crossed the sea and entered Sri Lanka", then decided to go home; they reached Lahore and from there came to Kanganpur (p. 163, folio 145).
  - In the reconstructed scene the Kanganpur villagers, taking Nanak and Mardana for thugs "who had come from Kasur" with black magic, stone them; "Partially bruised" [OCR?] [bruised], the two leave (pp. 162–164, folios 144–146).
  - The author mentions "Guru Nanak's prophecy" about Kanganpur without stating it in this chunk: "The city of Kanganpur is expanding and I cannot help but wonder if Guru Nanak's prophecy is coming true" (p. 164, folio 146).

- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar** (Baba Farid; "Sheikh Farid"; title "Shakarganj"; the book's "Pakpattan"):
  - Sheikh Ibrahim is presented as one of Baba Farid's descendants, in a position (Diwan) he found frustrating; he disapproved of the myths created from Baba Farid's life story (p. 146, folio 128). The shrine of Baba Farid sits on a mound larger than the one where Nanak sat (p. 146, folio 128).
  - Two poems of Baba Farid are quoted in the reconstructed scene ("If someone smites your face, O Farid …", p. 147, folio 129; "You did not launch your boat …", p. 148, folio 130).
  - Baba Farid "was born in the year 1173 CE" at the village of Tibba Haji Deen (formerly Kothewal); the house "is preserved", its courtyard holding graves of the saint's ancestors "back seven generations"; "Baba Farid's parents are also buried here" (p. 150, folio 132).
  - There the author met Ghulam Shabbir, "whose family has been serving this shrine for the past seven generations"; magical stories of the saint and his ancestors are passed down in his family and narrated to devotees (p. 150, folio 132).
  - Nearby, "another shrine dedicated to the childhood of Baba Farid": a well within a house, covered by an iron cage on whose railing devotees tie thread; a caretaker draws and serves the water; "Because of his presence, this well and its water became sacred" (p. 150, folio 132).
  - The author: Farid "first uses the symbolism of a wife being abandoned by her husband" for devotee and deity (p. 153, folio 135); "Baba Farid is considered to be the first poet of the Punjabi language"; he "wrote for the people in a language that they could understand", when the elite used Persian and Arabic (p. 153, folio 135).
  - "The Chistiyan school of thought in Sufism, of which Baba Farid was once the head, is one of the most syncretic sects of Islam"; its hallmark is qawwali, "the Muslim version of bhajan" (p. 153, folio 135).
  - "Once Baba Farid found a place in the Guru Granth Sahib, his personality and shrine became holy for the followers of Guru Nanak as well. Every year, hundreds of Sikhs and Hindus travel to Pakpattan to pay homage to the shrine of Baba Farid" (p. 153, folio 135).
  - "The shrine of Baba Farid is the focal point of the city of Pakpattan"; roads lead to it; it is the highest point of the area; it was "an abandoned mound, probably carrying within it the secrets of an ancient city, when Farid moved here with his students. Now it is a shrine spread over a vast tract of land"; shops on the approach are all named after Baba Farid or "his title Shakarganj" (p. 154, folio not stated).
  - Chistiyan, "a small city which was established by Baba Farid's son and named after his school of thought, according to legend" (p. 154, folio not stated).
  - Tibba Nanaksar is "about five kilometres from the shrine of Baba Farid" (p. 154, folio not stated); Iqbal Qaiser says pressure "even by the Diwan of the shrine of Baba Farid" led the government to renovate the Tibba Nanaksar gurdwara (p. 156, folio 138).
  - A descendant of Baba Farid, "Baba Fateh Ullah Shah Noori Chisti", has a shrine around which a mosque at Tibba Nanaksar was built (p. 155, folio 137).

- **madho-lal-hussain** (Shah Hussain): named with Baba Farid, Guru Nanak, Bulleh Shah and Waris Shah as figures who have "become a part of" the author's existence (p. 151, folio 133); "Shah Hussain's verse" is paraphrased: chanting the name of Ranjha "that I myself have become Ranjha, call me Ranjha" (p. 152, folio 134).

## Practices, institutions, economy
- Tibba Haji Deen gurdwara renovation "was another of Iqbal Qaiser's success stories" (p. 151, folio 133); now "several Sikhs had started visiting the shrine of Guru Nanak ever since its renovation" (p. 152, folio 134).
- Maha Chawar shrine: "two festivals", one by the Islamic calendar, one by the local calendar; the guardian heard from elders that Sikhs held their festival at the gurdwara on the same dates as the urs of Maha Chawar, with a yearly "kabaddi … competition between the Sikhs and the Muslims" (p. 152, folio 134); the author wonders whether the local-calendar urs is "a roundabout way of continuing to pay homage to the legacy of Guru Nanak" (p. 152, folio 134).
- A government official from the "Augaf [OCR?] [Auqaf] Department" present asked whether Nanak and Farid were contemporaries; Iqbal Qaiser: "There was a difference of three hundred years between them" (p. 152, folio 134).
- Tibba Nanaksar: gurdwara locked; keys with the family living across; mosque next door under renovation; supervisor reluctant and irritated; Iqbal Qaiser: "There was no boundary wall between the gurdwara and the mosque … Slowly, the guardians of the mosque were taking over the property of the gurdwara" (p. 155, folio 137); the key-holding woman: "A lot of Sikhs come here", renovated "a few years ago", "Now we have been given the responsibility for the maintenance" (p. 155, folio 137); Iqbal Qaiser claims it as "another contribution of my book" (p. 155, folio 137); pressure from "expat Si[khs]" and the Diwan of Baba Farid's shrine led to renovation (p. 156, folio 138).
- Kanganpur: "Before Partition there were quite a few Sikhs [and] Hindus here, now it was dominated by Muslims"; "'Untouchable' Christian quarters" of low-caste Christians (p. 160, folio 142). Gurdwara "had vast property attached to it, which technically came under the Augaf [OCR?] [Auqaf] Department. In reality though, it was under the control of local usurpers" (p. 174, folio 148); Ghaffar wants the gurdwara as office and library of his organisation, discusses taking it over forcefully; Iqbal Qaiser advises a letter to the Prime Minister, copied to "the Chief Minister and Chairman, Augaf [OCR?] [Auqaf] Department" (p. 174, folio 148).
- Tea and smoking in public during Ramzan in Kanganpur, "almost impossible" in Lahore (pp. 173–174, folios 147–148).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)
- Tibba Haji Deen caretaker: "Baba takes care of his shrine himself … Whoever now comes and tries to sleep inside the shrine, Baba beats him up in the night. I have seen it with my own eyes" (p. 151, folio 133); the author is sarcastic and Iqbal Qaiser "repulsed by the superstitious beliefs" (p. 151, folio 133).
- Childhood well of Baba Farid: its water sacred "Because of his presence" (p. 150, folio 132).
- In the reconstructed Kanganpur scene, villagers say the strangers can fly and "disappear into thin air" (pp. 162–163, folios 144–145); earlier jogis "wearing saffron cholas" had "hoodwinked many innocent villagers", after which elders resolved never to entertain holy men (p. 162, folio 144).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)
- Oral tradition "presented" Farid and Nanak "as contemporaries, not in the dimension of their time period but because of the similarity of their philosophies" (p. 153, folio 135).
- Punjabi, "this language of peasants was given the status of a holy language after Sikhism as a religion was institutionalised" (p. 153, folio 135).
- Iqbal Qaiser: the Kanganpur incident may never have happened ("It is likely that this incident never took place") (p. 159, folio 141); the author calls the stigma on Kanganpur "harsh on the part of Nanak" (p. 159, folio 141).
- Iqbal Qaiser on Tibba Nanaksar: if this is how "one of the most important shrine[s] of Guru Nanak in Pakistan" is treated, "one can imagine what would happen to those which are not as important" (p. 156, folio 138).
