# khalid_walking_with_nanak — chunk 003 — pp. 59–79

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **gurdwara-sacha-sauda** (the book's "Gurdwara Sacha Sauda", at the town the book calls "Farooqabad", historical name "Chuhrkhana" / "Chuharkhana"). The site-specific material of this chunk.
  - Footnote: "This incident is known as Sacha Sauda (honest trade) and is one of the most important events from Nanak's early life" — the incident being Nanak spending his father's money on food for ascetics (p. 66, folio 48).
  - In the author's reconstructed scene, Mehta Kalu gives the "eighteen-year-old Nanak" "the hefty amount of twenty rupees" to visit "the market town of Chuharkhana about twelve kos ( forty-five kilometres) from here" and buy something to trade (p. 63, folio 45).
  - In the reconstructed scene, Nanak tells Rai Bular that in "a jungle on the way" to "the market of Chuharkhana" he met a group of naked ascetics with matted hair, "Their leader was a wise man called Santrain", a group which "refers to itself as Nirbani" (p. 65, folio not stated); Santrain refused money as "against his religion" and told him to buy food instead, so "when I reached Chuharkha [OCR?] [Chuharkhana] ... I bought food for the ascetics ... That was my honest trade" (p. 66, folio 48). Mehta Kalu slapped him; Rai Bular offered to adopt him (p. 66, folio 48).
  - The reconstructed narrative also refers to "the naked ascetics in the jungle of Chuhrkhana" and their old teacher's lesson on "the purity of the body" (p. 61, folio 43); Nanak appreciated "that unlike other ascetics these naked yogis did not beg for food" (p. 62, folio 44).
  - Travelogue: driving with Iqbal Qaiser in "the monsoon season" during "Ramzan", the author passes the market; Iqbal Qaiser gives its new name "Farooqabad" and historical name "Chuhrkhana", says "This is still a mandi", and puts it at "About forty-five kilometres, I think" from Nankana Sahib "in a straight line" (p. 70, folio 52).
  - Iqbal Qaiser's account of the renaming: in "the Zia era" a resident "Farooq Dogar" was elected Member of Parliament "when we had non-party based elections in the eighties", supported the military dictator, was "killed over a personal feud", and the town was renamed "from Chuhrkhana to Faroogabad [OCR?] [Farooqabad] in his honour" (p. 71, folio 53). He explains "Chuhr" as two bangles worn at the start and end of regular bangles, "a Sanskrit word which means to lead" (p. 71, folio 53).
  - The building as seen: "At a little distance from the main road, was the Gurdwara Sacha Sauda. It was a splendid structure, recently renovated", surrounded by "a protective wall covered with barbed wire on top" (p. 71, folio 53).
  - Iqbal Qaiser: "I saw this gurdwara for the first time in 1993 when I was researching my book", accompanied by "Ilyas Ghuman"; its condition was "miserable" — "Buffaloes were tied in the courtyard while drug addicts and gamblers had occupied the rest of the space" (p. 79, folio 61).
  - Iqbal Qaiser: a local man "referred to as Shah" ("I don't remember his real name") tried to preserve the gurdwara's sanctity and was beaten by the addicts and gamblers; Iqbal Qaiser and Ghuman took him to hospital, filed a police report, the issue reached "a few national newspapers", and then "the Department of Augaf [OCR?] [Auqaf]" came, "appointed him the official guard of this place", and "In subsequent years, the gurdwara was renovated" (p. 79, folio 61).
  - Iqbal Qaiser: it is now "one of the major shrines that is visited by pilgrim tourists who come to Nankana Sahib and Hassan Abdal" (p. 79, folio 61); he affirms ("Yes.") the author's suggestion that he "played a major part in the renovation of this gurdwara, like in Shaheed Ganj" (p. 79, folio 61).
  - At the visit: "two security men sitting inside the gate while a policeman sat next to them with his gun resting on his shoulder"; Iqbal Qaiser says they are "new boys" who do not recognise him (p. 79, folio 61).
  - A local Muslim storyteller at Gurdwara Sachkand (below) says the yogis Nanak fed were meditating "on top of a mound. The mound is where the big gurdwara stands" (p. 74, folio 56) — i.e. he places the feeding at the Sacha Sauda site.
  - **Gurdwara Sachkand, same town (place-level under Farooqabad):** "Let us go see Gurdwara Sachkand first" — reached by driving on past the Sacha Sauda boundary wall (p. 71, folio 53). A "white structure" with "'Allah' ... clumsily inscribed on it with charcoal"; Iqbal Qaiser: "Oh, this is a new development ... The gurdwara has become a shrine", not so on his last visit (p. 72, folio 54). Facing it is the grave of the Muslim saint "whose shrine this abandoned gurdwara was now on the way to becoming", made "of mud and covered with glittery shawls", with a brick boundary and "a few rooms which looked recently constructed" (p. 73, folio 55). The saint's name: "Pir Munawar" (p. 73, folio 55). A local man with a sickle: "This gurdwara is called Sachkand. It belongs to Guru Nanak" (p. 74, folio 56). Inside: "a picture of the Muslim fakir wearing a green shawl", "posters of Kaaba and Masjid Nabwi", and "other inscriptions on the walls professing the Muslim identity of this building" (p. 75, folio 57). Iqbal Qaiser: "Something needs to be done about this" (p. 75, folio 57).

- **gurudwara-janam-asthan-nankana-sahib**, **gurdwara-babay-de-ber**, **gurdwara-balila-sahib-bal-lila-sahib**, **gurdwara-chakki-sahib**, **gurdwara-choa-sahib**, **gurdwara-darbar-sahib-kartarpur**, **gurdwara-khoohi-bhai-lalo-bhai-lalo-di-khooi**, **gurdwara-malji-sahib**, **gurdwara-panja-sahib**, **gurdwara-patti-sahib**, **gurdwara-pehli-patshahi-jind-pir-sukkur**, **gurdwara-rori-sahib**, **gurdwara-sach-khand-sahib**, **gurdwara-sacha-sauda**, **gurdwara-sahib-saidpur-guru-nanak-dev-ji**, **gurdwara-sri-kiara-sahib**, **gurdwara-sri-tilganji-sahib**, **gurdwara-tambo-sahib** (Guru Nanak, general material not tied to a named site; all scenes below are the AUTHOR'S RECONSTRUCTION unless marked travelogue).
  - In the reconstructed scene, Nanak sits at dusk under "this waan tree", with "pieces of cloths tied as supplication" on its branches, "about two furlongs" from his home, hearing the azaan and temple bells and asking why "mosques and temples, Hindus and Muslims, have such differences" (p. 59, folio 41; p. 60, folio 42).
  - "Years later, Nanak composed a poem" on those emotions, quoted: "All the sounds we hear are but a part of the mighty roar of Thy torrent ... My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One." (p. 60, folio 42); footnoted to "AKhishwait Singh [OCR?], traits Hymns of the Gurus [OCR?] (New Delhi: Viking, 2003)" (p. 60, folio 42).
  - Reconstruction: Nanak had decided to incorporate any view he "could not counter ... with an argument" and shed any tradition for which he found no "rational reason"; hence he refused the sacred thread ("janeu") proposed by his father and "the local priest of Rai Bhoi di Talwindi" (p. 61, folio 43). The janeu poem is quoted, "From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment ... Blessed is the man, O Nanak, / Who wears such a thread around his neck" (p. 61, folio 43; p. 62, folio 44). His refusal "caused a furore" in the town and "Respectable Hindus started to avoid the Bedi" family (p. 62, folio 44).
  - Reconstruction: at "the mound outside of Talwindi" he questioned "Hindu yogis and Sufi dervishes"; his mentor was "Sayyed Hassan, a Muslim Sufi, who lived near his house", who condemned mendicants and argued "Islam teaches one to adopt the middle path" (p. 62, folio 44).
  - Reconstruction: Mehta Kalu calls the family "Bedis ... the ancient orators of the Vedas" and himself "an accountant"; Nanak had been a buffalo herder whose buffaloes once entered a field of "Raj Kumar" [OCR?] (p. 63, folio 45; p. 64, folio 46). Nanak was found by his father and "his employer and friend, Rai Bular" hiding "in the growe [OCR?] [grove] of wax [OCR?] [waan] trees outside town" (p. 64, folio 46).
  - Reconstruction: after the quarrel Nanak spent more time "at the abandoned mound on the outskirts of Talwindi" (p. 66, folio 48). Bebe Nanaki arranged his marriage to "Sulakhni", "the daughter of Moolchand Khatri from the village of Pakhoke near Gurdaspur" (p. 67, folio 49); "Soon after their marriage the young couple was blessed with a son who was named Sri Chand" (p. 67, folio 49).
  - Reconstruction: he went to Sultanpur, where his brother-in-law got him a job "with the Governor of Sultanpur as a manager of the Nawyh's [OCR?] [Nawab's] provision store"; a year after Sulakhni joined him "they were blessed with another son, whom they named Lachman Das" (p. 68, folio 50).
  - Travelogue: a Muslim storyteller at Gurdwara Sachkand calls him "a true devotee of God. He was a rightly guided soul"; asked how he knew the story: "Our elders told us. They used to live with the Sikhs before Pakistan was made" (p. 75, folio 57).
  - Travelogue: Iqbal Qaiser's wife took down "a poster of Nanak" he put up, "thinking that it would be a sin to have a picture of a Sikh saint in a Muslim household" (p. 69, folio not stated); the author is called "a Hindu or a Sikh" by family, students and friends "because of my fascination with Guru Nanak" (p. 69, folio not stated).

- **gurdwara-shaheed-ganj-singh-singhnian** (the book's "Gurdwara Shaheed Ganj", Naulakha Bazaar, Lahore) — all told by Iqbal Qaiser, printed as his first-person account.
  - Location: "No, this is not the walled city of Lahore. This [is] outside Delhi gate. This area is known as Naulakha Bazaar" (p. 76, folio 58); "Prince Dara ... shikob [OCR?] [Shikoh] had his Naulakha palace here" (p. 76, folio 58; p. 77, folio 59).
  - "an old issue that started about two centuries a v0 [OCR?] [ago]"; a vacant plot "where there was a small mosque" from which "the Qazi would announce punishment to criminals" (p. 76, folio 58; p. 77, folio 59).
  - "Thousands of them, men, women and children alike were slaughtered and thrown into a well here on the orders of the Governor of Lahore, Moin-ul-Mulk also known as Meer Manu"; a room was built to hold prisoners before execution (p. 77, folio 59).
  - "The mosque was small and was never used as a place of worship"; under Maharaja Ranjit Singh the Sikhs "placed the Guru Granth Sahib inside it, converting it into a gurdwara" (p. 77, folio 59).
  - British era: Muslims applied in court for its return; "The Lahore High Court" asked for a living witness who had seen it used as a mosque, found none, and "decided in favour of the Sikhs"; the Sikhs then razed the building, leading to "rioting between Muslims and Sikhs", which Iqbal Qaiser blames on British "divide and rule"; "Muhammad Ali Jinnah and Allama Igbal [OCR?] [Iqbal] got involved"; he has seen a photograph of Jinnah addressing a crowd "at the Badshahi Mosque at the time of this riot"; "the gurdwara remained a gurdwara" (p. 77, folio 59).
  - After Partition, with "the Augaf [OCR?] [Auqaf] Department" created, the issue was raised again; the Lahore High Court decided it "would remain a gurdwara" — "Around 1957-58" (p. 77, folio 59; p. 78, folio 60).
  - "again in the late 1980s" petitioners asked for review; the judge would convert it if they produced "even one person who had prayed at the mosque"; none was produced (p. 78, folio 60).
  - "In about 1994-95, the Kar Sewa Committee of England" sought permission to renovate; the government eventually agreed; "Avtar Singh Panghedha" of the Committee asked Iqbal Qaiser to supervise construction, and "all the money, the contracts ... went through me" (p. 78, folio 60).
  - During construction "skeletons of children and adults would emerge", which he identifies as the Sikhs killed on Meer Manu's orders (p. 78, folio 60).
  - When newspapers reported the reconstruction, "the Muslims tried once again to create an issue"; an ironsmith named "Rana" persuaded local traders the gurdwara "would attract foreign pilgrims", and the traders of Naulakha Bazaar became stakeholders; "this gurdwara was eventually constructed in 2004" (p. 78, folio 60).
  - Afterwards "the Government of Pakistan woke up to the potential of Sikh pilgrimage tourism" and invited the Sikh community abroad "to invest in the renovation of any Sikh gurdwara in the country" (p. 79, folio 61).
  - The author and Iqbal Qaiser raise it as the feared outcome at Sachkand: "we could have a situation similar to Gurdwara Shaheed Ganj here?" — "Precisely." (p. 76, folio 58).

## Practices, institutions, economy
- Cloths "tied as supplication" on the branches of the waan tree (reconstruction, p. 59, folio 41).
- Custodianship and state: Auqaf appointed a local man as official guard of Sacha Sauda after press coverage, and it was later renovated (p. 79, folio 61); armed police and security at the Sacha Sauda gate (p. 79, folio 61); Shaheed Ganj's post-Partition dispute arose when the Auqaf Department was created (p. 77, folio 59).
- Sikh diaspora funding: Kar Sewa Committee of England renovation of Shaheed Ganj, c. "1994-95", built "2004"; Government of Pakistan then invited overseas Sikhs to invest in renovating "any Sikh gurdwara" (p. 78, folio 60; p. 79, folio 61).
- Local economy as argument: the ironsmith Rana argued the gurdwara "would attract foreign pilgrims and it would be beneficial for everyone" (p. 78, folio 60).
- Conversion of a gurdwara to a Muslim shrine: Sachkand has a Muslim grave, new rooms, Muslim posters and inscriptions inside (pp. 72–75, folios 54–57); men "serving the shrine of the Muslim saint" sit on a mat behind it (p. 73, folio 55); a visitor says Sheikh Saad "smokes up here" (p. 72, folio 54); cigarettes offered to all during Ramzan, "This was the shrine of a dervish, and dervishes are generally considered to be outside the orthodoxy of religiosity" (p. 75, folio 57).
- Syed status: "The Syed caste in South Asia derives their ancestry from Prophet Muhammad {PBUH} and thus enjoy a unique privilege in Islamic spirituality" (author's gloss, p. 73, folio 55).
- Oral transmission: local Muslims' Nanak story came from elders who "used to live with the Sikhs before Pakistan was made" (p. 75, folio 57).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)
- Footnoted legend ("It is believed"): a trader told Nanak his mules carried "Just some mud"; his "sugar and gur (jaggery) had turned into mud"; when he returned and repented, "his mud became Sugar and gur once again" (p. 71, folio 53).
- Local storyteller at Sachkand: Nanak loaded his mules with mud from the ground at the Sachkand spot; at Nankana his sister found "the mud had actually been changed into sugar and gur" (p. 75, folio 57).
- Pir Munawar stood in the canal "for twelve years and used to Cat [OCR?] [eat] rocks", per Furqan (p. 74, folio 56).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)
- The author: "The story of Sachkand and Sacha Sauda seem to merge with each other. The former, it seems, is an extension of the latter. Even though the Janamsakhis relate them as two different incidents"; "it is impossible to determine the historicity of either event" (p. 72, folio 54).
- The author: the storyteller's version "was different from the one that is recorded in the Janamsakhis", but historical accuracy "was not a concern" (p. 75, folio 57).
- The author: Pir Munawar's asceticism "was a continuation of a tradition" whose practitioners "were also yogis and santrics [OCR?] [tantrics] (follower[s] of Tantrism) from the Hindu tradition" (p. 74, folio 56).
- Debate on Sachkand's conversion: the author thinks it "really interesting" and that sanctity is preserved "if not as a Sikh shrine then at least as a Muslim shrine"; Iqbal Qaiser fears that "Ten years down the road" the pir's followers "would claim that there was no gurdwara here", as at Shaheed Ganj (p. 76, folio 58).
- Iqbal Qaiser's explanation of differing Muslim attitudes (pre-Partition locals vs. post-Partition migrants in Nankana Sahib) (p. 76, folio 58).
- The author on his own position: "In a country where everyone is researching Islam, it seems there is no space to do any research outside the fold of Islam" (p. 69, folio not stated).

**Internal contradictions.**
- Where Nanak was found after the Sacha Sauda episode: the author's reconstruction has him hiding "in the growe of wax trees outside town" near home (p. 64, folio 46; the waan tree "about two furlongs" from home, p. 59, folio 41); the Sachkand storyteller has him found "sitting in the jungle at this spot", i.e. Sachkand, near Chuhrkhana (p. 74, folio 56; p. 75, folio 57).
- Where the ascetics were: "a jungle on the way" to Chuharkhana (reconstruction, p. 65) vs. "on top of a mound ... where the big gurdwara stands" in the mandi (storyteller, p. 74, folio 56).
- The mud/sugar legend differs: footnote has a trader's sugar turning to mud and back (p. 71, folio 53); storyteller has Nanak's mud turning to sugar (p. 75, folio 57); the reconstruction has no miracle.
- p. 76, folio 58: Iqbal Qaiser's line "The Muslims in this village were responsible for all those atrocities on Hindus and Sikhs" sits oddly after he praises the same local Muslims' relationship with Sikhs; quoted as printed, possibly OCR-damaged or meant ironically — not interpreted.
- p. 69 opens mid-quotation ("living in a sister's house and a husband living in the house of his wife is the same as a dog") after p. 68 ends on Lachman Das; the start of the quotation and the dialogue's context appear lost in OCR at the foot of p. 68.
