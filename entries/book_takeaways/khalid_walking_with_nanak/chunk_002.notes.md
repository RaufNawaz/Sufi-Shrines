## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **gurdwara-babay-nanki** (the book's "Gurdwara Janamasthan Bebe Nanaki"; reached on "the Bedian road", apparently the "Gurdwara Dera Chahal" of p. 43):
  - Ghulab Singh, "a Pakistani Sikh who happens to be the first Sikh police officer in the revamped traffic police of Lahore", filed a case against ETPB in the Lahore High Court "for the alleged sale of agricultural land associated with Gurdwara Janamasthan Bebe Nanaki"; a few days before the author's visit the Supreme Court of Pakistan "took cognisance of the case" and asked the construction companies "to pay Rupees 986 million as surety while the hearings proceeded" (p. 35, folio 17).
  - Ghulab Singh stays at this gurdwara when he is in Lahore; his official bike was parked in the courtyard (p. 35, folio 17).
  - A young priest, Daleep Singh, led the author and Iqbal Qaiser inside; Iqbal Qaiser said the area "used to be the langar hall"; "Now it is a lawn with a few rooms around it" (p. 35, folio 17).
  - Daleep alleged documents prove ETPB corruption and that "Asif Hashmi (former head of the ETPB) has earned billions in his tenure of five years"; the author adds that "In April 2016 Asif Hashmi was arrested in Dubai" (p. 35, folio 17).
  - A mosque's loudspeaker sermon drowned the conversation; "Behind the once-sacred pool now occupied by buffaloes is the agricultural land in dispute"; a new apartment block was visible from the window (p. 36, folio 18).
  - Inside, Daleep bows before the Guru Granth Sahib "placed in a wooden Thara Sahib"; the author and Iqbal Qaiser do not bow (p. 37, folio 19).
  - Asked whether the gurdwara stands "on the exact spot where Nanak's maternal family home used to be", Iqbal Qaiser said "Yes"; the gurdwara "was one big hall with a small [OCR? "omall"] balcony opening towards the centre of the hall from the first floor" (p. 38, folio 20).
  - Iqbal Qaiser says Bhai Bannu, sent by Guru Arjan to Lahore to bind the Adi Granth, "came and stayed at this gurdwara" and there prepared another copy "without the Guru's permission", with additional verses the Guru rejected; "one of the movements also started from this particular gurdwara" (pp. 39, folio 21).
  - The party spent "about two hours here"; the visit was in "the month of June", having left at six in the morning (p. 40, folio 22).

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)
- **Gurdwara Rori Sahib, Jhaman** — border village on "a small mound", reached by turning left at the BRB canal (p. 45, folio 27); gurdwara "built in memory of Guru Nanak's visit"; named Rori because of pottery shards; the British found it "an ancient archaeological mound", "never excavated, as a splendid gurdwara had been built on the spot" (p. 48, folio 30). A local called it "Gurdwara Sahib", which surprised the author (p. 47, folio 29). State: "a picture of neglect", square small-brick structure with white dome, crumbled wall, bricks robbed, a bunker beside it, behind it "the last embankment on the Pakistan side" (p. 48, folio 30). The border is "not even a kilometre" away (p. 51, folio 33).
- **Ghavindi** — shrine in a grove of lahura (desert teak) trees, "facing India", commemorating "the spot Nanak and Mardana had sat in" (p. 53, folio 35); "a lone white structure with a dome" with the two plinths; a Rangers bunker opposite and a mound with remains of other buildings (p. 54, folio 36). A second gurdwara inside the village: only a thin-brick wall remains; building belongs to the "Department of Union Council", divided into houses; "All traces of the gurdwara had been erased" (p. 55, folio 37). Village "once dominated by Hindus and Sikhs" (p. 54, folio 36).
- **Dera Chahal** — on the Bedian road at the edge of Lahore (p. 43, folio 25, OCR damaged); Meherban's claimed birthplace of Nanak (p. 43, folio 25).
- **Peepali Pir** — "a Muslim shrine not far from here"; its guardians told Daleep it was as old as the gurdwara and their elders said Nanak visited it; they had no relic (p. 37, folio 19).
- **Prithi Chand's samadhi and shrine, Heir** — "hollow structure built of thin bricks" on a vacant lot behind a government school; "meant to rival the religious and political significance of the Golden Temple" (p. 43, folio 25); its pool "has been filled" (p. 39, folio 21); Prithi Chand's in-laws were from Heir (p. 39, folio 21). A Shahjahan baradari at Heir "has been converted into a madrassa" (pp. 38–39, folio 21).
- **Meherban's shrine** — "a huge shrine on the outskirts of Chunian" (p. 43, folio 25).
- **Bhai Bannu's gurdwara, Mandi Mangat** — abandoned, "elaborate frescoes", taken over by "an influential landlord" as his dera; the unsanctioned Granth was kept there "till Partition, after which it was taken to India" (p. 39, folio 21).
- **Bedian** — founded by Baba Sahib Singh Bedi; Bedis migrated at Partition, replaced by "Mewatis and Arains from Amritsar"; a Christian settlement of former Mazhabi Sikhs (p. 44, folio 26; p. 45, folio 27); "two gurdwaras inside the village" and samadhis of prominent Bedis (p. 45, folio 27), now destroyed/razed (pp. 45–46).
- **Sultanpur / Sultanpur Lodhi** — now in India, "a hundred-odd kilometres from this gurdwara" (p. 58, folio 40).
- Others named: Golden Temple, Amritsar (p. 38, folio 20); Guru Angad Dev (p. 38, folio 20); Guru Harkrishan (p. 51, folio 33); Nawab Daulat Khan Lodbi [OCR?] [Lodhi] (p. 55, folio 37); Rumi and Shams Tabriz (p. 42, folio 24); Bhagat Singh and the Naujawan Bharat Sabha office at Jhaman (p. 48, folio 30); Iqbal Qaiser, author of "Hastorscal [OCR?] [Historical] Shrines of Pakistan" (p. 41, folio 23).

## Practices, institutions, economy
- ETPB control of properties "amounting to thousands of acres all across the country" (p. 35, folio 17).
- PSGPC "formed in 1999 to look after the Sikh gurdwaras of Pakistan"; before it the SGPC in Amritsar "used to collect offerings from these gurdwaras in Pakistan and take them back to India" (p. 36, folio 18); interviewees called PSGPC members "cronies of the government"; alleged that some members "are not Sikhs but Muslims pretending to be Sikhs" (p. 36, folio 18).
- Gurdwara Act of 1925: per Daleep only three-generation Sikhs may run gurdwaras; the author: it brought gurdwaras "under a Sikh body, which was before that controlled by members of the Udasi" (p. 36, folio 18).
- Jhaman: Iqbal Qaiser says in "1993" the Pakistan Rangers "had set up a base inside this building", with the Pakistan flag on top; he was allowed one photograph from one angle (pp. 48–49, folios 30–31). Holes dug where the Thara Sahib stood, "in the search for buried treasure" after Partition; Islamic graffiti in "neat calligraphic style", which the author guesses was done when it served as a Rangers office (p. 50, folio 32). Frescoes: faces "wiped out while the bodies of the figures had been left unharmed" (p. 50, folio 32). Undercover intelligence personnel posing as herdsmen (pp. 51–52).
- Ghavindi: "packets of salt" on the plinths and "straws of sweep" on the floor; Iqbal Qaiser: salt "for a special prayer", sweep straw "to get rid of black magic", meaning "the locals still come here" (p. 54, folio 36); a well seen "in 2008" had gone — "In just five years it has disappeared" (p. 54, folio 36).
- Border: 1965-war embankments with a canal once carrying electric current, "three such embankments" (p. 47, folio 29); army blocks road-building in border areas (p. 46, folio 28).
- Caste: Mazhabi Sikhs (converted untouchables); Bedis considered "special people" by lineage from Guru Nanak (p. 44, folio 26).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)
- Nanak made a rubab for Mardana from the branches of the tree at Jhaman ("It is believed") (p. 48, folio 30).
- Ghavindi (Iqbal Qaiser's narration): Mardana, told not to beg, returned hungry from a family celebrating a son's birth; the child "died by an act of God"; the family sought Nanak's forgiveness (p. 56, folio 38).
- Peepali Pir guardians' claim that Nanak visited their shrine (p. 37, folio 19).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)
- The author holds Nanak was "influenced immensely by Islam", the Islamic culture of the time being "at its zenith" (p. 37, folio 19); similar view on the middle course (p. 53, folio 35).
- The author values folklore over verification: a Muslim shrine claiming Nanak's visit has significance "irrespective of historical veracity [OCR? "vera, ity"]" given Partition (pp. 37–38).
- The author imagines that had Nanak rested "a few kilometres further ahead", Jhaman would be a thriving pilgrimage town (p. 51, folio 33).
- Author: Zia-ul-Haq's Islamisation ("Zia-ul-Hagq" [OCR?] [Zia-ul-Haq]) made army officers "more ardent Muslims" (p. 50, folio 32).
