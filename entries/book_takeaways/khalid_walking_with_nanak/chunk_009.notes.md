  - Legend (set-off italic narrative, attributed as legend by the author): in Sialkot, the Sufi Hamza Ghous threatened to destroy the city after forty days of prayer; Nanak, settled under the berry tree, knocked on his door for several days; thunder struck the dome, Hamza Ghous gave up the curse, "Baba Guru Nanak saved the people of Sialkot from annibilation [OCR?] [annihilation]" (pp. 197–198, folios 171–172).
  - Legend of Pasrur, "similar to that of Sialkot": Nanak humbles "an arrogant Muslim Sufi by the name of Mian Mitha"; the author states "This discourse is recorded in the Guru Granth Sahib" (p. 200, folio 174). In the narrative, Mian Mitha sends a messenger threatening to "churn him"; Nanak replies he will squeeze him "as one squeezes a lemon"; Mian Mitha comes barefoot, questions Nanak on hell, heaven and punishments, Nanak answers "in Islamic iconography", and Mian Mitha touches Nanak's feet, kisses them [OCR? "hissed"] and becomes his devotee (pp. 200–201, folios 174–175). Verses quoted ("As sesame is heated and pressed…"; "The present are favoured; the absent are not…"), footnoted to Max Arthur MacAuliffe [OCR? "MacAulif Te"], *The Sikh Religion*, 1907, Internet Archive (pp. 201–202, folios 175–176).
  - Author's judgement: stories of Nanak humbling Muslim and Hindu ascetics are "pointless stories"; "His genius lies in the poetic use of words and not in miracles" (p. 199, folio 173). Iqbal Qaiser's explanation: "That is to show that our saint is more powerful than yours" (p. 199, folio 173).
  - In the author's reconstructed scene outside Deepalpur, Nanak refuses Mardana's suggestion to join a temple bhajan: "I would never go into to the city… If it was located in some wilderness, we could have spent the night there" (p. 207, folio 181).
  - In the author's reconstructed scene, Nanak longs for his mother and sister as they head home "after [his] journey to the East and South"; he narrates the poetry of Bhagat Kabir and Baba Farid and talks at night to "the imaginary Bhagat Kabir and Farid"; they had been away from home "for thirteen years now" (p. 208, folio 182).
  - In the author's reconstructed scene, Nanak and Mardana stay two days with Nuri, a leper abandoned by his sons, Nanak cooking and serving; evenings they sing "dirtan [OCR?] [kirtan] (devotional songs)"; then they leave "for Pakpattan to meet his friend Sheikh Ibrahim"; Nuri sings Nanak's poems and after his death "he was buried in his hut" (sentence cut off) (pp. 208–209, folios 182–183).
  - A poem of Nanak quoted by Ram Rai at Aurangzeb's court (OCR largely destroyed); the author reads it as implying disfigurement of the grave, "The clay of a Muslim's grave… Falls into the hands" of one "who makes pots and bricks", which "could be viewed" as offensive to Muslims (pp. 212, folio 186).
  - In the Clifton temple episode, Vijesh reasons the temple "had to be at least five hundred years old because Guru Nanak" is said to have visited it (garbled) (p. 216, folio 190).

- **gurdwara-babay-de-ber** (the Sialkot gurdwara at the berry tree, now "a shrine to Baba Bair"):
  - After Partition the gurdwara was abandoned but the place, "particularly the tree", remained sacred through its association with Guru Nanak; eventually a grave "of this non-existent saint was constructed"; Iqbal Qaiser said that when he last came here for his book "there was no such grave" (p. 196, folio 170).
  - The author's view: perhaps the tree was sacred before Nanak came, Nanak becoming one of the reasons; "Now this gurdwara has become a shrine to Baba Bair with the red flag at the entrance as his symbol" (p. 196, folio 170).
  - Graffiti "'Ya Allah' and 'Ya Muhammad'" on a wall; some Gurmukhi text wiped clean and the slogans "scribbled with a piece of coal"; "the building had been Islamised" (p. 196, folio 170).
  - A "newly built mosque next [to] the shrine" (p. 196, folio 170).
  - The shrine of Hamza Ghous lies in an old graveyard not far from "the berry tree under which Nanak sat" (p. 199, folio 173).

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Mian Mitha** (Pasrur): Muslim Sufi of the Pasrur legend (pp. 200–201, folios 174–175).
- **Pasrur gurdwara/garden**: "Hardly anything of the original gurdwara had been preserved. There was just the floor and towards one end was a niche"; a jamun tree beside it; a sacred pool attached; Sunday evening garden full of boys and girls playing; old trees that Iqbal Qaiser says are mentioned in *Tarikh Guru Khalsa* by Bhai "Se sagh" [OCR?], written "in the nineteenth century" (partly garbled); "This used to be a beautiful garden" (pp. 202–203, folios 176–177).
- **Gurdwara of Guru Nanak, "famously known as Chota Nankana"** (Deepalpur): at the edge of a mound "about twenty metres deep"; next to a pool of dirty water, once "the moat around the fort of Deepalpur", with buffaloes swimming (p. 204, folio 178); now inside a house; an old woman "in her early seventies" from Hissar moved in after Partition; white structure, borders with blue patterns and white animal/floral motifs, double storied; ground-floor rooms converted to residences, upper floor razed "leaving only the facade"; "Nothing of the original shrine remained on the inside"; she recalls a mud grave behind the building that the family tended with a cloth, her father planted a tree beside it, "over time the grave has vanished" (p. 206, folio 180); the author and Qaiser left "disappointed"; Qaiser: "There has to be a Muslim…" (garbled) (p. 207, folio 181).
- **Temple of Deepal** (Deepalpur): "the ancient Hindu temple of this city after whose namesake the city was named"; legend of the sadhu Deepal who, cursed by his mother, buried himself until only a lock of hair remained, which turned to stone (pp. 204–205, folios 178–179); now home of Usman's "migrant family from Hissar"; a "broad double storey house" in wood and brick, rundown; facing it a larger double-storeyed pilgrims' residence (caravanserai) for the annual festival, donors' names inscribed in Devanagari; vacant lot where the festival was held; abandoned well; the stone lock kept "in our living room", covered with a utensil; "Sometimes Hindus and Sikhs come… from India… They offer their prayers… Some even bow down to it" (p. 205, folio 179).
- **Deepalpur**: during the thirteenth century, "during the Delhi Sultanate", it briefly served as the capital of Punjab (garbled) (p. 205, folio 179); the party left by "the southern gate of the city" (p. 206, folio 180).
- **Nuri the leper**: figure of the author's reconstructed scene, hut in the jungle near the city (pp. 208–209, folios 182–183).
- **Sheikh Ibrahim**: Nanak's "friend" at Pakpattan (p. 209, folio 183).
- **Guru Har Rai, Dara Shikoh, Aurangzeb, Emperor Shahjahan, Ram Rai, Harkrishan**: Dara stopped at Goindwal, "where the fort of Guru Har Rai had been constructed only a few years ago" (stable, tank, warriors' residences); Har Rai escorted by armed guard; Har Rai's medicine cured the ill Dara; Dara "fifteen years older" (garbled); versions of the Guru blocking Aurangzeb's forces at the Beas, "rubbished by other claims"; Har Rai returned "to Kartarpur" (p. 211, folio 185); Aurangzeb summoned Har Rai to Delhi, who sent his son Ram Rai; Ram Rai altered Nanak's verse to avoid offending Muslims; Har Rai disowned him, "appointed Harkrishan as the guru", and ruled any Sikh associating with Ram Rai would be excommunicated (pp. 210–213, folios 184–187). ## Practices, institutions, economy

- Islamisation of an abandoned gurdwara: new grave, red flag, graffiti over Gurmukhi, adjacent new mosque (p. 196, folio 170).
- Conversion of shrines to other uses: a gurdwara as an institute for the blind (p. 196, folio 170); the Chota Nankana gurdwara and Deepal temple as family homes of post-Partition migrants from Hissar (pp. 205–206, folios 179–180).
- Auqaf [OCR? "Augaf"] lock on the dilapidated Hamza Ghous shrine (p. 199, folio 173).
- Visiting Hindus and Sikhs "from India" pray and bow before the Deepal stone lock of hair (p. 205, folio 179).
- Annual festival at the Deepal temple, donor inscriptions in Devanagari, pilgrims' residence (p. 205, folio 179).
- Clifton temple: sweets and flowers sold as offerings; sweets fed to ants; access controlled by a pandit and an administrator, Raj Kumar (pp. 216–217, folios 190–191).
- Architecture as dating evidence: Hamza Ghous shrine's oval/long dome "pre-Mughal", resembling Delhi Sultanate buildings; hole on top of domes in older shrines "a technique adopte[d] to facilitate the return of the soul to God" (p. 198, folio 172).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- Nanak humbles Hamza Ghous at Sialkot; thunder strikes the dome (legend, pp. 197–198, folios 171–172). The author thinks the hole in the dome is where "the legend of thunder striking the building probably developed" (p. 198, folio 172).
- Nanak humbles Mian Mitha at Pasrur (legend, pp. 200–201, folios 174–175).
- Deepal's lock of hair turned to stone (legend, p. 205, folio 179).
- The curse of Varuna drowning ships that pass without tribute (sailors' story in the author's narrative, pp. 214–215, folios 188–189).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- The berry-tree site: "the truth lies somewhere in the middle of these two stories" — the tree may have been sacred before Nanak (p. 196, folio 170).
- Devotees of the Baba Bair shrine "were subconsciously carrying forward the tradition of Guru Nanak… on the other hand, they were determine[d] to completely erase the legacy of this place" (p. 196, folio 170).
- From the architecture the author "had no doubt that Nanak and Hamza Ghous could not have been contemporaries" (p. 198, folio 172).
- Nanak's greatness lies in poetry, not miracles (p. 199, folio 173).
- The author holds, against the pandit, that the Clifton temple is the one Nanak visited in Karachi, relying on Iqbal Qaiser (p. 217, folio 191).
