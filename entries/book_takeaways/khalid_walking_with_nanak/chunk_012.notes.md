# khalid_walking_with_nanak — chunk 012 — pp. 260–279

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **gurdwara-panja-sahib** (the book's "Panja Sahib", "Gurdwara of Panja Sahib", at "Hassan Abdal"):
  - The author tells the rock's origin as a novelised scene (chapter "The Legend"). On "the eve of the Sikh invasion", with news of "Hari Singh Nalwa's approach", the residents of Hassan Abdal leave for "the town of Attock". Only the fakir "Naju" stays, singing, and the Sikh forces find him "sitting at the shrine" (p. 260, folio 234).
  - In the scene, a month before the attack Naju had seen "a Muslim mason called Kamma" carve the outline of his own hand into a rock. Kamma was childless, and the print was "like a signature that he was leaving for posterity" (pp. 262–263, folios 236–237).
  - In the scene, Kamma "had been responsible for creating numerous carvings in the temples of Hassan Abdal". His grandfather had converted to Islam "To avoid the jaziya tax that the Mughal Emperor Aurangzeb had reinitiated" (p. 262, folio 236).
  - In the scene, Naju gives the rock to Hari Singh as bearing "the hand print of your Guru Nanak". He says Nanak "came to Hassan Abdal and spent a few days here", as "recorded in the Janamsakhis of Bhai Bala" (p. 263, folio 237).
  - In the scene, Hari Singh's Granthi confirms that "on the 1 [OCR?] [1st] of Vaisakh, Nanak had visited Hassan Abdal as is recorded in the Janamsakhis of Bala" (p. 263, folio 237).
  - In the scene, local tradition held that Nanak stayed a few days under "a peepal tree on the outskirts of the city" (p. 264, folio 238).
  - In the scene, Hari Singh places his hand on the mark ("See how the hand of Nanak fits my hand perfectly"), kisses the rock and places gold coins on it. "Even while it was at the camp, the rock turned into a shrine": "Muslims, Hindus and Sikhs" make offerings, kirtan singers sing Nanak's compositions, and prayers go on into the night (p. 264, folio 238).
  - In the scene, the next morning Naju takes Hari Singh to the peepal tree outside the city, with a mountain facing it and a stream beside it. From there Hari Singh could see "gardens that had been constructed by Emperor Jahangir" (pp. 264–265, folios 238–239).
  - In the scene, Hari Singh orders "the rock to be placed there, under the tree, and for a shrine to be [built] to commemorate the spot where Nanak and Mardana had rested" (p. 265, folio 239).
  - The author (travelogue): "The Gurdwara of Panja Sahib is i[n] [OCR?] the plain below us" the mountain of Wali Qandhari. At "Baisakhi", when thousands "descend upon this shrine, several also travel up to this Muslim shrine" (p. 267, folio 241).
  - The festival calendar at the Wali Qandhari shrine records "Baisakh[i]" [OCR?], which "coincides with the [festival] at the shrine of Guru Nanak at the bottom of the mountain" (p. 269, folio 243; OCR heavily damaged).
  - Iqbal Qaiser, looking down at the "grand Gurdwara of Panja Sahib", asks: "if a rock was thrown from here, how could it possibly reach" it? He also asks why Nanak "need[ed] to stop [it with his] hand" (p. 269, folio 243; OCR damaged).
  - The standard legend, told in reply to Anam (speaker partly lost in OCR): Nanak "arrived here along with Mardana". The festival "is celebrated at the Gurdw[ara] Panja Sahib" where pilgrims gather, and they sat "under a peepal tre[e]" where the "gurdwara now stands" (p. 269, folio 243; OCR badly damaged).
  - Continuing the legend: Mardana climbs three times to ask the hermit Wali Qandhari for water and is refused. Nanak "removed a stone from the ground and water started to gush out". The Wali's water disappears, and he "hurled a rock at Nanak and Mardana, which Nanak stopped with his hand. The print of that hand remained on the rock" (pp. 269–270, folios 243–244).
  - The same speaker cites "Salman Rashid's book The Salt Range and the Potohar Plateau". Rashid argues "this pool of water next to the gurdwara" is thousands of years old and was "considered sacred even in the Buddhist era" (p. 270, folio 244).
  - The same speaker says "The Chinese traveller Hiuen Tsang, in the 7th century CE" reportedly observed it as "a sacred lake", and that people believed a dip would cure diseases (p. 270, folio 244).
  - Also from the same speaker: "After a gurdwara was built here during the tenure 4 [OCR?] [of] Maharaja Ranjit Singh", the pool "remained sacred but wa[s] given a different iconography", based on Nanak's miracles (p. 270, folio 244).
  - Author's visit: a pilgrim "in his sixties" in a "saffron headscarf" placed his hand on the mark. The author writes "his hand too fit the hand [of] Nanak perfectly. Miracle!" The pilgrim then placed "a ten rupee note" on the rock, kissed it, poured water from the pool on it and cleaned it (p. 271, folio 245).
  - Author's visit: a queue of devotees; "mysterious hands emerged from the windows [fl]anking the rock"; women "placed money o[n] top of the rock" (p. 271, folio 245).
  - The author placed his hand on the print, "at that moment genuinely believing". He also writes: "I knew this was a fictitious story dreamed up by a man called Naju the fakir to save his life, while the handprint [was chiselled] [OCR?] by a man called Kamma" (p. 271, folio 245).
  - "The gurdwara that was [built in the] nineteenth century was behind me" (words lost in OCR). It was "Baisakhi and the festival was only beginning", with Sikh pilgrims "all here to commemorate [the] formation of the Khalsa" (p. 271, folio 245).

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)
- **Naju the fakir**, Hassan Abdal, in the author's reconstructed scene:
  - An orphan who joined "a group of fakirs singing and dancing … the songs of Sufi saints". As an adult he walked "the narrow stree[ts] of Hassan Abdal" carrying an "ed/ara [OCR?]", glossed as "(A one-string Musical instrument)" (p. 260, folio 234).
  - Facing Hari Singh, he realises "His spirituality was a garb only for the outside" (p. 261, folio 235).
  - In Kamma's story he is a regular visitor to Kamma's shop and one of the holy men who receive Kamma's Thursday gifts of food (p. 262, folio 236).
- **Kamma**, Muslim mason, Hassan Abdal, in the author's scene: from a family of masons that "had been a Hindu family earlier", and "proud of his heritage". Barred as a Muslim from visiting Hindu temples, "he felt that he was present there through his structures". Every Thursday he visited the city's Sufi shrines, distributing sweetmeats and food (p. 262, folio 236).
- **Hari Singh Nalwa**, in the author's scene: Sikh commander. He is warned "He is no friend to Muslims" (p. 260, folio 234). His tent has "all the luxuries of [a] palace" (p. 261, folio 235). He wanted "to set a[n] [e]xample at the beginning of his invasion of the Khyber" (p. 262, folio 236), and he calls the Mughals "those brute Turks" (p. 264, folio 238).
- **Attock**: the town where Hassan Abdal's residents fled (p. 260, folio 234).
- **Shrine of Wali Qandhari**, on the mountain above Panja Sahib, Hassan Abdal:
  - The climb goes up steep stairs through a housing settlement. A stall sold cloth with Quranic inscriptions and items associated "with Shia Islam" (p. 265, folio 239).
  - Asked whether the shrine was Shia or Sunni, Iqbal Qaiser answered "I don't know". The author: "The distinction between Shia and Sunni did not really apply in the conventional sense at such Sufi shrines" [OCR?] (pp. 265–266, folios 239–240).
  - Most fellow pilgrims were school and college students "bunking classes" [OCR?], playing devotional music on their mobile phones (p. 266, folio 240).
  - Some descending pilgrims carried "their slippers in their hands" to make the pilgrimage harder (p. 266, folio 240).
  - Iqbal Qaiser: "They've made the path much easier now". The path used to go around the mountain, and pilgrims had to "lie down and crawl up under a huge rock" [OCR?] (p. 267, folio 241).
  - Halfway up there are shops and restaurants. Devotees tie coloured cloths on trees "as supplication", and "'Allah O Akbar' was written on several barren rocks on the way" (p. 267, folio 241).
  - The shrine is "modest", entered by a wooden [frame] and surrounded by a protective wall with shops. The climb took "about ninety ia [OCR?] [minutes]" (p. 268, folio 242).
  - An old man takes pilgrims' shoes and charges money to mind them. A dervish sells water from earthen pots (p. 268, folio 242).
  - Dervishes smoke hashish behind a grove of trees. Inside is the saint's grave, with "a green box … placed there by the Augaf [OCR?] [Auqaf] Department" for offerings (p. 268, folio 242).
  - The festival dates are posted on a wall (p. 268–269, folios 242–243).
  - In the Panja Sahib legend, Wali Qandhari is the hermit who "felt jealous of Guru Nanak" (p. 270, folio 244).
- **Salman Rashid**, *The Salt Range and the Potohar Plateau*: cited on the antiquity of the pool (p. 270, folio 244).

## Practices, institutions, economy
- Baisakhi at Panja Sahib: thousands descend on the gurdwara, and some also climb to the Wali Qandhari shrine (p. 267, folio 241). The author's visit coincided with Baisakhi and pilgrims commemorating "the formation of the Khalsa" (p. 271, folio 245).
- Offerings at the Panja Sahib rock: gold coins in the scene (p. 264, folio 238). Today devotees leave a ten rupee note and money from women behind the windows, and wash the rock with pool water (p. 271, folio 245).
- Water-carrying as a religious duty: before pumps, devotees carried water pots up to mountain shrines, and the water is "considered blessed". "In these days of pumps, the dervish here sold the water back to the pilgrims in the name of religious duty" (p. 268, folio 242).
- The Auqaf Department's green offering box at the Wali Qandhari shrine (p. 268, folio 242). A green box at the Uch Sharif shrine, with money said to go to the caretakers, "helping them expand their houses and buy bigger cars" (p. 279, folio 253).
- Custodianship at Uch Sharif: the "Gilanis and the Makhdooms" live in "massive havelis". The saintly family "dominate this city, economically, politically and socially, feeding off the charity of devotees" (pp. 276, 279, folios 250, 253).
- Security at the Uch urs: a metal-detector gate and a policeman (p. 278, folio 252). Attacks named on Data Ganj Baksh, Baba Farid's at Pakpattan and Abdullah Shah Ghazi's (p. 278, folio 252).
- The Department of Archaeology's board at the Moosa Pak Shaheed structure (p. 278, folio 252).
- The Masand system was introduced to fund the Amrit Sar tank (p. 274, folio 248). Langar at the Guru's darbar at Khadur (p. 272, folio 246).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)
- The Panja Sahib handprint, told two ways:
  - The Sikh legend, as told in the chunk: Nanak stops Wali Qandhari's rock with his hand after bringing out water (pp. 269–270, folios 243–244).
  - The author's counter-narrative: the print was carved by Kamma and "sold" to Hari Singh Nalwa by Naju to save his life (pp. 262–264, 271, folios 236–238, 245).
- "As is the specialty of this rock", every pilgrim's hand fits the print, which the author reports ironically: "Miracle!" (p. 271, folio 245). In the scene, Hari Singh says the same of his own hand (p. 264, folio 238).
- Naju hopes for "a miracle of Nanak" to escape death (p. 262, folio 236).
- Bibi Bhani's foot under the stool, in "the Sikh tradition" (p. 273, folio not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)
- The author judges the Panja Sahib rock story "fictitious". Of his own belief at the rock he writes: "such is the power of imagination [in] religion that I forgot all my rationality" (p. 271, folio 245).
- He says the handprint legend is "yet another story in which Nanak is depicted as competing with a Muslim saint", driving home "that 'our' saint is stronger than 'your' saint". This is the speaker of the legend passage (p. 270, folio 244).
- The pre-Sikh sacredness of the pool is re-iconised under Ranjit Singh. "One also sees this happening in several Muslim shrines where Hindu temples have been converted into Muslim shrines after Partition" (p. 270, folio 244).
- "Allah O Akbar" painted on rocks is read as marking "that this space was now Muslim", typical of contested places (p. 267, folio 241).
- Mountain-top sanctity across traditions: Hira, Sinai, Jesus' sermon, jogis and Sufis (p. 267, folio 241).
- Historiography: Pakistani nationalist historians say a religious divide always existed, while the Indian side blames the British. The author reads Nanak's struggle as evidence that identities existed then (p. 266, folio 240).
- In Pakistan, education breeds intolerance because it is tied to the "nationalistic agenda". Sufi shrine-going and Hindu–Sufi mixing are "no longer acceptable to the educated" elite, and shrines remain attractive to "the rural and [urban poor]" [OCR?] (pp. 277–278, folios 251–252).
- The author calls himself "the product of a post-9/11 world". He describes two opposite camps: Taliban apologists, and those for whom "anyone religious was a lesser being" (p. 279, folio 253).
- Following P. S. Grewal, the author links Ram Das's appointment to caste: he was "relieved" Jetha was Sodhi (p. 272, folio 246). He also links it to property and the "feudalisatio[n]" of Guruhood (p. 274, folio 248).

- **Speaker of the legend passage (pp. 269–270):** the OCR does not preserve who answers Anam's question. The legend, the Salman Rashid and Hiuen Tsang claims, the Ranjit Singh dating and the "our saint / your saint" remark may be Iqbal Qaiser's speech or the author's. "You should read Salman Rashid's book" suggests direct speech. Check against the print.
- **Person-level attachments:** these rest on the person, not a named site: Guru Nanak (all 18 rows), Mardana (gurdwara-rori-sahib), Guru Arjan (3), Guru Hargobind (6), Guru Gobind Singh, Ranjit Singh (samadhi). The Ranjit Singh material concerns Panja Sahib, not his samadhi.
- **Place-level attachments:** the Uch Sharif, Bahawalpur, Multan, Rawalpindi, Islamabad, Sialkot and Lahore bullets are place-level. Most are single passing mentions; the Lahore one is the bare phrase "Arjan's trip to Lahore".
  - Islamabad is named explicitly, so its Saidpur rows are included.
  - "Saidpur" itself does not occur in this chunk.
- **Kartarpur:** "settled at Kartarpur in Punjab" (p. 278). The book does not say which Kartarpur it means. It is left in the general Nanak bullet, not given to gurdwara-darbar-sahib-kartarpur specifically.
- **Hassan Abdal = Panja Sahib's location** (archive "Hasan Abdal"). Place-level Hassan Abdal material (the flight to Attock, Jahangir's gardens) is filed under the Panja Sahib bullet, since that is the only row there.
- **The Wali Qandhari shrine is not an archive row.** Its site facts are filed under Other.
- **shrine-of-syed-musa-pak:** the book's "Moosa Pak Shaheed" had a former grave at Uch, and the book says the body was "shifted to Multan". The name and Multan match the row "Syed Musa Pak Shaheed, Multan", but the book says "It's unclear who he was". This is a name-plus-place match; a consolidator should confirm it. The Uch structure itself is not the archive row.
- **"Syed Jalaludin Surkh Posh Bokhari"** is mapped to the Uch row by name and place. The Uch shrine actually visited is the Gilani one, which is not in the archive.
- **"Baba pyc [OCR?]"** among the posters (p. 277) is unreadable. It is not mapped; do not guess it.
- **The Ram Das succession list (p. 273)** is badly scrambled. The pairings "Arjan → Hargobind", "Hargobind → Har Rai" and "Tegh Bahadur → Gobind Rai (Later Gobind Singh)" are partly legible, and the Har Rai/Harkrishan links are not.
  - "Mohar [OCR?]" (Amar Das's eldest son) and "K[…]" (Ram Das's town) are truncated.
  - Footnote "Singh, The History of Sikh Gurus, page 5°" [OCR?] and "Grewal, Nanak's Doctrine, page 29" are the footnotes' own references, not this book's pages.
- **Internal tension:** the book says Tegh Bahadur's "grandsons (Guru Gobind Singh's sons)" were martyred (p. 273). This is printed as is and has not been corrected.
- **The date of the Hassan Abdal visit is not stated.** The author sets it "a few days" after tenth-Muharram riots in Rawalpindi (p. 265) and also at Baisakhi (p. 271). No year is given.
