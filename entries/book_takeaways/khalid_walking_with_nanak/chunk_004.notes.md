# khalid_walking_with_nanak — chunk 004 — pp. 80–101

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **gurdwara-sacha-sauda** (the book's "Chuhrkhana"; the gurdwara visited on pp. 80–83 is not named on those pages — see Doubts): travelogue visit by the author and Iqbal Qaiser.
  - Iqbal Qaiser had learnt "through his contacts" in the Sikh community "that Boota Singh was the name of the sewadg [OCR?] [sewadar] … appointed here by the Augaf Departmen [OCR?] [Auqaf Department]"; a boy at the gate would not let them in until the guard spoke to Boota Singh by phone (p. 80, folio 62).
  - Boota Singh: "about forty years old", "wearing a green shalwar kameez with a green turban", with his youngest son, "about ten years old"; the author describes him as "extremely shy" (p. 80, folio 62). Iqbal Qaiser says he has "always come here with a jatha (a group of Sikhs)" and Boota Singh shows them around (p. 80, folio 62).
  - Layout: through a portal into a courtyard "at the cent[re] of which stood a white gurdwara"; next to it "a few waan trees enclosed by a small boundary"; gurdwara, courtyard and trees "situated on an ancient mound, which was the highest point in and around the city" (p. 80, folio 62). The trees stand "in the middle of a marbled courtyard" and are called "the last remnants of the jungle that used to exist here" (p. 81, folio 63).
  - Iqbal Qaiser: "It was under these trees that Nanak discoursed with the leader of the yogis" (p. 80, folio 62); "This gurdwara was constructed to commemorate the event" (p. 81, folio 63).
  - Interior: "a wooden palanquin in which the Guru Granth Sahib was placed", in front of it "a couple of swords"; Iqbal Qaiser "took out a ten rupee note and placed it there", walked around the palanquin and fanned the Guru Granth Sahib with a "flying-whisk"; the author did the same (p. 81, folio 63).
  - Boota Singh showed a "new sukhasan": a room behind the gurdwara reserved for the Guru Granth Sahib, carpeted, walls "done up in wood", "a split air-conditioner" (off "as there was no electricity"), and a carved wooden bed; asked if it was made in "Armritsar [OCR?] [Amritsar]", he replied "It was made in Nankana Sahib" (p. 81, folio 63). Boota Singh left the room backwards so his back never faced the Guru Granth Sahib; Iqbal Qaiser and the author did likewise (p. 82, folio 64).
  - Boota Singh: "I have been working here for the past twelve years" (p. 83, folio 65).
  - "a newly constructed road that connects Chuhrkhana with Nankana Sahib", "constructed to facilitate Sikh pilgrims"; earlier pilgrims went via "Sheikupura"; now "only about twenty minutes" (p. 85, folio 67).
  - After Iqbal Qaiser's book was released "a debate raged about the renovation of a number of gurdwaras, one of which was the Gurdwara Sacha Sauda. Once the gurdwara was constructed, this new road also had to be made to connect it to Nankana Sahib" (p. 85, folio 67). The author tells Boota Singh "Sacha Sauda is already prominent" (p. 83, folio 65).
  - The author later refers to the visit as "while we were at Chuhrkhana" (p. 92, folio 74).

- **gurudwara-janam-asthan-nankana-sahib** (the book's "Gurdwara Janamasthan", also printed "Janamsthan", "Janamuasthan [OCR?]"):
  - "The grandest of them … a splendid structure spread over a vast area constructed over the place that was once the house of Mehta Kalu" (p. 90, folio 72).
  - "The actual shrine is a small room in the middle of a spacious marble-floored courtyard. At the centre of this room is Thara Sahib, where the Guru Granth Sahib is recited. According to tradition, Guru Nanak was born here" (p. 90, folio 72).
  - "Next to this shrine is a well, a board next to which says that it belonged to the household of Guru Nanak" (p. 90, folio 72).
  - Approach through "the small market of Nankana": "Most of the shops were run by Muslims but there were a few Sikh-owned shops as well"; the ancient mound lies behind it (p. 91, folio 73).
  - The author recalls sitting there "with my back towards the main shrine when a Sikh man came up to me and told me to not sit with my back towards the main shrine" (p. 93, folio 75).
  - At "the annual celebration of Guru Nanaks birthday" a "huge banner with pictures of al] [OCR?] [all] ten Sikh Gurus" stood in front of the building, Nanak "wearing his saffron chola and raising his right hand in blessing" at one end, Guru Gobind Singh at the other (p. 94, folio 76).

- **gurdwara-patti-sahib** (the book's "Gurdwara Patti Sahib"):
  - "This small curdwara [OCR?] [gurdwara] is only a few metres away from Gurdwara Janamasthan. It was here that Guru Nanak got his early education. Here, he wrote his first poern [OCR?] [poem] which is recorded in the Guru Granth Sahib" (p. 89, folio not stated); the poem's lines are quoted (p. 90, folio 72).
  - Jasdeep, a young Pathan Sikh, lives there and was "educated … in Sikh precepts at this gurdwara" (p. 89, folio not stated; p. 90, folio 72).
  - "During the early days of Sikh migration to Nankana Sahib after the War, of 1965 and 1971, a lot of Sikh families from Khyber Pakhtunkhw [OCR?] [Khyber Pakhtunkhwa] and the Tribal Areas found refuge in Gurdwara Patti Sahib" (p. 90, folio 72).
  - There "a teacher by the name of Giani Pratap started teaching young Sikh childrep [OCR?] [children] the Gurmukhi alphabet and the Guru Granth Sahib"; he was "an Indias [OCR?] [Indian] citizen who visited Pakistan sometime during the sixties for a Pilgrimage to Nankana Sahib" (p. 90, folio 72).
  - Tale Jasdeep "must have heard": Nanak composed the poem "in the same building" and "impressed his teacher, Pandit Gopal" (p. 90, folio 72).

- **gurdwara-balila-sahib-bal-lila-sahib** (the book's "Gurdwara Bal Lila"): "A few feet away from Gurdwara Patti Sahib"; "According to Sikh tradition, this was once an open field where Guru Nanak used to play as a child. Every year on the occasion of Lohri, sports competitions are arranged for children within the premises of this gurdwara, to mark the event" (p. 90, folio 72; p. 91, folio 73).

- **gurdwara-sri-kiara-sahib** (the book's "Kiari Sahib"): with Malji Sahib "the furthest from Gurdwara Janamsthan"; "said to mark the spot where Guru Nanak's buffaloes spoilt the fields of a local farmer who is reported to have complained to Nanak's father" (p. 91, folio 73).

- **gurdwara-malji-sahib** (the book's "Malji Sahib"): "It is believed that Guru Nanak once fell asleep while his buffaloes were grazing nearby at the spot that Gurdwara Malji Sahib was constructed"; when the sun fell on his face, "Miraculously, a cobra emerged at that time and extended its hood, protecting the Guru from the sun while he slept" (p. 91, folio 73).

- **gurdwara-tambo-sahib** (the book's "Gurdwara Tambu Sahib"):
  - Nanak, "after he had spent all his father's money on a good deed", "looked for sanctuary in a group of waan trees that existed outside the town. Those trees still exist and are held sacred by Sikh devotees" (p. 86, folio 68; p. 87, folio 69).
  - "Next to the trees is a small gurdwara, a yellow single storied structure. Behind it are two large hostels used by Sikh pilgrims at the time of the largest Sikh festival in Pakistan—the birthday of Guru Nanak, celebrated in November every year" (p. 87, folio 69).
  - "Pilgrims tie pieces of cloth onto the branches of these stooping trees as a supplication" (p. 87, folio 69).
  - Name "derived from the fambu [OCR?] [tambu] (tent) like trees under which Nanak hid" (p. 87, folio 69).
  - Lies "in between Janamsthan and Kiari Sahib and Malji Sahib" (p. 91, folio 73); has a "blue gate" (p. 91, folio 73).
  - Jatha Singh, "the self-appointed caretaker of the gurdwara", hosted them; "in-charge of the vast property associated with the gurdwara, which was allotted to it by the Sikh ruler of Punjab, Maharaja Ranjit Singh in the nineteenth century"; "receives millions of rupees in donations from Sikh expatriates residing in first world countries", to be spent "on the upliftment of the gurdwara and the Sikh community in Nankana Sahib" (p. 87, folio 69).
  - His room is "in one of the rooms of a hostel"; he was arbitrating between two feuding Muslim families; the guests were served "langar, vegetables along with lentils and hot rotts [OCR?] [rotis]" (p. 87, folio 69); a later dispute about a Muslim driver named Akram (p. 89, folio not stated).

- **gurudwara-janam-asthan-nankana-sahib**, **gurdwara-balila-sahib-bal-lila-sahib**, **gurdwara-malji-sahib**, **gurdwara-panjvi-chati-patshahi**, **gurdwara-patti-sahib**, **gurdwara-sri-kiara-sahib**, **gurdwara-tambo-sahib** (the book's "Nankana Sahib" / "Nankana"; also "Rai Bhoi di Talwindi", "Talwindi"):

  - "the Sikh community in Nankana Sahib, which amounts to about two hundred-odd households"; Jatha Singh "has for several years played the role of a leader" (p. 87, folio 69).
  - Giani Pratap, "After looking at the dilapidated condition of the shrines here he decided to stay and look after these holy buildings. That was the first time someone had taken the initiative to take care of the gurdwaras in Nankana Sahib after Partition" (p. 90, folio 72).
  - "There are a total of six gurdwaras associated with Guru Nanak at Nankana Sahib" (p. 90, folio 72).
  - The ancient mound: Iqbal Qaiser, "It is an archaeological mound. It "now rests" behind Gurdwara Janamsthan, "away from the hub of the new settlement" (p. 91, folio 73).
  - Mound: a steep climb "with no proper path", "at least twenty metres from the ground", "covered with small shrubs of karir (capparis deciduas)"; "shards of broken pottery and other mud utensils" underfoot (p. 92, folio 74).
  - A young Nanak "used to spend a lot of time in conversation with yogis, sadhus and dervish who would pass through Talwindi" on the mound (p. 91, folio 73; p. 92, folio 74); "It is believed that in the years between the incident of Sacha Sauda and his migration to Sultanpur, Nanak started to frequent this place"; "That period of six to seven years" (p. 92, folio 74).
  - Iqbal Qaiser asks Boota Singh "When did your family move to Nankana Sahib from the Tribal Areas?" (p. 83, folio 65).

- **gurdwara-dash-mesh-pita** (Guru Gobind Singh):
  - "According to tradition, the tenth Sikh Guru, Guru Gobind Singh, concluded the institution of Gurudom by declaring that the Guru Granth Sahib was the eternal Guru" (p. 81, folio 63).

  - "Guru Gobind is seen as someone who completed Nanak's mission"; "He codified the teachings of Nana[k] and founded the Khalsa" (p. 94, folio 76; p. 95, folio 77).
  - He concluded "both Hinduism and Islam were flawed religious traditions"; "any follower of Nanak who refused to obey the commandments of the subsequent Gurus was not a Sikh" (p. 96, folio 78).
  - After "the assassination of his father Guru Tegh Bahadur", "The gravest threat came from his uncle Ram Rai, who was the disgruntled son of Guru Har Rai"; Ram Rai approached "Emperor Aurangzeb"; the Punjab chiefs "surrounded Guru Gobind's abode in Anandpur" (p. 96, folio 78).
  - "On the first of Vaisakh, 1699 (14 April 1699)", at "Anandpur, a city that had been founded by his father", five volunteers offered their heads; "in reality, it was a goat's blood"; "the Panj Pyare or the five beloved"; "the five Ks (Aangi [OCR?] [kangi], kesh, kirpan, kachcha and hada [OCR?] [kada])" (p. 97, folio 79).

  - "He lived in a regal tent"; "in command of a standing army, whose weapons were procured through the offerings of devotees or through raids upon neighbouring villages"; "a war drum"; "referred to as Sacha Padshah, the True King, and his court was referred to as Sacha Darbar, the True Court" (p. 99, folio 81).
  - Fond of war exercises and hunting; "on the occasion of his birthday his devotees pay tribute to him by enacting scenes of war exercises"; "adept in Persian, Arabic and Sanskrit" (p. 99, folio 81).
  - In "Vichitr Natak which forms a portion of the Daswan Padshah ka Granth" he explained how the Sodhis replaced the Bedis (Sodhi Rai; Bedis at "Kashi"; the king of "Madar Des") (p. 100, folio 82).

## Practices, institutions, economy
- Sewadar appointed by the "Augaf Departmen [OCR?]" at the Chuhrkhana gurdwara (p. 80, folio 62).
- Sukhasan: "A room like this one is constructed for the Guru Granth Sahib at every gurdwara"; the book is laid on a bed at day's end, air-conditioner in summer, heater in winter; "the Granthi carries the Guru Granth Sahib on his head" to the room after evening prayer (p. 82, folio 64).
- Offering a ten rupee note and fanning the Guru Granth Sahib with a flying-whisk (p. 81, folio 63).
- Langar served at Tambu Sahib (p. 87, folio 69); pilgrims' hostels and cloth-tying at the trees (p. 87, folio 69).
- Guru Nanak's birthday in November: "the largest Sikh festival in Pakistan" (p. 87, folio 69); banner at Janamasthan during it (p. 94, folio 76).
- Lohri sports competitions at Bal Lila (p. 91, folio 73).
- Custodianship and money: Jatha Singh's control of Ranjit Singh-era property, diaspora donations, police contacts, arbitration of Muslim disputes (pp. 87, 89, folios 69, not stated).
- Road-building for pilgrims after Iqbal Qaiser's book triggered a renovation debate (p. 85, folio 67).
- Encroachment: dervishes' shrine at Gurdwara Sachkand; gurdwara used as a school at Nena Kot (pp. 83–84, folios 65–66).
- Nanak Panthi Hindus: devotees who refused the Khalsa; "thousands of such Sikhs living in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Punjab and Sindh in Pakistan"; "In their temples, along with pictures of Hindu deities, one also finds a picture of Guru Nanak" (p. 98, folio 80).
- Sikh posters of the ten Gurus: Nanak and Gobind always largest (p. 101, folio 83).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)
- At the Chuhrkhana trees, "According to legend", yogis "were hanging from the trees while there were others who were sitting within an empty trunk when Nanak passed through" (p. 81, folio 63; OCR partly garbled).
- Kaaba: "According to legend, Nanak once travelled to Mecca where he performed Hajj"; he slept with his feet toward the Kaaba, was rebuked, turned his feet "and as he did so, the Kaaba miraculously turned towards his feet" (p. 82, folio 64). The author says he doubts the miracle: "What is important in this story is Nanak's criticism of the concept of a 'sacred space'" (p. 82, folio 64).
- Malji Sahib cobra shading the sleeping Nanak (p. 91, folio 73).
- Kiari Sahib buffaloes spoiling a farmer's fields (p. 91, folio 73).
- Nanak hiding under the tent-like trees at Tambu Sahib (p. 87, folio 69).
- Bedi–Sodhi origin story from Vichitr Natak: the Bedis vowed they "would return the compliment in Kaliyuga when their descendant Nanak would raise the Sodhis to a prime position in the world" (p. 100, folio 82).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)
- "Nanak's philosophy is centred on the concept of redefining the sacred and the profane" (p. 82, folio 64).
- The author finds it "contradictory that Sikhism today has become [an] institutionalised religion"; "the subsequent Gurus missed the exsence [OCR?] [essence] of Nanak's movement when they institutionalised the seligion [OCR?] [religion]" (pp. 92–93, folios 74–75).

**Mapping doubts**
- The 18-id Guru Nanak bullet is person-level, not site-specific.
- The Nankana Sahib (7), Eminabad (3), Gujranwala (2), Lahore (35) and Sheikhupura (1) bullets are place-level. Eminabad/Gujranwala/Lahore are bare names in a list of cities (p. 88); "Sheikupura" is a bare route mention (p. 85) — the only Sheikhupura row is Waris Shah's mausoleum, which the book does not mention.
- "Rai Bhoi di Talwindi"/"Talwindi" mapped to the Nankana Sahib rows on the book's own identification ("Nankana Sahib, which at that point in time was still known as Rai Bhoi di Talwindi", p. 86).
- The gurdwara visited on pp. 80–83 is never named on those pages. I attached it to `gurdwara-sacha-sauda` from the book's context: the author calls the visit "while we were at Chuhrkhana" (p. 92), and toponym_map gives "formerly chuharkana" → gurdwara-sacha-sauda. The author's remark "Sacha Sauda is already prominent" (p. 83) is consistent with this but does not settle it. A consolidator should check against the preceding chunk.
- "Gurdwara Sachkand" (p. 83) is a gurdwara "close to" Chuhrkhana. It is NOT `gurdwara-sach-khand-sahib` (Shikarpur) and is left unmapped.
- Iqbal Qaiser's question about Boota Singh's family moving "to Nankana Sahib" (p. 83) sits awkwardly with Boota Singh working at the Chuhrkhana gurdwara; Boota Singh says only that he is "from the village of Nena Kot".
- Guru Arjan: the book says "Arjan becomes the fourth Nanak" and Gobind "the ninth Nanak" (p. 98). Recorded as printed. Gobind is also "the tenth Sikh Guru" (p. 81) — consistent with "ninth incarnation" counted after Nanak, but the Arjan ordinal is not checked here against anything outside the chunk.
- Baba Farid and Bhai Mardana rest on bare-name mentions as Guru Granth Sahib contributors (p. 82) — per the 18 Sept ruling.
- The Janamasthan description ("house of Mehta Kalu") does not say who Mehta Kalu was; left as printed.
- Nanak Panthi Hindus (p. 98) are relevant to the archive's Nanakpanthi/Udasi rows, but the book names no site, so nothing is mapped.
- No Saidpur, Kartarpur or Islamabad mention in this chunk.
