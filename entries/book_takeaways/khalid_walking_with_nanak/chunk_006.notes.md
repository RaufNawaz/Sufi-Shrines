# khalid_walking_with_nanak — chunk 006 — pp. 123–145

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **gurudwara-janam-asthan-nankana-sahib**, **gurdwara-babay-de-ber**, **gurdwara-balila-sahib-bal-lila-sahib**, **gurdwara-chakki-sahib**, **gurdwara-choa-sahib**, **gurdwara-darbar-sahib-kartarpur**, **gurdwara-khoohi-bhai-lalo-bhai-lalo-di-khooi**, **gurdwara-malji-sahib**, **gurdwara-panja-sahib**, **gurdwara-patti-sahib**, **gurdwara-pehli-patshahi-jind-pir-sukkur**, **gurdwara-rori-sahib**, **gurdwara-sach-khand-sahib**, **gurdwara-sacha-sauda**, **gurdwara-sahib-saidpur-guru-nanak-dev-ji**, **gurdwara-sri-kiara-sahib**, **gurdwara-sri-tilganji-sahib**, **gurdwara-tambo-sahib** (the book's "Nanak" / "Guru Nanak"; person-level, no named archive site):
  - The book says Nanak "was particularly fascinated" by Multan because "it signified the religious syncretism that he yearned for"; Mecca was sacred only to Muslims, Benaras to Hindus, "Multan was revered by all" (p. 123, folio 105).
  - The book says that when he later visited Multan "he was disappointed to see that the religious syncretism he had imagined was non-existent" (p. 123, folio 105).
  - In the author's reconstructed scene, after "a month of walking" Nanak wishes to sleep under "an ancient banyan tree" near "the village of Makhdoom Pur Pahuran", but Mardana's persistence takes them into "the caravanserai of Sajjan and Gaijan [OCR?] [Gajjan]" (p. 123, folio 105).
  - In the reconstructed scene, the innkeepers Sajjan and Gajjan plan to rob and kill Nanak and Mardana, suspecting "rich merchants from Lahore" disguised as fakirs (p. 124, folio 106); Nanak sings a hymn (the book quotes it with a footnote to the Guru Granth Sahib, srigranth.org "page 729") including "The herons in their white feathers dwell in the sacred shrines of pilgrimage" (p. 125, folio 107).
  - In the reconstructed scene, Sajjan falls at Nanak's feet, confesses "all the murders", and Nanak suggests they "convert their house into a House of God where people from all creeds, religions and sects would be welcome and provided with food and shelter"; the book states: "This was to become the first Gurdwara of the Sikh religion" (p. 126, folio 108).
  - The book states that Jahangir "was the great grandson of Emperor Babur who had incarcerated Guru Nanak" (p. 133, folio not stated).
  - The book says Guru Tegh Bahadur picked up many themes from "the poetry of Nanak" and "Like Nanak, he too discouraged extreme ascetic practices" (p. 134, folio 116).
  - Damaged passage: "according to Sikh tradition, reinterpreted some of Nanak's poetry to suit the religious inclination of the Emperor" — the subject of the sentence is lost in OCR; Ram Rai is named in the following lines (p. 135, folio 117).
  - In the author's reconstructed scene, Nanak, wearing "a saffron chola", sings on a mound in the forest with a rubab-playing companion and meets Sheikh Kamal, scribe of the Pakpattan shrine (p. 139, folio 121); Nanak says he has come across Sheikh Farid-ud-din's name "while I was living in Sultanpur and also while I was in Lahore" (p. 141, folio 123).
  - In the reconstructed scene, Kamal reports that Nanak "says that he is neither a Muslim nor a Hindu. In fact, he says that there is no Hindu, no Muslim" (p. 143, folio 125).
  - The book says this was not the first time Nanak was questioned about "his controversial statement that there is no Hindu or Muslim": it began "at the court of Nawab Daulat Khan Lodhi at Sultanpur", was asked again "at the court of Malik Bhago in Eminabad", and by friends and family "at Rai Bhoi Di Talwindi" (p. 144, folio 126).
  - Nanak's reply as the book quotes it (footnoted to Trilochan Singh, *Guru Nanak: Founder of Sikhism*, "page 108"): "God is One, / The Path is One. / Accept the Path of Truth / Reject all other ways." (p. 144, folio 126).
  - Nanak named among Punjabi poets ridiculed by "the educated elite of Punjab" (p. 128, folio 110).

- **gurdwara-rori-sahib** (the book's "Mardana"): in the author's reconstructed scenes, Mardana regards Nanak "as his mentor and Guru" but their relationship "was not constrained by formalities"; he "could sometimes blackmail his friend" (p. 123, folio 105); he plays the rubab and weeps, believing Nanak's song was "directed against him" (p. 125, folio 107); he notices "a lot of similarity between the symbolism of Nanak and Sheikh Farid" (p. 141, folio 123); Kamal calls him "a Mirasi bard" who "plays the rubab while Nanak sings his poems" (p. 143, folio 125).

- **prahladpuri-temple** (the book's "The temple of Prahlad Bhagat"): "one of the most important Hindu temples" in Multan, with the shrine of Bahauddin Zakariya "located beside the temple" (p. 123, folio 105).

- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya** (the book's "the shrine of Bahauddin Zakariya"): "one of the most popular Muslim shrines", located beside the temple of Prahlad Bhagat in Multan (p. 123, folio 105).

- **prahladpuri-temple**, **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**, **shrine-of-hafiz-muhammad-jamal-multani**, **shrine-of-hazrat-shah-ali-akbar-shah-ali-akbar-shamsi**, **shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam**, **shrine-of-shah-shams-ud-din-sabzwari**, **shrine-of-shah-yusaf-gardez**, **shrine-of-syed-musa-pak** (the book's "Multan"):
  - "At that time Multan was one of the biggest cities of Punjab and was known for the religious shrines of Muslim saints, and Jain and Hindu temples. It was a sacred space for people of all religious denominations" (p. 123, folio 105).
  - On Nanak's later visit, "The Hindus were assured of their superiority, while the Muslims were assured of theirs" (p. 123, folio 105).
  - Tulamba is "about midway between Lahore and Multan" (p. 123, folio 105); the single-lane road by the Makhdoom Pur Pahuran school "was the old Multan road" (p. 129, folio 111).

- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar** (the book's "Baba Farid", "Sheikh Farid", "Sheikh Farid-ud-din of Pakpattan", "the shrine of Sheikh Farid at Pakpattan"):
  - Baba Farid named among Punjabi poets ridiculed by the educated elite of Punjab (p. 128, folio 110).
  - In the reconstructed scene, Kamal sings "Sheikh Farid's most popular qawwali", sung the previous night by qawwal devotees who came "with the Diwan of Chawali Mashaikh" (p. 138, folio 120); the book quotes "I did not sleep with my love tonight / And every bit of my body aches" (p. 138, folio 120).
  - Sheikh Ibrahim is "the twelfth descendant of the great Sheikh Farid-ud-din of Pakpattan" (p. 141, folio 123) and "the twelfth successor of Baba Farid" (p. 142, folio 124).
  - Pakpattan "had once been known as Ajodhan, but its name was changed to Pakpattan after the head of the Chistiyan si/si/a [OCR?] [silsila] (religious order), Baba Farid, left Delhi and settled here"; it was then "a small insignificant city", next to which "on top of an abandoned mound Baba Farid used to teach his students"; the River Sutlej "snaked through" it (p. 141, folio 123).
  - As followers grew, the city became "a major trading city, with the shrine at the centre"; "the power of his descendants" grew and they "became major feudal lords and political players of the region" (p. 142, folio 124).
  - Damaged passage: the shrine "was in one corner" of the courtyard, next to it a tomb "which contained the graves of the descendants of the saint"; "several stalls around" (p. 142, folio 124).
  - "It had been three hundred years since the death of the great Sufi but his poetry was still sung by qawwals at the shrine and by fakirs in the cities of Punjab" (p. 142, folio 124).
  - Devotees believed the descendant "had inherited the spirituality of Farid"; the book's Sheikh Ibrahim "abhorred the institutionalisation of spirituality as was being practised in the name of his family" (p. 142, folio 124).
  - "the shrine of Sheikh Farid at Pakpattan, one of the biggest centres of the Sufi Chistiyan school of thought in all of South Asia" (p. 143, folio 125).
  - Sheikh Ibrahim "took immense pride in the fact that his ancestor Baba Farid was the best Punjabi poet ever" (p. 143, folio 125).
  - "The shrine at Tiba Haji Deen was older than the shrine of Baba Farid at Pakpattan, but it was less important. The importance, in this case, was determined by the number of followers each shrine had" (p. 145, folio 127).
  - Damaged passage: Baba Farid's "politeness"/sweet language, which Sheikh Ibrahim followed (p. 145, folio 127).

- **gurdwara-dera-sahib**, **gurdwara-baoli-sahib-guru-arjan-dev-ji-lahore**, **gurdwara-panjvi-chati-patshahi** (the book's "Guru Arjan"): Tegh Bahadur's "grandfather Guru Arjan had been martyred on the orders of the Mughal Emperor Jahangir", the first martyrdom (p. 133, folio not stated); Tegh Bahadur "was the first Guru since Guru Arjan to compose hymns" (p. 134, folio 116); Prithi Chand was the "brother of Guru Arjan" (p. 134, folio 116, OCR damaged).

- **gurdwara-dash-mesh-pita** (the book's "Guru Gobind Singh" / "Gobind"): Jaita recovered Tegh Bahadur's body and "presented it to Guru Gobind Singh at Anandpur, who then cremated the body"; Jaita was titled "the true son of the Guru" (p. 132, folio 114); it was "actually the young Gobind Singh" who urged his father to aid the Pandits (pp. 132–133); "when Guru Gobind Singh founded the Khalsa, he ordered his followers to keep a kirpan with them at all times" (p. 133, folio not stated); he was "much more militaristically inclined than his father" (p. 137, folio 119).

- **madho-lal-hussain**, **mausoleum-of-waris-shah**, **mazar-of-bulleh-shah** (the book's "Shah Hussain", "Waris Shah", "Bulleh Shah"): named with Baba Farid and Guru Nanak as Punjabi poets whose poetry "the educated elite of Punjab ends up disowning, in fact, ridiculing" (p. 128, folio 110).

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Gurdwara at Makhdoom Pur Pahuran, near Tulamba** — "the shrine of Guru Nanak there" / "the Gurdwara of Nanak"; the book calls the Sajjan–Gajjan caravanserai "the first Gurdwara of the Sikh religion" (pp. 126–131). Present state under Practices.
- **Tulamba** — "about midway between Lahore and Multan" (p. 123, folio 105); a journey "of about three hours" (p. 128, folio 110).
- **Sher Shah Suri serai** — Iqbal Qaiser: "another serai here which is said to have been built by Sher Shah Suri"; Humayun's retinue passed along this road, with reference to "Humayunnama" (p. 129, folio 111); now "converted into a primary school for girls", no sign of the older building, "an ancient graveyard" next to it (p. 131, folio 113).
- **Sajjan and Gajjan** — innkeepers ("thugs") of the caravanserai, converted by Nanak in the author's reconstruction (pp. 123–126).
- **Guru Tegh Bahadur** — his head "hung at the Chandni Chowk of Delhi" on Aurangzeb's orders; body recovered by "an untouchable Hindu, Jaita" (p. 132, folio 114); sacrificed his life, per Sikh tradition, for Kashmiri Pandits burdened by the jaziya (p. 132, folio 114); appointed Guru "in the year 1664 after the death of the child Guru Harkish. [OCR?] [Harkrishan]"; retreated to "Bakala in Bari Doab, where he lived for twenty years" (p. 134, folio 116); his poetry is in the Guru Granth Sahib (p. 134, folio 116); Cunningham, *A History of the Sikhs*, quoted on his plunder "between Hansi and the Sutlej" and league with "a Muhammadan zealot, named Adam Hafiz" (p. 136, folio not stated); the foundations of the Khalsa "had been laid during the reign of Guru Tegh Bahadur" (p. 137, folio 119).
- **Guru Har Rai** — challenged by elder brother "Dhir Mal" (p. 134, folio 116); sided with "Prince Dara Shikob" in the Mughal succession war (p. 135, folio 117); bypassed his son and made "Harkrishan, who was six years old at the time" Guru (p. 135, folio 117).
- **Ram Rai** — rebelled against his father's decision, using his relationship with the Emperor (p. 135, folio 117).
- **Guru Harkrishan** — damaged: "died of chickenpox before he could appear at court" (p. 135, folio 117).
- **Harji**, "grandson of Prithi Chand" — refused to accept the young Guru (p. 134, folio 116); "Dhirmal" / "Dhir Mal" (pp. 134, 136).
- **Aurangzeb, Jahangir, Babur, Shahjahan** — Mughal–Sikh conflict (pp. 132–135); the author calls the portrayal of Aurangzeb as oppressor "This simplistic view of history" (pp. 133–134).
- **Sheikh Kamal** — "the official scribe of Harzat Sheikh tbr shim [OCR?] [Hazrat Sheikh Ibrahim]", composes poems (p. 141, folio 123); a woodcutter in the reconstructed scene (p. 138, folio 120).

- **Diwan of Chawali Mashaikh** — head of a Chistiyan shrine; damaged text links it to "the ancestral village of Baba Farid" (p. 142, folio 124); he mocks Nanak's lack of conversion (p. 143, folio 125); his shrine "at Tiba Haji Deen was older than the shrine of Baba Farid" (p. 145, folio 127).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Makhdoom Pur Pahuran gurdwara, travelogue** (author and Iqbal Qaiser, on "the second dey [OCR?] [day] of Bid [OCR?] [Eid]") (p. 127, folio 109):
  - "The gurdwara was located in a school and there was a giant lock on the main gate, this being a public holiday"; a banner on the Punjab government's education policy over the door (p. 128, folio 110).
  - Iqbal Qaiser had "explored the shrine and its vicinity about a decade ago while researching his book" (p. 127, folio 109); the guard Awais recalled his last visit, "which had been in 1994" (p. 129, folio 111).
  - Inside: "models of two nuclear rockets … Ghori and Abdali"; "Behind them was the gurdwara, now converted into an office for the Principal"; Quranic verses on its wall; "A new boundary wall had been constructed, covering the gurdwara, on which the ninety-nine names of Allah were written" (p. 129, folio 111).
  - "hardly any Sikh ever comes here" (p. 130, folio not stated).
  - The school's history wall says it "began as a primary school in the latter half of the nineteenth century. There was no mention of the gurdwara" (p. 131, folio 113).
  - Awais: "The school was shifted here during the Ayub era, after the Department of Augaf [OCR?] [Auqaf] was created"; the gurdwara's land "was snatched by the land mafia. Now we only have a small tract in front of the building" (p. 131, folio 113).
- **Pakpattan shrine as institution** (author's reconstruction): hereditary descendants as feudal lords; qawwals at the shrine; hierarchy between shrines "determined by the number of followers" (pp. 141–145).
- **Sikh institutions**: Khalsa and the kirpan as religious duty (p. 133); "the feudalization of the [office] … had been completed with various parties vying for … monetary and political rewards" (p. 134, folio 116, OCR damaged).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- Nanak knew of the innkeepers' murderous intent "through divine knowledge", as Sajjan concludes in the author's reconstructed scene (p. 126, folio 108).
- A dust storm turned Delhi red, after which Tegh Bahadur's body and head "had disappeared" (p. 132, folio 114).
- Tegh Bahadur tied a paper spell round his neck; after the beheading it read "He gave his head, not his secret"; "The Sikhs believe that the Guru had performed a miracle while dying" (p. 133, folio not stated).
- In the reconstructed scene Nanak knows Kamal is sitting there "even though his eyes had been shut throughout the song" (p. 140, folio 122).
- The forest mound was believed to be where "the spirits and djinns of the forest resided" (p. 139, folio 121).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- The author argues that in Pakistani Punjab, Punjabi "is looked down upon", recounting that his elite school fined students for speaking it (p. 128, folio 110).
- The author argues that Pakistan Studies teaches "the separateness of Hindus from Muslims", that "nationalism is premised on hatred towards Hindus", and that government-school students emerge "more radicalised … than from your neighbourhood madrassas" (p. 130, folio not stated).
- Erasure: "Like the omission of Hinduism and the names of Hindu kings and queens from Pakistani history, the story of this gurdwara had also been erased" (p. 131, folio 113).
- On Tegh Bahadur, the author says the oppressor–oppressed view "The reality was a little more complicated" and cites Cunningham on "the machinations of Ram Rai" (pp. 134–136).

## Doubts

- **Damage inventory (once):** [source stamp: www.sikhbookclub.com watermark] on nearly every page; running-header and stray-symbol noise; letter confusions (Gaijan/Gajjan, Qgiser/Qgeet/Qgiver for Qaiser, Tulambs, Karnal for Kamal, Augaf, "Harzat Sheikh tbr shim", si/si/a, Kaliyue*). p. 124, 127, 130, 135, 142 and 143 are heavily garbled in places; damaged sentences are quoted only where the words are legible.
- **Folio doubts:** p. 130 header reads "718", p. 133 "15", p. 136 "+48" — each differs from the computed folio (112, 115, 118), so cited as folio not stated. p. 139 header "12]" read as 121 (]/1 confusion). pp. 124, 127, 132, 135, 138 have no legible header number; computed folio used.
- **Person-level:** the 18-id Guru Nanak bullet is person-level, not site-specific. The Arjan, Hargobind and Gobind Singh bullets also rest on biography, not on their Pakistani sites.
- **Place-level:** Multan (8 ids), Lahore (35 ids) and Eminabad (3 ids) bullets are place-level. The Lahore mentions are incidental (distance references, a speech, Qaiser's return) and carry no site information.
- **prahladpuri-temple:** the book says "temple of Prahlad Bhagat", beside Bahauddin Zakariya's shrine; the row's location note matches, but the name is the book's, and the row's principal figure is Narasimha. Consolidator should confirm.
- **Author-only mentions:** Shah Hussain, Waris Shah, Bulleh Shah (a bare list of names, p. 128) and Allama Iqbal (a painted picture, p. 129) are mapped under the 18 September ruling on bare-name evidence.
- **Rai Bhoi Di Talwindi (p. 144):** not mapped to the Nankana Sahib rows; this chunk does not identify it with Nankana Sahib or call it Nanak's birthplace.
- **Makhdoom Pur Pahuran gurdwara:** no archive row; material that may be useful for a future row. The book calls the caravanserai the "first Gurdwara", but that is the author's claim inside a reconstructed narrative.
- **Possible internal tension:** Iqbal Qaiser explored the shrine "about a decade ago" (p. 127) vs his last visit "in 1994" (p. 129). Not resolved here.
- **"Baba Gurdin" (p. 134)** is read as Baba Gurditta, the spelling the same paragraph uses next. **"Dhir Mal"/"Dhirmal"** spellings vary as printed.
- **Pakpattan "three hundred years since the death"** (p. 142) is inside the reconstruction and gives no year.
- Footnote page numbers (Guru Granth "page 729", Trilochan Singh "page 108", Kohli "page 1", Cunningham "page 110/109", Gandhi "page 634", Grewal "Pages144-146", Hymns of the Gurus "pages 116-117") are the cited works' pages, not this book's.
