  - Back-cover blurb: the book is "not merely a story about gurdwaras", but "a re-telling of the story of Nanak the son, the poet, the wanderer, the father, the friend"; it traces how "an unorganized spiritual movement evolved into the institutionalized Khalsa of Guru Gobind Singh" and how "Nanak the poet became Guru Nanak the saint" (p. 2, folio not stated).
  - The author, raised in Pakistani Punjab, heard of Guru Nanak as "the founder of Sikhism", a poet and a spiritual leader, but found no avenues in the Muslim-dominant culture to explore him; Nanak was "my own, being a Punjabi" yet "an alien, since he was a non-Muslim" (p. 15, folio not stated).
  - The author says he "truly discovered Guru Nanak for the first time" through his mentor Iqbal Qaiser, who recited Nanak's "Babur Bani", "a long poem that Nanak wrote at the time the Mughal marauder Babur attacked Punjab, destroying the cities of Eminabad and Lahore on the way" (p. 15, folio not stated); in the poem Nanak "lays the blame of Babur's wrath at God's feet" and "challenges the divinity of the Divine" (p. 16, folio not stated).
  - Nanak "spent a major portion of his life in present-day Pakistan. He was born here", first preached by travelling around this region, "and eventually settled in a small village which is now on the Pakistani side of the border" (p. 16, folio not stated).
  - Bhai Mardana is called "his Muslim companion"; the author imagines Nanak and Mardana at gurdwaras and frames his own travels with Iqbal Qaiser as an extension of their journey (p. 16, folio not stated).
  - Author's claims: Nanak "vehemently spoke against organised religion", "abhorred the concept of associating miracles with religious personalities", yet "his biography is nothing but a story of his miracles"; "On his deathbed, he appointed not his son but his most loyal student as his spiritual successor" (successor not named) (p. 16, folio not stated); a couple of successors later "'Guruhood' became a family affair" (p. 17, folio not stated).
  - The Janamsakhis are "Sikh texts on Nanak's life, written by his devotees after his death", "the primary sources"; they record encounters and miracles and exist in "different versions" (p. 17, folio not stated).
  - The author states the Nanak narrative "part of the book is fictionalised", drawn from Janamsakhis, poetry and his travels, limited "to a few areas in Pakistan" (p. 17, folio not stated).
  - The Naulakha Hazari shrine booklet (Auqaf) states "Guru Nanak was born after Baba Naulakha Hazari prayed for his mother" (p. 31, folio 13) — see Legends.

## Practices, institutions, economy

- Auqaf at the Naulakha Hazari shrine: "the officials of the Augaf [OCR?] [Auqaf] (Wakfin [OCR?] [Wakf in] India) Department, a government organisation responsible for managing the affairs of this Muslim shrine" gave the author a booklet on the saint; the officer-in-charge, "an old man, surrounded by a group of younger officials, none of whom had any work", said "'This is not authentic history though … All the information we have about this saint is through folk stories'" (p. 31, folio 13).
- Gurdwara property: under Sikh presence, gurdwaras and their property were "managed by their administrative bodies"; "After 1947, these buildings were abandoned and like orphans, their properties were snatched away"; many "were taken over by refugees from the other side of the border" (p. 34, folio 16).
- "When the Evacuee Trust Property Board (ET PB [OCR?] [ETPB]) was created along with the Augaf [OCR?] [Auqaf] Department in the 1960s, it brought under its control various neglected gurdwaras. However, in most cases official apathy and a religious bias against these buildings resulted in further deterioration. Newly appointed officials looked the other way as the land mafia took" (chunk ends mid-sentence) (p. 34, folio 16).
- Land endowments by Ranjit Singh were "believed to be a kind of religious service, made mandatory by Guru Nanak himself" (p. 34, folio 16).
- Pilgrimage access at Bebe Nanaki: shut to Sikhs, now opened to visiting pilgrims at festivals (p. 33, folio 15).
- Reconstructed temple scene (mound near Talwindi): a mound with "around one thousand steps", a temple "of thick black stone", mud-and-stone rooms for jogis and fakirs, a tent city of traders at the base, a toy-cart offering, prasad of halwa, thread with seven knots tied to an acacia tree (pp. 23–24, folios 5–6).
- Jogis defined in the book's footnote: "religious ascetics who travel in groups and rely upon the generosity of devotees for food … Historically, their adherent[s] have come from both Hindu and Muslim backgrounds" (p. 20, folio 2).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- Auqaf booklet: the saint lived "a few years before the birth of Guru Nanak" and "Guru Nanak was born after Baba Naulakha Hazari prayed for his mother" (p. 31, folio 13).
- Shrine keeper: Naulakha, alone on the mound, prayed to Allah when thirsty "and miraculously, water appeared in this bowl" (p. 30, folio not stated).
- Folk story: Naulakha Hazari "used to ride a lion" (p. 32, folio 14).
- Reconstruction (attributed to the fakir character): the goddess trampled a rakshasa under the acacia tree, leaving her footprint on the rock, which shrinks with age; when it disappears "the world will cease to be" (pp. 26–27).
- Shahjamal buried "an entire city under his foot in wrath" (p. 31, folio 13).
- Reconstruction: Tripta became pregnant "Five months after her meeting with the fakir" (p. 27, folio not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- The author argues oral history matters more in South Asia than the British-derived preference for written records; folk tales record ancient mounds before archaeologists (Shahjamal, Taxila examples) (pp. 31–32, folios 13–14).
- The Naulakha story "might not give us an accurate account", but Dhillon's claim "reinforces my perception that Mata probably visited this mound where there must have been a temple" (p. 32, folio 14).
- The author concludes the Naulakha mound was likely an ancient temple sacred to Hindus and Muslims (most Muslims there "were Hindu converts anyway"), converted into a Muslim shrine after Partition (pp. 32–33, folios 14–15); Naulakha Hazari "must have been a Malamati Sufi" (p. 33, folio 15).
- The book contrasts Nanak's philosophy with "the politicisation of the religious philosophy of Sikhism as the institutio[n of] Guruhood rose" (p. 18, folio not stated).
