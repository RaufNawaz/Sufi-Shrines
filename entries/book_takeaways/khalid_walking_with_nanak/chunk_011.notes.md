- **katas-raj-temples** (the book's "Katas Raj"; travelogue): 
  - The author and his wife Anam, "driving back to Islamabad after a weekend in Lahore", stop at Katas Raj "to see Nanak's Gurdwara" (p. 245, folio 219).
  - The author had been to Katas Raj "several times" but never seen the building associated with Guru Nanak; "A few years ago" he came "to document the festival of Maha Shivratri" and the local guide said "there was no such building here" (p. 245, folio 219).
  - The author: "historical records clearly state Nanak visited this sacred site"; Iqbal Qaiser "had seen and photographed the place for his book" (p. 245, folio 219).
  - Location: "only a few kilometres from the citof [OCR?] [city of] Kallar Kahar on the Motorway"; the National Highway Authority was expanding the single-lane road; "Cement factories dotting this area" and lobbying to expand the road "had been intense" (p. 245, folio 219).
  - Complex as described: "the haveli of Hari Sigh [OCR?] [Singh] Nalwa", behind it "the Kashmiri temples dedicated to s),., [OCR?] [Shiva]" "built in the seventh and eighth century"; "half excavated stupa ... at least four thousand years old"; next to it "the temple dedicated to Ram"; "The sacred pond, believed to be the tear of Lord Shiva" at the base; across the road "the offices of the Archaeology Department" and "a small hotel" (p. 246, folio 220).
  - The pond "had recently been renovated" and was "brimming with water"; no garbage "like the last time"; groups of "Muslim boys" were diving into it from the rooftops (p. 246, folio 220).
  - The water level "had started to fall" because "the cement factories around this area were sucking the groundwater"; after writers and journalists wrote about it "the government woke from its slumber"; "The water level has now been restored" (p. 246, folio 220).
  - A tourist guide insisted "There is no gurdwara here"; by phone "Igbal [OCR?] [Iqbal] Qaiser" said "It is next to the fort", the only fort being one "built by Hari singh Nalwa" (p. 247, folio 221).
  - The gurdwara "was at a little distance from the complex. It was not renovated as the rest of the complex was"; the path "had been neglected"; "a big building, with several pillars that once supported a roof"; "The roof was now gone"; "a staircase leading nowhere"; "bigger than I had expected" (p. 247, folio 221).
  - Guide: "We never knew what this building was" (p. 247, folio 221).

  - Iqbal Qaiser's account: with "a friend called Ilyas Ghuman", "in the month of June", he climbed a wall of the fort and saw the gurdwara "just under the shadow of the wall of the fort"; they walked for hours through thorny shrubs, "fell on the steps of the gurdwara and passed out" (p. 248, folio 222).

- **gurdwara-dera-sahib**, **gurdwara-baoli-sahib-guru-arjan-dev-ji-lahore**, **gurdwara-panjvi-chati-patshahi** (Guru Arjan, person-level):
  - Chapter 17 "Guru Arjan" opens with a quotation of Jahangir: "In Goindwal which is on the river Beas, there was a Hindu named Arjan"; Khusro "happened to halt at a place where he was"; Arjan made on his forehead "a finger-mark in saffron which the Indians call Qashga"; his property handed "to Murtaza Khan"; he was to be "dealt with the penal Laws of Yasa" (p. 252, folio 226; footnoted to Gandhi, History of the Sikh Gurus Retold).
  - "For five days the Guru was tortured" (p. 252, folio 226).
  - In the walled city of Lahore "there is a place near the Rang Mahal known as ... Khoi [OCR?] [Lal Khoi?], of the Red Well"; "It is believed that Arjan was kept in a room next to this well, which was the haveli of the Mughal Diwan of Lahore" (p. 253, folio 227).
  - Torture as described: in summer heat, boiling [sand, word damaged] poured on his head while he was "immersed in a cauldron"; after five days "the body of the Guru gave up"; "His body was then thrown into the river" (p. 253, folio 227).
  - "near Lahore Fort there is a small yellow structure which is now referred to [as] the samadhi of Guru Agjan [OCR?] [Arjan]. It is believed that the Guru lost his life there" (p. 253, folio 227).
  - Sikh version: Mian Mir asked permission "to bury alive his oppressors. The Guru refused"; on the fifth day he took "a dip in the waters of the Ravi" and "did not emerge from the water" (p. 253, folio 227).
  - Traditional Sikh explanation: the "connivance of a Hindu Khatri called Chandu Lal", Diwan of Lahore, whose proposal of his daughter for Hargobind was refused, allied with Arjan's brother "Prithi Chand" (p. 253, folio 227).
  - Prithi Chand hid Arjan's letters from Lahore to Guru Ram Das; Ram Das "disowned Prithi Chand" and "A few days later he appointed Arjan as the next Guru" (pp. 253–254, folios 227 / not stated).
  - After the compilation of the Adi Granth, Arjan was summoned to court (via Prithi Chand) to explain its alleged "anti-Muslim and anti-Hindu content"; he sent "Bhai Gurdas" who "clarified the misunderstanding" (p. 254, folio not stated; footnoted to Singh, The History of Sikh Gurus).
  - "Mughal sources and particularly the Tuzk-e-Ja hangiri [OCR?] [Tuzk-e-Jahangiri]" are "glaringly silent about the role of Chandu Lal"; Jahangir "clearly states that Arjan was punished because of his sympathies for his rebel son Khusro" (p. 255, folio 229).
  - Author: Arjan was "crushed in the fight between the Mughal 'elephants'" (p. 255, folio 229); the assassination is "the Karbala of Sikh history" and "precipitated the development of the Khalsa" (pp. 255–256, folios 229–230).
  - "He was the last Guru in the line of the Gurus who focused solely on spiritual matters" (p. 256, folio 230).
  - Compilation of the "Adi Granth" "played a significant role in the development of a separate Sikh identity"; one reason given: rumours that "Meherban, the son of Prithi Chand" was compiling his own version (p. 256, folio 230).
  - The "system of Daswane [OCR?] [Daswandh]": followers give "one-tenth of their earnings" through the Masands; "a tradition that was started by Guru Arjan" (pp. 256–257, folios 230 / not stated).
  - Arjan "is also credited with the completion of the Amrit Sar the tank of nectar that was started by his father at Ramdaspur"; then "the Harmandir Sahib was constructed there" (p. 257, folio not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)
- The author contrasts the Sikh account (Chandu Lal's connivance) with Jahangir's own text, which names Khusro as the reason and is silent on Chandu Lal; he concludes Arjan was caught in Mughal succession politics (pp. 253–255, folios 227–229).
- Sikh sources on Prithi Chand are "biased against him", the author says (p. 254, folio not stated).
- The author judges the Adi Granth's compilation central to "a separate Sikh identity" (p. 256, folio 230).
- On Rohtas, the author reflects that Nanak's journey "is really not for the faint-hearted" (p. 250, folio 224); Iqbal Qaiser: "When you have nothing to lose, there is no fear" (p. 251, folio not stated; "sothing" [OCR?]).
