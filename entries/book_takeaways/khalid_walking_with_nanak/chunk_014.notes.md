  - In the author's reconstructed scene, Nanak tells his son Sri Chand, after an "outburst" from son and wife, that he will always be his son but "it is not in your destiny to be my spiritual descendant. That has to be Bhai Lehna." (p. 301, folio 275)
  - In the same reconstructed scene, Nanak spends the day walking in his wheat fields and by "the currents of the Ravi", then returns in the evening "to the gurdwara he had constructed", where Bhai Lehna and "Baba Budha, another loyal disciple of Nanak" wait; he tells Lehna he fears his sons "don't think like me" and his message "would be lost forever" (p. 301, folio 275). The chunk does not name the place of the scene.
  - In the reconstructed scene, Nanak calls Lehna "my angad (of one's body)" and names him "the best person to continue my mission after my death" (pp. 301–302, folios 275–276); he has Baba Budha "apply saffron paste on Bhai Lehna's forehead", puts "five copper coins and a coconut at Lehna's feet and bowed to him", and announces "I have appointed Bhai Lehna, who is from now on to be known as Guru Angad, as my successor. He is your second Guru." (p. 302, folio 276)
  - Back-matter biography: "1. Guru Nanak (1469-1539)", "the first Guru, is believed to be the founder of Sikhism"; the OCR then loses text, resuming with "pilgrimages including Banaras, Hardwar, Mecca and Baghdad, challenging their rituals, dogma, and the caste system" (p. 314, folio 288). Birth and Kartarpur settlement are in the Nankana Sahib and Kartarpur bullets below.
  - Guru Angad "was given the title of Guru Angad by Guru Nanak when he appointed him as his successor bypassing his Progeny" (p. 314, folio 288).
  - Guru Arjan "has the greatest number of hymns in the Guru Granth Sahib followed by Guru Nanak" (p. 315, folio 289); the Granth "contains the poetry of several Sikh Gurus including Guru Nanak" (p. 317, folio 291); Ram Rai "misquoted a poem by Guru Nanak" to appease Aurangzeb (p. 316, folio 290).
  - At the Kartarpur fields the author writes: "Some scholars have argued that the beauty of Nanak's message is that through his own example, he glorified peasantry" — work "should be treated as a sacred activity and hence an act of worship" (p. 303, folio 277).
  - The author: "Nanak was aware of these fluid boundaries. That's what he preached and that's what he wanted his sect to be"; instead "Sikhism, a distinct religion with its own exclusive boundaries, emerged out of Nanak's philosophy" (p. 310, folio 284).

  - "The main shrine was protected by a newly constructed wall."   - "Next to the showcase with the missile, there was a well, covered with marble"; a plaque says it "was the well that was used by Guru Nanak to water his fields"; sugarcane and a peasant working in the fields nearby (p. 303, folio 277).

## Practices, institutions, economy
- Kartarpur Sahib practices (Muslim grave veneration, fateha, langar funded by Muslim families, Rangers' firewood, cleaning as service, Indian pilgrims' darshan across the border, snakes placed by the shrine): see the gurdwara-darbar-sahib-kartarpur bullet (pp. 302–309).
- The author: "It is due to the pressure of these wealthy and powerful Sikhs that the government initially renovated the Sikh gurdwaras." (p. 303, folio 277)
- The author on the establishment: "paranoid that talking about minority issues would present a bad image of Pakistan"; "Instances of violence against Sikhs and Hindus were reported from all over the country during the three wars" (p. 306, folio 280).
- Masand system, daswandh (10%), revenue of the Guru's seat: see Ram Das and Arjan bullets (p. 315, folio 289). Amar Das: "it was under him[] the feudalisation of the Gurudom began" (p. 314, folio 288).
- The Granth "is provided with a special room in all Sikh Gurdwaras and is present at every Sikh ceremony" (p. 317, folio 291).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)
- Kartarpur plaque: a missile "dropped here by the Indian forces in 1971 but due to the miracle of Guru Nanak, it did not blow up" (p. 303, folio 277).
- Local Muslims' "myth" as told by Inderjit: "the river floods every twenty odd years and comes right up to the shrine. This is the river paying tribute to the shrine of Nanak." (p. 307, folio 281)
- Inderjit: a "giant snake" "bigger than two humans combined"; snakes found in the fields are placed next to the shrine: "After paying their respects they return. All they want to do is to catch a sight of the shrine." (p. 309, folio not stated)
- Har Rai: "It is asserted" Dara Shikoh recovered "through a medicine that was sent to him by Guru Har Rai" (p. 316, folio 290).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)
- The author: Sikh heritage at Kartarpur (the missile display) "was being used to further antagonise the two countries" rather than bring them together (p. 303, folio 277).
- The author: the grave of Nanak kept outside the rebuilt main building shows Muslim and Sikh communities "had drifted further apart" (p. 305, folio 279).
- The author: "The shrine of Nanak as it was meant to be, continued to be sacred for Muslims as well as Hindus and Sikhs." (p. 308, folio 282)
- The author: architectural distinctions between Muslim, Hindu and Sikh are "only vague distinctions as architectural influences crept from one tradition to another" (p. 309); "Islam carried forward a lot of pre-Islamic Arabian religious practices. Hinduism was immensely influenced by Buddhism in the first century CE." (p. 310, folio 284)
- The author: without Partition's line the Ravi "would have been a holy river, like it was at the time of the Vedas" (p. 307, folio 281).
