# The Adventures of Hir and Ranjha — takeaways

*Source: Waris Shah, *The Adventures of Hir and Ranjha*, "Recounted in Panjabi by Waris Shah / And
translated into English by / Charles Frederick Usborne, 1874 - 1919" (p. 1, folio not stated), with
a later unnamed annotator's numbered notes; 2004 edition. 57 pages, transcribed `text_layer`;
provenance in `out/ocr/waris_shah_hir_ranjha/`. Page references are PDF page indices; folio =
printed page number. **The folio rule `folio = PDF − 1` was measured on every numbered page of all
seven chunks and held without a single exception** (p. 2 = 1 through p. 57 = 56; p. 1 prints no
number at all), and it is independently corroborated by the contents list at seven separate chapter
openings (p. 1, folio not stated). Reviewed: no.*

## What this book is

A Panjabi verse *qissa* — the Hir–Ranjha romance — rendered as continuous English prose across 32
chapters, an Invocation and an Epilogue, printed two columns to the page with a numbered "Notes"
block closing each chapter. The narrative itself yields almost nothing to this archive; **virtually
all of the archive-usable material sits in the notes**, in the Invocation, and in the Epilogue,
where Waris Shah speaks in his own voice and states his residence, his teacher, his date and his
place of composition.

**The edition has two annotating hands and marks only one of them.** Where a note is the
translator's it says so — "(Usborne's note.)" (p. 39, folio 38), "Usborne's note." (p. 28, folio
27), and note 8 on the last page is signed "— C. F. Usborne" (p. 57, folio 56). Every other note is
a later annotator's: he glosses Usborne in the third person ("Shroff woman — **Usborne means** a
woman belonging to a goldsmith family", p. 20, folio 19), corrects him by name ("`Ulsborne`
[text-layer?: Usborne] thought it was indigo, which is incorrect", p. 33, folio 32), and writes from
after Partition ("in West Pakistan", "now renamed Sahiwal", p. 29, folio 28; "a valuable export from
Pakistan", p. 26, folio 25). **The annotator is never named anywhere in the 57 pages** —
(not stated). His stance is debunking and sceptical: he corrects the poet on law, doctrine, history
and chronology, deflates the poem's supernatural machinery ("God `i5` [text-layer?: is] dragged in
here quite unnecessarily", p. 29, folio 28), and reads the poem throughout as evidence for a
vanished social order, in the recurring formula "**in the old Punjab**" with a present-tense
contrast attached each time.

**Reliability.** Usborne states his own editorial policy in his signed note: "This interesting but
somewhat rambling epilogue is translated at full length without any attempt at excision or
condensation. **The rest of the poem has been considerably condensed but `noting` [text-layer?]
[nothing] important has been omitted.** — C. F. Usborne" (p. 57, folio 56). He also removes one
passage by name, "(There follows in the text a tirade against Jats generally… it has nothing much to
do with the story so it is omitted here.)" (pp. 27–28, folios 26–27). **So only the Epilogue is
unabridged, on the translator's own statement.** The `text_layer` of the published file is heavily
damaged: **281 `[text-layer?]` flags across the seven chunk-note files**, inventoried by class under
Doubts. **No Panjabi, Shahmukhi, Gurmukhi, Persian or Arabic script is printed anywhere in the
book** — every name, term and date is romanized in Latin script, so the protocol's "quote the
original script where it is printed" rule cannot be exercised for any fact in this file.

### mausoleum-of-waris-shah — Mausoleum of Waris Shah

The archive row whose principal figure wrote the book. He is named on the running header of every
page, in the title block, in the annotator's notes, and — six times on the last page alone — in his
own voice. The damaged forms of his name are `Wads Shah` (p. 16, folio 15), `Wan, Shah` (p. 31,
folio 30) and `Wails Shah` (p. 45, folio 44), each [text-layer?: Waris Shah].

- **Saint**
  - *Residence and teacher — the single most citable sentence in the book for this row.* "**Waris
    Shah lives at Jandiala and is the pupil of the Saint of Kasur5**" (p. 57, folio 56). The book
    prints **"Jandiala"** alone and never "Jandiala Sher Khan"; it attaches **no tomb, grave, shrine,
    ʿurs or custodian** to the place, and the verb is present-tense residence. The teacher's name
    comes only from the annotator's note 5 and is damaged: "Saint of Kasur - **Mamdum Mafia Ghulamn
    Murtaza of Kasur** [text-layer?] — see Introduction, note 4." (p. 57, folio 56).
  - *Birthplace.* "Otherwise his wrath will descend on us like a sudden invasion of Ahmad Shahs and
    **God save Jandiala (the birthplace of the poet)**." (p. 43, folio 42) — a parenthesis set inside
    a verse line, and again "Jandiala" without "Sher Khan". **Birthplace and residence, never burial**
    — (not stated).
  - *Kasur, the one place he says he fears for.* "**Of all the wretched Punjab I am most concerned
    for Kasur.** I am concerned for my faith and conscience even as Moses was frightened on Holy
    Sinai." (p. 56, folio 55).
  - *Lineage.* The only statement of it is Luddan the ferryman's boast: "Even the son of a Pir like
    Waris,' [text-layer?] [Waris⁴] we will not take into our boat for nothing." (p. 6, folio 5), with
    note 4: "This is a reference to Waris Shah, the poet himself." (p. 6, folio 5).
  - *Affiliation — plural, and deliberately not named.* "It is not unusual for a person to owe
    allegiance to more than one sect of the Sufis. **This is the case with Waris Shah.**" (p. 2,
    folio 1, note 2). **The note does not say which sects.** It stands in a passage whose subject is
    the Qadiriyya (Abdul Qadir Gilani) and the Chishtiyya (Shakarganj), but it does not say those are
    his two. **No silsila, order or chain is named for him anywhere in the 57 pages** — the row's
    "Chishti" is (not stated), and so is its death year 1798.
  - *The woman he is said to have loved.* "Bhag Bhari — this is an apostrophe to the woman with whom
    the poet `Wails` [text-layer?] [Waris] Shah fell in love. `Ujsborne's` [text-layer?] [Usborne's]
    note.) As explained in earlier notes, `Thi5` [text-layer?] [this] need not necessarily have been
    her real name." (p. 45, folio 44). She is addressed by name in the verse: "Bhag Bhari,5 help me
    to meet my Ranjha." (p. 45, folio 44). The annotator adds an inference, explicitly hedged: "he
    must have wished for some such miracle for himself and **Bhag Bhari** as well, but it does not
    seem to have happened." (p. 51, folio 50).
  - *Aid claimed from a named saint.* "**With the aid of Shakarganj7 I have conferred this benefit on
    the world.**" (p. 57, folio 56) — the only relation the book states between the poet and any
    named saint other than his teacher. His Invocation is also reported to credit Abdul Qadir Gilani
    with power over destiny: "`Wan, Shah` [text-layer?: Waris Shah] himself, in speaking of Mohiuddin
    Abdul Qadir Gilani in the 'Invocation' says that he can score out even the writing of the Pen of
    Destiny." (p. 31, folio 30, note 7).
- **Composition — four statements of the date, and they do not agree. Keep all four side by side.**
  - The poet's own, in the Epilogue: "When love came to me I felt a desire to write this story in
    verse. **I wrote it in the year 1180 Hijri in the southern country. 2**" (p. 56, folio 55).
    Printed only as "1180 Hijri", in Latin script; **no Hijri numerals and no CE equivalent are
    given**.
  - The annotator, twice, in CE: "The tragic incident may have been present in Waris Shah's mind when
    he wrote **his Hir in 1766**." (p. 12, folio 11, note 4); and "It was the year 120 of the
    Bikramajit era. **(These two dates do not exactly correspond, but the poem was written in AD.
    1766.)**" (p. 57, folio 56, note 2 — "AD." punctuated as printed; the note itself admits the
    disagreement and shows no working).
  - The annotator, once, differently: "To Waris Shah, **who wrote his poem in `i 768` [text-layer?:
    1768]**, the invasion was a recent memory" (p. 33, folio 32, note 2). **The printed form is not a
    number**; the reading 1768 rests on the same sentence's contrast with Nadir Shah's invasion "`In
    1739` [text-layer?: in 1739]" as "a recent memory".
  - The annotator, once, by arithmetic he does not perform: Ahmad Shah Abdali "broke the Mahratta
    power at the third Battle of Panipat in `i1761` [text-layer?] [1761], **five years before Hir was
    written**." (p. 44, folio 43, note 8). **The book does not print the resulting year.**
  - *Place of composition.* "When I produced the tale among learned men it became known to the world.
    Waris, those who have recited the Holy Kalima have attained salvation. **Kharal Hans is a well
    known place. Here I composed this story.**" (p. 56, folio 55); and "the southern country" (p. 56,
    folio 55), glossed by Usborne: "Southern country — Usborne's note says: **Lammnan Des is the
    present Montgomery district of the Punjab.**" (p. 57, folio 56, note 2). **Jandiala Sher Khan is
    never named as a place of composition** — (not stated). See Doubts on "Kharal Hans".
  - *Commission and authorization.* "My friends came to me and said, 'Write for us afresh the
    forgotten story of the Love of Hir.' So we have written it right cunningly and plucked a new rose
    in the Garden of Poetry." (p. 2, folio 1); "By the Grace of God I have fulfilled the request of my
    dear friends." (p. 57, folio 56); "**Having finished the story I presented it to my master for his
    acceptance.**" (p. 57, folio 56). **The page does not say whether "my master" is the Saint of
    Kasur or another person** — (not stated). No patron, payment or occasion is named.
- **Practices & Events**
  - *A manuscript economy stated as a prayer.* "May he who reads or copies my poor efforts derive
    pleasure." and "**May God pardon him who copies these words and give his bounty to those who
    recite it.** May its readers enjoy the book." (p. 57, folio 56) — copyists, reciters and readers
    named as three distinct classes.
  - *Merit accounted commercially.* "Waris Shah, I have no capital of good works." (p. 57, folio 56).
- **Significance**
  - *His own claim for the poem, made repeatedly and immodestly.* "Poets, you should determine the
    worth of any poem. I have let my horse loose in the arena. Other poets have wasted their efforts
    in writing on petty themes.3 **I have composed a grand poem.'**" (p. 56, folio 55); "Only the
    poet's poetry remains `m` [text-layer?] [in] everlasting remembrance, **for no one has written
    such a beautiful Hir**." (p. 55, folio 54); "There are none so fortunate as the Kheras, no one so
    full of sin as the Poet Waris and there is no spell like that of his poetry."" (p. 39, folio 38);
    "Waris Shah, your name will be famous, if God be kind." and "Waris Shah has shed the light of his
    genius over all the faithful in the world." (p. 57, folio 56). Against these, his self-abasement:
    "I have no capital of good works. Of what can I be proud? I have no hope without Thy grace. I am
    only a poor sinner. Without the favour of the Prophet I am helpless." (p. 56, folio 55) and "`0
    Almighty` [text-layer?] [O Almighty] God, overlook the fault of poor Waris Shah." (p. 57,
    folio 56).
  - *The poem declared to have an inner sense — by the author, not an editor.* "Oh wise man, you
    should note that **there is a secret under the guise of my words**. I have written this Hir with
    care and meditation… I have planted a flower to give a sweet savour." (p. 56, folio 55); "**I have
    written it as a parable.**" (p. 57, folio 56); "The story of true lovers is like the scent of a
    rose in a garden. **He who reads it with love in his heart will be able to separate the true from
    the false.**" (p. 57, folio 56); and the figure itself, "**Waris Shah is anxious to see God's face
    even as Hir longed for her lover.** I make my request before the Holy Court of `Cod`
    [text-layer?] [God], who is the Lord of Mercy." (p. 57, folio 56). His disclaimer: "If I have let
    fall a word in ignorance may God forgive me." (p. 57, folio 56).
  - *The poet as a character in his own poem.* He intervenes as a named commentator throughout:
    "(Quoth the poet: 'This is not a boat but a marriage procession.')" (p. 6, folio 5); "(Poet Waris:
    'None can withstand when eyes fight eyes in the tourney of love.')" (p. 8, folio 7); "(Quoth the
    poet: 'See what a net of deceit Hir, the Jat girl, has spread.')2" (pp. 9–10, folios 8–9); "Ask
    Waris the poet, he knows this well." (p. 12, folio 11); "Waris,9 I shall be called the Hir of
    Ranjha in Heaven, in the assembly of Fatima, the daughter of the Holy Prophet." (p. 17, folio 16);
    "Waris, if you trust in God, he will give you your portion of the good things of this world."
    (p. 18, folio 17); "**May the curse of the Poet Waris Shah fall upon you, Hir, for you have robbed
    us of our dear brother-in-law.**" (p. 22, folio 21); "(Quoth the poet: Qazis have no fear of God
    and eat the bread of iniquity.')22" (p. 26, folio 25); "Oh poet Waris Shah! If I hear that my
    lover is returning, I will offer sweet cakes and light my lamps with rich butter for oil.'5"
    (p. 41, folio 40).
  - *The annotator's verdicts on him, kept as given.* Technique: "Waris Shah, like most Persian and
    Urdu poets who have written poetical romances, has a habit of making a personal appearance on the
    stage whenever it pleases him, and even entering into conversation with his characters at will. He
    often stands aside to deliver an `obiter didunh` [text-layer?] [dictum], or like the Greek chorus,
    make comment on the `event, of` [text-layer?] [events of] the story" (pp. 17–18, folios 16–17,
    note 9). Negative: "The poet's intervention as a commentator at this stage, however traditional in
    character, is utterly `unnecessary` [text-layer?] [unnecessary.]" (p. 10, folio 9, note 2); "Here
    the poet has jumped quite unnecessarily into the fray… even though on the wrong side" (p. 22,
    folio 21, note 5). Positive: "Self-approbation is quite common among Persian and Urdu poets. **As
    compared with most of them Waris Shah is modest — and true.**" (p. 40, folio 39, note 18).
    Defensive: "This is more an expression of sympathy with Hir in her distress on the part of Waris
    Shah than a personal opinion, although he certainly seems to be angry with this particular Qazi."
    (p. 27, folio 26, note 22); "Here Waris Shah is acting merely as a mouthpiece of popular opinion or
    sentiment, and does not `necesarily` [text-layer?] [necessarily] wish to convey personal
    approbation or condemnation." (p. 13, folio 12, note 9). Hostile: "The Five `Pin` [text-layer?]
    [Pirs], or Saints, as `Wads Shah` [text-layer?] [Waris Shah] presents them, seem to be concerned
    more with the success of an illicit love affair than with any idea of social morality!" (p. 16,
    folio 15, note 10). And as a period marker: "Arabic verbs, alas, are `s` [text-layer?] [as]
    mysterious today as they were in Waris Shah's days." (p. 40, folio 39, note 17).
  - *The poem as usable historical evidence — the annotator says so.* "The latter half of the
    eighteenth century, to which Waris Shah is referring here, marked the decline of the Mughal Empire
    in India, and was a period of great confusion and disturbance… **Waris Shah's description of the
    conditions of his day is probably not exaggerated.**" (p. 57, folio 56, note 1), glossing the
    Epilogue's catalogue of disorder quoted under Cross-cutting material.
  - *Place-level, under ruling (a).* **Sheikhupura District is named once**, and not of this row: the
    Ghazi Pir shrine is "buried at the foot of the hillock near Shah Kot in District Sheikhupura in
    West Pakistan", and Sheikhupura is one of the five districts whose people "even now congregate
    annually at this shrine" (p. 29, folio 28, note 4). `mausoleum-of-waris-shah` is the archive's
    only Sheikhupura row, so that material attaches here at place level. **The book draws no
    connection whatever between Ghazi Pir and Waris Shah** — see Doubts.

### shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar — Shrine of Fariduddin Ganjshakar

The book names him in six forms and never once as "Fariduddin Ganjshakar" in full: **"Pir Chishti
Shakarganj"**, **"Shakarganj"**, **"The Shakarganj of Pakpattan"**, **"the holy saint of
Pakpattan"**, **"Farid Shakarganj of Pakpattan"**, **"Fariduddin Shakarganj"**, and — in the
annotator's voice only — **"Baba Farid"**.

- **Saint**
  - *The identification that secures the mapping, given flatly by the annotator on the book's last
    page.* Note 7: "**Shakarganj is the saint of Pakpattan.** See notes on Introduction." (p. 57,
    folio 56).
  - *The order, stated twice.* "**The Shakarganj of Pakpattan mentioned here belonged to The
    Chishtiyya Sect.**" (p. 2, folio 1, note 2); and "**The Chishtiyya sect of Sufis, which counts
    Fariduddin Shakarganj among its leaders**, regards music (Sama) as a permissible means of
    experiencing spiritual exaltation." (p. 19, folio 18, note 1 to Ch. 12) — attached to Ranjha's
    playing the flute before the Five Pirs, and the only silsila named in that whole chunk.
  - *Pakpattan as home and burial place — the annotator's, not the poem's.* Note 4: "Much as one
    would like to agree with Waris Shah this is not historically correct. The Punjab has had plenty
    of troubles **even after Baba Farid honoured it by making it his home and his place of burial!**"
    (p. 2, folio 1). **This is the only statement of a burial place for any archive figure in the
    Invocation chapter.** The identification Shakarganj = Baba Farid is made here by the annotator,
    not by the poem.
  - **No death date, no custodian, no ʿurs month, no shrine fabric and no building date appear
    anywhere in the book** — the row's d. 1266 is (not stated).
- **Shrine / Darbar**
  - *A shrine fair, its pilgrims and the physical act, in one sentence used as a measure of crowd
    size.* "The crowd and the noise was as great as at the **Fairs of Pakpattan** or Nigah or Rattan
    or Thamman, **where women flock to kiss the tomb of the saint and attain the achievement of their
    desires**." (p. 24, folio 23). Three usable facts: Pakpattan holds a Fair; **women** are the
    actors named; the act is **kissing the tomb**, and its purpose is "to attain the achievement of
    their desires". No date, month, duration, custodian, offering or fee — all (not stated). The
    sentence never names the saint; the mapping is by place. See Doubts.
- **Legends & Miracles**
  - *His arrival delivers the Punjab — and the annotator denies it in the same breath.* Waris Shah's
    own Invocation: "Praise also to the beloved Pir Chishti Shakarganj. **When Shakarganj made his
    abode at Pakpattan, the Punjab was delivered of all its troubles.4**" (p. 2, folio 1); against
    note 4, quoted above.
  - *One of the Five Pirs who meet Ranjha, named second of the five.* "Good fortune however came to
    him and he met the Five `Pirs'` [text-layer?] [Pirs¹] on the way. First came Khwaja Khizr, lord of
    all the waters, **then Shakarganj, the holy saint of Pakpattan**." (p. 10, folio 9).
  - *He gives Ranjha a kerchief, and the relic is later used.* "The Five Pirs were gracious unto
    Ranjha, Khizr gave him a turban tuft,' Sayyid Jalal a dagger, `Zalkaria` [text-layer?] [Zakaria]
    a thick blanket, Lal Shahbaz a ring, **and Shakarganj a kerchief**. And they said, 'No one will do
    you harm. God has made you the owner of these cattle.'" (p. 11, folio 10); and at the crisis, "at
    midnight he remembered the Five Pirs. **He kissed the handkerchief of Shakarganj** and took the
    ring of Lal Shahbaz" (p. 50, folio 49).
  - *He is named as the Five Pirs' first-ranked "spiritual celebrity".* "the Five Pirs number among
    themselves such spiritual celebrities, as Khwaja Khizr, **Farid Shakarganj of Pakpattan**,
    Shahbaz Qalandar of Sind, Baha-ud-Din Zakaria and Makhdum `Jalianian` [text-layer?] [Jahanian] of
    Multan." (p. 16, folio 15, note 10).
- **Significance**
  - *Credited by Waris Shah with the poem itself.* "**With the aid of Shakarganj7 I have conferred
    this benefit on the world.**" (p. 57, folio 56).

### tilla-jogian — Tilla Jogian

The book's forms are **"'Tilla'"**, **"Tilla"**, **"your hill of Tilla"** and, in the annotator's
note, **"'Tilla Guruan' (the mound of the Gurus)"**. **"Tilla Jogian" is never written, Gorakhnath
is never named, and neither the Salt Range nor Dina is mentioned anywhere in the book** — all
(not stated). See Doubts: this mapping is on the site, not on the row's principal figure.

- **Shrine / Darbar**
  - *The site, its resident guru and his position in a lineage.* "So Ranjha set off for 'Tilla', the
    hill where `Balnath'` [text-layer?: Balnath¹] the Jogi dwelt, for he said to himself, 'Balnath
    surely knows the way of salvation.'" (p. 31, folio 30); "At last, after many days' journeying,
    Ranjha reached Tilla the hill on which Balnath lived" (p. 31, folio 30); and the note: "**According
    to tradition, Balnath was the fourth Guru of 'Tilla Guruan' (the mound of the Gurus) on the banks
    of the river Jhelum.**" (p. 33, folio 32, note 1).
  - *The site reported as surviving and the cult as still practised — the annotator's present tense,
    not the poet's.* "**The place is still preserved, and the 'Jog Mat' or the cult of the wandering
    mystics, with its emphasis on pantheism, is still practised there.**" (p. 33, folio 32, note 1).
    **No building, structure, custodian, ʿurs, date or district is given** — all (not stated).
  - *The resident community.* A body of **Chelas** under Balnath with "a house and kitchen" and a
    fixed term of service: "'For eighteen years we have been serving him. We have given up all and
    live by begging. All day and night we remember God. Yet he does not give us Jog.'" and "They left
    the Jogi's house and kitchen. They pierced the Guru with their shameless taunts." (p. 32, folio
    31). At Ranjha's arrival: "They were all engaged in religious contemplation and prayers. They were
    reading Gayan Gita Bhagvat and Bharat" (p. 31, folio 30).
- **Practices & Events**
  - *The initiation into Jog, itemized rite by rite — the fullest ritual description in the book.*
    "They obeyed the orders of their Guru and brought Balnath **the earrings** as he had told them,
    and **the razor** wherewith to shave Ranjha. And the Guru **took off Ranjha's clothes** and having
    **rubbed him in ashes** and **embraced him**, made him sit by his side. Then he took the razor of
    separation and **shaved him completely**. Then he **bored his ears and put earrings on him**. He
    gave him **the beggar's bowl, the rosary, the horn and the shell** in his hands, and **made him
    learn the word `Allah.1` [text-layer?: Allah¹⁴]**." (p. 32, folio 31). The moral rule taught with
    it: "'An old woman should be treated as your mother and a young woman as your sister.'" (p. 32,
    folio 31).
  - *The conditions of Jog as Balnath states them.* "You will have to dress as a Jogi, to wear dirty
    clothes, long hair, cropped skull and to beg your way through life. You will have to give up the
    pleasures of birth, to cease to rejoice when friends come or to grieve when they die. You will
    have to abstain from casting eyes on women. You will have to become divinely intoxicated by taking
    kand, mul, post, opium and other narcotic drugs.'" (p. 31, folio 30); and the pilgrimages demanded
    of a Jogi, "You will have to go on long pilgrimages to Jagannath, Godavari, Ganges and Jamna."
    (p. 31, folio 30).
  - *The offering placed before the guru.* "Ranjha bowed his head and placed a piece of gur3 before
    Balnath as an offering, and clasped the feet of all the Jogis." (p. 31, folio 30), with note 3:
    "Gur is raw sugar. **It is well known as a ceremonial offering in the Punjab countryside.** An
    invitation to a wedding or other social occasion accompanied by `gun` [text-layer?: gur] cannot be
    trifled with." (p. 33, folio 32).
  - *Ear-boring as the act that makes a Jogi, repeated four times across the book.* "He went to Tilla
    and got his ears bored and became a Jogi. He has today entered your village." (p. 41, folio 40);
    "Perhaps he has obeyed my word and got his ears bored." (p. 40, folio 39); "Fate has shaved your
    beard and bored your ears." (p. 43, folio 42); "He has bored his ears and has become a Jogi for my
    sake." (p. 45, folio 44). The marks are removed again at the end: "They took the Jogi's rings out
    of his ears. They `shaved'` him and put a rich turban on his head" (p. 54, folio 53).
  - *Cross-confessional discipleship, stated as normal for its time.* "**Here Ranjha, a Muslim,
    becomes a spiritual disciple of Balnath, a Hindu yogi. This may seem strange today, but was by no
    means unusual at the time.**" (p. 33, folio 32, note 4). And the parallel institutions: "A Murshid
    is a spiritual teacher." (note 4); "**Guru — the Hindu holy man, the teacher, the Hindu
    counterpart of the Muslim Murshid. Gurus have Chelas' and Murshids have Murids', although the
    bonds of spiritual subjugation are similar in both cases.**" (p. 33, folio 32, note 5).
  - *Departure.* "So Ranjha made haste to leave Tilla and he collected strange herbs and potent roots
    from the woods" (p. 34, folio 33); and "Had I known that you would try to keep me from my Love, I
    would never have set foot on your hill of Tilla." (p. 32, folio 31).
- **Legends & Miracles**
  - *The Guru's enchantment subdues the rebellious Chelas.* "Whereupon the Guru rebuked them and his
    anger blazed from his eyes. The Chelas instantly obeyed him, **so powerful was the enchantment
    that the Guru laid upon them**. All ill feeling vanished from their minds." (p. 32, folio 31).

## Cross-cutting material

*Practices, institutions and economy first, then the edition's own arguments. **No ʿurs, mela,
langar, sajjada nashin, Auqaf, endowment, land grant, revenue figure, visitor number, building date,
architectural description or shrine dispute appears anywhere in the 57 pages** — recorded as an
explicit negative. The shrine apparatus the archive usually collects is simply absent from this
book; what it has instead is a dense note-apparatus on pirs, fakirs, jogis, offerings and village
institutions.*

### What a Pir is, and what makes a shrine important

- *The definition, given twice.* "'Pir' is a person who is regarded as a spiritual leader. The
  disciples and followers of a Pir are known as Murids'. **Literally the word 'Pir' means an old
  man.**" (p. 2, folio 1, note 2). And, more fully: "'Pir' is literally an old man. **It has come to
  mean a saint, dead or alive.**" (p. 11, folio 10, note 1).
- **The annotator's general theory of shrine standing — the single most transferable sentence in the
  book.** "**The importance of a Pir' depends on the number of his disciples, or the visitors and
  offerings to his tomb, as the case may `he` [text-layer?] [be].** See also Invocation, note 2."
  (p. 11, folio 10, note 1). Living pirs are ranked by disciples, dead ones by footfall and offerings.
- *Multiple simultaneous allegiance stated as normal.* "**It is not unusual for a person to owe
  allegiance to more than one sect of the Sufis.**" (p. 2, folio 1, note 2), in a passage that names
  two orders — "the Qadiriyya Sect of the Sufis" and "The Chishtiyya Sect".
- *The ranks of the saintly hierarchy.* "Qutub tara is the polar star. **Qutub also means a great
  saint. 'Qutub', 'Abdal' 'Ghaus', etc., are stages of promotion in the saintly hierarchy.**" (p. 26,
  folio 25, note 20). **No saint in the book is said to hold any of these ranks.**
- *Intercession asserted as necessary.* "Let us also praise the holiness of Pirs;2 **without the help
  of holy men our boat cannot reach the shore.**" and "it is meet and proper to praise God and invoke
  the help of Saints and Prophets before essaying this story of Love." (p. 2, folio 1).
- *The fear of a holy man's displeasure, as the exact counterpart of hope in his blessing.* "This may
  seem strange in the mouth of a faqir who is dedicated to the service of God. In actual fact, **the
  displeasure of a faqir is as much feared and avoided as his blessing is sought after.**" (p. 40,
  folio 39, note 21). In the verse: "Beware, the fakir is dangerous. Do not tease him or he will cause
  trouble." (p. 43, folio 42); "Go and fall at the feet of the fakir or his curse will fall on you."
  (p. 43, folio 42); "**If I tell my Pir he will ruin your family.**" (p. 43, folio 42).
- *Power over destiny attributed to holy men, and reported as a current belief.* "**According to
  popular superstition in the Punjab countryside `Pin` [text-layer?: Pirs], Fakirs and Jogis can,
  with, the force of their piety, make the Moving Finger of Destiny cancel what it has written.**"
  (p. 31, folio 30, note 7); and "**Many people still pray to the Pirs more readily than to God.**"
  (p. 29, folio 28, note 6).
- *Rank governs the outcome of a petition.* The son who "stood with pride before him" is beaten out of
  the hut; the father who comes "with folded hands" is granted (pp. 49–50, folios 48–49). "Go to him
  at once **as a submissive subject with a present in your hand**, for a new governor has been
  appointed to rule over us." (p. 47, folio 46).
- *The vocabulary of religious status, each term defined by the edition.* "A 'faqir' is a poor man, a
  mendicant. **It also means a saint.**" (p. 11, folio 10, note 5); "Faqir is a mendicant." (p. 6,
  folio 5, note 3); "'Darvesh originally meant a wandering mystic. **Later, when the institution
  deteriorated, the word came to mean a mendicant living on charity.**" (p. 14, folio 13, note 6);
  "Malang — a free-living mendicant." (p. 19, folio 18, note 4); "A Bairagi is a holy mendicant, a
  homeless wanderer, who has severed all human ties. `H1e` [text-layer?] [He] is virtually a Jogi, but
  possibly a more hard-boiled one." (p. 39, folio 38); "Bairag-Sanyas, or renunciation of the world…
  **This is the last state of human life according to the Hindus.**" (p. 40, folio 39); "A mahant is a
  saintly devotee, a Guru." (p. 45, folio 44); "A Murshid is a spiritual teacher." and "Guru — the
  Hindu holy man… the Hindu counterpart of the Muslim Murshid." (p. 33, folio 32, notes 4, 5).
- **"Darbar" is glossed as a court, never as a shrine complex.** "`Darhar` [text-layer?] [Darbar] is
  the court of a king or notable." (p. 11, folio 10, note 4), glossing "Hir has been bestowed on you
  by the Darbar of God." (p. 11, folio 10); note 18 glosses "the Darbar18 of God" with the single word
  "Court." (p. 34, folio 33); and the one worldly darbar in the book is a ruler's, reached by bribe
  and nazrana (p. 43, folio 42; p. 44, folio 43, note 12).

### Fairs, offerings, vows and relics

- **Shrine fairs and tomb-kissing by women — the book's one description of shrine practice.** "The
  crowd and the noise was as great as at the Fairs of Pakpattan or Nigah or Rattan or Thamman, **where
  women flock to kiss the tomb of the saint and attain the achievement of their desires.**" (p. 24,
  folio 23). Four named fair-sites; women as the named pilgrims; the physical act; and the stated
  transaction. **The comparison itself is demographic — a shrine fair is the largest crowd the poet
  can name.**
- **An annual week-long congregation with its catchment named by district.** "The people of
  Montgomery (now renamed Sahiwal), Jhang, Sheikhupura, `Lyahlpur` [text-layer?: Lyallpur] and
  Sargodha Districts **even now congregate annually at this shrine and the celebrations last for more
  than a week**." (p. 29, folio 28, note 4). No month, ʿurs name, attendance figure or custodian —
  all (not stated).
- *Offerings at tombs, to secure a granted prayer.* "I long to meet him and **have given offerings to
  the tomb of Hasan and Husain,' to Shudaa Ghazi and Bhola Pir that my `player` [text-layer?: prayer]
  may be fulfilled.**" (p. 30, folio 29). The offering is not itemized — (not stated).
- **A cluster of four distinct votive practices in five lines, each conditional on an outcome.** "Come
  and set your `font` [text-layer?] [foot] in our courtyard. **We will present offerings to `Clod`
  [text-layer?] [God]** if you come back to Hazara. **We will dedicate a saucepan3 to the name of `Mi`
  [text-layer?].** **We will hold a wrestling match and offer garlands to Ghazi Pir.** Have we not
  promised to **light the lamps in honour of Khawaja Khizr** if you return to us?" (p. 28, folio 27).
  Note 3 of that chapter does not gloss the dedication; the recipient's name is damaged.
- *The dedicated vessel explained elsewhere.* "Saucepan is probably a reference to '**koondah**', an
  outsize dish which `i5` [text-layer?: is] filled with sweet preparations and **distributed to the
  poor `hi` [text-layer?: in] the name of members of the Prophets family and his descendants**."
  (p. 29, folio 28, note 3).
- *Lamp-lighting as a living vow, and the annotator's statement that the practice continues.* "Oh poet
  Waris Shah! If I hear that my lover is returning, **I will offer sweet cakes and light my lamps with
  rich butter for oil.'5**" (p. 41, folio 40), with note 5: "A proverb common to Punjabi and Urdu.
  'Rich butter' here is intended for ghee or melted butter. **Lighting lamps in mosque even with
  ordinary oil is still a well-known way of expression of gratitude to God for His favours. Punjabi
  mothers still promise lamp-lighting in mosques when they pray for their children,**" (p. 41,
  folio 40).
- *An offering brought to a holy man, itemized with cash.* "So Sehti filled a big dish with sugar and
  cream and covered it with a cloth and **put five rupees therein**. Then she went to the garden of
  Kalabagh and stood with her offering near the Jogi." (p. 45, folio 44) — against the next page's
  "five farthings5" and "sugar `andrice` [text-layer?] [and rice]" (p. 46, folio 45); see Doubts. The
  currency is the translator's: "**This is Usborne's expression. A farthing is an English coin quite
  unknown to Ranjha or Sehti. The reference in the text is to a small local coin.**" (p. 46, folio 45,
  note 6).
- **Relics of named saints, carried and used as instruments of power — the book's densest
  archive-usable passage.** "at midnight he remembered the Five Pirs. **He kissed the handkerchief of
  Shakarganj and took the ring of Lal Shahbaz; he smelt the sweet savour that came from the cudgel of
  Sayyad Jalal of Bokhara, -and he grasped the dagger given him by Makhdum `Jabnian` [text-layer?]
  [Jahanian].**" (p. 50, folio 49). Two are then used as weapons (p. 52, folio 51). **Four relic types
  — handkerchief, ring, cudgel, dagger — each tied to a different named saint**, and all four descend
  from the Five Pirs' original gifts (p. 11, folio 10).
- *Gur as the offering placed before a guru* (p. 31, folio 30; p. 33, folio 32, note 3, quoted above).
- *Falling at the feet as submission, and as remedy for offence.* "You should catch hold of the feet
  of the fakir in humility and with supplication instead of quarrelling with him." (p. 42, folio 41);
  "**Remember Alexander touched the feet of a fakir and then he conquered the fort of Daz. A fakir
  gave his blessing to Timurlane and sovereignty remained in his family for several generations.**"
  (p. 43, folio 42) — on which the annotator: "All this, of course, is poetic invention rather than
  history." (p. 44, folio 43). Also the buffaloes conciliated "**by kissing his feet**" (p. 27,
  folio 26), and seven prostrations glossed as Mughal court ritual: "Seven low curtseys, a milder
  version of the Chinese kow-tow, were part of the ritual of the Mughal Court." (p. 11, folio 10,
  note 3).

### The mendicant economy

- *The recruiting pitch, which is the book's clearest statement of what a fakir's status is worth.*
  "'Brothers come with me and be a fakir. **You have nothing to do but beg and eat. You get your ears
  bored and put some ashes on your body and all the world reveres you as a saint.** Without toil or
  labour you can be as eminent as `Nadir Shah?` [text-layer?: Nadir Shah²]… He sleeps in the mosque
  free from the cares of this impure world and begs and eats. He can scold people and incur no blame.
  **If you call yourself a Pir or a fakir everybody is your servant.**'" (p. 31, folio 30). The
  annotator's reply: "**The desire to achieve social or spiritual eminence by living on other people
  is an eternal temptation for those who suffer from ergophobia.**" (p. 33, folio 32, note 2).
- *Alms in kind, graded by grain, with the fakir's reputation staked on what he will accept.* "Some
  gave him flour, others bread, others dishes of food. They asked for his blessings and he invoked
  blessings upon them. **Some said, 'We shall acquire holiness through the power of his
  intercession.'**" — against "Said another, See he takes wheat `flower` [text-layer?: flour] and
  butter, but will not touch millet or bread crusts. **He is chasing the women and is no real
  fakir.**'" (p. 37, folio 36). And: "Let us give this fakir alms and turn him out Give him a handful
  of millet" (p. 42, folio 41); "You `ate` [text-layer?] [are] shaving my beard in giving me mere
  bird's food.'" (p. 42, folio 41); "Why do you find fault with millet? All Jats eat it. **It is the
  food of the hungry and `povertystricken` [text-layer?] [poverty-stricken]. It is the father and
  mother of the poor.**" (p. 42, folio 41); glossed "Birds food — **a proud beggar indeed!** Ranjha
  expected wheat and rice, instead of the millet grain given to him, **which is even now considered
  inferior food in the Punjab countryside**." (p. 44, folio 43).
- *The fakir's kit is property, and breaking it is an injury with a named remedy.* The inventory: "his
  beggar's bowl and his beaded necklaces" (p. 38, folio 37); "They put out his fire, threw away his
  beggar's bowl and wallet and scattered his bhang.2 They broke his pestle and mortar. They threw away
  his turban, his chain and his tongs, his cup and his horn." (p. 44, folio 43). The cup's provenance:
  "**My Pir gave it to me and it was very precious.**" (p. 42, folio 41); "**A great tyranny has been
  committed. You have ruined the fakir by breaking his cup.**" (p. 42, folio 41). Kaidu's stock when
  looted includes "**the bowl my master gave me**" (p. 20, folio 19), and the village elders
  compensate him by "promising to build him a new hut and to give him more post3 and 'bhang' and all
  the things the girls destroyed." (p. 21, folio 20).
- *The props of sanctity, and scepticism about them.* "She tore off his faqir's cap and ropes of beads
  and threw `tern` [text-layer?] [them] on the ground." (p. 13, folio 12); "`lie` [text-layer?] [he]
  thinks he becomes a perfect faqir by wearing a rosary. **He thinks the girdle makes the
  darvesh.8**" (pp. 13–14, folios 12–13).
- *The fakir's hut as sanctified space, with a shoe rule and a physical expulsion.* "'**You have come
  into my holy hut with your shoes on. You have profaned this holy place.**' And he thrust him out and
  beat him even as cattle are thrust out of the cattle-pen." (p. 49, folio 48), on which the
  annotator: "Strange proceedings on the part of a faqir even though a fake one" (p. 50, folio 49).
- *Alms-giving in God's name used as a disguise.* "Kaidu, in the guise of a mendicant faqir, came to
  him and begged for alms in the name God. And Ranjha, thinking he was truly a holy man gave him half
  of his pastry. **Kaidu gave him a faqir's blessing** and retired towards the village." (p. 13,
  folio 12).
- *Faqirs excluded from the ferry — a small, concrete economy of exclusion.* "He who is for yonder
  shore, let him pay his pence.2… even though he be a dacoit or a thief we will not repeat his name,
  but **we chase away all beggars, `faqirs5` [text-layer?] [faqirs³] and those who eat unlawful meat
  like dogs**… Even the son of a Pir like Waris,' we will not take into our boat for nothing."
  (pp. 5–6, folios 4–5), with the coin glossed: "Pice would be more appropriate here. Usborne has used
  the English equivalent as it came more naturally to him." (p. 6, folio 5, note 2).

### The fakir's trade: healing, divination, amulets, narcotics

- *The full service catalogue, in the Jogi's own voice.* "**Through the blessings of `try`
  [text-layer?] [my] Pir and teacher, I can tell the names of all diseases. I can whisper the call to
  prayer in the ears of a newly born babe,2 I can weave spells and put children to sleep with
  lullabies. I can dry up the womb of women** and slay liars, adulterers and infidels. **With cunning
  oils and potent herbs I can cure pains and paralysis and the eighteen kinds of leprosy… I can make a
  perfect cure of a barren woman by letting out blood from her ankle vein.**" and the last offices:
  "**At his last hour, when the expiring life sticks fast in the gullet of the dying man, I recite the
  Holy Quran and his soul passes away in peace.**" (p. 40, folio 39). The annotator: "Ranjha is
  speaking here more like a learned physician than a faqir. That, of course, is intended to impress
  his ignorant hearers." (p. 41, folio 40, note 1).
- *The azan whispered to the newborn, named as a current custom.* "**This is a regular and well-known
  custom among Muslims everywhere. The azan or call to prayer is whispered in the ear of the new-born
  baby either by a priest or a relative.**" (p. 41, folio 40, note 2).
- *Abortifacient practice, with the annotator hesitating over a faqir's part in it.* "With boiled
  Ghaghar3 herbs I can bring about miscarriage." (p. 40, folio 39), glossed "Ghaghar is a creeper, the
  leaves of which are boiled and used for fomentation. **It may also possibly be used for inducing
  abortion, although it would be unusual for a faqir to participate `is` [text-layer?] [in] bringing
  this about.**" (p. 41, folio 40).
- *The healing economy around a stricken woman, itemized and costed.* "We have consulted Mullahs and
  physicians and hakims and **they cost much money**." (p. 48, folio 47); "the Kheras brought hundreds
  of fakirs and hakims and enchanters and they gave her cunning drugs. They brought Tiriak10 for
  snakes from Hazara and amulets and incense… **They spent bags of money `tying` [text-layer?]
  [trying] to cure the bride.**" (p. 49, folio 48). Glossed only "Tiriak — Tiryaq or antidote."
  (p. 50, folio 49).
- *The holy man's terms of service at a sickbed, set out as a list of ritual requirements.*
  "Meanwhile, Sehti took charge of the Jogi and **lodged him in the hut belonging to the village
  minstrel**. And he gave his orders that **bread must be cooked for the holy man. 'No man or woman
  must come near or cast their shadow on it. A separate place must be prepared and Hir's couch placed
  on it. I will burn incense at night. I will read enchantments over her.** None must be allowed to
  come near her… **only a virgin girl must be allowed to cross the threshold.**' And the Kheras did as
  the Jogi bade them" (p. 50, folio 49).
- *Amulets and charmed threads, defined and priced to a holy man's prayer.* "'**Taweez', 'dhaga' and
  'gat' are all charmed devices for warding off evil. 'Dhaga' is a twisted cotton thread, over which a
  holy man says a prayer. The 'dhaga' may be tied into knots in which case it becomes a gat'. A 'gat'
  is sometimes made of a leather string. 'Taweez' is an amulet.**" (p. 39, folio 38, note 7). In
  practice: "Ranjha made all the girls sit close to him and told them to fetch freshly broken
  potsherds from the kiln. **On them he drew mysterious lines and designs. Some he told to wear them
  round their necks, others to bind them round their loins. Others he told to put them in pitchers of
  water and make all the family drink them.**" (p. 36, folio 35); "He set up as a conjurer and gave
  some of them charmed threads7 and lucky knots." (p. 37, folio 36). The clientele's complaints are
  itemized: fathers- and mothers-in-law, husbands who "beat `then` [text-layer?] [them]", unkind
  neighbours, sons gone "to a distant country", and the pain of love (p. 36, folio 35).
- *Divination by the Quran and by horoscope, with the annotator's disapproval attached.* "If you will
  sit near me **I will open the Holy Book and by casting `magic'` [text-layer?] [magic⁷] lots I will
  tell your horoscope.**" (p. 41, folio 40), glossed "**An unauthorised but popular use of the Holy
  Quran.**" (p. 41, folio 40). The verdict on the reading: "He is a true pandit and `Jotshi9`
  [text-layer?] [Jotshi⁹]" (p. 41, folio 40), glossed "Jotshi is an astrologer. 'Pandit' is a learned
  Brahmin, **but is now used by both Hindus and Muslims for a wise and erudite person.**" (p. 41,
  folio 40).
- *Oath-taking with a knife and a drawn square.* "the Jogi **drew a square on the ground and thrust a
  knife therein** and said, 'Sit down Jat, and **swear on the Quran** that you have never touched
  Hir.' He put the knife to his throat and made him swear" (p. 49, folio 48).
- *A forty-day austerity.* "**I will fast forty days and forty nights** and I will recite powerful
  enchantment which will overcome all difficulties and will unite me to my beloved." (p. 43, folio 42).
- *Breath control, glossed as a Hindu discipline.* "We become deaf and dumb by holding our breath in
  the tenth position.20" (p. 39, folio 38), glossed "This is a reference to `Vogic` [text-layer?]
  [Yogic] practices. '**Prana Yam**', as it is called, involves the holding of breath for long periods
  and simultaneously concentrating on the idea of the Deity." (p. 40, folio 39).
- **Narcotics, treated by the edition as the equipment of contemplative life.** "Bhang is the Indian
  hemp which `i5` [text-layer?] [is] used as an intoxicant and a narcotic. **It is a favourite with
  the mystically inclined, particularly among Muslims and Sikhs. It is still smoked, chewed, eaten or
  drunk in the old Punjab area by wandering mendicants and many of those seeking spiritual exaltation
  or sexual pleasure, to both of which it seems to minister with equal facility.** It is often
  euphemistically referred to as 'Thandyai', that is, cool drink." (p. 12, folio 11, note 4). "Post
  `i5` [text-layer?] [is] opium. An opium eater is called 'posti'. **Kaidu, as an aspirant to
  spiritual promotion, presumably required opium as an aid to contemplation.**" (p. 21, folio 20,
  note 3). "**Narcotic drugs are not an essential part of the theory or practice of Jog, but certainly
  common practice both among Hindus and Muslims who profess to be faqirs.**" (p. 33, folio 32, note 7);
  "The sole reason for this strange comparison is Balnath's familiarity with opium and bhang (hemp) as
  supposed `aid,` [text-layer?: aids] to spiritual contemplation." (p. 33, folio 32, note 11); "Kand
  and Mul — narcotic preparations of opium. (Usborne's note.)" (p. 39, folio 38); "Bhang — **this is
  the first time Ranjha is specifically mentioned as being in possession of bhang**, Indian hemp, like
  many other devotees of the contemplative life." (p. 45, folio 44, note 2).

### Music, samaʿ and performance

- **Samaʿ licensed by a named silsila.** "**The Chishtiyya sect of Sufis, which counts Fariduddin
  Shakarganj among its leaders, regards music (Sama) as a permissible means of experiencing spiritual
  exaltation.**" (p. 19, folio 18, note 1 to Ch. 12) — attached to Ranjha's flute performance before
  the Five Pirs, which they ask for: "'Sing to us two or three songs as our heart is yearning for
  song.'" (p. 18, folio 17), and which moves them: "with the singing and the music he became as one in
  a trance. He swayed like a cobra. And the hearts of the Five Pirs were `movedhearing` [text-layer?]
  [moved hearing] Ranjha sing, and they said, 'Ask any favour of us and we will give it.'" (p. 18,
  folio 17).
- *The performance itself, as a catalogue of modes, tunes and repertoires.* "He played the seven
  modes, even the modes of Kharj,2 `Raldiab` [text-layer?], Sanehar, `Panchant` [text-layer?] Maddham,
  `Durat` [text-layer?], and `Nikanli` [text-layer?]… And he sang all the songs that men sing, even
  the song of Bishenpatti and the songs that women of `Manjha` [text-layer?] sing, the song of Sohni
  and Mahiwal and the songs of the Gujars2 and the songs of the women of `he` [text-layer?] [the]
  east." (p. 18, folio 17), with note 2: "These are the names of the seven notes in the classical music
  of the Indo-Pakistan subcontinent." (p. 19, folio 18). **The individual mode and tune names are not
  recoverable from the file and no worker supplied them.**
- *Music at a mosque as a communal flashpoint, dated to the colonial period.* "**Playing the flute in
  a mosque is regarded as a sacrilege.** It is strange that throughout the somewhat vigorous exchanges
  that follow between Ranjha and the Mullah, the latter makes no objection to this. **Music before
  mosques was a serious point of dispute between Hindus and Muslims before the Independence and led to
  frequent inter-communal rioting.**" (p. 5, folio 4, note 1).
- *Instruments, dances and games named as "of the old Punjab".* "'King' [text-layer?: reading
  uncertain] and Nad' are both musical instruments, popular in the old Punjab."; "`Wa!an`
  [text-layer?: reading uncertain], Garidda, Choratori and Maya are dances accompanied by music and
  singing and are popular among girls."; "Kilbari — a game something like Here we go round the
  mulberry bush'. Sammi is a similar game. (`Usbome's` [text-layer?] [Usborne's] note.)" (all p. 39,
  folio 38). And the games recorded as vanishing: "**Some of these games were still known at the
  beginning of the twentieth century. They are now dying out** and the fun and frolic of the old days
  `i5` [text-layer?: is] giving place to more modern and less innocent amusements." (p. 29, folio 28,
  note 2).
- *Performers as an institution in decline.* "**The jesters, namely the 'bhands' and the 'Mirasis',
  who used to be the life of such occasions are vanishing fast along with the old feudal order which
  they adorned.**" (p. 26, folio 25, note 8). The turban tuft is credited to the same caste: "The
  tufted turban is a comparatively recent development, and `i5` [text-layer?] [is] said to have been
  **invented by the 'mirasis' or hereditary bards of the Punjab in the late nineteenth century**."
  (p. 11, folio 10, note 7). Tan Sen is glossed "a famous Indian singer in the court of the Emperor
  Akbar. (`Usbornes` [text-layer?] [Usborne's] note) **Mian Tan Sen, as he is generally called, was a
  Muslim.**" (p. 39, folio 38).

### The mosque, the mullah and the Qazi

- *Who may enter and sleep in a mosque.* "**Mosques are God's houses and evil `liven` [text-layer?]
  [lives / livers?] are not admitted therein.** You have abandoned prayer and keep long hair and
  scented moustaches. Such men we beat out of mosques." (p. 4, folio 3); the Mullah's concession is
  conditional and timed (p. 5, folio 4).
- *The mullahs' funeral economy, as the poem attacks it.* "**They are blind men, lepers and cripples,
  always waiting greedily for a death in the house so that they may take the dead `inn's`
  [text-layer?] [man's] raiment.** They arise at midnight their fat bellies are smitten with hunger…
  Under the shelter `0f` [text-layer?] [of] Holy Writ they curse the living and when poor wayfarers
  and strangers come to beg for succour they cry, begone, begone!'" (p. 5, folio 4); and "They sit at
  the feet of Mullahs and listen to the doctrines of Hypocrisy." (p. 46, folio 45).
- *The mullah as the village's only literate man.* "**The village Mullah was probably the only person
  in the village who could read and write. Ranjha is obviously illiterate like his sweetheart.**"
  (p. 31, folio 30, note 6); and "Hir `ins` [text-layer?] [was] obviously unlettered." (p. 22,
  folio 21, note 2).
- *The Qazi as the instrument of a bribed settlement, twice.* On land: "So they sent for the Qazi7 and
  the assembly of the elders to measure the family land. **They gave bribes to the Qazi and thus the
  good land was given to the brothers and the barren and inhospitable land was given to Ranjha**"
  (p. 2, folio 1), with the office modernized: "The job of measuring land today is one for the village
  'patwari, **a subordinate revenue official who wields great power and local influence**." (p. 4,
  folio 3, note 7). On marriage: "There is no man whom I respect and trust as I trust you. **I will
  give you anything you ask if you will bring the affair to a successful conclusion.**" (pp. 25–26,
  folios 24–25); and Hir's curse, "**God's curse on all Qazis and bribe takers.**" (p. 26, folio 25),
  with "What have Qazis and the '"Shara"'15 got to do with True religion? **There is a big well in
  Hell into which Qazis will be thrown by God.**" (p. 25, folio 24).
- *The nikah as procedure, itemized — then voided by force.* The Qazi is "given a seat on the floor.
  **They appointed two witnesses and an attorney** and prepared to offer prayers… **They made her read
  the six Kalmas' and taught her the Five Times of Prayer.**" (p. 25, folio 24). The law as he states
  it: "**Without witnesses a marriage is invalid.** These are the clear directions of the law of
  Muhammad." (p. 25, folio 24), repeated at the trial: "Fakir, have you got any witnesses? **Without
  witnesses to the marriage she can be no wife.**" (p. 53, folio 52). His remedy: "**It is only Pirs,
  Fakirs and Saints who are afraid of using violence.** Tell the bride's attorney that consent to the
  marriage must be wrung from Hir, even against her will. **Let us gag her and read the marriage
  service.**" (p. 26, folio 25); and the outcome, "So the Qazi, by guile, against `hr's` [text-layer?]
  [Hir's] will, solemnised the marriage. **The witnesses and the attorney ran away afterwards as a
  camel flees on seeing a lion.**" (p. 26, folio 25).
- *Definitions.* "Shara or Shariat' is the system of Muslim Law." (p. 26, folio 25, note 15); "Kalma
  is the Muslim declaration of Faith. 'There is no God but one and Muhammad is his messenger', `i5`
  [text-layer?] [is] the first Kalma. **There are `five ve` [text-layer?] others.**" (p. 26, folio 25,
  note 14) — against the verse's "the six Kalmas'" (p. 25, folio 24); see Doubts.
- *The bridge of salvation, and the annotator's flat denial of it.* "Bridge of salvation is the **pul
  sirat**, a mythological bridge no broader than a sword's edge over which saint and sinner have to
  pass… **Needless to add, the bridge is an invention of the pious imagination. The Quran does not
  mention it.** 'Sirat', in Arabic means 'path'." (p. 5, folio 4, note 3).

### Courts, elders, justice and its price

- *The Panchayat, and its recognition by the colonial state.* "**The Panchayat, as it is called, is
  the Council of the elders of the village which hears disputes and settles them without recourse to
  the ordinary law. It was formally recognised as an institution by the British régime.**" (p. 10,
  folio 9, note 5).
- *The assembly of elders as a court, and its partiality.* Kaidu appeals — "I come for justice.
  Administer justice, O Assembly of Elders" — and the elders "scolded the girls and consoled the
  cripple" (pp. 20–21, folios 19–20). His verdict: "**These elders have lost faith and have abandoned
  justice. They show partiality to their daughters.**… Verily it is a case of '"a blind king and
  oppressing officials"'." (p. 21, folio 20), which note 4 calls "a famous Punjabi proverb" (p. 22,
  folio 21). Publicity is by drum: "He has noised the whole affair in the bazaar by beat of drum."
  (p. 20, folio 19).
- *A Hindu ruler delegating to a Muslim judge, and the annotator's claim that this happened.* "Qazi —
  **It is very considerate of the Raja, obviously a Hindu to entrust matters pertaining to Muslims to
  a Qazi, a Muslim Judge. Such examples are not wanting in actual fact.**" (p. 54, folio 53).
- *Bribery and nazrana as the price of access.* "I have no money to give to the officers and no
  tribute to enable me to reach to the `darbar32` [text-layer?] [darbar¹²]" (p. 43, folio 42), glossed
  "This `refer,` [text-layer?] [refers] to **bribery and corruption in the administration, never
  entirely absent from India or any other country at any time. Tribute' refers to the Nazrana, a gift,
  usually presented to a ruler in olden times in the form of cash or other valuables.**" (p. 44,
  folio 43, note 12). Also "I am a poor fakir and have no money to give to the officers in whose hands
  the decision lies." (p. 53, folio 52), and the Raja's "I will cut `oil` [text-layer?] [off] the nose
  of those who take bribes.'" (p. 53, folio 52).
- *A chain of justice, and a colonial reform, both supplied by the annotator.* "**The Emperor
  Jehangir, and before him Sultan Ferozeshah Tughlaq, is famous for his 'chain of justice which was
  connected with the imperial bedroom and could be pulled at any time of night or day by anyone who
  wanted to appeal to the Emperor in person for redress.**" (p. 52, folio 51, note 3); and "**Thuggi
  was one of the evils combated by Lord William Bentinck** who was Governor-General `o~`
  [text-layer?] [of] India under the East India Company for some time in the first half of the
  nineteenth century. For further details see Meadows Taylor's History of the Thugs." (p. 53,
  folio 52).
- *Mutilation as a penalty on women, described as practice.* "The physical `cuffing` [text-layer?]
  [cutting] of the nose is not involved here, although in certain other cases it could happen. **An
  unfaithful wife, for example. was supposed to have her nose cut to prevent her from attracting any
  more paramours.**" (p. 52, folio 51).
- *State power reaching into the shrine relation — a single striking line.* "'**Set my ears right or I
  will bring the sepoy of the Sirkar15 here.**'" (p. 32, folio 31), glossed "Government. The word
  'Sirkar' is also an old form of address for kings and nobles. It is still applied by sycophants to
  lesser luminaries.." (pp. 33–34, folios 32–33, note 15).
- *State archaeology named as an active institution.* Bhambore is "an old city, **the ruins of which
  are now being excavated by the Pakistan Archaeological Department**." (p. 12, folio 11, note 5).

### Marriage, kinship, caste and the village economy

- **Hindu Brahmans fix the date of a Muslim wedding, and the annotator flags it as normal.** "the
  Kheras asked the Brahmans1 to consult the Stars and to fix the marriage. **The Brahmans fixed
  Virwati (Thursday) in the month of Sawan2 for the wedding**" (p. 24, folio 23), with note 1: "**It
  may appear strange that a Muslim family should depend on Hindu Brahmans for fixing a marriage but
  this was by no means unusual in the old Punjab.**" (p. 26, folio 25). Note 2 places the month:
  "Sawan and Bhadon are the two rainy months of the year in the old Punjab." (p. 26, folio 25). **This
  is the only calendar date in the book, and it is a weekday-plus-month, not a year.**
- *Caste endogamy stated as the rule that drives the plot.* "**the Sials had never given `theft`
  [text-layer?] [their] daughters to the Ranjha tribe** and… they would be disgraced if they gave
  their daughters to such lowly and needy folk. **The brotherhood recommended an alliance with the
  house of the Kheras as being Jats of good lineage**" (p. 23, folio 22) — and the decision is the
  brotherhood's, not the father's alone.
- *The Sial tribe's customary law on women's property — a hard economic claim.* "**the Sial tribe has
  always been more than usually considerate towards its women. It is one of the few tribes in the
  Punjab which allows women to inherit property under customary law.**" (p. 20, folio 19, note 13).
- *The village as one social unit across caste and creed — the annotator's most extended sociological
  note.* "Judging by their names, some of `Hirs` [text-layer?] [Hir's] girl friends are Hindus.
  **This symbolises the atmosphere of the village in the old Punjab, where the village community as a
  whole was regarded as one social unit irrespective of caste or creed.**… **Even a sweeper woman
  would be addressed as daughter', sister' or 'mother'** (depending on her age and the age of the
  speaker) **in the most aristocratic household in the village. If she was elderly, it would be rude
  to call her by her name.**" (p. 20, folio 19, note 12).
- *The wedding economy, itemized.* Dowry: "there was no end to the ornaments, amulets, anklets,
  necklaces, earrings and nose-rings which were prepared as a dowry for the bride" (p. 24, folio 23).
  Kitchen and labour: "There were multitudes of barbers cooking the food."; "The potter women brought
  earthen pots and bakers brought fuel from the forest The water carriers rushed about drawing water
  from the wells." (p. 24, folio 23). Its competitive function: "**The guests turned green with
  jealousy when they saw the abundance of good things.**" (p. 24, folio 23). Hospitality converted
  into standing: "**Chuchak has gained credit in the world and the people are praying for his long
  life and prosperity.**" (p. 24, folio 23). Basmati rice is glossed as "now a valuable export from
  Pakistan" (p. 26, folio 25, note 4).
- *Marriage by capture, named as a surviving symbolism.* "**A traditional Punjab marriage still
  retains the symbolism of 'marriage by capture'.** The bridal party… is led by the bridegroom who,
  complete with horse and dagger, is made to look like a general… **The 'invaders' celebrate their
  victory by carrying off the bride in the midst of fireworks and fanfare.** Resistance from the
  `brides` [text-layer?] [bride's] side now takes the form of abusive language" (p. 28, folio 27,
  note 5), borne out by "**The Kheras took her off as thieves drive off stolen cattle.**" (p. 27,
  folio 26).
- *Rites and objects, each glossed.* The *surma* exchange (p. 25, folio 24; note 5, p. 26, folio 25);
  ribald songs — "Ribald `longs` [text-layer?] [songs] are a well-known institution. **Most of the
  songs `sang` [text-layer?] [sung] on such occasions are of the unprintable variety.**" (p. 26,
  folio 25, note 6); the *arsi* thumb-mirror, worn "for a couple of months or so after marriage,
  **during which period the newly-married girl was not expected to do household work. This is still
  the case in the less sophisticated parts of the Punjab countryside.**" (p. 26, folio 25, note 7);
  the *sarbala* (p. 26, folio 25, note 11); the *huqqa*, which "`ants` [text-layer?] [acts] as a
  medium of peaceful social intercourse" (p. 26, folio 25, note 9); the arrival rites — "poured oil
  over the threshold. `hr's` [text-layer?] [Hir's] mother-in-law swung water round the bride's head
  and drank it and gave thanks to God." (p. 27, folio 26); the *dai*, who "still… accompany the bride
  in her in-laws' house for the first two or three occasions" (p. 28, folio 27, note 6); henna and the
  **'Suhag Pura'** packet (p. 33, folio 32, note 12); and the **'Mehndhi'** hair fringe, "**a symbol
  of virginity**", untied in "a touching ceremony at the bride's house… as a token of her goodbye to
  her unmarried past" (p. 43, folio 42, note 2).
- *Widowhood and the colour code of clothing.* "**White clothes, by tradition, were worn by widows,
  particularly among the Hindus, No ornaments were worn. Muslim widows, if they were childless, wore
  black or white. If they had children, particularly boys, they wore normal clothes, and ornaments
  too. The social pattern is now changed.**" (p. 28, folio 27, note 4).
- *Betrothal custom.* "**After the 'mangni' or betrothal, a girl does not see her fiancé until
  marriage**" (p. 48, folio 47); and bride price asserted then denied — "this bride whom we
  `purchased5` [text-layer?: marker for note 3] with so much difficulty" (p. 48, folio 47) against
  "'Purchase' here is only a strong expression… **and is not to be taken literally.**" (p. 50,
  folio 49, note 3).
- *Purdah described as urban, not rural.* "**The 'purdah' has never been an established institution in
  rural areas as it has been for a Long time among Muslims in towns and cities, and it is quite usual
  for girls to move freely in the villages without a veil,**" (p. 14, folio 13, note 8).
- *The spinning wheel as the organising institution of women's rural economy.* "**A spinning-wheel
  (charkha) used to be the inseparable companion of every woman, old or young, in the rural Punjab…
  The spinning-wheel was an important link in rural society. Women spun cotton yarn, and the village
  weaver made it into cloth.**" (p. 17, folio 16, note 1), which the same note connects to "**Mahatma
  Gandhi**… as a symbol of indigenous production to counter the imports of foreign cloth." Spinning
  parties: "**Spinning parties — the Punjabi name is 'tarinjan' or 'bhora' — were a famous institution
  of the Punjab village.** Young girls, generally unmarried ones, sat together `wit` [text-layer?]
  [with] their spinning-wheels and competed with each other in spinning skill and scandalous
  conversation." (p. 4, folio 3, note 14). The Qazi prescribes it as the correct female occupation
  (p. 16, folio 15), and its abandonment marks a ruined village: "Not a girl sings at her spinning
  wheel" (p. 37, folio 36).
- *Wage labour, a twelve-year term, and non-payment enforced by public opinion.* "'**For twelve
  years2 I have been grazing your buffaloes and now you turn me away without wages.3** You are looting
  me like a Bania' whose ledger stays quietly in his shop while the interest swells into a mountain.'"
  (p. 15, folio 14); "**All the people curse us for having turned the cowherd out without paying him
  his wages. Had he asked for his wages you would have had to pay him a whole `hag-full`
  [text-layer?] [bag-full] of money.**" (p. 15, folio 14). The annotator: "**Incidentally, the poet has
  never told us what wages were fixed by Chuchak with Ranjha. Indeed, there has been no mention of
  wages at all.**" (p. 16, folio 15, note 3); and on the twelve years, "merely a conventional
  expression… hardly meant to be taken literally." (p. 16, folio 15, note 2).
- *Buffalo herding as the economic substrate, and its collapse as leverage.* "as soon as Ranjha had
  gone, the buffaloes refused to graze any longer. Some were lost, some were drowned; others were
  devoured by `tiger5` [text-layer?] [tigers]" (p. 15, folio 14); the skills demanded are stock-keeping
  and theft recovery, "He can swim buffaloes across the river and recover stolen cattle… in a country
  where thieves `an` [text-layer?] [are] many and good servants scarce." (p. 10, folio 9).
- *Famine named as the circumstance of Ranjha's arrival.* "'**He came when there was a great famine
  and grain was very dear.** He grazed Chuchak's buffaloes and then claimed the hand of his
  daughter.'" (p. 53, folio 52).
- *Cash sums and quantities, every one in the book.* "hundreds of thousands of rupees" refused for
  Ranjha's return (p. 22, folio 21); "five rupees and a shawl were given to the Kheras" (p. 24,
  folio 23); "a Quran and five gold mohurs" placed before the bride (p. 27, folio 26); "five gold
  coins into Mithi's hands" (p. 18, folio 17); "five rupees" in the offering dish (p. 45, folio 44);
  "presents to the midwife and other menials" (p. 27, folio 26); "a few `moods` [text-layer?] of land"
  as the cause of Ranjha's expulsion (p. 22, folio 21 — **not an English land measure and left
  unrepaired**); "bushels of grain… the produce of all these years that he has been away" (p. 22,
  folio 21).
- *Titles, castes and trades, each glossed by the edition.* Chaudhri and Mihr as titles of local
  authority (p. 4, folio 3, note 4; p. 10, folio 9, note 1); Ranjha and Sial as Jat castes (p. 3,
  folio 2, note 2); Khojas as "a well-known tribe of Hindu converts to Islam… mainly engaged in trade"
  (p. 7, folio 6, note 6); Khatris as "the Hindu merchant class in Northern India" from "'Kshatriya'"
  (p. 7, folio 6, note 7; p. 20, folio 19); `Kizilhash` [text-layer?] [Kizilbash] as "a soldierly tribe
  of Turkish origin in Iran, a number of whom came to India with the armies of Nadir Shah" (p. 7,
  folio 6, note 8); "Bania is a Hindu shopkeeper." (p. 16, folio 15, note 4); "Kanjars are people who
  live on prostitution." (p. 20, folio 19); "Shroff woman — **Usborne means** a woman belonging to a
  goldsmith family." (p. 20, folio 19); "A `Nwab` [text-layer?] [Nawab] is a Muslim ruling chief or a
  big landlord." (p. 26, folio 25); Brahmans as "the 'twice-born'… the intellectual group in ancient
  `hindu` [text-layer?] society" (p. 26, folio 25); and the annotator's defence of the Jats,
  "`Actnally` [text-layer?] [Actually], **Jats have a reputation for being simple and honest folk.**"
  (p. 16, folio 15, note 6).
- *Joint-family housing as built form.* "**Old houses in the Punjab countryside had a big courtyard
  with a number of living-rooms and bedrooms round it. A number of brothers living with their parents
  as a joint family was quite common.**" (p. 16, folio 15, note 7); and the institution's origin —
  "though originally a Hindu institution, later on became `more less` [text-layer?] [more or less]
  common to Hindus and Muslim, alike." (p. 39, folio 38).
- *Death, burial and honour.* "Let us poison Hir, even if we become sinful in the sight of God."
  (p. 55, folio 54); "And the Sials gave her sherbet and mixed poison with it"; "And they buried her";
  "'Hir has been dead for the last eight watches. **They `baffled` [text-layer?] [bathed] her body and
  buried her yesterday and as soon as they began the last funeral rites**, they sent me to give you
  the news." (p. 55, folio 54). **Washing of the body, burial within a day, and "the last funeral
  rites" are the only mortuary sequence in the book; no grave, tomb, visitation or later observance is
  recorded for either lover** — (not stated). The motive is stated aloud: "**All over the world we are
  taunted with the story of Hir. We shall lose fame and gain great disgrace**" (p. 55, folio 54), and
  the narrator's verdict is "The parents of Hir killed her. **This was the doing of God.'**" (p. 55,
  folio 54).
- *Festivals named.* Eid, in a metaphor — "Somebody has been celebrating the high festival of Eid in
  the garden of Kalabagh. **The hungry have been filled and fakir have fed to their heart's
  content.**" (p. 47, folio 46); the Dushera, with Ravana's effigy burnt annually (p. 4, folio 3,
  note 13); and the Laila-tul-Qadr, "the Night of Nights on which the first revelation of the Quran
  came to the Prophet. **It is identified as the 27th night of Ramazan, the fasting month.**" (p. 4,
  folio 3, note 5).

### The edition's own arguments

- **The annotator corrects Waris Shah on fact, in a debunking register, repeatedly.** On Shakarganj
  and the Punjab's troubles (p. 2, folio 1, note 4); on Destiny — "This is not what Omar Khayyam has
  to say about the Moving Finger of Destiny!" (p. 2, folio 1, note 3); on the Quran and women —
  "Ranjha is certainly no scholar of the Quran, which does not lay down any general proposition about
  the `deoeitfulne~` [text-layer?] [deceitfulness] of women anywhere" (p. 4, folio 3, note 10); on the

  arsi "is still the case in the less sophisticated parts of the Punjab countryside" (p. 26, folio 25);

  spelling varies: "the old Punjab" and, once, "the old Panjab" (p. 26, folio 25, note 4).**

  event." (p. 44, folio 43); "The garden of the Feringhees is an obscure phrase." (p. 44, folio 43);
  "Shuda Ghazi and Bhola Pir are **presumably** some saintly celebrities of the neighbourhood."
  (p. 30, folio 29, note 3); "Ghazi Pir is the appellation of more than one saint. The reference here
  is **probably** to" (p. 29, folio 28, note 4).
- *The poem's own moral arguments, kept as the poem states them.* Ranjha against elopement: "**Love
  does not taste well if it is composed of theft and stealth and abduction.**" (p. 23, folio 22), with
  his patience doctrine — "**The patience of the heart is victorious over the world.**… **Patience
  alone can mend the cloven sky.**" (p. 23, folio 22). Hir that consent, not ceremony, constitutes
  marriage: "**The once plucked Belflower cannot be reset on the branch. Broken glass cannot be
  united.** The bones that have once been thrown in the Ganges3 cannot return… **The contract of love
  once entered on cannot be broken.**" (p. 22, folio 21), and "**The soul of the Prophet solemnised our
  marriage.** By the order of God, the polar20 star was my attorney. 'The angels Gabriel, Michael,
  Israel [text-layer?] and Israfil were the four witnesses.'" (p. 25, folio 24).
- **The Epilogue's catalogue of social disorder — the passage most likely to be cited as evidence of
  the poem's date and setting.** "There is no Governor, Ruler or Emperor. The country and the people
  have all been reduced to `rump` [text-layer?] [ruin?]. There is great disturbance throughout the
  country. Everybody carries a sword in his hand… **Thieves have become headmen and harlots have
  become mistresses of the household.** Bards of devils have multiplied exceedingly all over the land.
  **The nobles have fallen in their estate. Men of menial rank flourish. The peasantry are waxen
  fat.1 The Jats have become rulers in the country. Everybody has become lord of his own castle.**"
  (p. 56, folio 55) — which the annotator reads as reliable: "**Waris Shah's description of the
  conditions of his day is probably not exaggerated.**" (p. 57, folio 56, note 1). His closing
  judgement: "Poets and sinners offer counsel in the world. The counsel of the wise is held of no
  account. No one speaks the truth. Lying has become the custom of the world." (p. 56, folio 55).
- *The annotator sets tribal honour against religion.* Quoting Shakespeare — "As flies to wanton boys
  are we to the gods / They kill us for their sport." — and adding: "**Tribal pride was apparently
  more powerful than the laws of God. This is a frequent phenomenon.**" (p. 56, folio 55, note 7).
- *The edition treats the Hir story as one recension among several, with Persian antecedents.* "Mauju
  is a corruption of Muizud Din, which is the name given in **some of the Persian Hir-Ranjha
  Mathnavis**." (p. 4, folio 3, note 3); and Waris Shah's own framing is a re-telling — "'Write for us
  afresh the forgotten story of the Love of Hir.'" (p. 2, folio 1).

## Doubts and review points

### 1. Mapping judgements a human must confirm

*These are identification questions, not questions about rulings (a) or the person rule — those are
settled and are applied above without hedging.*

- **The Jalal / Jahanian split is the live trap in the book, and two archive rows turn on it.** The
  book names "Sayyid Jalal of Bukhara, **whom men also call Makhdum Jahanian**" as ONE figure at
  p. 11, folio 10, and note 2 adds "Jalal of `Bukkara` [text-layer?] [Bukhara] or Jalal Bukhari, who
  originally came from Uch, is also buried at Multan" — a single person. But at p. 50, folio 49 the
  book names "the cudgel of **Sayyad Jalal of Bokhara**" and "the dagger given him by **Makhdum
  `Jabnian` [text-layer?] [Jahanian]**" as **two distinct members of the same Five Pirs in one
  sentence**, which is the sole ground on which they were mapped to two rows. **If the two names
  denote one saint, four bullets collapse into one row and one of the two mappings must be struck.**
  The book may well be conflating two figures; a human must decide, and must also decide whether
  "Sayyid Jalal of Bukhara" belongs to
  `shrine-of-jalaluddin-surkh-posh-bukhari-jalaluddin-bukhari` (that row's own name) or to
  `shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht` (the epithet the book prints).
- **The relics attached to those two names swap between p. 50 and p. 52 and the book does not
  reconcile it.** Cudgel = Jalal and dagger = Jahanian at folio 49; cudgel = Jahanian and dagger =
  Jalal at folio 51. **A human must decide whether that is the poem's inconsistency, the
  translator's, or the text layer's.**
- **`shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya` at p. 50, folio 49 rests on the single bare word "Bahauddin".**
  "`Pit` [text-layer?] [Pir] Bahauddin" appears there with no Zakariya, no Multan, no date and no
  order. It is the archive's only row whose principal figure carries that name, but **the name is
  common enough that a human must confirm the identification is intended**; if it is reversed, the
  earth-shaking karamat moves out of the row. (The row's other material, from pp. 10–11 and 16, is
  secure: the book glosses "Zakaria" as "Baha-ud-Din Zakaria of Multan".)

### 5. The apparatus: who wrote the notes, and what is missing from the book

- **The annotator is never named anywhere in the 57 pages** — (not stated). He is demonstrably not
  Usborne (1874–1919): he glosses Usborne in the third person (p. 20, folio 19), corrects him by name
  (p. 33, folio 32), and writes from after Partition (pp. 11, 26, 29, folios 10, 25, 28). **A human
  must settle who he is, from front matter that is not in this PDF, before any note-derived fact
  enters an entry, and no note may be attributed to Usborne unless the book marks it.**
- **Notes the book marks as Usborne's:** Kand and Mul, Kilbari, Tan Sen (p. 39, folio 38); the snake
  and the bull (p. 41, folio 40); Bhag Bhari (p. 45, folio 44); the farthing (p. 46, folio 45); the
  Jat-tirade excision (p. 28, folio 27); the "Southern country" gloss (p. 57, folio 56); Umar the
  trickster, in part (p. 50, folio 49); and note 8, **signed** "— C. F. Usborne" (p. 57, folio 56).
  **Notes 3 and 4 on p. 57 end with a bare "(Usborne.)", which could mean either that he wrote them
  or that the gloss is taken from him** — not forced either way. Two notes consist of nothing but
  "Usborne's note." (p. 29, folio 28; p. 31, folio 30) and the Pass-1 worker could not tell whether
  those are the notes' texts or compositor's labels.
- **The two identifications that matter most to this archive are the annotator's, not Usborne's, and
  must not be cited as his:** "Shakarganj is the saint of Pakpattan." (note 7) and "Saint of Kasur -
  Mamdum Mafia Ghulamn Murtaza of Kasur" (note 5), both p. 57, folio 56, both unattributed.
- **The Introduction the notes repeatedly cross-refer to is NOT in this 57-page PDF.** Notes point to
  "Introduction, note 1" (p. 23, folio 22), "Introduction, note 4" (p. 12, folio 11; p. 57, folio 56),
  "Introduction, note 24" (p. 6, folio 5; p. 11, folio 10; p. 33, folio 32) and "notes on

  what occupies PDF pp. 55–57; chunks 6 and 7 answer it: Chapter 32, the Epilogue and the closing
  notes block.** So the Introduction sits outside this file, and **the glosses it holds — the barber,
  the faqir, and the fuller identification of the Saint of Kasur — are (not stated) for this source.**
  The contents list on p. 1 likewise omits both the Invocation and the Introduction, and lists 32
  chapter titles running folios 1–53.
- **Other cross-references that cannot be followed within the book:** "Chapter 19, note 7" (p. 2,
  folio 1) and "Chapter 1, note 9" (p. 26, folio 25) are inside it; but "Chapter 22's Notes block"
  falls between chunks and the glosses for the "King", kand and mul, Harut and Marut, Plato and
  Aesop, the charmed threads and the games Kilbari, Samsni, "Walan", Maya, Choratori and Garidda are

  any of them as absent.

### 6. Explicit negatives, recorded so nobody re-derives them

- **The folio rule holds everywhere.** `folio = PDF − 1` was measured page by page in all seven
  chunks — p. 2 = 1 through p. 57 = 56 — with **no competing offset and no disagreement anywhere**,
  and p. 1 prints no number at all. Unlike two earlier derived-rule failures in this project, this
  rule survived measurement.
- **No original script anywhere.** Not one character of Shahmukhi, Gurmukhi, Persian or Arabic occurs
  in the 57 pages; every vernacular term is romanized without diacritics (Sama, Ishq, Jog, Jog Mat,
  chela, Murshid, Murid, gur, koondah, Suhag Pura, fana fillah, Chit Akas, Bhut Kas, Juda Kas,
  Taweez, dhaga, gat, Prana Yam, Mehndhi, Ghaghar, Dhannia, Kalma, Shara, Qutub, Abdal, Ghaus, post,
  posti, bhang, Thandyai, surma, Arsi, bhands, Mirasis, huqqa, Sarbala, Shahbala, dai, Doli, Gana,
  charkha, tarinjan, bhora, halwa, turrah, mangni, pearha, Nazrana, Tiryaq, pul sirat,
  Laila-tul-Qadr, Mar bar sar-i-ganj, Sag-i-Laila, angusht-i-hairat dar dahan). **The protocol's
  "quote the original script where it is printed" rule cannot be satisfied for any name, place or date
  in this file.** Whether the edition prints a facing Panjabi text at all is (not stated).
- **No ʿurs, mela, langar, sajjada nashin, Auqaf, endowment, land grant, revenue figure, visitor
  number, building date, architectural description or shrine dispute appears anywhere in the book.**
  The nearest things to an ʿurs are the "Fairs of Pakpattan or Nigah or Rattan or Thamman" (p. 24,

- **`shaktipeeth-shri-hinglaj-mata-mandir` does not appear anywhere in the book.** Every one of the
  seven chunks checked for it and recorded the negative; Hinglaj, Hingol and Lasbela are never named.
- **No archive id used above is absent from `shrine_index.tsv`,** and no id was invented.
