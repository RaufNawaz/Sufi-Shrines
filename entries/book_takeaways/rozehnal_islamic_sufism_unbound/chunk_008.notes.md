## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The order's publishing establishment, quantified.** "At present, some fifty different texts are
  produced and distributed from a number of publishing houses" (p. 130, folio 121).
- **Mahfil-i Zauqiyya, Karachi** — "First and foremost among them is Mahfil-i Zauqiyya. Located in
  Karachi, it publishes the bulk of the original Urdu works of Muhammad Zauqi Shah and Shahidullah
  Faridi. Founded by Shahidullah himself, it is now administered by a senior male disciple, a man who
  was expressly ordered by the shaykh to spearhead the silsila's publication efforts" (p. 130, folio
  121).
- **The Association of Spiritual Training / *The Sufi Path*** — "Since 1989, The Association of
  Spiritual Training—founded by Wahid Bakhsh and administered by a number of his senior
  disciples—has published an English language journal titled The Sufi Path. Based in Islamabad, it
  has produced fourteen issues to date" (p. 130, folio 121).
- **The Lahore outlets** — The Sufi Foundation, "founded by another senior male murid, published a
  series of translations and original writings by Wahid Bakhsh during the 1980s"; the work continued
  "under the auspices of Bazm-i Ittehad al-Muslimin, founded in 1984 by the shaykh's nephew";
  "Beginning in 1996, Talifat-i Shaheedi, established by another murid of Shahidullah Faridi, has
  published four additional texts, three of them in English" (p. 130, folio 121).
- **Commercial houses used in Lahore** — "Ziya al-Qur'an, for example, currently publishes two of
  Wahid Bakhsh's Urdu translations of classical Persian malfuzat; Qausin Publishers prints two key
  Urdu malfuzat; and, most importantly, al-Faisal Publishers distributes a number of Wahid Bakhsh's
  works in both English and Urdu" (p. 130, folio 121).
- **Shrine bazaars as a distribution channel** — the silsila's books are sold in "the myriad
  bookstalls that flank the country's major Sufi shrines", above all at Baba Farid's tomb complex in
  Pakpattan (p. 130, folio 121).
- **Literacy as a constraint on the reading public** — "Given Pakistan's alarmingly low literacy
  rates—less than half of the adult population—the potential reading public is extremely limited and
  largely determined by education and class" (p. 130, folio 121).
- **Print economics** — "Typically, no more than a thousand copies of each text are printed at a
  time. Any profits from the sale of books are immediately rolled back into the system, making the
  production process a largely self-sustaining enterprise. Remarkably, no resources are committed to
  advertising" (p. 131, folio 122). A murid: "But marketing is not considered helpful for spiritual
  development" (October 3, 2002, Lahore) (p. 132, folio 123).
- **Pricing doctrine** — the senior disciple overseeing Mahfil-i Zauqiyya: "We print them not with
  the intention of making money, but to make them available to people at the least possible cost so
  that they can benefit from this as much as possible" (May 21, 2001, Karachi) (p. 131, folio 122).
- **Malaysia: an independent press** — the Malaysian disciples "recently established an independent
  printing press in Kuala Lumpur. To date, A.S. Noordeen Publishers has published four books: an
  English version of Wahid Bakhsh's translation of Kashf al-mahjub, a partial translation of the
  malfuzat of Shaykh Sayyid Shah Waris Hasan, an edition of Shahidullah Faridi's Inner Aspects of
  Faith, and Islamic Sufism, a reprint of Wahid Bakhsh's lengthy monograph" (pp. 132–133, folios
  123–124). "literature of any kind on tasawwuf is a rarity in Malaysia where—as in Pakistan—Sufism
  is a highly contested tradition and a controversial practice" (p. 133, folio 124). Malaysian
  publications are "limited to English", with Bahasa Malaysia intended but a failed Indonesian
  translation of *Islamic Sufism* blocking it (p. 133, folio 124).
- **The website and its traffic** — "Since 1996 they have operated a silsila Web site
  (http://www.moonovermedina.com). Entitled Moon Over Medina: A Sufi Bookstore" (p. 133, folio 124).
  The site manager, "a Malay businessman and disciple of Wahid Bakhsh who was educated in America":
  "When I started there were about ten to fifteen visitors a day … Now it is up to forty people a
  day, about 1,200 a month. But of those, only a few buy books"; "I do not accept credit cards, and
  that is the problem"; "It doesn't make any money, just enough to cover the fees" (pp. 133–134,
  folios 124–125).
- **Shrine visitation, sama', ta'ziya and milad as matters of state policy** — Wahid Bakhsh's
  proposal, quoted: "Our constitution should permit all sects to practice religion the way they want.
  The Government should enforce only those clauses on which all the sects have consensus, such as

  criminal procedures. Peripheral or controversial issues like visits to the shrines, sama'

  celebration's" is as printed; see Doubts.

- **Mass devotion contrasted with elite instruction** — a prominent male murid: "For the masses who

  (p. 131, folio 122). The book frames the underlying distinction as one "between the rational,

  knowledge of the Sufi master (ma'rifa)" (p. 131, folio 122).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Rozehnal's thesis about the book under review:** in it Wahid Bakhsh "places Sufism at the very
  center of both Islamic thought and practice and Pakistani national identity and ideology", so that
  "In Wahid Bakhsh's alternative reading of history, Pakistan is rightfully an Islamic republic
  rooted in Sufi piety" (p. 122, folio 113).
- **Historical framing supplied by Rozehnal:** the book's "parameters—and all of its polemics—are
  understood only against the backdrop of South Asian geopolitics in the late cold war period",
  namely the Zia dictatorship and "the proxy war in neighboring Afghanistan fought by U.S.- and
  Pakistan-sponsored mujahidin groups against the Soviets" (p. 122, folio 113).
- **Inverted Orientalism:** "Wahid Bakhsh inverts Orientalist essentialisms to valorize a
  spiritualized 'East' against a godless 'West.'… this rhetorical move parallels the ideological
  formations of early Indian nationalism in its campaign of resistance against the British Raj"
  (p. 122, folio 113); and, quoting a judgement passed on Nadwi, his East/West chasm "owes more to
  nineteenth and early twentieth century Orientalist notions than he seems to recognize" (p. 122,
  folio 113).
- **Jihad, as Wahid Bakhsh defines it:** "both an internal struggle against the lower self (the
  'Greater Jihad,' or jihad-i akbar) and an external resistance against the enemies of Islam (the
  'Lesser Jihad,' or jihad-i asghar)", with "Torture, mutilation, and the killing of noncombatants …
  strictly forbidden in the Muslim tradition of just war" (p. 125, folio 116).
- **Reform from below, not from the state:** Rozehnal's gloss — "it is only through the moral reform
  of the individual that social and political institutions can be revitalized" (p. 126, folio 117) —
  and the criticism that Wahid Bakhsh "offers few practical details on precisely how these reforms
  should be implemented", so that his vision "is reminiscent of Mawdudi who 'showed little interest
  in the actual working of institutions'" (p. 127, folio 118).
- **Against Deoband and the Salafis:** Wahid Bakhsh "decries the spread of Wahhabi and Salafi
  doctrine" and "levels an especially harsh critique of the Deobandi movement", which in Rozehnal's
  reading "has betrayed the legacy of its Chishti Sabiri predecessors by jettisoning its Sufi roots
  in a misguided attempt to purify the faith" (pp. 127–128, folios 118–119).
- **Publishing as politics:** "in printing and disseminating these texts, the silsila purposefully
  enters the contested public sphere to stake a claim to Islamic authority and authenticity. Within
  the combative landscape of today's Pakistan and Malaysia, such a proactive publishing campaign can
  only be seen as an overtly political act" (p. 132, folio 123).
- **Medium and message:** drawing on Jonah Blank's *Mullahs on the Mainframe* and on twenty-first
  century "fundamentalist movements—Jewish, Christian, and Muslim alike", Rozehnal writes "While
  employing the medium, they reject the message. I would argue that this is equally true of the
  contemporary Chishti Sabiri order" (p. 134, folio 125).
- **The chapter's conclusion:** the three shaykhs "imagine a distinct Indo-Muslim Sufi identity: a
  shari'a-minded and socially engaged Sufism, firmly rooted in South Asian tradition but responsive
  to the challenges of postcolonial modernity" (p. 135, folio 126); what "distinguishes" them "is the
  distinct way they conflated Sufi identity and Pakistani national identity", making Sufism "a middle
  way between the extremes of Islamism on the one hand and Westernization on the other" (p. 135,
  folio 126); and their legacy "runs counter to the prevalent Orientalist stereotypes of Sufism's
  devolution, rigidity, and intellectual stasis" (p. 135, folio 126).
- **Postcolonial agency:** the chapter closes by setting Gayatri Spivak ("The subaltern cannot
  speak") against Sudipta Kaviraj ("In historical fact, the Orient is never reduced to silence") and
  Homi Bhahbha's "contradictory and ambivalent space of enunciation", and siding with the latter:
  the three shaykhs "provide further evidence of this discursive freedom" (pp. 135–136, folios
  126–127).
- **Forward pointer, not a fact:** "chapter 4 examines today's Chishti Sabiri order as a teaching
  community centered on the intimate and intense master-disciple relationship" (p. 136, folio 127);
  texts "are fully integrated into contemporary Chishti Sabiri ritual practice", as chapter 4 "will
  demonstrate" (p. 131, folio 122).
