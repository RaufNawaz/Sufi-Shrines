## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Access to the shaykh is rationed by distance.** "For many, companionship with the shaykh (suhbat) is
  limited to either infrequent visits to Karachi or the annual communal pilgrimages to Sufi shrines"
  (p. 166, folio 158).
- **Digital discipleship.** "a significant number of murids also find 'virtual guidance' in cyberspace,"
  the shaykh advising "via e-mail"; the author calls this "the silsila's fluid incorporation of modern
  technology into Chishti Sabiri networks of knowledge and practice" (p. 166, folio 158).
- **Adab of listening.** "Ritual propriety dictates that disciples must sit passively and speak as little
  as possible in the presence of the shaykh"; "the impetus for conversation and questions should come
  from the spiritual master" (pp. 166–167, folios 158–159). A senior male disciple: "the adab is that you
  should really not initiate a discussion" (March 16, 2001, Lahore) (p. 167, folio 159).
- **Adab of the body.** Disciples "stand whenever a shaykh enters or leaves an assembly"; they "position
  themselves physically lower than the shaykh"; they "avoid pointing the soles of their feet at the
  shaykh or turning their back on him"; in conversation they "refrain from laughter, speak in a quiet
  voice, and avoid direct eye contact" (pp. 167–168, folios 159–160). The author calls these rules
  "common to Sufi orders throughout South Asia—the legacy of premodern Persian court culture" (p. 168,
  folio 160).
- **Ritual purity before audience.** Disciples "are also expected to be in a state of ritual purification
  whenever they enter the shaykh's presence"; per Zauqi Shah one should perform "the ritual washing
  [wudu]" but "should not perform supererogatory prayer [nawafil ] before entering the shaykh's presence,"
  only "his obligatory prayers [farz]" (p. 168, folio 160).
- **Pilgrimage and 'urs as institution.** "Among the most important venues for these interpersonal
  exchanges are the annual pilgrimages to Sufi shrines for the death anniversaries ('urs) of the silsila's
  major saints. Pilgrimage punctuates the Chishti Sabiri ritual calendar" (p. 176, folio 168).
  - Form of the gathering: "During the 'urs commemorations, disciples live together for an intensive week
    of spiritual immersion and collective ritual practice in the company of the teaching shaykh," sitting
    "in small groups between the organized periods for prayer and ritual disciplines" and "sharing tea and
    food" (p. 176, folio 168).
  - Attendance is constrained by work: a young Pakistani male novice, "I missed three or four 'urs because
    of my job. The 'urs in Allahabad … was one of the first 'urs I have been to in two years" (March 22,
    2001, Islamabad) (p. 176, folio 168).
- **Publishing as an institution of the order.** "Most of the shaykhs' works are now readily available in
  printed editions and widely distributed through the silsila's publication networks in both Pakistan and
  Malaysia. Published in both Urdu and English, these books are increasingly accessible to a broad,
  international community of readers" (p. 172, folio 164).
  - A named silsila publication: "The silsila has published an edition of Nizami's Urdu text and, more
    recently, a new English translation entitled A Diary of a Disciple of Nizamudin Aulia [sic]" (p. 172,
    folio 164). The "[sic]" is the book's own.
  - A prescribed reading order: "murids insist that the silsila's books should be read in a particular
    order, a sequence that mirrors the progressive stages of the Sufi path" (p. 173, folio 165).
- **Letters as a circulated genre.** Personal letters "are treasured as heirlooms," photocopied and
  distributed among fellow murids on the shaykh's own recommendation; "The circulation of personal letters
  is, in fact, a relatively common practice among disciples on both sides of the Indian Ocean" (p. 174,
  folio 166). A Malaysian disciple: "We have a whole stack of them from our brothers and sisters that we
  photocopied in the late 1980s" (October 3, 2001, Kuala Lumpur) (p. 174, folio 166).
- **Horizontal teaching.** "murids learn a great deal about the rules of adab by observing and listening to
  one another," through "pir-bhais ['brothers of the master,' or fellow disciples]"; "In this Sufi spiritual
  meritocracy, authority comes with experience" (p. 175, folio 167).
- **Internal friction, reported.** "I heard numerous disciples complain about backbiting, petty squabbling,
  and competition among their peers"; "On several separate occasions, female murids voiced feelings of
  alienation and marginalization, frustrated with what they perceived as entrenched patriarchal practices
  within both the silsila and society at large" (p. 177, folio 169).
- **Conduct made a religious duty.** Shahidullah Faridi's *wasyat nama*: "Any kind of selfish behavior,
  grouping, envy, quarreling, or not talking to one another is against the etiquette of society and must be
  abstained from strictly," and "it is obligatory on the elders of the silsila to treat the young with
  loving kindness" (p. 178, folio 170).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The shaykh answers the unasked question** — reported by a senior Pakistani male murid as common: "Many
  times a question has occurred in my mind and immediately the shaykh answers that question, without having
  been asked… This is the reality of the Unseen entering the phenomenal world" (March 16, 2001, Lahore)
  (p. 167, folio 159).
- **Powers increase after death** — a male Pakistani disciple: "The Sufi saint is like a sword unsheathed
  upon his death… In life they must strictly follow the shari'a. But once they have passed the threshold,
  God puts no limits on their powers" (October 22, 2000, Lahore) (p. 169, folio 161).
- **Zauqi Shah prefigures his own death and promises teaching from beyond the grave**, as recorded in
  *Tarbiyat al-'ushshaq* for September 1, 1942 (20 Sha'ban, 1361) (p. 169, folio 161).
- **The dead shaykh appears in crisis** — a male Pakistani disciple: "They appear in times of grave trouble,
  when you really need them, when you're absolutely helpless. They come and tell you, 'Do not worry, we are
  with you.' These are living souls" (September 1, 2001, Karachi) (p. 170, folio 162), justified by the
  Qur'an at "[3:169]" (p. 170, folio 162).
- **"the miraculous appearance of their own spiritual guide in moments of distress and personal tragedy"** —
  reported by the author as recalled by "several disciples" (p. 170, folio 162).
- **Baba Sahab speaks from the tomb** — "At that moment a voice came to me; it was the voice of Baba Sahab.
  He said, 'You are my books!'" — attributed by an elder Pakistani disciple to Shaykh Wahid Bakhsh
  (April 30, 2001, Bahawalpur) (p. 174, folio 166).
- **Books open to the answer** — a female Pakistani disciple: "often when I open a book the answer to my
  questions appears… I believe it is the shaykh working behind the scenes" (February 26, 2001, Lahore)
  (p. 173, folio 165).
- **Miracle subordinated to law** — a senior Pakistani murid: "If his teachings are against the traditions or
  against the Holy Qur'an, then he is no shaykh, even if he can fly in the air or walk on water!" (April 28,
  2001, Bahawalpur) (p. 179, folio 171).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Sufism accommodated to modernity.** "In my view, these salient modifications to traditional pedagogy also
  demonstrate how Chishti Sabiris have accommodated Sufism to modernity—an important point that I explore
  further in the next chapter" (p. 166, folio 158). Disciples themselves, the author notes, "mark the
  increased emphasis on individual action and adaptive use of new formats for communication as a necessary
  response to changing times" (p. 166, folio 158).
- **Storytelling as pedagogy, borrowed from Kirin Narayan.** The author quotes "Anthropologist Kirin
  Narayan's insights on storytelling … in a contemporary Hindu religious community" as "equally applicable
  to today's Chishti Sabiri silsila," including "Story worlds are compelling precisely because they relate
  back to everyday life" (p. 166, folio 158).
- **Sufi practice couched in legal idiom.** "The use of legal language in this passage is significant. It is
  illustrative of how Chishti Sabiris couch their own Sufi practices in the idioms of mainstream, normative
  Islamic practice. This anecdote also confirms once again the fundamental belief in the legality and
  sanctity of sainthood" (p. 168, folio 160).
- **Source criticism of *Nizami Bansari*.** "no record of either the original Persian text or its elusive
  author exists… it is possible to speculate that the book may have been the product of Khwaja Hasan
  Nizami's own prodigious imagination. Regardless of its historical authenticity, however, contemporary
  Chishti Sabiris view Nizami Bansari as an authoritative account" (p. 172, folio 164).
- **Books as corporate memory.** "For them, books preserve the order's collective memory. At the same time,
  they serve as signposts to guide the individual seeker's journey" (p. 172, folio 164); the order's books
  "effectively enshrine a distinct Chishti Sabiri canon" and, "For the uninitiated, however, texts are at
  best invitations to Sufi practice" (p. 175, folio 167).
- **Ambivalence about Sufi identity, via Catherine Bell and Katherine Ewing.** Bell is quoted that "even the
  basic symbols of a community's ritual life, can be very unclear to participants or interpreted by them in
  very dissimilar ways" (p. 177, folio 169); Ewing, in "her ethnography of popular Pakistani Sufism," that
  "Many are drawn to Sufism and yet avoid identifying themselves as Sufis" (p. 177, folio 169). The author
  concludes that "a pervasive and deep-seated ambivalence about Sufi identity underlies daily life in
  contemporary, postcolonial Pakistan and Malaysia" (p. 177, folio 169).
- **Concealment outward, pride inward.** "Murids are noticeably wary of announcing their Sufi affiliations to
  strangers… In private—and among their fellow disciples—however, Chishti Sabiris wear their silsila
  affiliation with immense pride" (p. 178, folio 170).
- **The two "nows," via Dipesh Chakrabarty.** The disciples' worldview "is rooted in what Dipesh Chakrabarty
  calls 'the temporal heterogeneity of the "now."'" They "live within two interdependent spheres: a
  'historical now' and a 'sacred now,'" the first "measured by the calendar and the clock," the second one
  "positivist history simply does not have the vocabulary to explain or the tools to measure" (p. 179,
  folio 171).
- **The chapter's closing thesis.** Sufi practice "initiates a process of deconstruction"; the aim is "the
  total transformation of a secular, heteronomous self into a sacralized, moral Muslim self," and "Chishti
  Sabiri pedagogy is primarily oral and aural" (p. 180, folio 172).
