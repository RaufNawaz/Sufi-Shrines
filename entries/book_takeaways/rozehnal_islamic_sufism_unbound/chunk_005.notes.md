# rozehnal_islamic_sufism_unbound — chunk 005 — pp. 76–90 (folios 66–80)

**What this chunk is.** Continuous body text from the middle of chapter 2, whose recto running header
throughout is `Muslim, Mystic, and Modern`. It completes the sacred biography of **Shahidullah
Faridi** (p. 76, folio 66 through p. 85, folio 75) and opens that of **Shaykh Wahid Bakhsh Sial
Rabbani** (p. 85, folio 75 to the chunk end). Section headings crossed, in order: "Qualities as a
Teaching Shaykh" (p. 76, folio 66), "Married Life" (p. 78, folio 68), "Miracles" (p. 81, folio 71),
"Ecstasy and Sobriety" (p. 82, folio 72), "Lifestyle" (p. 82, folio 72), "Public and Political Life"
(p. 84, folio 74), "Shaykh Wahid Bakhsh Sial Rabbani (1910–1995)" (p. 85, folio 75), "Family
Background and Spiritual Initiation" (p. 86, folio 76), "Education" (p. 87, folio 77), "Spiritual
Training" (p. 88, folio 78), and "Qualities as a Teaching Shaykh" a second time (p. 90, folio 80).
The chunk opens mid-sentence — "provide a plot within the mazar" (p. 76, folio 66) — and ends
mid-sentence — "even when incapacitated" (p. 90, folio 80). One illustration falls in range:
figure 2.2 (p. 85, folio 75).

**Folio.** Every page in the range prints its folio inside the running header, and the table supplied

    dramatically in 1955. After returning to Karachi from the 'urs of Baba Farid ad-Din Ganj-i

  - **The saint is the source of a living shaykh's name.** "Soon after he officially dropped his

  - **A hagiographic parallel drawn by disciples: the saint met his wife in a dream.** "Several
    disciples recounted a similar story about the saint Baba Farid Ganj-i Shakkar who also met his
    future wife in a prescient dream. These hagiographic parallels of Shahidullah Faridi and his
    namesake are seen as another important marker of legitimacy, authority, and authenticity"
    (p. 79, folio 69).
  - **A second living devotee's lifelong link to him.** "Like her husband, Ammi Jan is remembered for
    her unique connection to the saint, Baba Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakkar", introduced as "this
    lifelong link with the saint of Pakpattan" (p. 80, folio 70).
  - **The saint appears bodily in Zauqi Shah's room (disciple's narration).** A closed door to Zauqi
    Shah's room "was a sign that some saint, or even the Holy Prophet, God bless him and grant him
    salvation, was present there"; the child Ammi Jan opened it, and "the saintly person with him was
    sitting there and said, 'No, no. Let her come. She is my daughter.' And then he put her on his
    lap. Afterward Hazrat Sahab told Ammi Jan that the man was Baba Sahab [the saint, Farid ad-Din]"
    (p. 80, folio 70). The author's own framing: "While this intriguing hagiographic anecdote is not
    recorded in any text, it too is now integrated into the contemporary silsila's collective memory"
    (p. 80, folio 70).
  - **Ziyarat to the mazar, and the reception there.** "Whenever she would visit his mazar [Pakpattan
    Sharif], there was a wonderful scene. People would gather, and there were such blessings. She
    attracted a lot of women; no queen could receive such a reception" (p. 80, folio 70) — narrated

    (p. 80, folio 70).
  - **Twelve years of suluk, paired with 'Ali Ahmad Sabir.** "several murids highlighted the symbolic
    significance of twelve years, noting that the saints Baba Farid Ganj-i Shakkar and 'Ali Ahmad

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Exhumation and relocation of graves for an underground car park.** "In interviews, I was told that
  when an underground parking garage was constructed at the shrine during the 1980s, many such tombs
  were exhumed and relocated" (p. 76, folio 66). The shrine is **(not stated)** in this chunk.
- **Custodianship of a single grave by a devotee, not an institution.** Faruq Ahmad's tomb "is
  maintained by an elderly devotee and frequently visited by Chishti Sabiri murids" (p. 76, folio 66).
- **Domestic khanaqah-substitute after Partition.** Zauqi Shah's household and "several murids, their
  families shared a humble two-bedroom flat on the upper floors of a building near the main railway
  station"; "These cramped living quarters doubled as a space for spiritual practices, including the
  weekly performance of communal zikr" (p. 76, folio 66).
- **A four-year interregnum with no shaykh.** "for the next four years the silsila was effectively
  without a guiding shaykh" after 1951 (p. 76, folio 66); disciples "implored Shahidullah to accept
  disciples of his own" and he refused for want of "explicit permission" (p. 76, folio 66).
- **Two modes of taking disciples.** "Shahidullah accepted murids in the name of his teacher, Zauqi
  Shah (khilafat-i niabati), but eventually he accepted bay'at independently" (p. 77, folio 67).
- **Women in the order.** "Shahidullah was fully accessible to women and treated them as the spiritual
  equals of his male murids" (p. 77, folio 67); in a "last will and testament (wasyat nama) composed
  a year before his death in 1978" he names senior female disciples and asserts that "If the Chishti
  Sabiri tradition allowed … he would have named them his successors" (p. 77, folio 67); "To this
  day, many of the most respected disciples in the silsila are women" (p. 77, folio 67).
- **Membership, uncounted.** "While no official records were ever maintained, Shahidullah's formal
  disciples are said to have numbered in the thousands" (p. 77, folio 67).
- **Transnational recruitment.** "he met regularly with a group of foreign students at a large Islamic
  Center in Karachi, giving lectures in both English and Urdu, some of which were recorded on tape"
  (p. 78, folio 68); followers in East Pakistan, visited at Rangpur (p. 78, folio 68); Wahid Bakhsh's
  later "large contingent of murids from Malaysia" (p. 78, folio 68), to which he travelled "on
  several occasions" (p. 90, folio 80).
- **Refusal of the amulet trade.** The shaykh "had little regard for those who came to him only with
  worldly problems … and discouraged disciples from sending anyone to him merely for amulets
  (tawiz)" (p. 78, folio 68).
- **No hospice, three generations running.** "Just as Zauqi Shah before him, he never lived in a
  khanaqah. Instead, he held weekly zikr sessions and met individually with disciples at his home
  near the central train station in Karachi" (p. 78, folio 68); "Like Zauqi Shah and Shahidullah
  Faridi before him, Wahid Bakhsh never maintained a permanent hospice (khanaqah). In fact, he rarely
  stayed in one place for more than a few weeks" (p. 90, folio 80).
- **Shrine-building described as administratively easy, and read as a miracle.** "the complex
  arrangements necessary for the construction of a shrine were made with remarkable speed and
  ease—a fact that murids see as nothing short of miraculous (interview: September 2, 2002,
  Karachi)" (p. 78, folio 68).
- **Burial near the master, sold or allotted as plots.** "An adjacent graveyard contains plots for
  silsila members who wish to be buried near their spiritual master" (p. 78, folio 68); the chunk
  opens on the same practice at the unnamed shrine — "provide a plot within the mazar" (p. 76,
  folio 66).
- **Marriage as Sufi practice, and marriage into the shaykh's family as legitimation.** Shahidullah
  "viewed marriage as an essential component of Sufi training" (p. 78, folio 68); "Shahidullah's
  marriage into the sayyid family of his own mentor was seen as a vital public affirmation of his
  spiritual status" (p. 79, folio 69).
- **Business and the world.** Shahidullah "owned and operated a small business importing high-quality
  paper and stationary when he first lived in Bombay. Originally called J.G Lennard and Company, he
  later transferred the company to Karachi following Partition (interview: September 1, 2001,
  Karachi)" (p. 83, folio 73).
- **Refusal of family wealth.** His father "arranged to purchase a thousand Morris Minor taxis so that
  Shahidullah might start a new business and generate a large income"; "Shahidullah then distributed
  the taxis to those in need around the city, keeping nothing for himself" (p. 83, folio 73). He
  "never accepted the offers of financial support from his concerned family" (p. 83, folio 73), and
  refused his mother's jewelry (p. 84, folio 74).
- **A territorial grant of spiritual jurisdiction.** Shahidullah's will: Wahid Bakhsh "has initiated
  halqa zikr and guidance in Bahawalpur … I counsel that he should spread the silsila in this place
  only and not move from place to place as that will affect his work" (p. 90, folio 80); the author
  glosses this — "Here Shahidullah effectively delineates the southern Punjab as Wahid Bakhsh's
  personal spiritual domain (wilayat)" (p. 90, folio 80).
- **The ʿurs circuit as a shaykh's annual obligation.** Wahid Bakhsh "regularly attended the cycle of
  annual 'urs celebrations at Sufi shrines" (p. 90, folio 80); earlier he travelled "to Ajmer Sharif
  during Ramadan and to attend the annual 'urs celebrations" (p. 89, folio 79).
- **Succession made public by turban.** "Hazrat Sahab brought a turban (dastar) and tied it on his
  [Wahid Bakhsh's] head" at the 1975 announcement (p. 90, folio 80).
- **Publishing as an institution of the order.** "A recent edition of the silsila's English magazine
  The Sufi Path offers a portrait of the shaykh through a collection of short articles, poems, and
  letters composed by several of his disciples" (pp. 85–86, folios 75–76); Wahid Bakhsh's "voluminous
  corpus of books and articles includes translations of classical Persian malfuzat texts, original
  commentaries on Sufi practice, and polemical tracts on Pakistani identity and political life"
  (p. 85, folio 75).
- **State employment of a Sufi shaykh.** Wahid Bakhsh was "commissioned in the British Indian Army in
  1933" (p. 87, folio 77), attended the academy at Dehra Dun "from 1935 to 1937" (p. 87, folio 77),
  served "as a captain in the Bahawalpur State Army" (p. 87, folio 77), "retired from military
  service altogether in 1946", joined "the Civil Secretariat, serving as the personal assistant to
  the prime minister of the state of Bahawalpur", and "In 1955 he was transferred to the West
  Pakistan Secretariat in Lahore where he worked as a civil servant until his retirement in 1968"
  (p. 88, folio 78).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The undecayed body.** Workers moving Faruq Ahmad's grave "discovered that his body was still
  fresh and had not decomposed—one of the well-known tropes of sainthood"; the author adds "Though it
  is impossible to verify this remarkable story, Faruq Ahmad's tomb was indeed left undisturbed"
  (p. 76, folio 66). Attributed to interviews (p. 76, folio 66).
- **The Pakpattan dream that authorised a shaykh** — narrated by "a highly respected male murid—a
  disciple of Zauqi Shah and close personal friend of Shahidullah", dated "(May 21, 2001, Karachi)":
  Baba Sahab orders "You make these people murids!", Shahidullah objects "My shaykh has not allowed
  me", the dead Zauqi Shah appears and answers "Of course I've allowed you!" (pp. 76–77, folios
  66–67). The author's framing: "This paradigmatic experience is not recorded in any text, but during
  my interviews I heard it narrated—with slight variations—on numerous occasions" (p. 76, folio 66).
- **An Uwaysi summons from Ajmer.** A man named Yasin told Shahidullah that "Khwaja Sahab himself—the
  saint of Ajmer, Mu'in ad-Din Chishti—had specifically directed him in a dream to seek out Hazrat
  Shahidullah"; "This mystical, Uwaysi connection also prefigures Wahid Bakhsh Rabbani's subsequent
  relationship with a large contingent of murids from Malaysia" (p. 78, folio 68), dated to "a stay
  in Ajmer Sharif in 1956" (p. 78, folio 68).
- **The prescient dream of a bride.** Shahidullah dreamt "of an unknown shaykh, holding the hand of a
  small girl" and years later met that girl in Zauqi Shah's house (p. 79, folio 69); the disciple's
  narration is dated "(April 28, 2001, Bahawalpur)" (p. 79, folio 69).
- **Baba Farid claims the child Ammi Jan as "my daughter"** — see the archive bullet above (p. 80,
  folio 70).
- **The vanished corpse.** A disciple who wished to die at Medina was buried "somewhere near Maleer";
  his wife dreamt he said "I am here in Medina"; "The next morning they went to the grave site and
  found only an empty hole there. His body and even the soil was gone. This is a living miracle of
  Hazrat Sahab!" (p. 81, folio 71), dated "(September 2, 2001, Karachi)". The author replies: "It is
  of course impossible to objectively verify such accounts—in this case, a secondhand retelling of an
  orally transmitted story of anonymous origin" (p. 81, folio 71).
- **Languages as karamat.** "his keen knowledge of numerous regional languages—Arabic, Persian,
  Sanskrit, Hindi, and even Saraiki—was considered miraculous" (p. 81, folio 71), alongside
  "knowledge of future events and his powers as a healer and protector" (p. 81, folio 71).
- **A corpse that sits up to greet the Prophet.** Wahid Bakhsh's father, who "would recite Durud
  Sharif … five thousand times daily", died, "but he suddenly sat up and said, 'Why have you tied my
  toes?' … He addressed everyone there, saying, 'The Holy Prophet has come, all of you should stand
  up!' After repeating this several times, he laid down in the same position [and passed away]"
  (p. 87, folio 77), dated "(April 29, 2001, Bahawalpur)".
- **Insubordination as deliverance.** Discharged for refusing to cut his beard or wear shorts, Wahid
  Bakhsh left Malaysia, and "the Japanese overran the garrison just days after his departure. The
  entire regiment was taken as prisoners of war" — "in keeping with the hagiographical model—it saved
  Wahid Bakhsh from disaster" (p. 88, folio 78).
- **The dream that made a concealed khilafat public.** "The body of Hazrat Zauqi Shah Sahab was there,
  and people wanted to take it away. But instead it was brought to his house" (p. 90, folio 80),
  dated "(May 2, 2001, Bahawalpur)".

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Private dreams become collective property.** The author's recurring thesis in this chunk: the
  dream account "illustrates how even private dreams are progressively incorporated into the
  silsila's collective memory" (p. 76, folio 66); the anecdote "is not recorded in any text" yet "is
  now integrated into the contemporary silsila's collective memory" (p. 80, folio 70); "a private
  dream is transformed into public knowledge and then recirculated through a complex network of
  storytelling to reaffirm the Chishti Sabiri hagiographical habitus" (p. 90, folio 80).
- **"Hagiographical habitus" as the book's governing term.** Used of Shahidullah's marriage — "the
  norms of the Chishti hagiograph-ical habitus" (p. 78, folio 68); of his frugality — "Such stories
  reinforce the Chishti hagiographical habitus" (p. 84, folio 74); of Wahid Bakhsh — "a reaffirmation
  of the traditional trajectory—the hagiographical habitus—of Chishti Indo-Muslim sainthood" (p. 85,
  folio 75).
- **The author declines to adjudicate miracle claims but insists on their social force.** "It is of
  course impossible to objectively verify such accounts" (p. 81, folio 71); "Nonetheless, their impact
  on the lives of contemporary disciples is beyond question. Such hagiographical stories serve as key
  tropes in the construction and narration of sacred biography. They mark moments of rupture in the
  natural order that, for Chishti Sabiri believers, confirms the tangible reality of Islamic
  sainthood" (p. 82, folio 72).
- **A convert measured against the premodern paradigm.** "Despite his atypical background,
  Shahidullah's temperament, lifestyle, and teaching style conformed to the premodern paradigm of an
  Indo-Muslim Sufi shaykh" (p. 82, folio 72); the eulogy's prose "mirrors the premodern paradigm of
  Indo-Muslim sainthood—affirming the continuity of the Chishti Sabiri discursive tradition even in
  the face of widespread societal transformation" (p. 86, folio 76).
- **A hagiography observed in the making.** Wahid Bakhsh's life story is "preserved in a nexus of oral
  narratives"; "It is safe to assume that in time Wahid Bakhsh's teachings and life experiences will
  also be permanently recorded for posterity in a formal hagiography. But for the moment he remains a
  saint under construction" (p. 86, folio 76). The eulogy in The Sufi Path is called "the first trace
  of an incipient, proto-malfuzat" (p. 86, folio 76).
- **Politics: quietism explained by outsider status.** "the shaykh never formally joined a political
  party, and he was never as politically outspoken as either his spiritual master, Zauqi Shah, or his
  friend and fellow disciple Wahid Bakhsh"; "Perhaps his status as an outsider—and an Englishman
  living in South Asia in the twilight of the British Raj, no less—prompted Shahidullah to be wary of
  voicing his personal political views in public" (p. 84, folio 74).
- **Spiritual reform as precondition of political reform.** "Time and again he emphasizes that
  spiritual development is an essential prerequisite for anyone who hopes to engage in meaningful
  political activism or institutional reform" (p. 84, folio 74); the author's gloss: "For Shahidullah
  Faridi, Pakistan could never hope to fulfill its promise as an Islamic republic in the absence of
  widespread, fundamental moral reform" (pp. 84–85, folios 74–75).
- **Partition as biography.** Wahid Bakhsh's "personal biography and worldview were profoundly shaped
  by the politics of Partition", and he "continued the efforts of his predecessors to root the
  Chishti Sabiri tradition within the spatial and cultural landscape of postcolonial, independent

  profound impact on his worldview and political perspective" (p. 88, folio 78).

  catalogue of oral histories from the disciples of Wahid Bakhsh. Collectively, these accounts offer

## Doubts

- **One-row mapping, stated as an explicit positive and an explicit negative.**
  `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` is the **only** archive row named in pp. 76–90, and it is named
  on five of the fifteen pages (pp. 76, 77, 79, 80, 81; folios 66, 67, 69, 70, 71). **No other
  archive row appears anywhere in this chunk.** This is not a keyword miss: the chunk's shrines are
  Ajmer (India), Sakhi Hassan (Karachi) and Allahabad (southern Punjab), and none is in the 169 rows.
- **Which Farid.** The index carries two Farid rows: `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` (principal
  figure "Baba Fariduddin Mas'ud Ganjshakar", Pakpattan) and `mithankot-kot-mithan` (principal figure
  "Khwaja Ghulam Farid", Mithankot). The book fixes the first beyond doubt by naming Pakpattan four
  times in direct apposition — "the 'urs of Baba Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakkar in Pakpattan" (p. 76,
  folio 66), "the saint in Pakpattan" (p. 77, folio 67), "the saint of Pakpattan" (p. 80, folio 70),
  "his mazar [Pakpattan Sharif]" (p. 80, folio 70). **Mithankot does not appear.** Recorded so the
  check is not redone.
- **The unnamed shrine with the car park (p. 76, folio 66) is the one real gap in this chunk.** The
  chunk begins mid-sentence at "provide a plot within the mazar", so the shrine housing Faruq Ahmad's
  subterranean grave — the one where "an underground parking garage was constructed … during the
  1980s, many such tombs were exhumed and relocated" — **is named, if at all, on p. 75, in chunk
  004** (p. 76, folio 66). If it turns out to be a Pakistani shrine with an archive row, three facts
  here become that row's: the plot-within-the-mazar practice, the 1980s exhumations, and the
  undecayed body. **Flagged for the consolidator to resolve against chunk 004; no id assigned and
  none guessed.**
- **Wahid Bakhsh's Allahabad shrine has no archive row, and its toponym is a trap.** The book's
  "Allahabad" is explicitly "the village of Allahabad in the southern Punjab (sixty miles southwest
  of the city of Bahawalpur)" (p. 86, folio 76) — a Pakistani village, **not** the Indian city. No
  index row matches it, and the task brief confirms Wahid Bakhsh has none. Recorded as a candidate
  for a future row, since the author photographed the shrine himself (p. 85, folio 75).
- **"Sakhi Hassan" is a Karachi cemetery, not a Sakhi shrine.** "a large cemetery called Sakhi Hassan"
  (p. 78, folio 68) collides by name with `sakhi-sarwar` (Sultan Sakhi Sarwar, Dera Ghazi Khan) and
  with `purana-bhalwal` (principal figure "Hazrat Shah Sulaiman Nuri (Sakhi Badshah)"). It is neither.
  Noted because it is the kind of match a keyword pass would make wrongly.
- **"Waris Hasan" is not Waris Shah.** "Shah Sayyid Waris Hasan" / "Mawlana Waris Hasan Sahab"
  (p. 80, folio 70; p. 83, folio 73) is Zauqi Shah's own guide and is **not** Waris Shah of
  `mausoleum-of-waris-shah` (Jandiala Sher Khan). No id assigned.

  rechecked:** Muhammad Zauqi Shah, Shahidullah Faridi (Lennard), Wahid Bakhsh Sial Rabbani, Faruq
