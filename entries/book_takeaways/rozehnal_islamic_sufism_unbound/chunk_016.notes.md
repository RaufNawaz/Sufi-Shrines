## Practices, institutions, economy

- **'Urs at Pakpattan, crowds and repeated fatal crushes.** "the massive crowds that attend the
  shrine's annual 'urs commemoration"; accidents "are not uncommon"; the 1911 report of "several lives
  … lost by the crush of the Door of Paradise, and before British rule, it is said, this was of yearly
  occurrence" (Ch 1 n. 20, p. 242, folio 235). The 2001 event is called a "Wedding Anniversary" in the
  title of Haroon's unpublished article (Ch 1 n. 24, p. 242, folio 235) — the book gives **no 'urs
  date** for it: (not stated).
- **Custodianship and state endowment at Pakpattan.** "the Diwan" versus "the representatives of the
  Awqaf Department"; the demand for "an annuity of 1,500,000 rupees rather than the 150,000 rupees
  they were promised" (Ch 1 n. 21, p. 242, folio 235). A locked door is implied by the cited headline
  "Who Locked the Door at Shrine?" (Ch 1 n. 22, p. 242, folio 235).
- **Colonial state and shrines.** "For a more detailed examination of the British policy toward local
  Sufi institutions and exemplars in the Punjab, see Sarah F. D. Ansari, Sufi Saints and State Power:
  The Pirs of Sind, 1843–1947 (Lahore: Vanguard Books, 1992); David Gilmartin, Empire and Islam:
  Punjab and the Making of Pakistan (Berkeley: University of California Press, 1988), 39–72, 205–224"
  (Ch 1 n. 10, p. 241, folio 234). See also Gilmartin, "Shrines, Succession, and Sources of Moral
  Authority" (Ch 1 nn. 11 and 16, p. 241, folio 234) and Eaton, "Court of Man, Court of God,"
  Contributions to Asian Studies, Vol. XII (1982): 58 (Ch 1 n. 16, p. 241, folio 234).
- **State politics of sainthood.** "Katherine P. Ewing, 'The Politics of Sufism: Redefining the Saints
  of Pakistan,' Journal of Asian Studies, Vol. XLII, No. 2 (February 1983): 253" and "Saifur Rahman
  Sherani, 'Ulema and Pir in the Politics of Pakistan,' in Economy and Culture in Pakistan: Migrants
  and Cities in a Muslim Society, ed. Hastings Donnan and Pnina Werbner (New York: St. Martin's Press,
  1991), 216–246" (Ch 1 n. 12, p. 241, folio 234).
- **Initiation.** "The term in Arabic is bay'a, but here I follow the Persian rendering (bay'at)
  favored by contemporary Chishti Sabiri disciples" (Ch 4 n. 17, p. 254, folio 247). Uwaysi initiation
  is glossed from Ernst and Lawrence: "The nonphysical binding of two like-minded Sufis is called
  Uwaysi initiation, and it shows up with particular force in the Sabiri branch of the Chishtiyya"
  (Ch 2 n. 38, p. 245, folio 238).
- **faizan.** "On faizan and its connection to Sufi doctrine and ritual practice, see Buehler, Sufi
  Heirs of the Prophet, 117–118" (Ch 1 n. 26, p. 242, folio 235).
- **Women, succession and the order.** "In Tarbiyat al-'ushshaq, Muhammad Zauqi Shah asserts that

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Initiation in a dream**, attributed to al-Hasan: "According to Sayyid Sharif al-Hasan's account,
  Zauqi Shah also received bay'at directly from Rashid Ahmad Gangohi in a dream on October 4, 1934.
  See al-Hasan, Sirat-i Zauqi, 494" (Ch 2 n. 44, p. 245, folio 238).
- **Bodily heat**, attributed to al-Hasan and glossed by the author: "Another discourse notes that due
  to excessive bodily strength (jismani hiddat) and an inner heat, Zauqi Shah was often forced to
  drink excess amounts of water, even in winters" (Ch 2 n. 35, p. 245, folio 238).
- **The teaching on miracles**, attributed to Rabbani: he "summarizes Zauqi Shah's teachings on
  hijabat (veils from God), kashf (unveiling), and karamat (miracles) in detail" (Ch 2 n. 56, p. 246,
  folio 239).
- **Progress after death**, quoted by the author from Zauqi Shah in Tarbiyat al-'ushshaq: "The friends
  of God continue to progress even after death. There is no limit to the divine essence [dhat], so

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The book's claim to originality, stated in a note.** On Ernst and Lawrence's treatment of Zauqi
  Shah: "Their analysis, however, focuses largely on a single source: the short biography by Sayyid
  Sharif al-Hasan, Sirat-i Zauqi… Furthermore, their study does not include a detailed survey of
  either Shahidullah Faridi or Wahid Bakhsh Sial Rabbani" (Ch 2 n. 20, p. 244, folio 237). This is the
  author's own justification for the three-master structure of Chapter 2.
- **A judgement on a rival ethnography.** Of Pinto's Piri-Muridi Relationship: "Pinto's analysis is
  valuable more for its ethnographic mate- / rial—the book offers lengthy quotes from contemporary
  Chishti murids— / than its interpretive analysis" (Ch 4 n. 19, pp. 254–255, folios 247–248). In the
  same note: "Contemporary firsthand accounts of the Sufi master-disciple relationship are rare. Among
  the more accessible and insightful works are a number of spiri- / tual diaries written by women"
  (p. 254, folio 247).
- **Two judgements against Wahid Bakhsh's programme.** "Clearly, Wahid Bakhsh views the implementation
  of shari'a as the first step to social reform, but he provides no specific details on the
  codification, interpretation, and institutionalization of Islamic law" (Ch 3 n. 93, p. 252,
  folio 245); and "Wahid Bakhsh provides a broad outline of 'Islamic democ- / racy' but few details on
  its practical implementation" (Ch 3 n. 97, p. 252, folio 245). His military proposals are called
  "sweep- / ing" (Ch 3 n. 96, p. 252, folio 245).
- **A comparative method declared.** "Scholarship on medieval Christian sainthood offers important
  theoretical and methodological insights for a study of Muslim sainthood" (Ch 2 n. 3, p. 243,
  folio 236), followed by a long list of Peter Brown, Coon, Delooz, Kleinberg, Reynolds and Capps.
- **A cross-religious reading of ascetic practice.** "This emphasis on inner heat, implicitly caused by
  ascetic spiritual exercises, recalls the Hindu notion of tapas" (Ch 2 n. 35, p. 245, folio 238).
- **Theoretical borrowings named.** habitus "is borrowed from Pierre Bourdieu … The Logic of Practice
  … 52", with Csordas cited as "a trenchant critique" (Ch 2 n. 4, p. 243, folio 236); the taxonomy
  "modernists, traditionalists, Islamists" "is based on Marilyn Robinson Waldman, 'Tradition as a
  Modality of Change: Islamic Examples,' History of Religions, Vol. 25, No. 4 (1986): 318–340" (Ch 3
  n. 16, p. 249, folio 242).
- **The post-9/11 frame of the book's politics.** Ch 1 n. 6 (cont.) points to "Asia-Caucasus Analyst
  (Johns Hopkins University, SAIS, November 6, 2002)" and, "On the regime of General Musharraf and the
  landscape of post-9/11 Pakistan", to "Ahmed Rashid, 'Pakistan on the Edge,' New York Review of
  Books, October 10, 2002, 36–40; Isabel Hilton, 'The General in His Labyrinth: Where Will Pervez
  Musharraf Lead His Country?' New Yorker, August 12, 2002, 42–55" (p. 241, folio 234). On jihad:
  "Jihad is a misunderstood and maligned concept, especially in the wake of the attacks of September
  11, 2001" (Ch 3 n. 89, p. 252, folio 245).
- **Sufism as contested.** "For detailed cross-cultural and historical studies on the polemics over
  Sufism in various Islamic societies, see the essays in Frederick de Jong and Bernd Radtke, eds.,
  Islamic Mysticism Contested… For a more focused study of the debates over Sufism in the context of
