# rozehnal_islamic_sufism_unbound — chunk 013 — pp. 195–209 (folios 187–201)

**This chunk is the ritual core of chapter 5.** It runs unbroken inside "Experiencing Sufism: The
Discipline of Ritual Practice" (the running header `Experiencing Sufism` prints on every recto,
pp. 195, 197, 199, 201, 203, 205, 207, 209; the verso header prints `Islamic Sufism Unbound`,
pp. 196, 198, 200, 202, 204, 206, 208). It carries three display sub-headings: "Zikr : The

**The chunk delivers the promised shrine material, but only in its second half.** pp. 195–204

muraqaba heading (p. 204, folio 196) and runs to the end. **Two archive rows are named**, one of

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The order's written rule-book is named.** "the shajarah confirms that prayer is the core of
  Chishti Sabiri practice" (p. 195, folio 187), and the manual **Shajara tayyaba** "prescribes a daily
  repertoire that combines these litanies into a standardized format" (p. 199, folio 191). The daily
  round comprises "devotional exercises (awrad-i rozana), recitations for Friday prayers (ma'mulat-i
  jum'a al-mubarak), and annual recitations tied to the Muslim lunar calendar (ma'mulat-i salana)"
  (p. 195, folio 187).
- **Prescribed counts, as printed:** zikr-i ism-i dhat begun "with five hundred, and then extend it
  up to as many as ten or twelve thousand… the minimum is reciting one thousand in a sitting. Most
  people do up to six or seven thousand, because that takes about half an hour" (p. 198, folio 190).
  The Shajara tayyaba sequence: "After a hundred strikes… Do this two hundred times. Then strike the
  heart four hundred times with only 'illa'llah.' After that recite 'Allahu Allah' six hundred
  times… Repeat this through the entire rosary [tasbih] [one thousand two hundred repetitions]. If
  time is short… recite 'Allah' with the voice onto the heart five hundred times." (p. 199, folio 191)
- **Habs-i dam** ("the keeping of the breath"): "complete submersion in water for prolonged periods
  of time, a method that is said to cool the 'heat' generated by intense zikr… known as a particular
  specialty of past Chishti Sabiri spiritual masters" (p. 199, folio 191); "generally, habs-i dam is
  performed while submerged in water during the winter… begins with the recitation of 'Allah' twenty
  one times and then gradually increases" (p. 199, folio 191).
- **Halqa-i zikr — the weekly assembly, with its venues named.** Held "On Thursday evenings" (p. 201,
  folio 193). Locations: **Karachi** — Shaykh Siraj 'Ali Muhammad "leads a large zikr circle in a
  private home near the tomb complex of Shahidullah Faridi"; **Lahore** — "a senior male murid hosts
  a weekly halqa-i zikr in his home," and "A female disciple of Shaykh Wahid Bakhsh Rabbani does the
  same, leading a separate session comprised mostly of women"; **Islamabad** — "Another female murid
  used to host a similar weekly halqa in Islamabad, but this practice has recently been
  discontinued"; **Malaysia** — "disciples take turns hosting a communal zikr in private homes in and
  around Kuala Lumpur" (all p. 201, folio 193). **All venues are private homes; no shrine, khanqah or
  institutional building is named as a zikr venue.**
- **Dress, gender and seating at the halqa:** "They dress in clean clothes—typically white shalwar
  kamiz. Men wear a white prayer cap (topi) and women a thin head covering (dupatta)… disciples sit
  with legs crossed in ordered rows on the floor. The ritual space is gendered, with women sitting in
  rows behind the men. The lights are dimmed" (p. 201, folio 193).
- **Order of service:** maghrib prayers, then "the imam (prayer leader) turns from the qibla (prayer
  niche) to face the audience, and the zikr immediately begins," proceeding as call and response
  (p. 201, folio 193). The eleven-item repertoire recorded "at a gathering on a Thursday evening in
  October 2000" in Lahore is printed in full, from "Rabb ighfir" to "salla Allahu 'alaihi wa sallam"
  (p. 202, folio 194).
- **Duration and the explicit ban on hospitality — an economic fact in the negative.** "The entire
  halqa-i zikr typically lasts about half an hour" (p. 203, folio 195); "That is why serving any food
  or even a cup of tea is strictly forbidden, because some people then might come just to socialize"
  (August 26, 2001, Lahore) (p. 203, folio 195). **There is no langar here, and none is mentioned
  anywhere in the chunk.**
- **Change in the institution over time, from an elder:** "the length of halqa has shortened. It used
  to last well over an hour. Traditionally it has always held on Thursdays, except for the month of
  Ramadan when it used to be every day… The shaykhs also used to hold a stick" (September 1, 2001,
  Karachi) (p. 204, folio 196).
- **Shrine etiquette, prescribed in print.** Wahid Bakhsh in *Islamic Sufism*: "The first and
  foremost duty of the visitor is to have a bath, change clothes, use perfumes and enter the shrine
  with love and reverence for the saint… Next he has to recite the Fatiha… and sit down facing the
  grave with closed eyes… one must sit, or stand if there is no room to sit, for about half an hour
  with full attention focused on him." (pp. 206–207, folios 198–199)
- **Pilgrimage rhythm at Data Darbar:** Thursday evenings before the halqa, often Sundays too;
  privately or as a family, never as a group; **not** for the ʿurs; "one-day attendance" (p. 207,
  folio 199; p. 208, folio 200).
- **Architecture and building history, Pulau Besar (Malaysia).** "The shrine of Sultan al-'Arifin is
  located in a prominent position near the beachfront. The grave was covered with an elaborate wooden
  structure until the 1970s when it was torn down. It has recently been rebuilt as a modest open-air
  tomb constructed of tiles, covered with a metal roof, and surrounded by a gate." (p. 209, folio 201)
- **Funding and administration of that shrine — sentence incomplete at the chunk boundary.** "The
  mazar is now funded and administered through a" (p. 209, folio 201). The predicate falls in the next
  chunk; recorded as a pointer, not a fact.
- **Site context, Pulau Besar:** "a small island (ten square miles)," "served as a Japanese military
  base during the Second World War, and today it is the site of Pandanusa Resort—a small-scale
  tourist attraction with a restaurant, swimming pool, and a modest eighteen-hole golf course"
  (p. 209, folio 201).
- **Private meditation chambers:** "Many murids have arranged a private meditation chamber in their
  homes for just this purpose" (p. 201, folio 193).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The shaykh submerged all night**, reported by Zauqi Shah of his own master and labelled by the
  author as hagiographic: "My shaykh, Mawlana Sahab [Shah Sayyyid Waris Hasan]… sometimes went into a
  pond after night prayers and remained under water the entire night… During the entire night he
  would take only two or three breaths." (p. 200, folio 192). The author's own framing: "Zauqi Shah's
  account of his own experiences and the miraculous story of his shaykh's spiritual prowess echo
  common tropes in Sufi hagiographies." (p. 200, folio 192)
- **The grant of South Asia at al-Hujwiri's tomb**, given by the book as tradition: "According to
  tradition Mu'in ad-Din Chishti Ajmeri (d. 1236)… was granted the dominion (wilayat) of all of South
  Asia, for himself and his successors, at al-Hujwiri's tomb." (p. 207, folio 199)
- **The saints are alive in their graves**, given as the disciples' belief: "because they believe
  that the Friends of God are alive and accessible in their graves, entry into a saint's presence
  requires careful preparation" (p. 206, folio 198); contemplation is "an intimate conversation
  between the Sufi disciple and the patron saint, alive in his tomb" (p. 207, folio 199).
- **Data Sahab appears and speaks**, in a female disciple's words: "When you visualize Data Sahab,
  you see Data Sahab. Many times he will appear to you and give you messages." (p. 208, folio 200)
- **The current as spiritual energy:** murids "compare the spiritual energy (faizan) that radiates
  from the saint's tomb during muraqaba to an intense electric current surging through the body"
  (p. 208, folio 200).
- **A hadith cited by Wahid Bakhsh for the lata'if:** "There is a piece of flesh in the body called
  Qalb (heart), the heart is in the Soul, the Soul is in the Sirr, the Sirr is in the Khafi, and the
  Khafi is in the Akhfa which is in the Divine 'I' (Ana)." (p. 196, folio 188)

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Insularity is the chapter's key finding about shrine practice.** "As a rule, Chishti Sabiri
  murids are discouraged from visiting the tombs of Sufi saints outside the silsila. Though they
  acknowledge that any saint's tomb is potentially a conduit for Divine inspiration, Chishti Sabiri
  attitudes toward sacred space are remarkably insular." (p. 207, folio 199)
- **A three-node sacred geography is asserted for the contemporary order** — Pakpattan at the centre,
  with Karachi and Allahabad "now marked as Chishti Sabiri domain as well" (p. 206, folio 198) — and
  then extended overseas: "the expansion of Chishti Sabiri spiritual domain is not limited to
  Pakistan… a Sufi shrine in Malaysia now provides an alternative outlet for pilgrimage and worship"
  (p. 209, folio 201).
- **Practice over theory.** "most Chishti Sabiri disciples were largely unaware of this nuanced
  psychophysiology. When asked about the spiritual body, in fact, most murids admitted ignorance…
  as a rule Chishti Sabiri shaykhs stress practice over theory." (p. 196, folio 188)
- **A denominational contrast drawn on the form of zikr:** Chishti Sabiri zikr is vocal and loud,
  "This distinguishes Chishti Sabiris from their Naqshbandi counterparts who typically practice a
  silent, internal form of zikr." (p. 198, folio 190)
- **Zikr is held at arm's length from music.** "Although it is not considered 'music' per se, there
  is a definite rhythm and lyricism to Chishti Sabiri vocal zikr (zikr-i lisani). As an oral and
  aural performance, it resembles the art of Qur'an recitation (tajwid)." (p. 198, folio 190) — the
  nearest the chunk comes to sama'.
- **A cautious cross-tradition suggestion:** habs-i dam "and in particular the concept of spiritual
  'heat' (tapas)—suggests a possible connection with Hindu yogic practices" (p. 199, folio 191).
- **Comparison with the Naqshbandiyya, via Buehler:** the Mujaddidi tradition "developed a complex,
  formalized routine of twenty-six distinct contemplations," against which "The contemporary Chishti
  Sabiri system is, by comparison, far less nuanced and systematized" (p. 204, folio 196); Buehler is
  also quoted that visualizing the shaykh "involves both an emotional tie of love and a specific
  psychological tie of modeling" (p. 206, folio 198).
- **Kugle quoted on Ziya' al-Qulub:** it is "not about metaphysics, but rather about
  metaphysiology: that is, how the human body and especially the heart can be trained to adopt
  postures and motions that have transcendent ethical effects, so that the body itself can become a
  mirror for a just cosmic order." (p. 195, folio 187)
- **Sobriety, not intoxication, claimed as the order's speciality.** Wahid Bakhsh: "Sobriety in the
  middle of storms of intoxication is our system or method… this is the real specialty of our silsila"
  (pp. 205–206, folios 197–198).
- **Ritual as continuity under change:** "For today's Chishti Sabiris the persistence of ritual
  practice provides a vital sense of continuity… Its performance also cements a powerful link between
  twenty-first-century murids and their spiritual predecessors." (p. 204, folio 196)
