## Practices, institutions, economy

- **'Urs defined by the book:** "'Urs literally means 'marriage.' In the context of Sufi practice, it
  serves as a metaphor for the wali Allah's final union with God." (p. 214, folio 206).
- **The economic character of 'urs, stated generally:** "Throughout South Asia, 'urs celebrations draw
  massive crowds to the shrines of Sufi saints. These festivals combine religious fervor, public
  spectacle, and a bustling economic exchange." (p. 214, folio 206).
- **Offerings named, and repudiated for this order:** "The festive part of 'urs has existed for a very
  long time, with people offering ritual gifts [nazrana], flowers, and cloths to cover the grave
  [chadar]. But the kind of attendance we have is not for nazrana. It is only for the company of the
  shakyh [text-layer?: shaykh] at the mazar." (p. 216, folio 208).
- **Cost of attendance:** "They [Chishti Sabiri shaykhs] encourage people who fly from Canada, or even
  Jeddah to come and attend the 'urs. It is that valuable. People are encouraged to part with a lot of
  money." (p. 216, folio 208); "Many disciples accept significant personal and financial sacrifice in
  order to participate in these annual events." (p. 216, folio 208).
- **Endowment and patronage at Pulau Besar:** the shrine is sustained by a "local religious endowment
  (waqf )", per an interview of "October 6, 2001, Kuala Lumpur" (p. 210, folio 202).
- **Custodianship:** Pulau Besar has a "shrine's caretaker" who "possesses a spiritual genealogy
  (shajara) written in Jawi, the local Malay language in Arabic script"; he "was absent that day" of
  the author's October 2001 visit, and a villager guided the author instead (p. 210, folio 202). No
  sajjada nashin, Auqaf department or custodial family is named for any Pakistani shrine in this chunk
  — (not stated).
- **A monthly pilgrimage cycle:** "On the last Sunday of each month, a group of Malaysian murids
  regularly travel to Pulau Besar for pilgrimage. They also make the trip during the annual
  commemoration of the death anniversary ('urs) of major Chishti Sabiri shaykhs when they are unable to
  travel to Pakistan or India." (p. 211, folio 203).
- **State destruction of shrines in Malaysia:** "In fact, instead of celebrating they destroy the
  mazars. One of the largest ones, in fact, was demolished by the state government" (p. 211, folio 203);
  murids there "maintain a low profile" and "keep quiet because of the government" (p. 211, folio 203).
- **Retreat length and facilities:** "'urs retreats" are "weeklong" (p. 217, folio 209); "Everyone
  receives a short refresher course, six or seven days" (p. 216, folio 208); "It's like going to a
  monastery and staying there for a week" (p. 215, folio 207). "The living conditions are austere but
  comfortable" (p. 217, folio 209).
- **Gender segregation:** "The Chishti Sabiri living quarters and ritual spaces are segregated by
  gender during 'urs. While men and women travel together, once they reach the shrine they separate for
  the duration of the spiritual retreat. The access for women at the mazar varies according to
  location." (p. 219, folio 211).
- **Dress and ablution rules:** "Before entering any shrine, murids perform ablutions and dress in clean
  clothes. Both men and women typically wear simple, white shalwar kamiz. The men cover their heads
  with a skull cap or a modest head covering; the women cover their heads with a scarf, or dupatta."
  (p. 217, folio 209).
- **Sama' performers and instruments:** "the singers—professional musicians known as qawwals
  ('reciters')—perform poetic verses (kalam), accompanied by the rhythm of hand clapping, drums
  (tabla), and the harmonium" (p. 224, folio 216). Payment of the qawwals is (not stated).
- **Where and when the mahfil is held:** "The Chishti Sabiri musical assemblies are usually held on the
  night of a Sufi saint's death anniversary, most often within an enclosed ritual space adjacent to the
  tomb" (p. 224, folio 216), with access restricted: "With rare exception, only a select, inner circle
  of disciples are allowed to participate." (p. 224, folio 216).
- **Colonial-era politics of the shrine, as a murid tells it:** "At the time, the British were trying to
  exploit Sufism, saying that it was not Islamic. So in the original school of Deoband, they said, 'No,
  do not go to mazars because the Hindus also go there.'" (p. 214, folio 206).
- **Deobandi fatwas against shrine worship** are discussed as a live institutional conflict in
  contemporary Pakistan (p. 213, folio 205), with the counter-argument that the fatwa "is for those who
  are completely illiterate" and does not bind "the people of distinction [khawas]" (p. 213, folio 205).
- **Partition as an institutional cause:** "The redrawing of South Asia's geopolitical boundaries in
  1947 reconstituted the nation and citizenship, mandating new maps and new passports. With the
  emergence of an independent (and Islamic) Pakistan, the absence of the Jain, Sikh, and Hindu
  communities profoundly altered traditional cultural practices and local religious identities."
  (p. 215, folio 207).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The saint of Pulau Besar summoned at Medina** — attributed by the author to local legend: "Local
  legend suggests that the shaykh received instructions to travel to the area for proselytization
  (tabligh) at the tomb of the Prophet during 'umra"; "He arrived on a merchant ship"; "The saint is
  credited with converting large numbers of the local population to Islam" (p. 210, folio 202). The
  author calls these "standard saintly tropes" (p. 210, folio 202).
- **Seven shaykhs guided to the island by the wali sanga** — attributed as report: "These are said to
  include seven shaykhs from Indonesia who were spiritually guided to the island by the nine legendary
  saints of Java (wali sanga), along with two shaykhs from Baghdad." (p. 210, folio 202).
- **The saint's territorial guardianship of Malaysia** — quoted from a senior Malaysian disciple,
  reporting his shaykh: "According to my shaykh, this mazar here at Pulau Besar oversees the care of
  all Malaysia . . . He is the real source of spiritualism in this country" (p. 211, folio 203).
- **Faizan felt physically at the tomb** — quoted from a senior murid: "It's just like electric
  current, like this wind blowing towards you . . . It makes your hair stand up. You feel high"
  (p. 211, folio 203).
- **The saint is bountiful on his 'urs day** — Wahid Bakhsh Sial Rabbani's claim: "because the departed
  soul himself is the recipient of enhanced favor and blessings characteristic of this occasion, he is
  in a bountiful mood and bestows more and more favors on his people" (p. 214, folio 206).
- **Madmen and clowns at a shrine as a protective buffer** — Wahid Bakhsh's explanation: "they are
  there to counterbalance the enormous torrents of blessings, charms and fascinations which, if left
  unimpaired by the clowns, would turn people mad by their intensity." (p. 217, folio 209).
- **Knowledge at the shrine surpasses the universities** — told of Shahidullah Faridi at Pakpattan's
  bihishti darwaza by a senior Pakistani disciple: "These are the real universities, these places. Real
  knowledge is given to you through the attention of the saint, through inspiration." (p. 220,
  folio 212).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Shrines as heterotopias.** "Sufi shrines function as heterotopias. As loci of alterity, Sufi tomb
  complexes provide alternative, liminal spaces that stand in sharp contrast to the realm of everyday
  experience" — glossed with an unattributed quotation about "counter-sites, a kind of effectively
  enacted utopia" (p. 212, folio 204).
- **The shrine as ritual theatre**, via Richard Eaton: "In India these shrines displayed,
  theater-style and in microcosm, the moral order of the Islamic macrocosm . . . their fundamental
  raison d'etre was religious." (p. 212, folio 204).
- **The order's counter-polemic is that shrine devotion is orthodox, not shirk.** "As the 'signs of
  God' (ayat Allah), the Sufi saints are the very embodiment of Islamic orthodoxy. The outpouring of
  love and respect at Sufi tombs, Shahidullah concludes, is therefore a natural expression of Muslim
  devotion that violates neither sunna nor shari'a." (p. 212, folio 204).
- **Deoband as a contested inheritance.** Chishti Sabiris "view this as a misreading and ultimately a
  betrayal of the teachings of their nineteenth-century predecessors" and "note that the founders of
  Deoband were themselves active patrons of shrines" (p. 213, folio 205); supported with Ernst and
  Lawrence: "The presence of Hindu practices at a Muslim shrine cannot explain centuries of
  participation of educated Sufi masters in pilgrimage" (p. 213, folio 205).
- **'Urs as "serial khanaqah" — the chapter's own coinage.** "For today's Chishti Sabiris, 'urs
  effectively serves as a serial khanaqah. Four times a year murids—both men and women—travel from all
  over Pakistan and beyond" (p. 215, folio 207); disciples "mark this break with tradition as a
  necessary innovation, a pragmatic response to the ground-level realities of the times" (p. 215,
  folio 207).
- **Communitas and a Tablighi parallel.** The journey "forges a deep bond between murids—a heightened
  sense of communal identity that anthropologist Victor Turner famously labeled communitas" (p. 216,
  folio 208); the parallel with Tablighi Jama'at is drawn and then qualified — "The difference, of
  course, is one of emphasis. While the Tablighi's mission focuses on proselytization (da'wa), Chishti
  Sabiris view pilgrimage as a ritual discipline aimed expressly at the individual murid's spiritual
  development." (p. 216, folio 208).
- **Partition reshaped Sufi sacred geography**, producing both a transferred 'urs and "the emergence of
  entirely new Chishti Sabiri shrines on Pakistani soil" (p. 215, folio 207).
- **The Indian Ocean network.** "The symmetry of this Indian Ocean network is remarkable. The humble
  tomb of sixteenth-century South Asian Sufi saint buried on a remote island in the heart of Southeast
  Asia now serves as an alternative center of pilgrimage for the Malaysian disciples of a
  twentieth-century Pakistani Sufi master." (p. 211, folio 203); the shrine "plays a major role in
  transplanting the silsila into the landscape of Southeast Asia, marking Malaysia as Chishti Sabiri
  sacred space" (p. 211, folio 203).
- **Sama' as the Chishti order's defining practice**, via Bruce Lawrence: "sama' assumed a unique
  significance as the integrating modus operandi of the Chishti silsila . . . sama' became, if not the
  monopoly of the Chishtiyya, the preeminent symbol crystallizing their position vis-a-vis other
  Indo-Muslim leadership groups." (p. 222, folio 214).
- **The order's sama' apologetics read as defensive.** "They too feel the need to marshal evidence and
  arguments to demonstrate the spiritual efficacy and legality of Sufi music—a defensive posturing that
  suggests they are aware of (and threatened by) critics of the tradition." (p. 222, folio 214).
- **Wahid Bakhsh's distinctive contribution**, via Ernst and Lawrence: "The approach of Capt. Wahid
  Bakhsh is at once bolder and more 'rational.' He explicates sama' as the culmination of spiritual
  discipline . . . It is no longer ancillary; it is central." (p. 223, folio 215).
- **Private mahfil against commercial qawwali.** "The careful attention to atmosphere and audience,
  they insist, distinguishes the closed, private performance of the mahfil-i sama' from the daily,
  public displays of qawwali at Sufi shrines throughout Pakistan" (p. 224, folio 216), and disciples'
  "criticism of popular qawwali reduces mass-produced music to a form of commercialized entertainment
  that is often insensitive to the Sufi tradition" (p. 224, folio 216).
- **Sama' as pedagogy, not entertainment.** "In the context of the Sufi path, however, sama' is first
  and foremost a pedagogical tool—a ritual technology for the molding and shaping of a moral, virtuous
  self." (p. 222, folio 214).

## Doubts

- **`mithankot-kot-mithan` rests on eight words and a date range.** The whole evidence is "the
  nineteenth-century Chishti master Ghulam Farid (1844–1901)" as the author of the malfuzat
  *Maqabis al-majalis* (p. 223, folio 215). The book gives **no Mithankot, no Kot Mithan, no tomb, no
  'urs and no second name** for him here, and the id is assigned only under the 18 Sep 2026
  author-only ruling. The consolidator should weigh it as a name-plus-date hit. Two further checks,
  recorded so they are not redone: (i) this is **not** Baba Farid of Pakpattan
  (`shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar`), who appears throughout the same chunk under different names and
  a different century; (ii) it is **not** Shahidullah **Faridi**, per the standing refusal.
- **Interim convention (a): toponyms held back, and the rows they would take on reversal.**
  (1) **Multan** (p. 210, folio 202) appears only as a distance marker — Uch Sharif is "seventy miles
  southwest of Multan in Pakistan" — with no Multan shrine, saint or 'urs named. On reversal it would
  have to choose among the archive's eight Multan rows (`shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya`,
  `shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam`, `shrine-of-shah-shams-ud-din-sabzwari`, `shrine-of-shah-yusaf-gardez`,
  `shrine-of-syed-musa-pak`, `shrine-of-hafiz-muhammad-jamal-multani`,
  `shrine-of-hazrat-shah-ali-akbar-shah-ali-akbar-shamsi`, `prahladpuri-temple`) and **nothing in the
  chunk discriminates**, so a reversal still leaves it unmapped.
  (2) **Uch Sharif** (p. 210, folio 202) is named with a saint — "Shaykh 'Abd al-Qadir Jilani Thani
  (d. 1533), the famous Qadiri saint of Uch Sharif" — but **no tomb, 'urs or custodian at Uch is
  stated**, and he is the principal figure of none of the archive's five Uch Sharif rows
  (`shrine-of-jalaluddin-surkh-posh-bukhari-jalaluddin-bukhari`,
  `shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht`, `tomb-of-baha-al-halim-uch-sharif`, `tomb-of-javindi-bibi`,
  `tomb-of-ustad-nuriya`). So this falls short of the "town plus a named saint's tomb" exception on two
  counts and would remain unmapped on reversal. **This is the one place a human may wish to look
  again**, since it is a Pakistani archive location named with a dated saint.
  (3) **Peshawar** (p. 219, folio 211) — "the northwest frontier town of Peshawar", the home town of a
  quoted female disciple who adds "In the frontier, we have so many shrines where you can walk into the
  saint's shrines." **No Peshawar shrine is named**, so none of the archive's ten Peshawar rows can be
  chosen; a reversal changes nothing.
  (4) **Lahore** (pp. 214, 216, 218, 219, folios 206, 208, 210, 211) and **Bahawalpur** (p. 220,
  folio 212) appear **only as interview datelines** — "(March 16, 2001, Lahore)", "(August 26, 2001,
  Lahore)", "(April 25, 2001, Lahore)", "(February 26, 2001, Lahore)", "(October 31, 2000, Lahore)",
  "(April 29, 2001, Bahawalpur)" — with no shrine, saint or site attached to either. A reversal would
  leave Lahore undiscriminated among the archive's many Lahore rows, and Bahawalpur has no town row at
  all (four rows carry "Bahawalpur District" only via Uch Sharif).
  (5) **Karachi** and **Allahabad** are *not* convention-(a) cases: each is named with a saint's tomb
  and 'urs (Shahidullah Faridi, p. 215, folio 207; Wahid Bakhsh Sial Rabbani, p. 215, folio 207), so
  the exception fires — but **both saints are under standing refusals with no archive row**, so no id
  exists to assign and they sit under "Other saints, sites and events" for that reason, not for
  convention (a). Do not fold either into an existing Karachi row.
  (6) **Ajmer / Rajasthan, Deoband, Gangoh (implied in 'Abd al-Quddus Gangohi), Delhi (in "the Delhi
  Sultanate"), Mecca, 'Arafat, Baghdad, Jeddah, Kuala Lumpur, Pulau Besar, Palembang, Sumatra, Java,
  Indonesia, Malaysia, Canada** — **no archive row corresponds to any of them**, so the convention
  returns empty and a reversal changes nothing here.
