## Practices, institutions, economy

- **ʿUrs and crowd disaster at Pakpattan.** The stampede occurred at night during qawwali: a
  disciple describes the "rhythm of the qawwali [music]" and the bodily urge to "get up and dance
  along with the rhythm" as happening "that same night, around the time of the mob stampede in
  Pakpattan Sharif" (p. 44, folio 34).
- **Shrine administration and crowd control.** The two blame narratives at (p. 48, folio 38) turn
  precisely on shrine management — "the greed of the local shrine authorities" versus administrators
  "overturning established crowd control policies and shirking responsibility". The book names no
  Auqaf department, no sajjada nishin by name and no revenue figure for Pakpattan in this chunk:
  (not stated).
- **Custodianship vocabulary.** The book distinguishes "the genealogical heirs to the Sufi saints
  (sajjada nishin)" from the Sufi shaykhs whose authority "must be earned through self-discipline
  and experiential knowledge", and ranks the latter above the former, the "'ulama" and the "maulvis"
  (p. 47, folio 37).
- **Shrine visiting as a marked practice.** "Today we go to the Sufi shrines [mazars] like the
  Barelwis, but we are neither Deobandis nor Barelwis. We simply are following the way of our
  spiritual masters" (May 21, 2001, Karachi) (p. 46, folio 36).
- **Pilgrimage and baraka.** "For the faithful who flock to Sufi shrines on pilgrimage, the wali
  Allah represents a tangible and renewable source of blessing" (p. 53, folio 43).
- **State co-optation of shrines and leaders.** "Sufi leaders are co-opted into the ideology and
  institutions of the state as political agents and power brokers, mediators in regionalized
  networks of local identity and power" (p. 47, folio 37).
- **Donations, in the premodern norm the book quotes from Bruce Lawrence:** the shaykh, "Poor and
  unmindful of worldly possessions, he must yet be receptive to large donations of money and be able
  to dispense them quickly for the benefit of those in need" (p. 55, folio 45); and he must live
  "in proximity to" worldly people and kings, "i.e., near a city", staying "in touch with them
  through his lay disciples" (p. 55, folio 45).
- **Khanaqah as institution.** The hospice appears both historically — Gul Surkh "residing for some
  time in the hospice (khanaqah)" of Baha ad-Din Zakaria (p. 57, folio 47) — and normatively, the
  shaykh attending "those needs evidenced by his disciples and visitors to his hospice [khanaqah]"
  (p. 55, folio 45).
- **Madrasas.** A murid "now runs an informal religious school (madrasa) for women in Lahore"
  (p. 44, folio 34); "the Deoband madrasa" is "a religious educational institution with roots in
  nineteenth-century India" (p. 46, folio 36) founded by scholars mentored by Hajji Imdad Allah
  (p. 58, folio 48).
- **Print and publishing as the order's infrastructure.** Tarbiyat al-'ushshaq was "First published
  in 1958" (p. 50, folio 40); the two principal biographies "were written in Urdu in the late 1950s
  and are now published in Pakistan by the contemporary Chishti Sabiri order" (p. 56, folio 46);
  Shamamat al-'Ambar "is now published by the Chishti Sabiri silsila in Pakistan", and its partial
  English translation A Guide for Spiritual Aspirants "has recently been published by Chishti Sabiri
  disciples in Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia as well" (p. 59, folio 49). Zauqi Shah "clearly benefited from
  the rapid spread of print technology" (p. 59, folio 49).
- **The order's textual corpus, itemised.** Tarbiyat al-'ushshaq "comprises four separate works":
  1. Muqaddama, by Wahid Bakhsh Rabbani; 2. Sirat-i Zauqi, by Sayyid Sharif al-Hasan (p. 56, folio
  46); 3. Malfuzat compiled by Shahidullah Faridi, "delivered to disciples between 1942 and 1950";
  4. Malfuzat compiled by Wahid Bakhsh Rabbani, "covering the period from 1940 to 1951" (p. 57,
  folio 47); plus Hajj-i Zauqi on "Zauqi Shah's final Hajj pilgrimage to Mecca in 1951" (p. 57,
  folio 47).
- **Sama'.** Named as a normative risk in the Lawrence typology: the shaykh "Prone to ecstasy,
  whether in silent solitude or abetted by music and verse while in the company of other Sufis
  [sama'], he must yet be able to recall and to perform his obligatory duties as a Muslim" (p. 55,
  folio 45).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- The book attributes to Chishti Sabiri disciples, not to itself, the reading of the Pakpattan
  deaths as demanded by Baba Farid: "these pious Sufi devotees found a deeper meaning and,
  ultimately, a transformative sense of redemption amid their grief", interpreting their losses "as
  a ritual sacrifice demanded by the saint" (p. 48, folio 38).
- The Tarbiyat al-'ushshaq introduction promises the reader "obtaining the abilities of visions
  (kashf ) and saintly miracles (karamat)", and annihilation "in the Prophet (fana' fi al-rasul) and
  in God (fana' fi Allah)" (p. 50, folio 40).
- The saint's exemption from nature, stated as the Sufi view: "the Sufi saint is unbound from the
  laws of nature by virtue of his intimacy with God" (p. 53, folio 43), and the wali Allah is
  "empowered to perform miracles … empowered to mediate the course of destiny" (p. 53, folio 43,
  quoting Vincent Cornell).
- Premodern norm, per Lawrence: "Capable of performing miracles [karamat, 'saintly graces'], he must
  yet be careful to suppress them on most occasions" (p. 55, folio 45).
- Attributed to Zauqi Shah's own lecture: "Zauqi Shah reveals to his disciples that his family's
  lineage was confirmed for him in a vision of the Prophet himself" (p. 57, folio 47).
- Attributed to Zauqi Shah's hagiographers: at Ajmer in 1913 "he prayed for guidance and was
  rewarded with a vivid dream of a mysterious Sufi master", later recognised as Shah Sayyid Waris
  Hasan (p. 58, folio 48); and "he remained in direct spiritual contact with the saint [Hajji Imdad
  Allah] throughout his life via dreams and visions" (p. 58, folio 48).
- Stated as general Sufi practice: "Dreams and visions of the Prophet serve as key markers of
  spiritual progress along the path" (p. 54, folio 44).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The chapter's thesis about the Pakpattan counternarrative.** The disciples' oral accounts "shift
  the discursive frame from the level of personal tragedy into a story of national trauma"; they
  transform "the loss of individual lives at the shrine into a symptom of the internal moral decay
  of the nation", by "a logic and worldview directly opposed to the rationalist discourse of the
  state" (p. 44, folio 34). Rozehnal's evidential claim is that this counternarrative stakes a
  proprietary claim on the shrine itself (p. 44, folio 34, quoted in full above).
- **The order's programme, in Rozehnal's formulation:** "the order's overarching project to put
  Islam back into Islamic Sufism" (p. 44, folio 34), a "sunna-centric and shari'a-minded Sufism
  couched in a distinctively Pakistani idiom" distinguished by "its unique response to the
  challenges and changes spurred by Partition" (p. 45, folio 35).
- **Orthodoxy is contested, not fixed** — argued via Daniel Brown, quoted at length: the ambiguity
  "reflects a struggle over the question of who has the authority to represent the Prophet" (p. 45,
  folio 35).
- **The order's double refusal.** Chishti Sabiris criticise "the Ahl-i Hadith, Wahhabis, Jama'at-i
  Islami, Tablighi Jama'at, and a variety of pro-Taliban religious parties" (p. 46, folio 36) and
  also "avoid association with the pro-Sufi Barelwi movement", steering "between the doctrinal
  conservatism of the contemporary Deoband movement and the populism of the Barelwis" (p. 46, folio
  36); they "remain wary of all political labels and actively avoid direct involvement in any
  political party or movement" (p. 46, folio 36).
- **Chapter 1's closing generalisation about Pakistan:** "Sufism remains a malleable and
  controversial signifier within the combative discursive landscape of twenty-first-century
  Pakistan" (p. 47, folio 37), and "In today's Pakistan, Islam itself is argued and debated. And when
  it comes to Sufism, lines are drawn and sides are chosen" (p. 47, folio 37).
- **Three stated assertions governing Chapter 2** (pp. 51–52, folios 41–42): "1. Sainthood blurs the
  boundaries between history and myth"; "2. Sainthood is simultaneously paradigmatic and protean",
  introducing Rozehnal's own coinage, "a paradigm I refer to as the hagiographical habitus"; "3.
  Sainthood is socially constructed", so that "If disciples fail to recognize and publicize their
  master's piety, the aspiring saint is doomed to historical anonymity" (p. 51, folio 41).
- **The walaya / wilaya distinction** is set out, with the book conceding it is unsettled: "In
  Arabic, walaya conveys a sense of 'closeness' … Conversely, wilaya … carries a connotation of
  power, patronage, and authority" (p. 52, folio 42) — but "Here Nizam ad-Din reverses the
  definitions" (p. 52, folio 42), Chodkiewicz and Cornell disagree (pp. 52–53, folios 42–43), and
  "For premodern Sufi masters and contemporary academics alike, there is no consensus on the
  interpretation of walaya or wilaya" (p. 53, folio 43).
- **Bruce Lawrence's nine-point profile of the premodern Indo-Muslim Sufi shaykh** is quoted entire
  (p. 55, folio 45) and made the chapter's measuring rod: Rozehnal will "demonstrate how this
  typology … continues to inform the contemporary construction of Chishti Sabiri sainthood" (p. 56,
  folio 46), and finds "how closely the sacred biographies of these modern masters adhere to the
  premodern model of Indo-Muslim sainthood" (p. 56, folio 46).
- **Method, stated as a disclaimer.** "My survey is by no means an exhaustive, year-by-year
  recounting of the lives of Muhammad Zauqi Shah, Shahidullah Faridi, and Wahid Bakhsh Sial
  Rabbani"; "Here I am less concerned with compiling historical 'facts' than in measuring how the
  legacies of these modern Sufi exemplars are remembered and re-presented in hagiographical
  narratives" (p. 56, folio 46).
- **The one clean break from the premodern paradigm that Rozehnal identifies:** Aligarh. Graduating
  there "likely made Zauqi Shah the first Chishti master to be educated in a modern university.
  This distinction marks a major deviation from the premodern paradigm of Indo-Muslim sainthood"
  (p. 59, folio 49). Note the hedge "likely" — the book does not assert it as established.

## Doubts

- **Two location contradictions between the book and the archive, in one sentence-run at (p. 57,
  folio 47), and they are mirror images of each other. This is the chunk's single most important
  review point.** (1) The book calls Baha ad-Din Zakaria "the well-known Suhrawardi saint of Uch
  Sharif"; the archive row `shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya` gives his location as "Multan City,
  Multan, Punjab". (2) The book calls Sayyid Jalal ad-Din Bukhari Makhdum-i Jahaniyan "the famous
  fourteenth-century Suhrawardi master of Multan"; the archive row
