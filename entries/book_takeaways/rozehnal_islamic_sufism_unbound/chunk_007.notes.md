## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Succession and spiritual office.** Wahid Bakhsh: "After Hazrat Mawlana Zauqi Shah, may God have
  mercy upon him, his principal successor [khalifa-i a'zam], Hazrat Shah Shahidullah Faridi, may God
  have mercy upon him, received the political charge of India. The people of spiritual insight know
  well the apparent [zahiri] and hidden [batini] work he carried out for the politics of the
  country" (p. 110, folio 101).
- **Publishing as an institution of the silsila.** Shahidullah Faridi "played a central role in
  continuing and expanding the silsila's publication campaign", and "In his last will and testament,
  the shaykh emphasizes the vital importance of the order's books and directs several senior murids
  to carry on with this important work" (p. 110, folio 101). Zauqi Shah's political writings,
  "including his reciprocal correspondence with Jinnah", were "compiled and published by the Chishti
  Sabiri order in 1949 in a volume entitled Muzamin-i Zauqi [The Articles of Zauqi]" (pp. 108–109,
  folios 99–100); "An English version of this book was reprinted in Lahore in 1998 under the title
  Letters of a Sufi Saint to Jinnah" (p. 109, folio 100).
- **Book market.** Of Wahid Bakhsh's Urdu output: "Many of these works are now easily found in the
  Urdu bookstores of Karachi and Lahore" (p. 111, folio 102).
- **Press and periodicals used by the order.** "New Searchlight on the Vedic Aryans" ran in the
  Delhi Urdu paper Manshur "in installments on June 20, June 27, and July 4, 1943", with "An English
  version … simultaneously published in the Allahabad paper Onward on June 14 and June 21" (p. 107,
  folio 98). Zauqi Shah published "numerous essays that appeared in the Karachi paper Dawn in 1945
  and 1946, as well as a regular column in the short-lived English language weekly The People's
  Voice, which ran for several months beginning in December 1947" (p. 108, folio 99); and "a short
  Urdu pamphlet titled Islam, Pakistan aur jamhuriyat [Islam, Pakistan and Democracy] published in
  1950" (pp. 108–109, folios 99–100).
- **Party and committee office.** Zauqi Shah "attended the first meeting of the Muslim League in
  Karachi in 1907 and formally joined the organization in Ajmer in 1940"; "was elected vice
  president of the League's district and provincial administration and served as the head of the
  Ajmer Sirat Committee"; and "As a member of the All India Council … attended the Muslim League's
  annual session in Madras on April 8, 1941" (p. 108, folio 99). "Between 1937 and 1947, the shaykh
  exchanged a series of letters in English with Pakistan's future leader Muhammad 'Ali Jinnah
  (1876–1948)" (p. 108, folio 99).
- **Ziyarat — shrine visitation, defended and conditioned.** Wahid Bakhsh "comments on the efficacy
  of visiting the graves of deceased spiritual masters (ziyarat)––a ritual act widely condemned by
  Islamists. Here he evokes numerous hadith in defense of the practice" (p. 119, folio 110).
  "Visiting shrines, Wahid Bakhsh maintains, is a powerful catalyst to spiritual development but
  only when complemented by a vigilant adherence to discipline and decorum (adab)" (p. 119, folio
  110). In his own words: "The amount of inspiration you get from shaykh of the mazar very much
  depends on the effort which you put in at home during the year … work at home and inspiration at
  the shrine are interdependent and supplement each other" (p. 119, folio 110).
- **Zikr and sama'.** "Wahid Bakhsh commits an entire chapter to the practice of zikr––the
  recollection and recitation of the names of God", covering "a range of vocal recitations, physical
  movements, and breathing techniques, along with the resulting spiritual effects" (p. 119, folio
  110). "Another chapter examines the efficacy of listening to music (sama'), extolling the benefits
  of Sufi spiritual poetry performed by a singer (qawwal) under the strict supervision of a shaykh"
  (p. 119, folio 110). Both are called "distinctly Chishti Sabiri practices" (p. 119, folio 110).
- **Shari'a as precondition, and the charge against false faqirs.** "strict adherence to the shari'a
  is a vital precursor to the Sufi path (tariqa) toward gnosis (ma'arifa)" (p. 118, folio 109);
  Wahid Bakhsh condemns "charlatans and pseudo-faqirs, who in order to fleece the simple innocent
  people, have appeared in different garbs and guises", whose "most common" tactic is "the cult of
  pantheism of the Hindu type under whose cover they get rid of all the rituals of Islam", and who
  "resort to heavy drugs" (p. 119, folio 110).
- **The book as object.** *Islamic Sufism* "was first published in English in 1984 and subsequently
  reprinted in several editions, both in Pakistan and Malaysia" (p. 112, folio 103); it runs to "400
  pages", "a third" of which is "a detailed critique of Orientalist characterizations of Sufism in a
  chapter entitled 'The Myth of Foreign Influence of Sufism Exploded'" (p. 112, folio 103); and "It
  contains technical vocabulary, an index, bibliography, glossary, graphs, and even a color
  photograph of the shaykh" (p. 120, folio 111). *Pakistan ki azim ush-shan difai quwwat [The
  Magnificent Power Potential of Pakistan]* "was first published in Urdu in 1986. It was
  subsequently reissued in 2000 in an English translation" (p. 121, folio 112).
- **The state and the shrine.** "As the 2001 Pakpattan tragedy clearly illustrated, Islamic
  authority and authenticity are intensely argued and debated in Pakistani public discourse. In
  response to the state's attempts to appropriate Sufi tradition, Chishti Sabiri writings imagine an
  alternative past, present, and future" (p. 121, folio 112).
- **Fieldwork apparatus.** Interviews are datelined by place: "(March 22, 2001, Islamabad)" (p. 109,
  folio 100); "(June 17, 2001, Lahore)" (p. 111, folio 102); "(December 10, 2000, Karachi)" (p. 111,
  folio 102).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Revelation as political warrant**, quoted by the author from Zauqi Shah's letter to Jinnah of
  February 25, 1939: "My recent Sufistic revelations fully confirm what I have been feeling
  intuitively for some time—that, before long, Islam will become the only dominating force in the
  world" (p. 108, folio 99).
- **Zauqi Shah as the hidden founder of Pakistan**, attributed by the author to Wahid Bakhsh's
  introduction to Tarbiyat al-'ushshaq: "This matter is not generally known by the people, but the
  elect know it: while Qaid-i Azam [Muhammad 'Ali Jinnah] was the outward founder of Pakistan,
  Hazrat Muhammad Zauqi Shah was its inward, spiritual founder" (p. 109, folio 100).
- **Chishti saints as conquerors of Hindustan**, attributed by the author to Wahid Bakhsh quoting
  Zauqi Shah: "from ancient times Chishti saints have played a major role in the conquest of
  Hindustan . . . That is why Hazrat, may God have mercy upon him, often used to say, 'Hindustan is
  the inheritance of the Chishtis.'" (p. 109, folio 100).
- **Hidden spiritual charge over India**, attributed to Wahid Bakhsh on Shahidullah Faridi's
  "apparent [zahiri] and hidden [batini] work … for the politics of the country" (p. 110, folio 101).
- **Prophetic presence transferred**, attributed by the author to "A senior Pakistani female
  disciple": "He was a replica of our Prophet… The moment they would sit with him, they would be
  quiet and they would not dare utter a word. I think that was the presence that was transferred to
  him from the Prophet" (pp. 110–111, folios 101–102).
- **Concealment as attainment**, Wahid Bakhsh's fifth point: the highest stage, "baqa-bi-Allah
  ['remaining in God']", is one in which "the seeker looks so ordinary in the midst of inner storms
  of fascinations and charms that it becomes exceedingly difficult to detect his high spiritual
  attainments" (p. 115, folio 106).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The author's frame for Zauqi Shah's communalism.** "Zauqi Shah's polemic needs to be understood
  in the context of the confrontational communal politics of the late colonial period"; the shaykh
  "responds to attacks against Indo-Muslim civilization by denigrating Hindu history and identity
  and then conflating it with a vision of a parallel global Jewish conspiracy" (p. 107, folio 98).
  The author quotes Ernst and Lawrence: "Here Zauqi Shah regrettably opened the door to acceptance
  of notorious anti-Semitic propaganda, such as the apocryphal 'Protocols of the Elders of Zion,'
  within the Chishti order" (pp. 107–108, folios 98–99), and adds "These attitudes have indeed
  continued", instancing Wahid Bakhsh's *Reactivization of Islam*, which quotes "extensively from the
  'Protocols' in a chapter entitled 'Glimpses of a Zionist Conspiracy'" (p. 108, folio 99).
- **Zauqi Shah and Mawdudi, per Ernst and Lawrence, quoted by the author:** "Despite his opposition
  to Maududi's authoritarianism, Zauqi essentially agreed with Maududi's analysis of the crisis
  facing Indian Muslims; both felt that reviving Islam through political means was absolutely
  necessary, but they differed considerably in their interpretation of what Islam actually meant"
  (p. 107, folio 98).
- **The counterpolemic thesis.** "the Chishti Sabiri historical project deviates radically from that
  of its Islamist counterparts. Here Sufi saints are anything but marginalized, navel-gazing
  mystics. Instead, Chishti Sabiri masters are positioned at the forefront of both religious and
  political life" (p. 109, folio 100).
- **Continuity across three shaykhs.** "there is a remarkable continuity of practice, teaching
  style, and public posturing in the twenty-first-century Chishti Sabiri order" (p. 109, folio 100);
  the two successors "never matched their predecessor's political activism" but "followed Zauqi
  Shah's example by taking up the pen" (p. 109, folio 100).
- **The author's explanation of Shahidullah Faridi's political silence** (offered as conjecture, and
  marked as such): "it is likely that Shahidullah's personal background as an Englishman and Muslim
  convert may have contributed to this reluctance to explicitly address the political realm"
  (p. 110, folio 101). Set against this, "His Urdu spiritual discourses, in fact, contain selective
  but insightful lectures on a broad range of relevant political topics", Malfuzat-i shaykh covering
  "Islamic political theory and the support of Sufis for just political rule, secularism in Europe,
  hudud punishments and social reform, Shi'ism and sectarian politics, and the spiritual duties of
  jihad" (p. 110, folio 101).
- **Wahid Bakhsh's seven refutations, as the author numbers and quotes them** (p. 112, folio 103,
  through p. 117, folio 108): (1) Orientalists misunderstand Sufism because they misunderstand Islam
  (p. 113, folio 104); (2) the word tasawwuf was said not to be "in vogue in the time of the Prophet"
  (p. 113, folio 104); (3) "the similarity of Sufi phraseology to that of Christian and Hindu
  mysticism and Neoplatonism" (p. 114, folio 105); (4) "the pantheism of the Sufis [the doctrine of
  wahdat al-wujud]" (p. 114, folio 105); (5) the undetectability of "baqa-bi-Allah" (p. 115, folio
  106); (6) judging "Islam on the standards of Christianity" (p. 116, folio 107); (7) "some
  difference of opinion between the Sufis and the orthodox theologians of Islam" (p. 117, folio 108).
- **Experience over scholarship.** The author reads Wahid Bakhsh's preface as a claim of standing:
  "Wahid Bakhsh asserts an expertise rooted in direct, unmediated experience gained through the
  rigors of Sufi practice" (p. 112, folio 103); and "For Wahid Bakhsh, the Sufi's intuitive,
  experiential knowledge eclipses the abstractions and positivism of both the Orientalists and the
  'ulama" (p. 115, folio 106).
- **Influence reversed.** On the chapters "The Strong Influence of Islamic Sufism on Hindu Mysticism"
  and "The Influence of Sufism on the Hindu Sects, Lingayats (Jangamas) and Siddhars [sic]", the
  author writes that Wahid Bakhsh "turns the Orientalist argument on its head to assert the
  widespread influence of Sufi mystics on their South Asian religious counterparts" (p. 114, folio
  105), and judges the resulting claims "certainly debatable" while granting that they "offer key
  insights into the logic and rhetorical strategy of Wahid Bakhsh's counterpolemic" (p. 114, folio
  105).
- **Parallel with Nadwi.** "Wahid Bakhsh's rhetoric parallels the historical project of his
  contemporary Sayyid Abu'l Hasan 'Ali Nadwi (1914–1999)" (p. 114, folio 105).
- **The author's own reading of the book's thinness on doctrine.** "While Wahid Bakhsh does not say
  so, I suspect that the relative absence of detailed attention to doctrine has much to do with
  Islamic Sufism's intended audience: educated Pakistanis and international disciples, scholars and
  potential converts" (p. 118, folio 109).
- **Ewing, quoted on contested Pakistani Islam:** "Today in Pakistan, after many years of reformist
  activity, there is a wide range of opinions about which activities fall within the boundaries of
  proper Islam and which fall outside, despite many efforts to articulate an Islam that would draw
  all Pakistanis together as a nation" (p. 117, folio 108).
- **Asad, quoted to explain why the shaykhs write politics at all:** "It is not only that the state
  intervenes directly in the social body for purposes of reform; it is that all social activity
  requires the consent of the law, and therefore of the nation-state. The way social spaces are
  defined, ordered and regulated makes them all political" (p. 121, folio 112).
- **The chunk's closing verdict on *Islamic Sufism*.** "In appropriating select categories of
  modernity (rationalism and science) and simultaneously rejecting others (Westernization,
  materialism, and secularization) the shaykh walks an ideological tightrope"; the book is "a
  quintessentially Sufi text designed to communicate the Chishti Sabiri tradition to a global
  audience" (p. 121, folio 112).
