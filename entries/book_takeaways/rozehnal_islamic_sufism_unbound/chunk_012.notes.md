## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Fieldwork dates and places, as printed after each interview quotation** (rule 1: digits in this
  book are reliable): October 30, 2000, Lahore (p. 193, folio 185); November 7, 2000, Lahore (p. 188,
  folio 180); March 22, 2001, Islamabad (p. 190, folio 182); March 24, 2001, Islamabad (p. 186, folio
  178); April 25, 2001, Lahore (p. 191, folio 183); May 2, 2001, Bahawalpur (p. 191, folio 183); May
  23, 2001, Karachi (p. 193, folio 185); June 17, 2001, Lahore, twice (p. 190, folio 182); August 26,
  2001, Lahore, four times (p. 183, folio 175; p. 184, folio 176; p. 193, folio 185; p. 194, folio
  186); September 1, 2001, Karachi (p. 192, folio 184); September 30, 2001, Kuala Lumpur (p. 187,
  folio 179); October 1, 2001, Kuala Lumpur (p. 186, folio 178); October 3, 2001, Kuala Lumpur
  (p. 190, folio 182).
- **The khanaqah is treated as defunct.** Asked "if the khanaqah was an institution that could
  function in the modern world," a senior Pakistani male disciple answered, "I think that the saints
  have concluded that it is not possible now—because of the requirements of the times, the
  requirements of jobs, with everyone living such a fast pace of life" (p. 183, folio 175). The
  author calls it "the premodern Sufi lodge memorialized in classical texts" whose days "have come
  and gone" (p. 183, folio 175). A young Pakistani murid adds that "Early on, disciples could come to
  the khanaqah and stay there, so they would stay in close touch in any case" (p. 193, folio 185).
- **Bay'at** is the bond of entry: it "ensures blessings, protection, and guidance," and by joining
  "the moral community of the silsila a novice murid gains immediate access to the order's teaching
  networks and support system as well" (p. 192, folio 184). Bay'at can be taken in a dream: "According
  to the shaykhs, it is the same thing [taking initiation in dreams or in person]" (p. 187, folio
  179).
- **The order's handbook.** On receiving bay'at, "she/he receives a copy of the silsila's official
  handbook," *Shajara tayyaba ma'a awrad aur waza'if*, compiled by Zauqi Shah; it "is distributed to
  all disciples and is not available for public consumption" (p. 194, folio 186). It contains copies
  — "one of them in verse" — of the shajara, "Beginning with the current teaching shaykh … back to
  the Prophet Muhammad"; a list of general precepts (hidayat-i 'am) covering "the dietary practices,
  rules of decorum (adab), and supererogatory prayers (nawafil) required of each murid"; and suras
  for specific occasions, du'a and durud, "divided into subcategories: the requisite daily prayers
  (waza'if)" (p. 194, folio 186).
- **Daily practice is now solitary and unshared.** "In daily practice, Chisthi Sabiris perform the
  majority of their spiritual exercises (awrad) in isolation" (p. 192, folio 184 — the book prints
  "Chisthi"; see Doubts). A senior Pakistani disciple: "Suluk is much more isolated now … We don't
  ask each other, 'Do you do zikr 5,000 times, 7,000 times, or 10,000 times a day?' In fact, you are
  discouraged from discussing these things. But the shaykh knows what every murid is doing"
  (pp. 192–193, folios 184–185). The author calls this "a significant break from Chishti Sabiri
  tradition, a necessary concession to the demands of twenty-first-century life" (p. 193, folio 185).
- **The shaykhs are said to be restricting numbers.** Disciples "suggest that the immense challenge
  of integrating suluk into modern life has also prompted Chishti Sabiri shaykhs to close ranks,
  restricting the numbers of disciples" (p. 193, folio 185); a young Pakistani male murid speculates,
  "Maybe that is why the shaykhs do not like to expand [the silsila] too much now, because of the low
  quality of disciples! They are very selective" (p. 193, folio 185).
- **The time budget of suluk, as disciples state it.** "A serious person on the Sufi path is
  recommended to do spiritual work [mujahada] four to five hours a day, in addition to their job and
  daily routine—two to three hours in the morning after morning prayers and one to two hours after
  evening prayers" (p. 193, folio 185). Another: "That is the main block of time you have, a
  three-hour block in the morning. And in addition to that, one or two hours in the evening,
  especially between the afternoon and night prayers" (p. 194, folio 186); many "sit until ishraq [a
  supererogatory prayer after sunrise]," which "In winter especially … is very close to office time,"
  so "you depart for the office about half an hour after ishraq" (p. 194, folio 186).
- **Istikhara.** "One such technique known as istikhara involves the recitation of a litany of
  special prayers in prescribed locations––often in a mosque or the vicinity of a saint's
  shrine––with the expectation of an answering dream" (p. 190, folio 182). Shahidullah Faridi's rite,
  from *Malfuzat-i shaykh*: "After the evening prayer, one should declare the intention [niyyat] …
  Recite Durud Sharif … before and after, and recite Du'a-i Qunut ['supplication said standing']
  seven times. Then go to sleep" (p. 191, folio 183). "Chishti Sabiri murids typically perform
  istikhara on Thursday or Sunday evenings," and it is held "particularly useful for guidance and
  clarity regarding such important matters as marriage, education, financial matters, and
  employment" (p. 191, folio 183). A worked case: a senior Pakistani disciple weighing "a job offer
  that promised higher pay but more responsibilities" said "I will do istikhara and if it is negative
  I will not make the change" (p. 191, folio 183).
- **Adab of dream-telling.** "Chishti Sabiri adab dictates that disciples must narrate their dreams
  directly to their spiritual mentor" (p. 188, folio 180), and secrecy is enforced by the shaykhs
  themselves: a male Pakistani murid quotes Wahid Bakhsh, "If you tell these secrets then, sorry, no
  more secrets for you" (p. 188, folio 180). "Chishti Sabiris view dreams as a form of communication
  with God, a personalized message that is decidedly not for public consumption" (p. 188, folio 180).
- **Ritual as curriculum.** "In Chishti Sabiri practice, rituals are taught to individual disciples
  by the teaching shaykh and subsequently reinforced and rationalized through a social network of
  learning" (p. 182, folio 174). After interpreting a dream "the spiritual master assigns a regimen
  of spiritual exercises" (p. 191, folio 183). The three ritual complexes the chapter names as "at
  the very heart of Chishti Sabiri identity" are "dreams and dream interpretation, the daily rituals
  of remembrance, and the annual cycle of pilgrimage networks and musical assemblies at Sufi shrines"
  (p. 184, folio 176).
- **The five pillars come first.** "Like any pious Muslim, the Sufi adept begins with a strict
  adherence to the arkan," which are "then supplemented with a regimen of extra-canonical Sufi
  rituals expressly aimed at taming the ego" (p. 182, folio 174).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Conversion by a dream of Jesus**, narrated by "a senior Malaysian murid of Chinese ethnicity"
  brought up "in a Taoist family": he dreamt of Nabi Issa in white robes floating in the sky, who
  "called out, 'Anyone who wants to be saved, kneel down.' So I knelt down"; he embraced Islam
  "together with my wife" (pp. 185–186, folios 177–178). The author comments, "Here a vision of Jesus
  precipitates a change of faith" (p. 186, folio 178).
- **A dream that came true the same day**, narrated by a senior Pakistani disciple who fell asleep
  reading *Tarbiyat al-'ushshaq*: he dreamt of a "very holy person" by a pond who "was the true copy
  of a photograph [of the shaykh] I had seen," woke to be told "Today Hazrat Shahidullah Sahab came
  here and gave you some books," and found him "standing in the very same spot that I had dreamt
  about"; "That evening everyone became his murid, my wife, my daughter, and my sons" (p. 186, folio
  178).
- **Uwaysi-style initiation in a dream** — a male Malaysian disciple's unfulfilled desire for bay'at
  with Wahid Bakhsh "resulted in a surprising Uwaysi-style initiation at the hands of Shaykh
  Shahidullah Faridi" (pp. 186–187, folios 178–179), whom he had "never met" (p. 187, folio 179).
- **The luminous footsteps of the Prophet** — a female Malaysian disciple of Wahid Bakhsh, in a place
  "full of shaitan [demons] and jinn [spirits]," saw "the footsteps of Rasul … They just lit up," and
  following them "I was safe from all the shaitan and jinn. They could not touch me" (p. 190, folio
  182).
- **Satan cannot counterfeit the Prophet or the shaykh** — the hadith Wahid Bakhsh invokes, "Whoever
  has seen me has seen me truly, and Satan cannot take my form" (p. 189, folio 181); and a female
  Pakistani murid's parallel claim, "Sometimes in dreams, the shaitan can divert you. But they cannot
  take the form of a shaykh" (p. 190, folio 182).
- **A dead shaykh still guides in dreams** — "spontaneous dreams of the spiritual master continue
  even after the shaykh's death," which murids treat as "further proof that a Sufi master's power and
  blessing (baraka) transcend the limits of space and time" (p. 190, folio 182).
- **Dream omens, as Wahid Bakhsh lists them** (his letter, quoted whole): drowning in water "is
  always good. It means purification"; nakedness is good, "It means veils will be lifted"; "Being
  garlanded in a dream is not good. It may mean death"; "Seeing fire is bad"; wild animals are good
  because "the wild animals are the nafs"; "Seeing teeth falling out is bad—it means troubles"; "Being
  bit by a dog means wealth is to come, especially if it is a mad dog"; "Seeing filth or being covered
  with filth means wealth"; seeing oneself dead "means he is to reach the status of annihilation in
  God (fana fi Allah)"; "Praying in a dream means you will perform Hajj" (p. 189, folio 181).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Sufi ritual as Foucauldian technique.** "Borrowing from Foucault, it is useful to describe Sufi
  ritual practices as 'technologies of the self'" (p. 182, folio 174). The author's corollary: "For
  Chishti Sabiris, true knowledge about the nature of the self and its relationship to God is not
  found in abstract philosophizing or the academic parsing of legal and theological debate. Instead,
  spiritual progress demands work" (p. 182, folio 174).
- **The body is the site.** "The locus and focus of Sufi ritual techniques is the physical body …
  understanding comes only from doing" (p. 182, folio 174). Scott Kugle is quoted at length: "By
  advocating that one die to one's own self, mystics loosen the habitual bonds that bind the self to
  the socially constituted body … mystics offer a method of acquiring a whole new bodily habitus"
  (p. 183, folio 175). The author invokes "the famous prophetic hadith, 'die before you die'"
  (p. 183, folio 175).
- **Mujahada is glossed against jihad.** "The word mujahada (spiritual 'striving') comes from the
  same Arabic root as jihad" (p. 182, folio 174); the chapter closes its remembrance section by
  calling daily prayer, zikr and muraqaba "the real proving ground for this spiritual jihad" (p. 194,
  folio 186).
- **Adaptation as ijtihad.** "Embracing a form of ijtihad—independent reasoning—today's Chishti Sabiri
  order has developed a range of practical strategies to negotiate change and accommodate Sufism to
  the ground-level contingencies of everyday life in the twenty-first century" (p. 184, folio 176).
  The disciples' own formula: "Our silsila represents compatibility with modern life" (p. 184, folio
  176).
- **Method statement for the chapter.** "Here I focus on ethnographic contexts over doctrinal texts.
  My analysis is concerned less with theological and polemical debates than with documenting how
  today's Chishti Sabiri disciples actually experience and explain the ritual techniques that cement
  their Sufi identity" (p. 184, folio 176); the chapter "traces both the continuities and changes in
  postcolonial Sufi praxis" (p. 184, folio 176).
- **Sufi dreaming is distinguished from classical ta'bir.** "Whereas ta'bir aims to translate the
  imagery of dreams in order to forecast the future, Sufis cultivate dreams as spiritual tools and
  catalysts for spiritual growth" (p. 184, folio 176). Wahid Bakhsh's own contrast is with psychology:
  "The psychologists know only one kind of dream … But actually there are other kinds," rahmani and
  shaitani (p. 185, folio 177).
- **Ewing's three-part typology is adopted:** "'dreams of initiation,' 'spontaneous dreams,' and
  'induced dreams'" (p. 185, folio 177). Ewing is also cited for longing (shauq) preceding initiatory
  dreams in Pakistan (p. 186, folio 178) and for the dream as "a pivot in terms of which the dreamer
  reorients his life" (p. 187, folio 179).
- **Hoffman is cited and then contradicted.** Hoffman on recognition through "dream-visions" (p. 185,
  folio 177); her Egyptian finding that visions "provide an especially important outlet for women and
  others who would otherwise have little opportunity for direct social interaction with a spiritual
  mentor" is met with "This is demonstrably not the case among Chishti Sabiri practitioners, however"
  (p. 188, folio 180).
- **Dreams eventually taper off, and this is read as purposive.** "disciples note that while
  visionary experiences are prevalent in the early stages of suluk they often decrease in frequency
  and intensity over time," and a senior Pakistani murid explains it as the removal of a distraction
  (p. 191, folio 183).
- **Din and dunya as the central modern test.** "Chishti Sabiris view this struggle to combine din
  and dunya as the most important test of a Sufi's piety and faith" (p. 194, folio 186); murids "are
  hyperaware of the clock" (p. 193, folio 185).
- **Continuity claim.** Murids "view these spiritual resources as the direct legacy of the Prophet
  Muhammad" and hold the path to be "a timeless, universal teaching—as relevant today as it was in
  the seventh century," while also accepting that "it must respond and adapt to social change if it
  is to survive" (p. 183, folio 175).
