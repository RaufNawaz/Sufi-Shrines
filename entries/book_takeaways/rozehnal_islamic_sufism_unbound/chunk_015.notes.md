## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Seating as hierarchy in the mahfil-i sama'.** "This spatial layout places the Sufi master at the
  very center of the ritual action, even as it confines the singers to the periphery" (p. 225, folio
  217). "The most senior murids position themselves nearest the shaykh, while others take seats in
  the front or back of the rows according to their relative status" (p. 225, folio 217).
- **Gendered ritual space.** "Female disciples typically sit together in rows directly behind the
  shaykh, divided off from the central space by a thin, white sheet" (p. 225, folio 217); they are
  "physically present, but sequestered, silent, and invisible" (p. 225, folio 217), which the author
  calls "in sharp contrast to the dynamics of everyday experience where many women play integral
  roles in the Chishti Sabiri order's social and ritual life" (p. 225, folio 217). "Several female
  murids, in fact, are renowned as spiritually advanced mentors with expertise in Sufi ritual
  practice, including sama'" (p. 225, folio 217).
- **Shari'a defence of sama'.** A senior Pakistani disciple: "The women, for example, remain in
  pardah. We are in wudu … neatly dressed, listening to the recitation of hamd and naat" (p. 225,
  folio 217), and "Here there is not a single utterance" (p. 225, folio 217).
- **Adab as the measure of standing.** "To a large extent, an individual's standing in the community
  is measured by her/his knowledge of and adherence to these rules of ritual etiquette" (p. 226,
  folio 218).
- **Zauqi Shah's rules for the assembly** (from *Tarbiyat al-'ushshaq*, p. 226, folio 218): everyone
  performs ablutions and sits cross-legged; no looking around; **payment is routed through the
  shaykh** — "Whatever gifts they give [for the qawwals] should be presented in front of the master
  of the assembly [i.e., the shaykh]. It is against etiquette to give it directly to the qawwals";
  "Talking, using prayer beads [tasbih] or performing other forms of worship is against the
  etiquette of the musical assembly"; those in ecstasy stay seated, and if one stands all stand;
  "those who have reached perfection are exempt from these rules."
- **Ordering of the kalam to the stages of suluk** (Zauqi Shah, pp. 227–228, folios 219–220):
  qawwali "should commence with poems in honor of the Prophet [na't sharif]", then "songs in praise
  of the saints of the [Chishti Sabiri] order [manqabat]", then "words of love", then kalam of
  annihilation [muraqaba-i fana'], then kalam of servitude; the singers must not go back a stage,
  and must repeat a couplet that has sent a salik into wajd until the state passes (p. 228, folio
  220).
- **Languages of the repertoire.** Persian, Punjabi and classical Hindi (p. 229, folio 221); a
  senior Pakistani disciple: "Apart from Farsi, you have kalam in Saraiki, Hindi, Sindhi, and
  Punjabi. Urdu has not been selected by our saints for this purpose. In our mahfil-i sama', the
  qawwals are directed to focus on Farsi" (p. 229, folio 221).
- **The 'urs economy.** "The qawwals represent just a single node in a vast web of economic networks
  and minimarkets that surround the 'urs pilgrimage and its public rituals" (p. 229, folio 221). "An
  accomplished qawwal is valued—and financially rewarded—for his musical and poetic talents" (p.
  229, folio 221), yet "As paid service professionals, however, they are simultaneously (and
  paradoxically) held in low esteem" (p. 229, folio 221).
- **Payment as control.** Quoting Qureshi: "the only way to control the Qawwal's musical power over
  his audience is to give his audience religiously legitimized social and economic power over him"
  (p. 229, folio 221). And from a murid: "They must come to us for the blessings of our shaykhs and
  not for the money. They never get much money from us" (p. 230, folio 222); "The qawwals only
  perform for money. They only want to play the songs that people will pay money to hear" (p. 230,
  folio 222).
- **Policing of performers.** "Loud drumming and theatrical gestures are discouraged … pandering to
  popular tastes by playing 'film qawwali' is strictly prohibited. Singers who violate these rules
  are immediately ushered out of the assembly and not invited to return" (p. 230, folio 222). Senior
  murids "assist the shaykh in controlling the mahfil-i sama', selecting and guiding the qawwals'
  performance" (p. 230, folio 222), so that "Chishti Sabiris effectively maintain a monopoly as the
  gatekeepers of tradition" (p. 231, folio 223).
- **'Urs at a distance, by cassette.** Malaysian disciples unable to travel "gather together in
  private homes in Kuala Lumpur … and, most intriguingly, listen to taped recordings of sama'"
  (p. 231, folio 223); "We play qawwali on the tape cassette. It is fantastic!" (p. 231, folio 223).
- **The annual cycle.** "Four times a year, disciples travel and live together en masse for short
  but intensive periods of spiritual immersion, creating a serial khanaqah during the annual 'urs
  pilgrimages" (p. 233, folio 225); "Many attend weekly halqa-i zikr sessions and regularly visit
  local shrines in groups for muraqaba" (p. 233, folio 225).
- **Interview datelines recorded in this chunk** (endnote-free evidence of the fieldwork's shape):
  (October 7, 2000, Lahore) (p. 225, folio 217); (September 2, 2001, Karachi) and (September 1,
  2001, Karachi) (p. 229, folio 221); (December 15, 2000, Karachi) and (September 30, 2000,
  Pakpattan) (p. 230, folio 222); (October 3, 2001, Kuala Lumpur), (September 1, 2001, Karachi) and

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Death by sama'.** Zauqi Shah's warning, as the book paraphrases it: his "final cautionary note
  invokes the memory of Shaykh Qutb ad-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki (d. 1235), the famous thirteenth-century
  Chishti master who died in a state of prolonged spiritual rapture while listening to sama'"
  (p. 228, folio 220). In Zauqi Shah's own quoted words, if a couplet that has sent a listener into
  wajd is not repeated, "he could die under certain circumstances" (p. 228, folio 220).
- **Nizam ad-Din on sama' as fire**, from *Fawa'id al-Fu'ad*: "the taste which is evoked in them
  through sama' resembles a fire set ablaze. If this were not the case, where would one find
  eternity (baqa')…?" (p. 227, folio 219). Attributed by the book to the shaykh through Hasan Sijzi.
- **Blessing is stronger at the tomb.** A senior female murid reports the shaykh's answer: "In both
  places you receive faizan, blessings come down. But the blessings you get at the mazar itself you
  do not receive at your house. It is much stronger at the mazar because the Shaykh intensifies it"
  (p. 231, folio 223).
- **The saint compensates for a degraded assembly.** Wahid Bakhsh at Pakpattan: "During mob qawwali
  we fixed our attention on the saint himself who made up for all the deficiency" (p. 232, folio
  224).
- **The blind man in the sun** — a parable the disciples attribute to the awliya' Allah, that kalam
  works even when not understood: "The blind man does not see the sun, but he can sense the warmth
  of the sun, just as a man with eyes" (p. 231, folio 223).
- **Deaths read as sacrifice.** The Pakpattan dead are commemorated as an 'urs: "In both cases,
  death has been transformed into martyrdom" (p. 234, folio not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Sama' is gendered against women even where daily life is not.** "Despite the high standing of
  women in Chishti Sabiri daily affairs, however, they remain noticeably marginalized within the
  ritualized confines of the mahfil-i sama'" (p. 225, folio 217).
- **Adab is not decoration but doctrine.** "For Chishti Sabiris, the requirements of adab are as
  essential to Sufi practice as the ritual prescriptions of formal worship ('ibadat)" (p. 226, folio
  218); adab "also forms a fundamental part of the silsila's historical memory, collective wisdom,
  and corporate identity" (p. 227, folio 219).
- **Poetry as pedagogy.** "Sufi poetry provides a blueprint for spiritual development—a 'model of'
  and a 'model for' mystical experience" (p. 227, folio 219); the repertoire "encapsulates suluk
  itself, serving in effect as a lyrical microcosm of the Sufi's arduous spiritual journey to God"
  (p. 228, folio 220).
- **Sama' as spiritual jazz.** "the mahfil-i sama' resembles a form of spiritual jazz—fluid, open,
  and responsive to audience feedback and participation" (p. 229, folio 221).
- **Ritual, not books, makes the Sufi.** "For these twenty-first-century Sufis, spiritual wisdom is
  not found in books. It is possible only through internal combat" (p. 232, folio 224); "Chishti
  Sabiris view Sufism as a spiritual meritocracy" (p. 232, folio 224).
- **The individual self is made in the silsila.** "The individual Sufi self, in other words, is
  ultimately molded within the moral community of the silsila" (p. 233, folio 225).
- **The state's grip on Sufism is not total — the book's central claim.** "I argue, however, that
  the state's control of Sufi tradition—its worldview, its sacred spaces, its spiritual leaders—has
  never been totalizing or hegemonic" (p. 234, folio not stated); Chishti Sabiri identity is
  constituted "beyond the gaze of the nation and outside the machinery of the state" (pp. 234–235,
  folios not stated and 228).
- **The four "alternatives" of the Conclusions** (p. 235, folio 228), as printed as bullets: an
  **alternative geography** — "an expansive Indo-Muslim sacred landscape centered on a constellation
  of Sufi shrines"; an **alternative history** — a spiritual genealogy linking disciples "with the
  Prophet Muhammad"; an **alternative community** "rooted in the silsila's broad teaching network";
  and an **alternative authority** "rooted in an experiential knowledge acquired through the
  discipline of Sufi ritual practice."
- **Method.** "Combing [text-layer?: Combining] the philological methods of Islamic Studies with the
  theory and methodology of Cultural Anthropology, I have examined both texts and contexts" (p. 235,
  folio 228).
- **The shaykhs as nationalists.** "Muhammad Zauqi Shah, Shahidullah Faridi, and Wahid Bakhsh Sial
  Rabbani were also ardent Pakistani nationalists" who "envisioned Pakistan as a tabula rasa"
  (p. 236, folio 229), portraying "Sufi masters as the true heirs to the Prophet and, by extension,
  the true spiritual founders of Pakistan" (p. 236, folio 229); their vision made Partition "much
  more palatable to these Chishti Sabiri shaykhs than it was for many of their 'ulama counterparts
  who resisted Partition" (p. 236, folio 229).
- **Three ritual complexes examined.** "dreams and dream interpretation; rituals of remembrance
  (prayer, zikr, and muraqaba); and the annual pilgrimage networks and musical assemblies at Sufi
  shrines ('urs and sama')" (p. 237, folio 230).
- **Change within continuity.** "this study demonstrates that Chishti Sabiri silsila is both
  paradigmatic and protean" (p. 237, folio 230); "Chishti Sabiris have altered the frequency and
  location of their ritual practices in order to accommodate the Sufi path to contemporary
  ground-level realities" (p. 237, folio 230); yet "there remains a fundamental continuity to
  Chishti Sabiri identity" (p. 237, folio 230).
- **What the endnotes tell us about the book's own positioning.** The Introduction's note 2 calls
  Khaliq Ahmad Nizami's *Tarikh-i mashayikh-i Chisht* "The definitive work on the Chishtiyya"
  (p. 238, folio not stated); note 21 records the author's rejection of Trimingham's "threefold
  theory of Sufism's historical devolution" by referring the reader to "Ernst and Lawrence, Sufi
  Martyrs of Love" (p. 239, folio 232); note 25 states that "The term 'discursive tradition' is
  borrowed from anthropologist Talal Asad" (p. 239, folio 232); note 28 records that the author
  reviewed Pnina Werbner's *Pilgrims of Love* in the *Journal of Asian Studies*, Vol. 63, No. 4
  (November 2004) (p. 240, folio 233); note 30 sends the reader for the Chishti Sabiri order itself
  to Ernst and Lawrence (p. 240, folio 233).
- **The MMA, defined only in an endnote.** Chapter 1's note 6: "The MMA comprises six diverse
  religious parties—the Jama'at-i Islami; two factions of the Deobandi Jamiat 'Ulama-i Islam; the
  Barelwi party, the Jamiat 'Ulama-i Pakistan; a small Wahhabi group, the Jamiat Ahl-i Hadith; and a
  Shi'a group, the Islami Tehrik Pakistan" (p. 240, folio 233). This list appears nowhere in the
  body text of this chunk.

## Doubts

- **`golra-sharif` and `mithankot-kot-mithan` each rest on two words in one clause.** The whole
  evidence is "the Punjabi poetry of Mehr 'Ali Shah and Ghulam Farid" (p. 229, folio 221). Neither
  name carries a shrine, a town, a date or a silsila anywhere in this chunk. The ids are assigned
  under the 18 Sep 2026 author-only/literary-reference ruling; **the consolidator should weigh both
  as name-only hits.** The book's spelling is "Mehr 'Ali Shah" against the archive's "Pir Sayyid
  Meher Ali Shah" (`golra-sharif`); nothing in the chunk confirms they are the same man.
- **Which Farid, in the Ghulam Farid clause.** The archive carries two Farid rows:
  `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` (Baba Fariduddin Mas'ud Ganjshakar, Pakpattan) and
  `mithankot-kot-mithan` (Khwaja Ghulam Farid). The clause's "Ghulam Farid" is the second, on the
  word "Ghulam"; the chunk's many "Baba Farid" mentions are the first, on "Baba". They are kept
  apart deliberately, and the fact that both appear in this chunk is exactly the collision a keyword
  pass would get wrong. Recorded so the check is not redone.
- **"Shahidullah Faridi" is not a Farid row.** Already refused for this book; recorded again because
  his name sits three lines from "Pakpattan" on (p. 225, folio 217) and again on (p. 230, folio 222).
- **Interim convention (a): bare toponyms in this chunk, and the rows they would take on reversal.**
