## Practices, institutions, economy

- **'Urs pilgrimage, unnamed shrines.** The author's fieldwork included "numerous pilgrimages in the
  company of murids to Sufi shrine complexes in Pakistan for the annual death anniversaries ('urs)
  of celebrated Chishti saints" (p. 141, folio 133). **Which shrines, and which saints, are not
  stated** — the single most consequential omission in this chunk for the archive.
- **Weekly and regular gatherings.** Fieldwork included "several visits to the weekly gatherings
  with Shaykh Siraj 'Ali in Karachi" and "frequent attendance at the zikr circles in Lahore"
  (p. 141, folio 133). The Malaysian murids "continue their own zikr circles in Kuala Lumpur, and
  many of them travel frequently to Pakistan and India for pilgrimage" (p. 142, folio 134).
- **The order keeps no register and no public count.** "The silisla [text-layer?: silsila] does not
  maintain public murid registers, and, as disciples note, Chishti Sabiri shaykhs as a rule never
  openly talk about their followers" (p. 140, folio 132). "In the absence of verifiable data,
  estimates of the total numbers of contemporary disciples vary" (p. 140, folio 132).
- **Numbers, such as the book gives them.** "Anecdotal evidence and my own informal calculations
  suggest that Chishti Sabiri murids certainly number in the thousands" (p. 140, folio 132); "an
  elite inner circle of **several hundred** disciples" sits "at the very center of the order's life"
  (p. 141, folio 133); and there is "a core group of **nearly one hundred** Malaysian disciples"
  (p. 142, folio 134). The author immediately warns the counts are the wrong measure: "it is
  extremely misleading to measure the order's impact by counting heads" (p. 140, folio 132).
- **Composition of the membership.** Mostly Pakistani, with "a significant and growing contingent of
  foreigners as well: Malaysians, South Africans, and Europeans" (p. 140, folio 132); ethnically
  "primarily . . . ethnic Punjabis and Sindhis, along with a smaller representation of Pathans from
  the Northwest Frontier Province", with **Urdu** as "the standard medium of exchange among
  Pakistani disciples" (p. 142, folio 134). The inner core are "mostly educated, middle-class, urban
  professionals" and "predominantly Sunni", with "several female Shi'a murids" excepted (p. 141,
  folio 133). Occupations listed: "educators, engineers, publishers, bankers, computer programmers,
  medical professionals, technocrats, and businessmen" (p. 142, folio 134). Malaysian murids are
  "mostly well-educated, middle-class, urban professionals", "majority . . . ethnic Malay Muslims"
  with "a significant contingent of Chinese and Indian converts", speaking English with their
  shaykhs and Pakistani peers (p. 143, folio 135).
- **Women in the order.** "The silsila is distinguished from many other Sufi orders by the numbers
  and prominence of its female disciples. Women are full participants in all the order's ritual
  activities, and select senior female disciples are renowned for their advanced spiritual status
  and authority" (p. 140, folio 132); "Chishti Sabiri shaykhs are fully accessible to women, and
  treat their female disciples as the spiritual equals of their male counterparts" (p. 140, folio
  132).
- **How the Malaysian branch began.** "these Malaysian murids all attribute their introduction to
  the silsila to a single individual: a medical doctor from the northern state of Kalantan who
  joined the Chishti Sabiri order in **1979** while studying in Karachi. Following his lead, several
  other Malaysian students who were at the time studying in Pakistan also joined the silsila"
  (p. 142, folio 134). With one exception — "a novice female murid of Shaykh Siraj 'Ali" — the
  Malaysians "are all the disciples of Shaykh Wahid Bakhsh Sial Rabbani" (p. 142, folio 134).
- **Madrasas, as the disciples describe them.** Disciples "view the madrasa as an important symbol
  of Muslim identity" and as "repositories of religious learning and social welfare centers", but
  "do not typically attend madrasas themselves" (p. 145, folio 137). A disciple's economic account:
  "Madrasas are popular because the have-nots of the community send their children there for food,
  shelter, and education. There is a steady stream of students coming in. They cannot do anything
  else. Either they work as tea stall boys or auto shop boys" (pp. 145–146, folios 137–138).
  The counter-practice: "Chishti Sabiri shaykhs typically encourage their **non-Pakistani** disciples
  to study for a short period in a madrasa" (p. 146, folio 138).
- **Education of murids' children.** "disciples typically encourage their own sons and daughters to
  pursue a secular education while maintaining their religious devotions", several in "Western
  universities", taking "advanced degrees in engineering, computer science, accounting, and finance,
  both at home and abroad" (p. 145, folio 137).
- **Adab as institution.** "the relations between the Chishti Sabiri shaykh and individual murids
  are mediated by an elaborate matrix of rules of conduct that fall under the rubric of adab"
  (p. 139, folio 131); adab "impacts their physical comportment, the manner of their dress and
  speech, and in the way they interact with others" (p. 146, folio 138). Transmission is domestic:
  "Parents introduce their children to the rules of decorum from an early age" (p. 151, folio 143),
  and a murid reports his sons "kiss Hazrat Siraj Sahab's hand. I didn't teach them that, but they
  know it" (p. 151, folio 143).
- **Kinship and inheritance of affiliation.** Relationships "are frequently passed down from
  generation to generation", and "extended families are connected through dynamic interpersonal
  networks, often cemented by marriage" (p. 138, folio 130). One named-by-role case: "a young
  disciple, a French convert who married the granddaughter of Shaykh Wahid Bakhsh and who now lives
  with his family in Karachi" (p. 144, folio 136).
- **The shajara as the order's central document.** "the most important thing a disciple possesses is
  the spiritual genealogy (shajara). If there is a manual to Sufism then it is the order's shajara.
  It is a tree, that's the literal translation, a genealogical table" (pp. 150–151, folios 142–143).
- **Publication as outreach.** "the Malaysian's [text-layer?: Malaysians'] publication efforts have
  proved instrumental in spreading the Chishti Sabiri message across the Indian Ocean and into
  cyberspace" (p. 143, folio 135), a back-reference to chapter 3.

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Dreams as a recruitment and confirmation channel, reported as the disciples' own understanding.**
  "In Chishti Sabiri practice, this relationship with past masters is also confirmed through dreams
  and visionary experiences" (p. 138, folio 130); and of newcomers, "others were introduced to the
  silsila through interpersonal networks, word of mouth, and even dreams" (p. 141, folio 133),
  repeated at the chunk's end: "others were led to a spiritual master through dreams. As chapter 5
  will demonstrate" (p. 151, folio 143).
- **An explicit warning AGAINST karamat as a test of a master**, quoted by the author from Ashraf
  'Ali Thanawi: "Do not look for other kinds of power—for example, the power to say something and
  make it happen; or to remove sickness by breathing on someone; or to make a wish come true by
  preparing an amulet; or to make someone feel agitated through direction of the pir's attention
  (tawajjuh) toward that person. Never be deceived by these powers" (p. 149, folio 141). The only
  power Thanawi allows is "spiritual power (tasir)" measured by the state of the disciples: "If
  among ten disciples five or six are good, you can judge him to be a master with spiritual power"
  (p. 149, folio 141).
- **Annihilation, given as doctrine rather than miracle.** "the Sufi disciple's ego is first
  dissolved in the shaykh (fana' fi al-shaykh), before becoming annihilated in the Prophet (fana' fi
  al-rasul) and, eventually, God (fana' fi Allah)" (p. 150, folio 142), illustrated by Zauqi Shah:
  "If a glass of water is poured into the ocean where does it go? This water is absorbed (fana')
  into the ocean" (p. 150, folio 142).
- **Contact with dead shaykhs through muraqaba**, in a novice's words: "You are supposed to be in
  touch with each of those shaykhs through spiritual contact. You seek guidance from each of them
  through muraqaba [contemplation]" (p. 151, folio 143).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The chapter's stated turn and method.** "This chapter turns from texts to ethnographic contexts
  to explore how knowledge is transmitted within the contemporary Chishti Sabiri order. By quoting
  liberally from personal interviews, I aim to allow murids to articulate their own experiences and
  understanding of the Sufi path" (p. 137, folio not stated). "this inquiry is less concerned with
  the ideals of Sufi doctrine than with the realities of everyday, ordinary Sufi practice" (p. 140,
  folio 132).
- **The three-dimensional framework of the chapter**, set out as a numbered list: "1. The silsila as
  a spiritual pedigree", "2. The silsila as a teaching network", "3. The silsila as a social nexus"
  (p. 138, folio 130).
- **The order as a counter-institution.** "these interlocking networks constitute the Chishti Sabiri
  order as an alternative locus of Islamic piety: a Sufi spiritual brotherhood cemented in shared
  values, ethics, ritual practices, and historical perspective" (p. 138, folio 130); and "the
  contemporary Chishti Sabiri order is a distinct corporate institution—a moral community, united by
  the rules of adab and the discipline of ritual practice" (p. 146, folio 138).
- **Modernity thesis.** "The abundance of technological metaphors reflects the worldview of
  contemporary Chishti Sabiri devotees who are fully enmeshed in the complexities and contradictions
  of the mundane world of twenty-first-century life. At the same time, they maintain a discursive
  tradition and ritual discipline that both links them to a sacralized Islamic past and sacralizes
  the living present" (p. 137, folio not stated). "the core group of Chishti Sabiri disciples is
  well acquainted with the instruments and ideology of modernity" (p. 141, folio 133).
- **Against the standard stereotype of Sufi constituencies — directly relevant to shrine
  scholarship.** "the critics of Sufism often dismiss the tradition as a backwater for marginalized,
  rural, and uneducated Muslims in an attempt to denigrate Sufi allegiances and practices. A closer
  look at today's Chishti Sabiri silsila, however, presents a different picture altogether"
  (p. 143, folio 135).
- **Transnationalism as a break, not a continuity.** "the order's spread into Southeast Asia signals
  an important shift in its makeup and modus operandi. While the Chishti Sabiri silsila has always
  adapted to change, its transplantation into entirely new geographical and cultural contexts marks
  a radical break with historical precedent" (p. 143, folio 135).
- **The author names an elitism in his subjects, and does not excuse it.** "There is, as this quote
  suggests, an underlying elitism to Chishti Sabiri views on education and the pursuit of knowledge.
  The madrasa is viewed as an appropriate outlet for training in the traditional Islamic sciences,
  particularly for the socially marginalized who lack other career options. For those with
  privileged access to the teachings of a Sufi shaykh, however, madrasa training is seen as
  superfluous" (p. 146, folio 138).
- **Continuity with the premodern, argued through adab.** "This constant emphasis on both inner
  moral perfection and outward social decorum is yet another theme that links the Chishti Sabiri
  silsila with its premodern antecedents" (p. 139, folio 131). One of the chapter's guiding
  questions is "In what ways do these networks of knowledge and practice confirm or contradict
  premodern traditions?" (p. 140, folio 132).
- **A conscious limit on the ethnography.** "the interactions between Shaykh Siraj 'Ali and his
  disciples take place mostly in private sessions. As an outsider, I was not often privy to these
  intimate, face-to-face exchanges" (p. 140, folio 132) — the author's own statement of what his
  access did not reach.
- **Sectarian identity subordinated to Sufi allegiance.** "Chishti Sabiris insist that Sufi
  allegiances ultimately trump any divisions between Shi'a and Sunni" (p. 141, folio 133), with the
  author adding "among Chishti Sabiri murids there is a deep and abiding respect for the Prophet's
  family and a vehement rejection of narrow, exclusive sectarian identities" (p. 141, folio 133).
- **Din/dunya as the marker of the order's self-definition.** "For today's Chishti Sabiris, it is
  precisely the imperative for a direct engagement with ordinary, everyday life that distinguishes
  Islam from other faiths and Sufism from other practices" (p. 144, folio 136); "Chishti Sabiris
  view the balancing of din and dunya as a central marker of their Sufi identity" (p. 144, folio
  136).
- **Forward pointer, not a fact.** "This survey of the mechanics of master-disciple relations serves
  as a springboard for a detailed ethnography of contemporary Chishti Sabiri ritual practices in
  chapter 5" (p. 140, folio 132) — the ritual and pilgrimage material, where named shrines are most
  likely to appear, is **not in this chunk**.
