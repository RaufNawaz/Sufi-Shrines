## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Bay'at defined:** "the ritual ceremony that marks formal initiation into the order," by which "the Sufi
  aspirant is transformed into a murid—a full participant in the silsila's teaching network and corporate
  identity" (p. 153, folio 145). "The ceremony itself is simple yet symbolic. With prayers and invocations,
  the novice acknowledges the absolute authority of the shaykh and pledges obedience and submission to him"
  (p. 153, folio 145).
- **Pre-Islamic precedent:** "In pre-Islamic Arab society, bay'at was the traditional way of pledging
  allegiance to a tribal chief" (p. 153, folio 145).
- **Initiation into four silsilas at once,** in a senior disciple's words: "The masters of our order give
  initiation in four silsilas … 'I take bay'at at the hands of my shaykh in the Chishtiyya Sabiriyya and
  Nizamiyya, Suhrawardiyya, Qadiriyya, Naqshbandiyya.' These are the primary orders … The teachings [and
  practices] are that of the Sabiriyaa silsila, but we receive the blessings [barakat] of all four silsilas"
  (p. 154, folio 146). Printed `Sabiriyaa` — reproduced verbatim; see Doubts.
- **Scale of the order in one city, as an informant states it:** "Just here in Karachi there may be three or
  four hundred branches" of the Chishti silsila; "There are many Chishti Sabiris too—some branches have been
  merging. We do not maintain contact with them at all" (p. 154, folio 146). This is the only quantity given
  anywhere in the chunk.
- **Exclusivity rule after initiation:** "Once they have taken bay'at, disciples are strongly discouraged
  from seeking spiritual advice outside the insulated boundaries of the silsila" (p. 154, folio 146); before
  initiation, "it is alright to visit other silsilas though, and other shaykhs" (p. 154, folio 146).
- **Faiz is silsila-bound:** "You can only receive spiritual blessing ( faiz) from your own silsila. Other
  shaykhs don't even want to meet you" (p. 155, folio 147; the space inside the parenthesis is as printed).
- **Initiation by post:** Wahid Bakhsh's Malaysian disciples "took bay'at by letter, pledging their oath of
  allegiance with a signature long before they had even met the shaykh in person (interview: October 3,
  2001, Kuala Lumpur)" (p. 156, folio 148).
- **Fixed occasion for initiation:** "There is a specific time and date to become a murid" (p. 156, folio 148).
- **Current Karachi teaching schedule (the chunk's most concrete institutional fact):** "In Karachi, the
  order's current teaching shaykh, Siraj 'Ali Muhammad, leads weekly halqa (communal zikr sessions) on
  Thursday evenings. The shaykh also hosts an informal public question-and-answer lecture session (dars) at
  his home on Sunday mornings. Disciples who live in other parts of Pakistan—and beyond South Asia
  altogether—are rarely able to attend these gatherings" (p. 165, folio 157).
- **Madrasa training for foreign disciples:** the shaykh directs the French convert, "You ought to go to
  Karachi, to the Islamic Center. You should learn Qur'an and Arabic at the madrasa … I was there for three
  years"; "Most of the foreign disciples from the time of Hazrat Shahidullah Faridi … used to go there"
  (p. 163, folio 155).
- **Radio as a devotional medium, refused:** a disciple invites Zauqi Shah "to listen to a Ramadan Qur'an
  recitation broadcast on the radio from Egypt"; the shaykh declines (p. 161, folio 153).
- **Adab as the order's regulating institution:** "Chishti Sabiri pedagogy—both past and present—rests on
  the cardinal values of etiquette and propriety … adab molds character, conditions behavior, and shapes
  social interaction" (p. 162, folio 154).
- **Ritual washing at initiation:** "He said, 'Ok, make wudu' [the ritual washing for prayer].' I became his
  murid right then and there." (p. 157, folio 149).
- **No economic material of any kind in this chunk** — no offerings, no langar, no endowment, no revenue, no
  Auqaf, no custodianship or sajjada nashin dispute, no visitor numbers, no building dates. Stated as an
  explicit negative so the consolidator does not look for it here.

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **A miracle refused, reported by the disciple who asked for it:** "I asked him [Wahid Bakhsh] to show me a
  miracle because I'd heard about fake pirs … And he told me, 'We are not here for that. This will keep you
  from coming near to Allah.'" (p. 152, folio 144). The author calls the request's "audacity … nonetheless
  remarkable" (p. 152, folio 144).
- **Pakpattan grants every request** — reported as what the disciple was told before going: "I was told that
  in Pakpattan nobody's requests are rejected" (p. 157, folio 149).
- **Collapse and illumination at the shrine of Baba Farid, in the disciple's own words:** "Suddenly, I
  received some illuminations [kashfiyyat]. I fell down … I sensed that I was unconscious, but I could still
  hear people talking around me. They were saying, 'He has had a heart attack!'" (p. 157, folio 149).
- **Revival by the shaykh's breath, same account:** "Captain Sahab came and blew his breath on me. While I
  was unconscious I saw something. I experienced something very beautiful." The shaykh then confirms it:
  "Yes. This is what happened. You do not have a shaykh, but you have a great deal of concentration. You are
  open to these things, but you don't have a filter. A shaykh is a filter." (p. 157, folio 149). The author
  frames this as "Shaykh Wahid Bakhsh appears to restore order, bringing the man back to normal consciousness
  and confirming the verity of his spiritual experience" (p. 158, folio 150).
- **A dream demanded as a test, and not supplied:** "'It's too much for you. But tomorrow, if you see
  something in a dream, then come to me.' … Captain Sahab asked, 'Did you see anything?' I said, 'No, sir.
  But please, I want to become your murid!'" — and he is initiated anyway (p. 157, folio 149).
- **White light at first meeting**, reported by a female Pakistani disciple of Wahid Bakhsh: "while he was
  speaking to me, it seemed like he was looking inside me and I was filled with this white light" (p. 156,
  folio 148); afterwards "I felt as if I was in a trance. I was transformed … I felt that I was in the
  presence of God" (p. 156, folio 148).
- **Zauqi Shah's teaching story of the sleeping murid** — a saint lies down and "focused his spiritual
  attention on the Prophet of God"; the imitating disciple falls asleep and a second Sufi master "kicked the
  sleeping murid, saying, 'Wake up and go pray your namaz without ablutions [wudu] too!'" (p. 164, folio 156).
  Attributed by the author to Zauqi Shah's discourse "The Difference between Obedience and Imitation"
  (p. 164, folio 156).
- **Tawajjuh as transference of spiritual substance**, quoted from Shahidullah Faridi: "The qualified Shaykh
  possesses as a gift of God the power of transference of spiritual qualities; a kind of outpouring (faiz)
  from the always full vessel of his heart … compared to the mother giving milk to the child" (p. 160,
  folio 152).
- **Preordained discipleship** — the author reports it as the disciples' belief, not his own: "Chishti Sabiri
  disciples believe that the relationship with the shaykh is preordained" (p. 152, folio 144); in the murid's
  words, "In the end, you meet the person who is destined for you" (p. 152, folio 144).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The quest for a shaykh is a hagiographic trope that living disciples reproduce:** "Journeying for
  spiritual guidance is a common trope in Sufi hagiography. In narrating their own stories, many Chishti
  Sabiris describe the challenges and difficulties of finding a suitable spiritual mentor" (p. 152, folio 144).
- **Devotion is bounded, not blind** — the author's summary of his informants' position against a standing
  criticism of Sufism: "it is precisely this kind of unwavering devotion to Sufi masters that has led some
  critics to question Sufism. In the past, Sufi masters have been feared as much as respected" (p. 153, folio
  145); but "Chishti Sabiri disciples insist that love for the shaykh is not blind," each shaykh's status
  depending on "personal charisma … piety … righteousness of his actions, and the strength of the genealogy
  that links the master to the authority of the Prophet Muhammad" (p. 153, folio 145).
- **Contemporary exclusivity is a break with the order's own tradition, explained as a response to
  modernity.** The author poses it as a question — "Why this marked change in attitude and practice? Why
  this break with the silsila's own traditions?" — and answers from his informants: exclusivity is "a
  necessary response to the corruption and dangers of the modern age," guarding against "ideologues and
  unqualified religious leaders" and "spiritual dilettantism" (p. 154, folio 146).
- **Multiple initiation was the historical norm:** "It was in fact common practice for premodern Chishti
  Sabiri masters … to accept initiation into multiple Sufi orders," but "For the majority of today's
  disciples, however, these connections with multiple tariqas are largely symbolic" (p. 154, folio 146).
- **The computer metaphor as evidence of class and education** — the author's reading of a disciple's simile
  ("the awliya' Allah are like data that is available on a computer … It is like the Internet"): "This
  technological metaphor is, once again, an important indication of the educational background and worldview
  of many twenty-first-century Chishti Sabiri disciples. Here spiritual power is likened to computer
  software—it is transferable, universally communicable, and widely accessible" (p. 155, folio 147).
- **Pluralism and boundary-keeping held together:** "even as murids acknowledge the reality and viability of
  other spiritual paths, they are equally resolute in maintaining boundaries" (p. 155, folio 147); many
  elder disciples "have a thorough knowledge of the history of Sufism well beyond the cultural landscape of
  South Asia" (p. 155, folio 147).
- **One shrine episode explains a transnational order.** "To a large extent, the Chishti Sabiri order's
  growing foothold in Southeast Asia can be traced to this singular ecstatic experience at a rural Sufi
  shrine in Pakistan almost three decades ago" (p. 158, folio 150) — a causal claim tying Malaysian
  membership to the 'urs at Pakpattan.
- **Vertical, asymmetrical pedagogy:** "the shaykh's authority is absolute and his orders are not to be
  questioned" (p. 158, folio 150); "the flow of knowledge and power is top-down and unidirectional: the
  shaykh leads and the murid follows" (p. 158, folio 150); "the Sufi subject remains under the constant gaze
  of hierarchical authority" (p. 159, folio 151); "The Sufi system of spiritual teaching is based on a
  benign authoritarianism" (p. 162, folio 154).
- **Sufi learning is oral, aural and mimetic:** "Knowledge is transmitted directly from master to student by
  word of mouth" (p. 159, folio 151); "the shaykh also teaches through his actions … there is, in fact, a
  fundamental understanding of the limits of language" (p. 159, folio 151).
- **Borrowed frames, named and qualified.** Arthur Buehler is quoted at length on the shaykh instructing
  "through example and personal conduct," with "father-son, physician-patient, and beloved-lover roles"
  (p. 159, folio 151); the author then qualifies: "While Buehler's research focuses on the Indian
  Naqshbandi-Mujaddidi silsila, his comments also provide an apt summation of contemporary Chishti Sabiri
  pedagogy" (p. 159, folio 151). Frances Trix supplies "attunement," "an increasing coordination of the
  heart of the talib [student] and murshid [guide]" (p. 159, folio 151). Katherine Ewing supplies the
  contrast with Western psychology: "Psychoanalytic theory has embedded within it an ideology of
  independence as a sign of maturity. Sufi theory and Islam, in contrast, rest on an ideology of maturity
  manifested in submission and relationship" (pp. 162–163, folios 154–155).
- **Instruction is individualised, not standardised:** "The Sufi master shapes his words and adapts the
  training regimen to fit the specific needs and capacities of each individual disciple" (p. 159, folio 151);
  "Every murid is not a salik … Only a select few do suluk" (p. 160, folio 152).
- **A spiritual meritocracy** — the author's own term: "Chishti Sabiris view this pedagogical system as a
  spiritual meritocracy. They acknowledge that not every murid is capable of the disciple and sacrifices
  required of suluk" (p. 160, folio 152; `disciple` printed where `discipline` is expected — see Doubts).
- **The shaykh's plural office:** "he is a teacher, mentor, translator, healer, advisor, and disciplinarian"
  (p. 161, folio 153).
- **The chunk's closing tension, posed by the author as a question he put to informants:** "there is an
  inherent tension between these dual imperatives of acquiescence and action … each murid must learn to
  navigate between absolute submission to hierarchical authority and an enduring imperative for individual
  action and moral responsibility. But how? I often asked disciples this very question" (p. 165, folio 157).
- **Solitary practice is the emerging norm:** "This emphasis on personal responsibility and self-reliance is
  doubly important in an environment where Chishti Sabiri ritual practices are increasingly pursued in
  isolation … direct contact with the shaykh … in everyday practice it is a rare occasion" (p. 165, folio 157).
- **Method note visible in the chunk.** The evidence is dated, located interviews, twenty-two of them
  identified by date and city between **December 15, 2000** (p. 154, folio 146) and **October 6, 2001**
  (p. 157, folio 149), across Karachi, Lahore, Islamabad and Kuala Lumpur; informants are anonymised and
  labelled by seniority, gender and nationality ("a prominent female murid," "a senior Malaysian disciple,"
  "an advanced male murid"). Useful for weighing the book's reliability (pp. 152–165, folios 144–157).

  here**; 1993 + 2 is arithmetic on an informant's recollection, not a printed fact, and must not enter an
  entry from this chunk. Likewise "almost three decades ago" (p. 158, folio 150) is the author's approximation
  of the 1979 episode, not a date.
