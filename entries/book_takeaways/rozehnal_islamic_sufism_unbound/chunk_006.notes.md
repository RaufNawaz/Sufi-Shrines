## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Funeral and death anniversary as mass public events.** "Thousands of people attended Hazrat
  Sahab's funeral" — quoted from **"the order's English language journal The Sufi Path"**, of the
  funeral "on April 21, 1995" (p. 91, folio 81). The author "personally attended Wahid Bakhsh's
  first death anniversary in Allahabad in 1996 and again during the course of my fieldwork research
  in February 2001. On both occasions, these public events were well attended, drawing hundreds of
  murids from Pakistan and abroad, along with large numbers of local villagers and curious
  onlookers." (p. 91, folio 81)
- **Composition of the following.** "The shaykh's inner circle of disciples was composed largely of
  middle-class, educated Pakistani urbanites. Yet he also had a large following among the rural
  communities surrounding his hometown of Allahabad." (p. 91, folio 81) "he attracted both male and
  female murids from the United States, Europe, and, in particular, Malaysia" (p. 92, folio 82).
  "Wahid Bakhsh had a disproportionately large number of female disciples" (p. 94, folio 84).
- **The library at the tomb complex.** "Among volumes of classical tafsir (Qur'an interpretation),
  fiqh (jurisprudence), and Sufi poetry in Arabic, Persian, Urdu, Saraiki, and Punjabi are thousands
  of letters from Wahid Bakhsh' [text-layer?: Bakhsh's] murids. They are stacked in piles and stored
  in large bags." (p. 92, folio 82)
- **Letter-writing as the order's guidance institution.** "much of his interaction with disciples
  came in the form of letter writing. A large portion of his daily routine, in fact, was occupied
  with this correspondence" (p. 92, folio 82); letters "address an immense range of material… dream
  interpretation, prescriptions for spiritual practices, and practical advice on personal and family
  matters (marriage, divorce, finances, children's education, etc.)" (p. 92, folio 82); a Malaysian
  murid: "every time we sent a letter to him, we knew that a reply would come within three weeks"
  (p. 93, folio 83, interview Kuala Lumpur, September 30, 2001). Transmission is "both through
  suhbat (direct, interpersonal exchanges) and the pen" (p. 93, folio 83).
- **Zikr open to non-Muslims.** "He would allow everyone, even foreigners, a person of any religion,
  to sit in his zikrs." (p. 92, folio 82, interview Bahawalpur, April 28, 2001)
- **Sama'.** "he was especially known as a passionate defender of the Chishti tradition of sama'.
  Yet like his mentor Zauqi Shah the shaykh was careful to insist on the absolute necessity of
  maintaining sobriety and ritual propriety." (p. 94, folio 84; the book defers the treatment to
  chapter 5)
- **Marriage as institution.** "he too viewed the institution of marriage as a central pillars
  [printed so] of Sufi spiritual practice" (p. 93, folio 83). Household economy appears in the
  deathbed scene: "You have four sons to take care of you, and I have a pension that you can
  receive." (p. 94, folio 84)
- **Print economy of Sufism.** "The widespread distribution of inexpensive books to middle-class
  consumers opened Sufi knowledge to a vast audience—well beyond the privileged few with access to
  manuscripts" (p. 102, folio 93); Urdu translations of premodern masters "are readily found on the
  shelves of local libraries, bookstores, and in the stalls of Urdu bazaars" (p. 102, folio 93);
  Ernst's phrase "a mass marketing of Sufism on an unprecedented scale" (p. 102, folio 93). Muhsin
  Mahdi is cited for the claim that "aside from state governments Sufis were in fact the main
  patrons of printing in Muslim countries during the nineteenth century" (p. 101, folio 92).
- **Nineteenth-century South Asian Muslim print, as quoted from Francis Robinson:** "By the 1820's,
  Muslim reformist leaders in the Indian subcontinent were busily printing tracts. By the 1830s the
  first Muslim newspapers were being printed. By the 1870s, editions of the Qur'an, and other
  religious books, were selling in tens of thousands. In the last thirty years of the century, over
  seven hundred newspapers and magazines in Urdu were started." (pp. 100–101, folios 91–92) Print
  came to South Asian Muslims "not until the nineteenth century, 400 years after Gutenberg's
  invention of moveable type" (p. 100, folio 91).
- **Named Sufi periodicals and presses in the chunk:** *The Sufi Path* (the order's English-language
  journal, p. 91, folio 81); *Risala-yi anwar al-sufiyya* [Magazine of Sufi Illuminations], monthly,
  which "played a vital role in the propagation and popularity of the teachings of the Naqshbandi
  master, Jama'at 'Ali Shah" (p. 102, folio 93); *al-Haq* and *al-Wakil* (p. 105, folio 96);
  *Anwar al-Quds* [The Lights of Holiness], "published in Bombay from October 1925 to February 1927"
  (p. 106, folio 97); *The Islamic Review*, London, 1933 (p. 106, folio 97).
- **Zauqi Shah's books:** *Sirr-i dilbaran* [The Heart-Ravishing Secret], "a comprehensive
  encyclopedia of Sufi terminology… Begun in Peshawar in 1923, this lexicon, written in Urdu, was
  compiled over a number of years. The text was first published by the silsila in 1951 and is now in
  its fifth edition (1985)." (p. 106, folio 97) *Bada u Saghir* [The Wine and the Cup], "this unusual
  Urdu spiritual novel", set in colonial Bombay (p. 106, folio 97).
- **The contemporary publication campaign.** "I conclude with an analysis of the current Chishti
  Sabiri publication campaign in both Pakistan and Malaysia. Through the translation and publication
  of important Sufi texts—in particular the writings of its own shaykhs—the contemporary silsila uses
  texts to defend its history and practices from critics." Reach is extended "through the creative
  use of emergent technologies such as the Internet" (p. 99, folio 90).
- **Organisation founded:** the **Anjuman-i Khuddam as-Sufiyya**, founded by Jama'at 'Ali Shah to
  "promote a mediatory brand of Sufism and to meet the attacks of Deobandis, Ahmadis, Ahl-i Hadith
  and Arya Samajis" (p. 97, folio 87).
- **The shrine at Ahmedabad as a source of a boon:** "This shrine confers the blessings of poetry."
  (p. 106, folio 97, quoted from *Tarbiyat al-'ushshaq*)

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Maqsuda Begam's dreams and visions**, attributed by the author to "numerous murids" in interview:
  "spiritual encounters with the Prophet Muhammad, Khizr, Jesus, Baba Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakkar,
  Muhammad Ali Jinnah, Shah Sayyid Waris Hasan, and Zauqi Shah, among others" (p. 93, folio 83).
- **Her role as intermediary**, in a senior male disciple's words: "The Holy Prophet, God bless him
  and grant him salvation, and all the saints of the silsila were very kind to her, and they would
  give her indications concerning the work of her husband. They would come to her in dreams or in
  visions, or even in person." (p. 93, folio 83, interview Bahawalpur, April 30, 2001)
- **The dream of Khwaja Gharib Nawaz and the jewelry**, told by a senior male murid: she sold her
  ornaments for her husband's "good purpose", missed them, and "At night she saw a dream of Khwaja
  Gharib Nawaz [the saint, Mu'in ad-Din Chishti], may God have mercy upon him, riding on a horse. He
  was in the form of a very handsome prince, and wearing a lot of jewelry… He questioned her, 'Do you
  want me or this jewelry?' She said, 'I want you.'" (p. 95, folio 85, interview Bahawalpur, April 29,
  2001) The author reads it as pedagogy, not report: "Here again a private dream enters into the
  silsila's shared hagiographical narrative, serving as a vital pedagogical device." (p. 95, folio 85)
- **Wahid Bakhsh's refusal of miracles**, in his own words as the book quotes him: "Miracles relate
  to a much lower level and are sometimes possible at the hands of the novices and ordinary people
  having a strong dose of spirituality by birth… But the awliya' Allah [Friends of God] do not like
  to indulge in miracles because miracles cause distraction and block the process of progress."
  (p. 94, folio 84) Yet "many of Wahid Bakhsh's disciples recall tangible demonstrations of his
  powers of insight and knowledge" (p. 94, folio 84); a novice murid reports the shaykh saying "Our
  Prophet Hazrat Muhammad, God bless and grant him salvation, never performed miracles, so why
  should I?" (pp. 94–95, folios 84–85, interview Lahore, November 5, 2000).
- **The gift of the pen**, told by a female disciple and family member: "when he was very young he
  went for pilgrimage to Mecca (umra). He was coming out of the Prophet's mosque when somebody came
  up to him and gave him a pen. From that day he started writing." (p. 96, folio 86, interview
  Lahore, June 17, 2001) See Doubts on Mecca/the Prophet's mosque.
- **The all-night vigil**, reported by a family member and senior male disciple: after a broken-down
  car and a journey in "the month of June, very hot", the disciple slept while "I opened my eyes and
  saw him in meditation [muraqaba]. It was only when morning prayers were almost over that he nudged
  me" (p. 91, folio 81, interview Lahore, August 26, 2001).
- **Zauqi Shah's three miracles at Ahmedabad, 1919**, and the composition that followed: "He was
  overpowered with such enthusiasm that he continued writing [the novel] while seated on a bullock
  cart. When he ran out of paper, he began writing on scraps of newspaper." (p. 106, folio 97,
  quoted from *Tarbiyat al-'ushshaq*)

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Sainthood is made, not found.** "Holy persons are born, live, and die, but they are made into
  saints. The process of emplotting the lives of pious individuals in sacred biographies involves a
  complex and creative act of interpretation. Sainthood is negotiated in dynamic public discourses
  and then memorialized in both oral narratives and hagiographical texts." (p. 96, folio 86) In
  contemporary Pakistan the wrangling over doctrine "has effectively transformed the Sufi saint into
  'a kind of nodal point where these political and personal processes come together.'" (p. 96,
  folio 86)
- **The trio conform to the paradigm yet are marked by modernity.** They "conform to the
  hagiographical habitus of Indo-Muslim sainthood" and are remembered for the same virtues as "their
  premodern Chishti predecessors"; "At the same time, the shaykhs' lives and legacies are indelibly
  marked by the ideologies and institutions of modernity." (p. 96, folio 86) "Unlike their
  madrasa-trained 'ulama counterparts, these shaykhs were educated in Western-style universities.
  They were multilingual, urban, and cosmopolitan" (pp. 96–97, folios 86–87), and "Their collective
  legacy established a new style of Sufi leadership that now shapes the Chishti Sabiri order in the
  twenty-first century." (p. 97, folio 87)
- **Not unique, but distinguished by Pakistan.** Jama'at 'Ali Shah and Khwaja Hasan Nizami are
  offered as parallels, "Yet Muhammad Zauqi Shah, Shahidullah Faridi, and Wahid Bakhsh Sial Rabbani
  are distinguished by their concerted efforts to root Chishti Sabiri history, doctrine, and practice
  in the contested landscape of postcolonial Pakistan." (p. 97, folio 87)
- **Chapter 3's thesis.** The trio "imagined and articulated an alternative Pakistani identity that
  was simultaneously Muslim, modern, and mystic" (p. 99, folio 90); "The bulk of my analysis
  spotlights two lengthy works by Wahid Bakhsh Sial Rabbani." (p. 99, folio 90) The author frames the
  campaign as "a striking case study of postcolonial, mass media Sufism—and yet another example of
  the Chishti Sabiri order's dynamic attempt to forge an alternative modernity in the twenty-first
  century." (p. 99, folio 90)
- **Identity as constructed and contextual.** "Identity is neither a priori nor inherited. Instead,
  it is imagined, constructed, and then articulated in narratives… identity is invariably a fluid
  construct. It is 'relational and incomplete'" (p. 99, folio 90), supported with Sudipta Kaviraj on
  the "narrative contract" (pp. 99–100, folios 90–91), Benedict Anderson's *Imagined Communities*
  and its "imagined political community" bound by a common "print language" (p. 100, folio 91), and
  Armando Salvatore on a "transformation in the 'episteme' of an age" (p. 100, folio 91).
- **Muslim print was defensive in origin.** "This sudden influx of bibliomania among South Asia's
  Muslims was largely a defensive response. Muslim communities generated their own publications as a
  weapon in the defense of the faith against the polemical attacks of such Hindu reformists groups
  such as the Arya Samaj, as well as the campaigns of British colonial administrators and Christian
  missionaries." (p. 101, folio 92) Its effects: Urdu cemented "as the official language of Muslim
  discourse and identity", a rise in "Islamist scripturalist movements", internationalisation, and
  "the democratization of religious knowledge as new religious intellects entered the public sphere
  to challenge the traditional authority of the 'ulama" (p. 101, folio 92).
- **Print turned Sufism into a public institution.** "In effect, Sufism was transformed from an
  esoteric community based exclusively on the oral instruction between master and disciple into a
  public institution." (p. 102, folio 93) Carl Ernst is quoted: "the media permit Sufism to be
  contested and defended in the public sphere as one ideology alongside others." (p. 102, folio 93)
- **A break with the premodern Chishti pattern.** The twentieth-century leaders' entry into public
  writing is "a marked deviation from the precedent of their premodern predecessors who largely
  avoided urban spaces and networks of royal patronage in favor of a life of spiritual quietism and
  withdrawal in rural locales." (p. 103, folio 94) Ernst and Lawrence are cited for the shaykhs'
  "creative responses to new realities" (p. 103, folio 94).
- **Middle ground between modernists, 'ulama and Islamists.** Framed against Marshall Hodgson's
  "Great Western Transmutation" (p. 103, folio 94): they "embrace science, educational reform, and
  independent legal reasoning (ijtihad)" and "champion egalitarianism, civil liberties, and
  socioeconomic justice", yet "warn against the wholesale adoption of Western political models of
  liberal democracy"; with the 'ulama they hold the Qur'an, sunna and shari'a indispensable but deny
  the 'ulama's interpretive monopoly, grounding authority in "ma'rifa… rather than 'ilm"; with the
  Islamists they reject secularization but "critique the Islamists' exclusivist and literal
  interpretations of scripture". "Chishti Sabiris lament the spread of Wahhabi doctrine and the rise
  of sectarian identities" (pp. 103–104, folios 94–95). The author calls this "a dynamic approach
  that itself belies the Orientalist portrait of contemporary Sufism as an intransigent, ossified
  relic." (p. 104, folio 95)
- **Modernity reconstructed, not rejected.** The writing project "does not necessarily represent a
  rejection of modernity per se, but can be seen as a reconstruction of modernity according to
  Islamic models and motifs." (p. 104, folio 95) Muhammad Qasim Zaman is used in parallel on the
  Pakistani 'ulama (p. 104, folio 95).
- **Pakistani nationalism as doctrine.** "Muhammad Zauqi, Shahidullah Faridi, and Wahid Bakhsh Sial
  Rabbani were ardent Pakistani nationalists… This too distinguishes them from their Islamist and
  'ulama peers, many of whom resisted the independence movement and lamented Partition". Invoking
  "the famous hadith, 'Love of the homeland is part of one's faith' (hubb al-watan min al-iman), the
  shaykhs' writings sacralize Pakistan as nothing less than a Divine reward. At the same time, they
  define the silsila as a sacred moral community antecedent to the nation-state." (p. 105, folio 96)
- **Zauqi Shah as apologist.** "Through his writings, he transformed himself into a public apologist
  for Sufism. His works can be seen as an attempt to counter the discourses of British Orientalism
  and Islamist movements on the one hand and the anti-Muslim polemics of Christian missionaries and
  Hindu nationalists on the other." (p. 106, folio 97) He "rejected the scriptural reformism of the
  movement that championed their legacy while erasing their Chishti Sabiri roots." (p. 106, folio 97)
