# rozehnal_islamic_sufism_unbound — chunk 004 — pp. 60–75

**What this chunk is.** A continuous run inside chapter 2 ("Muslim, Mystic, and Modern" — the running
recto header on every odd page here), covering the second half of the book's life of **Muhammad Zauqi
Shah** (sections: Spiritual Training, Qualities as a Teaching Shaykh, Married Life, Miracles, Ecstasy
and Sobriety, Lifestyle, Public and Political Life) and then the opening of the life of **Shaykh
Shahidullah Faridi (1915–1978)** (sections: Family Background and Spiritual Initiation, Education,
Spiritual Training). It carries one full-page plate, Figure 2.1 (p. 71, folio not stated). The chunk
**ends mid-sentence** on p. 75: "a stranger suddenly arrived and announced that he would" (p. 75,
folio 65) — the completion is in chunk 005.

**Folio.** The supplied table was confirmed against the printed running headers on fourteen of the
sixteen pages (verso `<folio>  Islamic Sufism Unbound`, recto `Muslim, Mystic, and Modern  <folio>`).
**One discrepancy, flagged under Doubts:** the table gives p. 71 as "folio not stated", but the text
layer of that plate page does print a bare `61` (p. 71, folio not stated — written per the table).

**Mapping: two archive rows are securely named in this chunk, plus one deity-name hit taken under the
18 Sep ruling.** The chunk's shrine geography is overwhelmingly Indian (Ajmer, Delhi, Kalyar Sharif,
Hyderabad Deccan, Daulatabad, Khuldabad, Bombay) and Arabian (Mecca); its Pakistani shrine material is
confined to Lahore (Mian Mir, Hujwiri) and to the shrine of Shahidullah Faridi in Karachi, which has
no archive row. See Doubts for the full list of near-misses checked and refused.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **shrine-of-mian-mir** (the book's name for him: "the saint Miyan Mir"): named once, in an anecdote
  narrated to the author by "another senior male murid" — "Once Captain Wahid Bakhsh Sahab and Hazrat
  Shahidullah Sahab went to visit the shrine of the saint Miyan Mir in Lahore" (p. 72, folio 62). The
  book gives **no death date, no silsila, no street location and no second name** for him here: all
  (not stated). The shrine functions in the passage purely as the setting for the majhzub encounter
  recorded under Legends below (p. 72, folio 62). The book's spelling is `Miyan Mir`, against the
  archive's `Mian Mir` — recorded so the consolidator does not read it as a different figure.

- **data-darbar** (the book's names for him: "'Ali Hujwiri (d. 1072)", "his patron saint 'Ali
  Hujwiri", "Hujwiri's shrine"): the strongest archive material in this chunk, in three distinct
  registers.
  - **As author.** Shahidullah Faridi's "interest in Sufism was reportedly sparked by his discovery of
    Reynold A. Nicholson's (1868–1945) translation of Kashf al-mahjub [The Unveiling of the Secrets],
    the famous Persian treatise on Sufism written by the eleventh-century master 'Ali Hujwiri
    (d. 1072)" (p. 72, folio 62). The death year **1072 is printed as a digit string** and, per this
    book's measured damage profile, is reliable; the bracketed English title gloss is the book's own.
    Note it is Nicholson's translation, not the Persian, that the book credits with the conversion of
    interest — the book does not say which edition or year (not stated) (p. 72, folio 62).
  - **As the site of a burial.** Faruq Ahmad (Shahidullah's elder brother) "died in Lahore on February
    13, 1945 after a prolonged bout of pneumonia. The details of his tragic death and burial within
    the tomb complex of his patron saint 'Ali Hujwiri are outlined in Tarbiyat al-'ushshaq" (p. 75,
    folio 65). The phrase the book uses is **"the tomb complex"**, and the relationship it asserts is
    **"his patron saint"** — the book does not use the names Data Darbar or Data Ganj Bakhsh anywhere
    in this chunk (not stated) (p. 75, folio 65).
  - **As an economy of burial.** "According to the text, there were insufficient funds to fulfill
    Faruq Ahmad's desire to be buried in the compound of Hujwiri's shrine, and an alternative location
    was hastily arranged. As the burial was about to commence, however, a stranger suddenly arrived
    and announced that he would" — **the sentence is cut by the chunk boundary** (p. 75, folio 65).
    That burial in the compound cost money the mourners did not have is a direct statement about
    shrine economy and belongs in the entry; **what the stranger did is not in this chunk.**
  - The book's vision-narrative locates the funeral itself at Lahore: "a light rain fell from that
    cloud of mercy only above that spot in Lahore" (p. 75, folio 65). It does **not** say the final
    grave is inside the shrine compound — on the contrary it says an alternative location was arranged
    (p. 75, folio 65).

- **ram-mandir-saidpur-ram-kund-mandir** (the book's name: "Rama"): named **once, in a bare list and
  nothing more.** Reporting Zauqi Shah's lectures, the book says "In certain lectures the shaykh
  describes non-Muslim spiritual luminaries—Krishna, Rama, Sita, Lachman, and Guru Nanak—as believers
  in the one true God and religious leaders with 'Divine connection' (nisbat)" (p. 63, folio 53). No
  temple, no Saidpur, no site of any kind is attached (not stated). The id is assigned only under the
  ruling of 18 Sep 2026 because "Rama" is that row's principal figure and matches **exactly one**
  archive row; **see Doubts — this is a name-only, deity-in-a-doctrinal-list hit and the consolidator
  should weigh it as near-worthless for the temple's entry** (p. 63, folio 53).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **A shaykh's library as an institution.** "Zauqi Shah's love of books is evidenced in his expansive
  personal library preserved in a house near the shrine of Shahidullah Faridi in Karachi. The library
  contains a large collection of Arabic, Urdu, and Persian texts on tafsir (Qur'an interpretation) and
  fiqh (Islamic law), along with a broad selection of Sufi poetry and biographical works. Catalogued
  and well maintained, the shaykh's personal collection is accessible to Chishti Sabiri disciples"
  (p. 60, folio 50). Note the library is sited **by** the Karachi shrine, not in it (p. 60, folio 50).
- **Custodianship, and the order's polemic against it.** Sirat-i Zauqi: "He had neither inherited it as
  a sajjada nishin, nor was it founded on an empty display of spirituality" (p. 61, folio 51). The
  author reads this directly: "The denigration of hereditary Sufi leaders (sajjada nishin) in this
  passage is particularly telling. It suggests that they may not have any particular spiritual
  accomplishments to match their auspicious family lineage" (p. 61, folio 51).
- **No khanaqah — the modern substitutes.** "Unlike his Chishti ancestors, Zauqi Shah never lived in a
  traditional khanaqah—the premodern Sufi hospice where disciples and visitors either lived or visited
  for spiritual guidance. Instead, he met privately with disciples in his modest home in Karachi or
  traveled to meet them in person. Under his leadership the silsila's collective gatherings were
  limited to weekly zikr sessions (also in the Zauqi Shah's home), along with the annual pilgrimages
  to local shrines for the death anniversaries ('urs) of prominent Sufi saints" (p. 63, folio 53).
- **Chilla.** "His malfuzat recounts two forty-day spiritual retreats (chilla) in particular, one in
  Delhi at the mazar of Nizam ad-Din Awliya' and another in Kalyar Sharif" (p. 63, folio 53).
- **Langar, and who serves it.** "The brothers also traveled frequently to Sufi shrines for extended
  spiritual retreats and regularly attended the annual 'urs celebrations of local saints. At Ajmer
  Sharif they often worked in the langar, serving food and water to impoverished pilgrims" (p. 74,
  folio 64; attributed to an interview, March 24, 2001, Islamabad).
- **Sama' at 'urs, and its decorum.** Sirat-i Zauqi: "He did not listen to it often, but on the
  occasion of the death anniversaries ['urs] of Sufi saints he always listened to it. He was never
  seen in ecstasy… There were no outcries of ecstasy, and no unrefined poems [ghazals]" (p. 67,
  folio 57). Zauqi Shah "found the carnival-like atmosphere of many public qawwali sessions especially
  distasteful" and held that "sama' was appropriate only for the elite practitioners on the Sufi path"
  (p. 67, folio 57).
- **Qawwali against a state prohibition.** During the 1951 Hajj "the shaykh managed to arrange for a
  qawwali performance in a private home within the sacred precincts of Mecca—a practice prohibited by
  anti-Sufi, Wahhabi doctrine" (pp. 67–68, folios 57–58); Hajj-i Zauqi: "In Saudi Arabia, qawwali is
  prohibited, but Allah Almighty arranged for qawwali inside the noble sanctuary [haram sharif] in
  Mecca" (p. 68, folio 58). Fatiha was offered for Mu'in ad-Din Chishti before the assembly (p. 68,
  folio 58).
- **Money, donations and welfare.** "Zauqi Shah's biographers also note, however, that the shaykh
  recognized the importance of accepting donations and handling money in the interest of social
  welfare" (p. 68, folio 58), and in a lecture: "The status of those saints [awliya' Allah] who have
  worldly possessions and wealth is very high. Their powers are so strong that they can perform both
  their spiritual and worldly duties" (p. 69, folio 59). Against this, Sirat-i Zauqi has him following
  Hajji Imdad Allah in "insisting that dervishes should avoid directly asking for assistance" (p. 68,
  folio 58).
- **Burial costs at a shrine.** "there were insufficient funds to fulfill Faruq Ahmad's desire to be
  buried in the compound of Hujwiri's shrine, and an alternative location was hastily arranged"
  (p. 75, folio 65).
- **Patronage and state employment.** The Nawab of Bahawalpur met the brothers in England "while
  vacationing as the guest of their father", encouraged them to come to India, and "His patronage
  proved invaluable to the young Englishmen" (pp. 72–73, folios 62–63); in September 1939 he "secured
  a position for them as captains in the Bahawalpur State Army. This arrangement allowed the brothers
  to commit themselves to their spiritual disciplines while avoiding conscription in the British Army
  during the course of the Second World War" (p. 74, folio 64).
- **Citizenship after Partition.** "Following Partition in 1947, Shahidullah surrendered his British
  passport and accepted Pakistani citizenship" (p. 72, folio 62).
- **The order's own publishing.** "The shaykh's own corpus of writings in Urdu and English are printed
  and distributed by the contemporary order. In addition, a record of his spiritual discourses—compiled
  by his designated successor, Shaykh Sira 'Ali Muhammad—was published by the silsila in 1996. This
  book titled Malfuzat-i shaykh comprises nineteen eclectic lectures dating from September 1970 to
  August 1977" (p. 70, folio 60).
- **Oral transmission as the archive.** "More than a quarter of a century after Shahidullah's death,
  this repository of personal memories remains the primary source of collective knowledge about his
  life and legacy" (p. 70, folio 60).
- **Politics as an institution of the silsila.** Zauqi Shah "participated directly in politics as a
  writer, an active member of the Muslim League, and a personal confidant of Muhammad Ali Jinnah"
  (p. 69, folio 59), and preserved "copies of all the letters he wrote to Qaid-i Azam [Muhammad Ali
  Jinnah], Pickthall … Habib Allah Lovegrove … and other important personages" (p. 60, folio 50).
- **Access across religion and class.** Sirat-i Zauqi: "Hazrat Shah Sahab's … circle of followers
  included people from every religion and social class. There were no restrictions against Muslims,
  Hindus, Parsis, or Christians… nor did he demand that non-Muslims convert" (p. 63, folio 53).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Doctrine first: miracles are to be concealed.** Attributed by the author to Nizam ad-Din Awliya'
  in Fawa'id al-Fu'ad: "God Almighty has commanded His Saints to conceal their miracles (karamat),
  just as He has commanded His prophets to demonstrate theirs (mu'jizat)… There are one hundred stages
  on the spiritual path. The seventeenth stage provides divine inspiration to perform miraculous acts.
  Now, if the traveler stops at this stage, how will he reach the other eighty-three?" (p. 65,
  folio 55). Zauqi Shah "dismissed miracles as child's play" (p. 65, folio 55).
- **Visions of Khizr and of the Prophet** — "his discourses record an early encounter with the
  immortal prophet Khizr along with frequent visions of the Prophet Muhammad", attributed to Zauqi
  Shah's discourses (p. 61, folio 51).
- **Dream of Mu'in ad-Din Chishti as a youth of seventeen** — the two travel "instantaneously from
  Ajmer to Multan" to help a sick friend who lacks money to return to Delhi; "his friend subsequently
  verified that he had been miraculously cured and woke to find a stash of money under his pillow",
  attributed to Zauqi Shah's own narration (pp. 61–62, folios 51–52).
- **Succession announced in a dream at Ajmer, 1914** — "Zauqi Shah also received news of his status as
  the successor (khalifa) to Waris Hasan Shah directly from Khwaja Mu'in ad-Din Chishti in a dream
  during pilgrimage to the saint's shrine in Ajmer in 1914", as a "parallel narrative" (p. 62,
  folio 52).
- **Nizam ad-Din in disguise** — at the mazar in Delhi a Punjabi mendicant (majhzub) asks Zauqi Shah
  for money; "Later, Waris Hasan Shah tells him that the majhzub was none other than Nizam ad-Din
  himself in disguise" (p. 62, folio 52).
- **Vision of 'Ali Ahmad Sabir at Kalyar Sharif**, who "gives him a lengthy discourse on the spiritual
  qualities and benefits of listening to music (sama')" (p. 62, folio 52).
- **Dream of October 24, 1927** during prolonged fasting: Hajji Imdad Allah teaches a breathing
  exercise "that allows him to merge into the Divine essence (dhat)"; at the shrine of the Prophet
  Yusuf they are joined by his master, who on hearing of it in Bombay "immediately announced that it
  was an indication that Zauqi Shah had been granted wilayat-i Yusufi (the station of the 'authority
  of the Prophet Joseph')" (p. 62, folio 52).
- **Dream of Rashid Ahmad Gangohi at Ajmer Sharif**, after which Zauqi Shah "is immediately granted
  permission to initiate his own disciples in the name of Waris Hasan Shah (wilayat-i niyabati)"
  (p. 62, folio 52).
- **Clairvoyance before the Chief Minister of Hyderabad** — Sirat-i Zauqi records that at a first
  meeting "the shaykh immediately asks the politician why he has abandoned a particular meditation
  exercise. Duly impressed, the prime minister promises to return to the practice" (p. 65, folio 55).
- **At Gandhi's ashram** — finding Gandhi absent, Zauqi Shah advises a young Hindu woman on
  concentration; she tells him, "[Y]ou are a very spiritual man and I will certainly tell Mr. Gandhi
  about you" (p. 65, folio 55).
- **Healing by transfer of impurity** — a boy "overwhelmed by excessive illuminations from continuous
  acts of worship" is cured when the shaykh "transferred the effects of the food and the filth of
  those base thoughts into the boy's heart through concentration"; his own shaykh approves, "A dervish
  ought to exercise this kind of independent reasoning [ijtihad]!" (p. 66, folio 56).
- **Cure of spinal tuberculosis by breath** — told to the author in a personal interview by "one of
  Zauqi Shah's oldest and most revered disciples", who as a child "was taken to Zauqi Shah who cured
  his condition by breathing into his open mouth" (p. 66, folio 56; interview May 21, 2001, Karachi).
- **The beating heart after death** — Hajj-i Zauqi, in Wahid Bakhsh's narrative: "'There is no doubt
  that he has passed away [wisal], but amazingly his heart continues to beat as before!'", as
  confirmed by "a Pakistani doctor" (p. 66, folio 56).
- **A saint present at a Meccan sama'** — when the qawwal "sang in praise of Khwaja Gharib Nawaz
  [Mu'in ad-Din Chishti (d. 1236)] … it seemed as though the saint was actually present"; Zauqi Shah
  explains, "I offered Fatiha for him … so from the very beginning he was attentive to us" (p. 68,
  folio 58).
- **The majhzub at Mian Mir's shrine, Lahore** — asked his hometown, Shahidullah answers "My hometown
  is London"; the majhzub laughs, "No, no. Your hometown is Medina!" and "repeated this many times"
  (p. 72, folio 62). Attributed to "another senior male murid", May 2, 2001, Bahawalpur.
- **Shahidullah's mother's deathbed conversion** — "he returned only once—to visit his dying mother
  who, according to the hagiographical record, converted to Islam on her deathbed" (p. 72, folio 62).
- **The funeral of Faruq Ahmad in Lahore** — Shahidullah's letter to Zauqi Shah records the Prophet at
  the dying man's head, "The saints [Awliya' Allah] had formed a circle and were dancing", and "at the
  time of prayer a small cloud appeared directly over the site and stopped there. Throughout the time
  of the funeral, a light rain fell from that cloud of mercy only above that spot in Lahore" (p. 75,
  folio 65).
- **Blessing withheld then given at a shrine** — at Sayyid Yusuf Husseini's shrine Shahidullah "did
  not perceive any spiritual blessings in my heart"; sent back alone the next morning by Zauqi Shah,
  "during this second visit I began to feel the spiritual blessings and sensed a strong connection
  [nisbat] [with the saint]" (p. 75, folio 65). The shaykh's explanation invokes a "'skeleton
  program' [sic, in English]" prepared for each disciple (p. 75, folio 65).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Dreams are the genre's credentials.** "For an audience steeped in Sufi sacred biographies, these
  dreams serve as vital markers—direct and unambiguous signs of spiritual power and accomplishment.
  Zauqi Shah emerges from these accounts as a pious, disciplined, and highly accomplished Sufi
  spiritual master: the twentieth-century reflection of his nineteenth-century Chishti Sabiri
  predecessors" (p. 62, folio 52). Of the visionary encounters generally: "The sheer abundance of
  these encounters is clearly meant to signal Zauqi Shah's acceptance into the Chishti pantheon"
  (p. 62, folio 52).
- **Personal attainment set against hereditary succession** — the author's reading of the sajjada
  nishin passage (p. 61, folio 51), and: "the hagiographer asserts that Zauqi Shah earned his lofty
  spiritual status through personal effort and raw determination" (p. 61, folio 51).
- **Colonial-era context for cross-creedal openness.** "The frequency of such cross-creedal encounters
  in his hagiographies is best understood against the backdrop of the late colonial period. Faced with
  the confrontational politics of religious communalism, Zauqi Shah became a public apologist for
  Sufism. Within the charged polemical atmosphere surrounding Partition, he actively defended Sufi
  piety and practice from its detractors, both Muslim and non-Muslim alike" (p. 63, folio 53). Also:
  "he is portrayed as a firm advocate of Islamic orthodoxy and a staunch defender of Sufi doctrine and
  ritual practices" (p. 64, folio 54), and he "aggressively championed the superiority of Islam as the
  final revelation and Sufism as the most highly developed spiritual path" (p. 63, folio 53).
- **Sufism as an alternative episteme** — the chunk's strongest theoretical claim: miracle stories
  "suggest that a Sufi saint's powers trump the wisdom and technological wonders of Western science
  and medicine. Taken together, such miraculous anecdotes mark Sufism as an alternative episteme—a
  parallel system of inner, esoteric knowledge with real power and efficacy in the mundane world"
  (p. 66, folio 56).
- **Two explicit "breaks" from the premodern Chishti paradigm** are asserted: openness to outsiders
  ("Zauqi Shah's attitude toward outsiders represents another important break from the traditional
  paradigm of Chishti leadership", p. 63, folio 53) and political engagement ("They also deviate
  sharply from the traditional, premodern pattern of Chishti leadership that favored withdrawal from
  public, political life", p. 69, folio 59).
- **Hagiography's internal tension, named by the author.** "Zauqi Shah's biographers chart his
  political activities. At the same time, there is a marked attempt to downplay their importance for
  his legacy as a Chishti Sabiri spiritual master" (p. 69, folio 59), yet "Clearly, Zauqi Shah found
  no contradiction in balancing his duties as a spiritual teacher with his strong political
  commitments" (p. 69, folio 59). A forward pointer: this is taken up "in chapter 3" (p. 69,
  folio 59).
- **Reverse orientalism.** "the trajectory of Shahidullah Faridi's life inverts the dynamics of the
  colonial encounter. For contemporary Chishti Sabiri disciples he remains a shining example of the
  universal truth of Sufism's power to transcend the mundane boundaries of class, ethnicity, and race"
  (p. 70, folio 60); he came "not, as for so many of his British contemporaries, in pursuit of a
  career as a colonial administrator but instead for a life of sacrifice, poverty, and piety as a Sufi
