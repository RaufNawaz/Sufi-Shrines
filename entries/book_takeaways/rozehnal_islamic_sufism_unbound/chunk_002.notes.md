# rozehnal_islamic_sufism_unbound — chunk 002 — pp. 27–43

**What this chunk is.** It runs from the last two pages of the Introduction's chapter-by-chapter
summary (pp. 27–28, folios 16–17) into the whole of Chapter 1, "Sufism and the Politics of Islamic
Identity" (pp. 29–43, folio not stated then folios 20–33), breaking off mid-quotation at the end of
p. 43. The chapter has three named sections: "FAQIR or Faker? The Public Battle over Sufism"
(p. 29, folio not stated), "The 2001 Pakpattan Tragedy" (p. 35, folio 25), and "Chishti Sabiri Sufism
as Islamic Orthodoxy" (p. 40, folio 30). It also carries two figure captions: "Map 1.1 Map of
Pakistan / Source: The World Factbook" (p. 30, folio 20) and "Figure 1.1 The Shrine of Baba Farid
ad-Din Mas'ud Ganj-i Shakkar (d. 1265), Pakpattan, Pakistan / Source: Photograph by Robert Rozehnal"
(p. 35, folio 25).

**This is the book's single densest chunk on one archive shrine.** Chapter 1 is a firsthand account
of the fatal stampede at Pakpattan on the night of 1 April 2001 and of the competing explanations
offered for it. **Exactly one archive row is named in pp. 27–43** —
`shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` — and it carries almost the whole chunk. Every other named site
(Ajmer Sharif, Kalyar Sharif, Nizam ad-Din in Delhi, Mecca, Medina, Karbala, Kuala Lumpur) is
outside the archive; Lahore and Islamabad appear as bare toponyms only, see Doubts.

**Folio.** Taken from the measured table for this chunk, not computed: pp. 27–28 = folios 16–17,
p. 29 = folio not stated (chapter-opening display page), pp. 30–43 = folios 20–33. The offset step

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar** (the book's names for him: "Baba Farid ad-Din Mas'ud Ganj-i
  Shakkar (d. 1265)", "Baba Farid", "the saint", "Hazrat Baba Sahab", and in the pilgrims' shouts
  "Ya Farid, Haq Farid!"):
  - **The disaster, as the book's own opening sentence states it:** "On the night of April 1, 2001
    more than one hundred Pakistani men lost their lives, crushed to death in a mob stampede at the
    tomb of one of South Asia's most renowned Sufi saints: Baba Farid ad-Din Mas'ud Ganj-i Shakkar
    (d. 1265)" (p. 29, folio not stated). The author writes that he was present: "I too was there
    that night, accompanying a select group of Chishti Sabiri Sufi disciples (murids)" (p. 37,
    folio 27).
  - **Death date as printed: "(d. 1265)"**, given twice — in the opening sentence (p. 29, folio not
    stated) and in the caption to Figure 1.1 (p. 35, folio 25). Digits are reliable in this text
    layer. **This disagrees with the archive row's `figure_died` of 1266** — see Doubts.
  - **Location and distance:** "The regional shrine complex in the small pilgrimage town of
    Pakpattan—120 miles southwest of Lahore—has for centuries served as a central marker in the
    regional history of Punjab" (p. 35, folio 25). The caption gives "Pakpattan, Pakistan" (p. 35,
    folio 25).
  - **Antiquity:** "Since the thirteenth century, the tomb of the saint, Baba Farid, has linked the
    local population to a wider universe of Islamic piety and Chishti Sufi spiritual hierarchy"
    (p. 35, folio 25). A disciple's account refers to "a six-hundred-and-fifty-year-old tradition"
    of custodianship broken by the Awqaf takeover (p. 41, folio 31).
  - **Standing after Partition:** with pilgrimage to "the major Chishti shrines in India—including
    Ajmer Sharif, Kalyar Sharif, and Nizam ad-Din in Delhi" made "prohibitively difficult for
    Pakistani Muslims" by "new passports and the redrawing of local maps", "Pakpattan now stands as
    the unrivaled center of a distinctly Pakistani Sufism" (p. 36, folio 26). He is called "the
    preeminent saint of Pakistan's Punjab" (p. 36, folio 26).
  - **Communal composition, as the book frames it:** "In the absence of other religious
    communities—the Hindu, Jain, or Sikh 'Other'—Pakpattan is no longer a site of intercommunal
    conflict or cooperation. Instead, the shrine is now marked as exclusively Muslim space"
    (pp. 35–36, folios 25–26).
  - **The 'urs and its date:** the stampede fell "Late in the night of April 1, 2001—the fifth day
    of Muharram, the first month of the Muslim lunar calendar", "the eve of Baba Farid's death
    anniversary ('urs)", when "tens of thousands of devotees inundated the provincial town of
    Pakpattan" (p. 36, folio 26).
  - **The bihishti darwaza:** "the narrow Gate of Heaven (bihishti darwaza)"; "This famous southern
    door of the saint's tomb remains closed throughout the year until the evening of the anniversary
    when it is opened by the Diwan, a direct lineal descendant of the saint and the shrine's official
    representative (sajjada nishin)" (p. 36, folio 26). Passing it is "a direct, salvational
    encounter with Baba Farid's living grace (baraka), a ritual reenactment of the saint's eternal
    union ('urs, or 'marriage') with God" (p. 36, folio 26).
  - **Custodian named:** "Diwan Maudud Mas'ud Chishti", "the shrine's chief custodian" (p. 39,
    folio 29), who issued "the personal invitation" under which the Chishti Sabiri party entered the
    complex early (p. 37, folio 27).
  - **The state's account of the cause:** "the Diwan had delayed the public ceremonies and the
    opening of the bihishti darwaza by four hours while bickering over the annual payment allotted
    to him from the Awqaf Department"; the day after, "the lead article in the national Urdu
    newspaper Jang quoted the district commissioner who insisted that this financial quarrel
    precipitated the crisis" (p. 39, folio 29).
  - **The custodians' rebuttal:** "Badr Moeenuddin, the Diwan's maternal uncle", writing in "the
    English daily newspaper The Nation", "blames poor crowd control and security arrangements as the
    underlying cause of the stampede", noting "the tragedy took place some two hours after the
    Diwan's arrival for the door-opening ceremony" (p. 39, folio 29).
  - **Mechanics of the crush, as the author reconstructs it:** victims "ascended the narrow stairwell
    at the extreme north end of the shrine, a passageway typically reserved for the Diwan and his
    personal guests"; "an impatient crowd of several thousand onlookers broke through a weak police
    cordon and descended upon them from above"; "a police contingent posted at the entrance point
    made the fateful decision to close and lock the doors, sealing off the only means of escape"
    (pp. 36–37, folios 26–27).
  - **Death toll, contradictory in the book's own pages.** The opening states "more than one hundred
    Pakistani men lost their lives" (p. 29, folio not stated) and the quoted German disciple writes
    of "more than one hundred people who breathed their last there" (p. 41, folio 31); but the
    reporter Tahir Jehangir, quoted from "the Lahore-based English language weekly, The Friday
    Times", says "The newspapers reported over forty, but I can vouch for the fact that there were
    many more" (p. 37, folio 27). Both figures are recorded as the book prints them.
  - **The named victim:** "A fourteen-year-old boy from the group was not so fortunate, however,
    perishing amid the melee . . . He was the son of a prominent devotee and, significantly, the
    great-grandson of the prominent Chishti Sabiri master, Shaykh Muhammad Zauqi Shah (1877–1951)"
    (p. 38, folio 28). He is not named. He is described as "a member of a sayyid family (i.e., a
    lineal descendant of the Prophet Muhammad)" (p. 42, folio 32).
  - **Ritual distribution at the 'urs:** "the Diwan mounted a large stage in the center of the
    courtyard to distribute saffron-soaked strips of cloth known as pechas to the assembled masses"
    (pp. 37–38, folios 27–28).
  - **Earlier ritual described at first hand:** the male Chishti Sabiri disciples, "Dressed in white
    shalwar kamiz and following the strict rules of etiquette and decorum (adab)", "formed a series
    of straight rows parallel to the main door of the saint's tomb" and "sat or stood in quiet
    contemplation (muraqaba) as the female disciples looked down from the rooftop balcony above"
    (p. 37, folio 27). The party left "through the northern gate, as was the custom" (p. 38,
    folio 28).
  - **Colonial-era interference at this shrine specifically:** "At times, the British interfered
    directly in the administration of local shrines such as `Pakattan` [text-layer?: Pakpattan],
    going so far as to mediate succession disputes in colonial courts" (p. 33, folio 23).
  - **An 1833 European description of the door ceremony, quoted by the book:** "Numbers of pilgrims,
    both Hindus and Mussalmans, come to visit the shrine, and all who pass through this doorway are
    considered saved from the fires of perdition. The doorway is about two feet wide . . . the
    apartment itself is not capable of containing thirty people crowded together" (p. 36, folio 26).
    The visitor is not named; the year is printed "1833" (p. 36, folio 26).
  - **Richard Eaton on the shrine, quoted at length:** "The shrine of Baba Farid in Pakpattan
    provides a striking example of how Islam, the religion par excellence 'of the Book,' has been in
    one instance mediated among common villagers most of whom were illiterate" — the shrine
    "displayed, in short, the Court of God", and Baba Farid was the devotee's "friend in court"
    (p. 35, folio 25).
  - **Commemoration at a distance:** Malaysian disciples unable to travel "gathered together at a
    private home in Kuala Lumpur on the evening of the 'urs", "Listening to recordings of sama' on a
    small cassette player", and "commemorated the death of anniversary [text-layer?: death
    anniversary] of Baba Farid on the opposite side of the Indian Ocean" (p. 43, folio 33).
  - **The book gives, in this chunk, no silsila for Baba Farid himself, no birth date, no building
    dates for the complex, no visitor count beyond "tens of thousands", and no endowment or land

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **'Urs.** Defined in practice at Pakpattan as "Baba Farid's death anniversary ('urs)" (p. 36,
  folio 26) and glossed as the saint's "eternal union ('urs, or 'marriage') with God" (p. 36,
  folio 26). Attendance: "tens of thousands of devotees" (p. 36, folio 26).
- **Sajjada nishin / Diwan.** "the Diwan, a direct lineal descendant of the saint and the shrine's
  official representative (sajjada nishin)" (p. 36, folio 26). Only he opens the bihishti darwaza,
  and only on the 'urs evening (p. 36, folio 26).
- **Awqaf payment as the state's stated trigger.** "the annual payment allotted to him from the
  Awqaf Department" (p. 39, folio 29); a disciple writes that custodians were "coerced into
  surrendering the management of the shrine" to the Awqaf Department, an act that "dragged an
  institution of an essentially spiritual nature for reasons of revenue collection onto the mundane
  plane of government administration" (p. 41, folio 31).
- **The legal instrument of state control, dated.** "The state's hegemony over religious spaces and
  local religious leaders was formally institutionalized with the passage of the West Pakistan Waqf
  Properties Ordinance of 1959. This act provided legal precedent for the state to appropriate
  control over religious endowments and to manage shrines, mosques, and other properties dedicated
  to religious purposes" (p. 33, folio 23). Its result: "a further dissolution of the independence
  and political power of both the hereditary Sufi families and the local 'ulama" (pp. 33–34,
  folios 23–24).
- **British colonial shrine policy.** Ewing, quoted: "The hereditary saints [sajjada nishin] were
  seen primarily in terms of their economic power. British policy, especially in the Punjab and
  Sind, was to treat them in essentially the same way that landlords and tribal leaders were
  treated" (p. 33, folio 23). Gilmartin, paraphrased: under colonial rule the shrine's position "as
  a hinge between the culture of the locality and the larger Muslim community" held while "the role
  of the saint's living heirs was increasingly politicized" (p. 33, folio 23).
- **Shrines as instruments of rural development policy.** Nasr, quoted: "By taking over the
  management of the shrines, state leaders were able to use them to propagate a new interpretation
  of Sufism and rural Islam as compatible with development . . . rural religious festivals were used
  as venues for agricultural and industrial fairs. By becoming the keeper of shrines, the state was
  able to find a presence in rural areas, which was otherwise closed to it by the landed elite"
  (p. 34, folio 24).
- **Pechas.** "saffron-soaked strips of cloth known as pechas" distributed by the Diwan from "a large
  stage in the center of the courtyard" (pp. 37–38, folios 27–28).
- **Crowd management as a state function.** Pilgrims "waited for hours in the long lines weaving
  through the town" (p. 36, folio 26); Badr Moeenuddin on police conduct: pilgrims "are frightened of
  the police lathi-charge so at the slightest chance they tend to make a dash to the entrance"
  (p. 39, folio 29). Police resources were "already stretched thin by the state's concerted efforts
  to prevent sectarian violence throughout the district during the month of Muharram" (p. 39,
  folio 29).
- **Chishti Sabiri ritual complexes, as Chapter 5 will treat them:** "dreams and dream
  interpretation; rituals of remembrance (prayer, zikr, and muraqaba); and the annual pilgrimage
  networks and musical assemblies at Sufi shrines ('urs and sama')" (p. 27, folio 16).
- **Initiation and discipleship.** "murids narrate their own experiences of initiation (bay'at) and
  interaction with a spiritual guide" (p. 27, folio 16); the order is described as "a moral community
  mediated by strict rules of etiquette and decorum (adab)" (p. 27, folio 16).
- **Publication as an institution.** The Chapter 3 summary describes "the silsila's ongoing
  publication campaign in Pakistan and Malaysia, highlighting its adroit use of mass media technology
  to reach a broad, international audience" (p. 27, folio 16).
- **Composition of the Pakpattan party.** "more than a hundred and fifty men and women, most of them
  Pakistanis but including a large contingent from Malaysia as well", who "had traveled to Pakpattan
  . . . for a week of intensive spiritual immersion" (p. 37, folio 27). Two Malaysian murids present
  were "both of them medical doctors" (p. 38, folio 28).
- **Election figures (digits reliable).** "The national elections of October 2002"; the Muttahida
  Majlis-i Amal (MMA) captured "19 percent of the seats in the National Assembly, a controlling
  majority of the Provincial Assembly of the Northwest Frontier Province, and an upper hand as the
  largest party in Baluchistan" (p. 32, folio 22).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Faizan felt in the shaykh's wounds**, narrated by "a female Malaysian murid, a medical doctor who
  helped treat Shaykh Siraj's injuries": "I touched his wounds and the faizan [spiritual grace] was
  very strong. That is when I understood the meaning of the majesty of a saint" (p. 43, folio 33).
  The author glosses the term: "Faizan—literally meaning 'overflowing' or 'abundance'—is a common
  term used to describe the Divine blessing and power that radiate from sacred places and holy
  persons"; "Similar to the Arabic term baraka, it is understood as a marker of sacrality, here
  described in visceral, material terms as an electric charge or a surge of energy" (p. 43,
  folio 33).
- **Nothing accidental near a tomb**, reported as the murids' reasoning: "With a firm belief that
  nothing accidental happens in proximity to a saint's tomb, murids reasoned that the loss of life
  must be sacralized and understood in terms of martyrdom (shahadat)" (p. 41, folio 31).
- **The deaths as divine vengeance and reward**, in the words of the German disciple: "Against whom
  was this outburst of divine vengeance directed? Certainly not the more than one hundred people who
  breathed their last there. They, or at least a good number of them, embraced shahadat
  (martyrdom)" (p. 41, folio 31).
- **Saints intervening in worldly matters**, in the words of "a prominent female disciple": "I
  believe it was ordained to happen. Sometimes the saints, they do interfere in things, in worldly
  matters" (p. 42, folio 32).
- **Portents and dreams before the 'urs**, reported by the author from interviews: "many disciples
  recalled strange circumstances and portents of impending disaster"; "A number of disciples narrated
  dreams that they experienced in the days leading up to the 'urs: visions of Chishti Sabiri shaykhs,
  the death of family members and, in the case of one female murid, an attack by a black goat"; and
  "others noted sudden shifts in the weather as the ceremonies began that evening" (p. 43, folio 33).
- **Ecstasy at the Kuala Lumpur gathering:** "The faizan that night was fantastic. We thought we were
  going into hal [a state of ecstasy], all of us. We were so scared that we immediately stopped"
  (p. 43, folio 33).
- **Salvation through the doorway**, in the 1833 account: "all who pass through this doorway are
  considered saved from the fires of perdition"; "A superlative heaven is allotted to those who are
  first to enter the tomb on the day mentioned" (p. 36, folio 26).
- **The boy's completed path**, in a disciple's words: "He was a lovely child, a perfect human being.
  I really believe he had completed suluk [the Sufi path]" (p. 42, folio 32).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The chapter's stated purpose:** to use the Pakpattan stampede to "explore a broader issue: how
  Sufism functions as a lightening rod in the public debates over Islamic identity and authority in
  today's Pakistan" (p. 29, folio not stated). The author frames the whole as "Faqir or faker?"
  (p. 29, folio not stated) and concludes the framing: the chapter "highlights how Chishti Sabiris
  transformed the Pakpattan tragedy from story of personal loss and national trauma into a narrative
  of sacrifice and sacralization, with Sufism repositioned as the center of Islamic orthodoxy"
  (p. 29, folio not stated).
- **Three interpretive camps are compared**, which is the chapter's analytic structure: "the
  divergent interpretations of the state authorities, the shrine's hereditary custodians, and a group
  of Chishti Sabiri Sufi disciples" (p. 29, folio not stated). The author notes that state and
  custodians differed on blame but shared a frame: "both camps viewed the tragedy as the result of
  administrative mismanagement—a breakdown in logistics, communication, and the allocation of
  resources" (p. 40, folio 30), and that "What is missing from both accounts are the experiences and
  views of actual participants" (p. 40, folio 30).
- **The sacralizing reading as political resistance:** the disciples' account "stands in marked
  contrast to the logic of the modernist, rationalist state. In my view, it can also be read as a
  statement of resistance, a counternarrative to the hegemony of the state" (p. 40, folio 30),
  supported by Werbner and Basu on "the opposition between a morally grounded charisma and the
  rationalized authority of the state" (p. 40, folio 30).
- **Deprivatization of religion:** Casanova is quoted on religions "entering the public sphere and
  the arena of political contestation" (p. 30, folio 20); the author adds that this "progressive
  deprivatization of religion" is "Undermining entrenched theories of modernization and
  secularization" and mandates "a wholesale rethinking of the parameters of modernity" (pp. 30–31,
  folios 20–21).
- **But not new in the Muslim world:** "While the public emergence of religious symbols and actors
  may be a growing trend in the predominantly Christian West, this is nothing new in the Muslim
  world" (p. 31, folio 21).
- **Islamization as indigenization of the postcolonial state**, via Nasr: "[S]tate-led Islamization
  is in essence the indigenization of the postcolonial state . . . it is more efficient to be Islamic
  than not" (p. 32, folio 22). The author reads the 2002 MMA result as "the culmination of a gradual
  process of Islamization that has dominated Pakistani politics for the past quarter of a century"
  (p. 32, folio 22).
- **The attack on Sufism is modern, not traditional:** "The increasing attacks on and widespread
  marginalization of Sufism by both secular nationalists and Islamists are a distinctly modern
  phenomenon, however. In fact, the attack on Sufism runs counter to Islamic intellectual history and
  pervasive cultural norms" (p. 32, folio 22), evidenced by the Ernst quotation on the exclusion of
  Sufism from a taught "story of the Islamic religious tradition" (p. 33, folio 23).
- **Pakistani national identity as inherently unresolved**, via Ewing: "The idea of a distinctive
  'Pakistani Islam' . . . was inconsistent with the universality of Islam at the ideological level"
  (p. 31, folio 21); the author's own gloss: "Neither Islam nor Pakistan are inherently united"
  (p. 31, folio 21).
- **The limits of state capture**, the chapter's pivot to the Chishti Sabiris: Sufi knowledge
  "survived and thrived beyond the purview of the state" because it is "Communicated orally via the
  intimate master-disciple (pir-murid) relationship and experienced primarily in private ritual
  arenas" (p. 34, folio 24); Ewing's "excess of meaning" is "precisely . . . the key to understanding
  the Chishti Sabiri response to the tragedy in Pakpattan" (p. 34, folio 24).
- **The book's overall portrait of the order** (Introduction, close): the silsila is "simultaneously"
  four things — "a central marker in an imagined Pakistani (and now Malaysian) national identity"; "a
  discursive tradition, inscribed in texts and debated in the public sphere"; "an expression of
  Muslim subjectivity that constructs a modern, moral self"; "and above all a spiritual discipline,
  communicated in a dynamic teaching network and embodied in ritual performances" (p. 28, folio 17).
- **Hagiography as historical source** (Chapter 2 summary): the accounts of the three masters "offer
  unique insights into the historical context of twentieth-century South Asia. At the same time, they
  each conform to the traditional model of Indo-Muslim sainthood established in premodern Sufi
  biographical literature" (p. 27, folio 16).

## Doubts

- **One-row mapping, stated as an explicit positive-and-negative.** `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar`
  is the **only** archive row named in pp. 27–43, and it is named richly, not in passing — so no
  author-only ruling is needed to justify it. **No second archive row appears anywhere in this
  chunk**; the check was made against all 169 principal-figure fields and is recorded here so it is
  not redone.
- **Death-date conflict with the archive itself.** The book prints "(d. 1265)" twice (p. 29, folio
  not stated; p. 35, folio 25). The archive row `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` records
  `figure_died` **1266**. Digits survive cleanly in this text layer (measured), so this is a genuine
  disagreement between the book and the archive, not a transcription artefact. **A human must decide
  which stands; the notes above keep the book's 1265 as printed.**
