## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Shrine worship as the everyday face of Sufism, and as women's space:** "At the level of everyday
  social practice, Sufism is often equated with 'popular' worship at shrines. In South Asia, the
  tombs of Sufi saints provide an alternative outlet for piety and pilgrimage, especially for women
  who are often marginalized from the public, gendered space of the mosque (figure I.1)." (p. 13,
  folio 2)
- **What figure I.1 shows, in the author's words:** "Here women dance in a state of ecstasy in front
  of the saint's tomb while a group of musicians (qawwals) perform to a crowd of pilgrims." (p. 13,
  folio 2) Source line: "Photograph by Robert Rozehnal" (p. 13, folio 2).
- **Khanaqah functions:** Chishti shaykhs "established centers of learning and hospices (khanaqahs)
  that offered food and shelter for the wayfarers and mendicants who survived on their charity,
  solace for the pilgrims who visited them for spiritual blessings (baraka), and intensive spiritual
  training for their select disciples." (p. 14, folio 3)
- **How dargahs come into being, and what they do:** "The posthumous reputation of many Chishti
  shaykhs often led to the development of elaborate shrine complexes (dargahs) where devotees
  continued to flock in search of spiritual power to alleviate their worldly troubles. As loci of
  sacred geography and fonts for public social welfare, these regional shrines continue to thrive as
  pilgrimage sites and symbols of local Muslim culture and identity." (p. 14, folio 3) **The phrase
  "fonts for public social welfare" is the chunk's only statement on shrine economy.**
- **Sabiri anti-shrine tendency, stated as a branch characteristic:** Sabiri shaykhs "stuck to more
  rural locales, made fewer public appearances, trained fewer devotees, wrote fewer books, and
  generally avoided the construction of large shrine complexes (dargahs). As a result, much of the
  silsila's early history is elusive and opaque, shrouded in legend." (p. 14, folio 3)
- **'Urs, as the book uses the term here:** "the first death anniversary ('urs) of Shaykh Wahid
  Bakhsh Sial Rabbani", April 1996, at Allahabad; the author "remained there for a full week, living
  among disciples, sharing their meals and company, listening to their stories, and observing their
  ritual devotions" (p. 21, folio 10). Qawwali is performed at this 'urs, per figure 5.2's caption
  for the 2001 festival (p. 8, folio not stated).
- **The April 2001 Pakpattan stampede:** "over a hundred devotees were crushed to death in a mob
  stampede during the annual pilgrimage" (p. 26, folio 15). The book names three sets of actors
  whose responses it will examine: "the state authorities, the shrine custodians, and the Chishti
  Sabiri disciples" (p. 26, folio 15).
- **Ritual repertoire observed in fieldwork:** "communal prayers, Qur'an recitations, meditative
  chanting (zikr), contemplation (muraqaba), musical assemblies (sama'), and pilgrimages (ziyarat)
  to Sufi tomb complexes" (p. 26, folio 15).
- **Texts collected:** "an array of texts that encompass several distinct genres, from the
  discourses of Sufi masters (malfuzat), to private letters, diaries, and photographs" (p. 26,
  folio 15).
- **Colonial-era institution-building by Chishti Sabiris:** "In the late nineteenth century,
  prominent shaykhs linked themselves to social reform movements, founding educational institutions
  and publishing a broad range of texts." (p. 14, folio 3)
- **Who today's disciples are, socially:** "The inner circle of murids is comprised largely of
  urban, middle-class professionals who are fully integrated into the global, capitalist economy.
  Many of them are active participants in public life and civil society." (p. 19, folio 8) "A

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The photograph of a shaykh dead more than a year.** The author photographed an elderly stranger
  at Uch Sharif in April 1996 and sent copies to friends in Lahore (p. 21, folio 10). Returning in
  August 2000 he learned "the picture had caused an immediate sensation when the elderly stranger in
  the picture was identified as the deceased spiritual master Shaykh Wahid Bakhsh Sial Rabbani"
  (p. 21, folio 10) — "This was, to put it mildly, perplexing since the shaykh had been deceased for
  more than a year when the photograph was taken." (p. 22, folio 11) The author reports this as the
  disciples' identification, not as his own claim.
- **The widow's authentication.** "I was also told that the photograph had been shown to Wahid
  Bakhsh's wife shortly before her death. She declared it authentic, noting that the glasses, staff,
  and shoes were those of her late husband." (p. 22, folio 11) Attributed by the author to report
  ("I was also told"), not witnessed.
- **The photograph's circulation and effect.** "this photograph had also been copied and widely
  distributed among Shaykh Wahid Bakhsh's disciples—along with the story of my role as the
  photographer. As a result, murids I had never met would inevitably ask, 'Are you the one who took
  the picture of my shaykh?' In their eyes, this photograph legitimized my presence and my work."
  (p. 22, folio 11)
- **The stranger as described before identification** — carried here because the description is what
  the widow is said to have matched: "tall, thin, and dark-skinned, with long, flowing white hair
  and a beard … dressed in a plain, white shalwar kamiz, with simple leather sandals on his feet, a
  thick white blanket draped over his shoulders, and a white cap covering his head. He also wore a
  pair of thick, black glasses. In one hand he carried a string of prayer wooden beads, and in the
  other a long wooden staff." (p. 21, folio 10)

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The book's subject, stated plainly:** "This book is a study of a living Sufi tradition. Drawing
  on both textual and ethnographic materials, I trace the identity politics and ritual practices of
  a contemporary South Asian Sufi order (silsila) through the contexts of late colonial India and
  postcolonial Pakistan." (p. 12, folio not stated)
- **Sufism as alternative authority:** "As the inner or 'mystical' dimension of Islam, Sufism
  (tasawwuf) stands as an alternative nexus of Islamic authority, piety, and practice. Neither a
  sect nor a cult, it is best understood as a spiritual quest" (p. 12, folio not stated).
- **Against the shrine-only picture of Sufism:** "Yet the story of Sufism in South Asia does not end
  with saints and shrines." (p. 13, folio 2) And: "Above all, Sufism in twenty-first-century South
  Asia remains a spiritual teaching tradition—mediated in the intimate exchange between master and
  disciple and experienced through ritual performance." (p. 14, folio 3)
- **The Islamist critique the book is answering:** "For conservative Islamist critics who reject any
  intermediaries between human beings and God, the cult of Sufi saints is blasphemous idolatry and
  the worship at shrines corrupt superstition." (p. 13, folio 2)
- **The Chishtiyya's standing and doctrine:** "the Chishtiyya has been the most prominent Sufi
  brotherhood since the twelfth century" in South Asia, "With its doctrine of social equality,
  tolerance, and spiritual discipline" (p. 14, folio 3).
- **The colonial break in Sabiri practice:** "For the Chishti Sabiri order, however, this model of
  socially engaged Sufism represented a radical break with historical precedent." (p. 14, folio 3)
- **Tradition as contested, via Muhammad Qasim Zaman:** dichotomous constructions "are increasingly
  challenged by scholars who argue that 'appeals to tradition are not necessarily a way of opposing
  change but can equally facilitate change; that what passes for tradition is, not infrequently, of
  quite recent vintage; and that definitions of what constitutes tradition are often a product of
  bitter and continuing conflicts within a culture.'" (p. 17, folio 6) The author's own summary:
  "Tradition, in short, is up for grabs." (p. 17, folio 6)
- **The 'ulama's authority is slipping:** "In today's postcolonial Muslim world, the authority of the
  'ulama— religious scholars, literally 'those who know'—as arbiters of tradition is increasingly
  challenged", with "an increasing 'objectification of Muslim consciousness'" (p. 17, folio 6).
- **Modernity, decentered:** "This book aims to decenter modernity. In my view, modernity is neither
  monolithic nor hegemonic—it has no metanarrative, no universal form, no singular function."
  (p. 18, folio 7) Quoting Talal Asad: "Modernity is a project—or rather, a series of interlinked
  projects—that certain people in power seek to achieve." (p. 18, folio 7) Following Timothy
  Mitchell, the author charts "the local articulation and dissemination of modernity" (p. 18,
  folio 7). Quoting Katherine Ewing: "modernity itself is not a single force but rather the temporary
  conjunction of practices and ideologies that have diverse sources and divergent trajectories."
  (p. 20, folio 9)
- **Why the author uses the word at all:** "Nevertheless, I employ the term because the Chishti
  Sabiris I study do." (p. 18, folio 7) Disciples' own usage is "a selective and utilitarian
  bricolage" (p. 19, folio 8).
- **Disciples' selective rejection:** "Chishti Sabiris utterly reject any attempt to defend or
  promote secularism", defining it as "the removal of religion from the public sphere"; this
  "distinguishes them (and Pakistanis generally) from their Muslim counterparts in India who—amid a
  vast Hindu majority—equate secular politics with the defense of religious pluralism and the
  protection of religious minorities" (p. 19, folio 8).
- **Din and dunya as the disciples' own frame:** "they assert that the real struggle—the true
  jihad—is the age-old dilemma of balancing religion (din) with worldly life (dunya)" (p. 19,
  folio 8). Compared to Jonah Blank's Daudi Bohra study (pp. 19–20, folios 8–9).
- **The book's central thesis term:** Chishti Sabiris "have thus shaped their own alternative
  modernity —an identity that is simultaneously Muslim, mystic, and modern." (p. 20, folio 9)
- **Reflexivity and the limits of the outsider:** "I acknowledge my own positioning as a white,
  middle-class, American male of Christian background." (p. 22, folio 11) "As an academic outsider,
  I claim no direct knowledge of the inner states and experiences that are the essence and goal—the
  raison d'etre—of Sufi practice." (p. 23, folio 12) "while it is not a hagiography, it is narrated
  with humility, gratitude, and profound respect." (p. 23, folio 12)
- **The title explained:** the order's narrative "is unbound from the prevailing story of the nation
  and its religious roots", "increasingly unbound from its historical moorings in South Asia", and
  "contemporary Chishti Sabiris have effectively unbound the trajectory of their own tradition"
  (p. 24, folio 13). Chishti Sabiris "champion Pakistan as the teleological culmination of Islamic
  history" (p. 24, folio 13).
- **Critique of the field, via Carl Ernst:** the term "Sufism" is "itself an invention of late
  eighteenth-century European Orientalist scholarship", serving as a catch-all "for anyone connected
  to the texts, saints, shrines, orders, and practices broadly associated with Islamic spirituality."
  (p. 24, folio 13) Sufism is typically cast as one of three things: "an abstract mystical philosophy
  inscribed in 'classical' texts; a thinly veiled political ideology; or an ossified relic, a once
  dynamic tradition now devolved into popular tomb cults." (p. 24, folio 13)
- **Scholars and fundamentalists converge:** Ernst, quoted, notes that Euro-American scholars and
  Muslim fundamentalists "share (for somewhat different reasons) a 'Golden Age' view of history,
  which lauds safely dead 'classical' Sufis while scorning more recent examples of the tradition."
  (p. 24, folio 13)
- **Disciplinary split diagnosed:** Islamicists "tend to focus on the literary texts of select
  premodern Sufi masters", while anthropologists "focus instead on the public negotiation of Sufi
  power and authority" and "most anthropological studies of South Asian Sufism concentrate on
  'objectively' visible phenomena such as saintly charisma and the popular ritual practices at Sufi
  shrines." (p. 25, folio 14)
- **The methodological claim:** "This book argues that Sufism (like the broader category of 'Islam')
  is best understood as a verb rather than a noun." (p. 25, folio 14) "Furthermore, there is no
  Sufism without Sufis." (p. 25, folio 14) Catherine Bell is quoted on texts and rites as "practices,
  not social developments of a fixed nature and significance" (p. 25, folio 14).
- **Named predecessors and the author's criticism of each** (p. 25, folio 14): Katherine Ewing,
  *Arguing Sainthood* — "provides unique psychological insights into contemporary Pakistani Sufism …
  though her ethnography gives less attention to Sufi ritual practices"; Pnina Werbner's study of "a
  Pakistani Naqshbandi order with a growing transnational reach in England" — "examines a range of
  public rituals, but glosses over the essential core of Sufi practice: the master-disciple teaching
  relationship"; Arthur Buehler on "the Naqshbandi Sufi order in South Asia" — "focuses largely on
  premodern texts and lacks a nuanced theoretical framework"; and Carl W. Ernst and Bruce B.
  Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love: The Chishti Order in South Asia and Beyond* — "the most relevant
  contribution", "an unparalleled account of the Chishti order, both past and present", but it
  "focuses predominantly on premodern shaykhs within the Chishti Nizami lineage, with less attention
  to the Sabiri subbranch."
- **Scope disclaimer:** "This book is not a comprehensive history of postcolonial Pakistan. Nor is
  it an exhaustive account of either Indo-Muslim culture or Sufi tradition." (p. 26, folio 15)
