## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **lal-shahbaz-qalandar** (the book calls him **"Lal"**, and once "Lal [the saint]"): **the chunk
  never prints his full name.** He is "the saint" throughout, and "Lal" in eleven places. See
  Doubts — the identification rests on the name "Lal" plus the Sehwan setting plus "the shrine of
  Lal", all of which are in the chunk; **nothing is imported from outside it.**
  - **His shrine is named and its governance is contrasted with Bodlo's**: the lodge at Bodlo "is
    also prominent because it has a prominent shrine attached to it, though, **unlike the shrine of
    Lal, this one is privately run**" (p. 128, folio 111). This is the chunk's only statement about
    how Lal's own shrine is run, and it is made by contrast — **the chunk does not say who runs
    Lal's shrine**, only that Bodlo's is not run that way; at Bodlo "It is these fakirs, **and not
    state officials**, who perform ritual services … and manage its everyday routines and
    revenues" (p. 128, folio 111). **(not stated)** which body administers Lal's shrine.
  - **Pilgrims are required to visit Bodlo before Lal.** "'Whoever comes to me must first go to
    Bodlo,' Lal is believed to have said, or so goes the saying in Sehwan" (p. 127, folio 110) —
    the book attributes this to popular saying, not to a text. "Most pilgrims therefore make their
    first offerings at Bodlo, making the otherwise small shrine a destination of great importance
    in Sehwan" (p. 127, folio 110). The custom is enacted in Murad's own arrival story: "just when
    he was about to enter **the shrine of Lal**, an old bearded fellow had directed him to this
    place, instructing him on the custom that one ought to make the first offerings to Bodlo"
    (p. 132, folio 115).
  - **A system of fourteen lodges is attached to him.** Bodlo's lodge "is considered foundational
    to the establishment of a system of **fourteen kafis in Sehwan**, prominent lodges associated
    with Lal himself" (pp. 127–128, folios 110–111). These are "fraternities whose histories are
    tied to **Lal's earliest disciples**. Many of these lodges are in fact named after such figures
    and run by local elite, most of whom also trace their lineage to contemporaries of the saint"
    (p. 126, folio 109). **The chunk names none of the fourteen except Bodlo's**, and the count of
    lesser lodges is expressly unknown: "The exact number of secondary fakir lodges, however, is
    hard to determine in Sehwan" (p. 128, folio 111).
  - **His celibacy is the model the whole chapter turns on.** "Following Lal's example, Bodlo too
    remained unattached to women, foregoing relations of family and matters of the household"
    (p. 127, folio 110). Dost states it of the saint directly: "Because he [the saint] did not
    marry either, he had cast it [his loins] in iron" (p. 139, folio 122).
  - **A fakir can be said to marry him.** Murad "had in fact quite literally spoken of the
    possibility of contracting a marriage with Lal, describing it as the distinguishing character
    that sets apart formally initiated fakirs of a lodge from ordinary ones that hang out on their
    own at shrines: '[Those] fakirs only have a fling; they do not have a ladder, because a nikah
    [Muslim marriage] is not contracted. **When Lal [the saint] contracts a nikah with someone,
    that someone is then his wife**'" (p. 138, folio 121).
  - **He is one half of the chunk's central devotional image.** "Even in popular visual
    representations, Bodlo was inseparable from Lal. In a widely circulated image, the duo is
    depicted in a loving embrace … Bodlo's head rests on the knees of his master, while the saint
    is shown returning his gesture by lowering his head" (p. 127, folio 110). Kasmani ties this to
    the same "photo template that had irked my mother, an episode I described in the opening pages
    of this book" (p. 127, folio 110) — **that episode is not in this chunk.**
  - **He is credited with one karamat here** (the inverted fortress) — see Legends below.
  - **No date, no death year, no silsila, no birthplace and no alternative name for him appears
    anywhere in these eighteen pages: (not stated)** on every one of those. Nothing of the kind is
    supplied here.

- **bibi-pak-daman** (the book prints **"Bibiyan-pak-daman"**): named once, as the place Murad was
  reported to have gone after leaving Sehwan. "Or that he was currently based at **Bibiyan-pak-
  daman, a shrine in Lahore dedicated to women saints and particularly revered by the Shiʿa**"
  (p. 125, folio 108). That single sentence is the whole of the evidence — see Doubts, both for the
  spelling and for the thinness.
  - The report is **hearsay carried at one remove and expressly uncertain**: it is introduced with

    really explained why. He had left us wondering, as my notes from the day confirm" (p. 125,
    folio 108). The chunk **never confirms** that Murad was at this shrine; a later, firmer report
    places him only "in Lahore" (p. 136, folio 119).
  - **The chunk gives no number of saints buried there, no names, no dates, no custodian and no
    practices at this shrine: (not stated)** on all of them. What it gives is exactly three
    attributes — in Lahore, dedicated to women saints, particularly revered by the Shiʿa.

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **A privately run shrine beside a state-run one.** "The one at Bodlo is also prominent because it
  has a prominent shrine attached to it, though, unlike the shrine of Lal, this one is privately
  run" (p. 128, folio 111). "Both lodge and shrine are headed by a **single custodian figure** who
  is also the patron and spiritual master of all fakirs residing at the lodge. It is these fakirs,
  and not state officials, who perform ritual services at Bodlo's shrine and **manage its everyday
  routines and revenues**" (p. 128, folio 111). **No Auqaf department, no state body and no
  sajjada-nashin term is used anywhere in this chunk: (not stated).**
- **A headcount, attributed to another scholar.** The lodge "is the largest in Sehwan, which,
  according to one estimate, had **eighty initiates, at least twenty of whom were long-term or
  permanent residents** (Ortis 2020, 316)" (p. 128, folio 111). **Both numerals are clean; the
  estimate is Ortis's, not Kasmani's, and the book marks it as an estimate.**
- **Fourteen kafis, and an uncountable tail.** A "system of **fourteen kafis** in Sehwan, prominent
  lodges associated with Lal himself" (pp. 127–128, folios 110–111); but "The exact number of
  **secondary** fakir lodges … is hard to determine in Sehwan" (p. 128, folio 111).
- **The physical complex, itemised.** "a growing complex that includes several secondary units
  spread around the fabled hill. These include **a community kitchen, a number of fireplaces
  associated with fakir gathering (machh), minor shrines and pavilions, a section of the custodian's
  old family home, a rest house for visiting pilgrims, and an underused mosque as well as a stable
  for horses**" (p. 129, folio 112). **No building dates, no architect and no cost: (not stated)** —
  the complex is described only as "growing".
- **The custodian owns the bazaar that funds the lodge.** "Though supplementary to the lodge's main
  purpose, **most shops in the bazaar leading to the shrine are in fact properties of the
  custodian**, which confirm his growing influence in this part of town but also **provide the
  economic means to run the lodge**" (p. 129, folio 112). This is the chunk's single clearest
  statement of a shrine economy.
- **Satellite dwellings as an outreach and recruitment network.** "An entire network of **satellite
  fakir dwellings** can be found beyond Sehwan. These are reclusive settings established on the
  edges of towns and outside villages by journeying fakirs of the lodge, whose companies inspire a
  young and male following on the way. Many of these men arrive in Sehwan in pursuit of the saint or
  his fakirs" (p. 129, folio 112).
- **Daily dhamal facing the tomb.** "The overlapping spheres of the shrine and the kafi are made
  manifest **every evening** when the lodge's fakirs perform the ritual of **dhamal facing the tomb
  of Bodlo**" (p. 128, folio 111); the figure caption reads "Fakirs during dhamal in the forecourt
  of Bodlo's shrine, **2011**" (p. 128, folio 111). Dhamal is glossed once as "the drumming ritual
  (dhamal)" (p. 132, folio 115).
- **The dachi round: a nightly food-and-milk collection through the town.** "Every evening, right
  after the drumming ritual (dhamal) was over at the shrine of Bodlo, he would perform the
  traditional role of **Bodlo's she-camel (dachi)**. Ringing a large bell in one hand, which was
  hung around his neck, he would do the rounds of Sehwan for about **forty-five minutes**. Barefoot
  and completely mute, head and eyes lowered, his task as a dachi was to **walk the streets of
  Sehwan, collecting milk in a bucket and food in a big pouch**, carried on the shoulder and across
  the chest. The physically demanding nature of this daily performance brought respect among
  onlookers as well as the fakirs of the lodge" (p. 132, folio 115).
- **The duty roster.** "Fakirs at the lodge are assigned different duties like **the safekeeping of
  visitors' shoes at the entrance of the shrine, the preparation of day meals, and the daily
  collection of food donations from the various quarters of the city in the evenings**" (p. 131,
  folio 114). "Most duties are rotated and are held by different fakirs for different periods of
  time" (p. 131, folio 114). Murad moved from dachi to shoe-collector: "He was now assigned the task
  of **collecting shoes at the shrine gates**" (p. 132, folio 115), and for a time was "caretaker of
  Nilam, the lodge's resident horse" (p. 133, folio 116).
- **Unwaged dependent labour with a stipend.** "Fakirs also serve as the **labor force of the
  custodian**; some may **work his lands**, and others might **help construct new units of the
  lodge**. Fakirs of a lodge are **not employed** but are dependent on their **sayyid masters** for
  their subsistence; they receive a **daily stipend**" (p. 131, folio 114). **The stipend's amount
  is (not stated).**
- **Patronage as the organising principle, and submission as its term.** "A key organizing principle
  of kafis in Sehwan is that they **tie fakirs with their patrons in intimate bonds of dependence**.
  This is confirmed in rites and rituals through which fakirs are initiated. **The master, most
  often of sayyid descent**, acts as their guarantor and protector, meaning that fakirs must profess
  **full submission to his will and authority**" (p. 131, folio 114). Fakirs "can also depend on the
  material generosity of their masters, especially when faced with misfortune and illnesses"
  (p. 131, folio 114). The arrangement is glossed earlier as "(unwritten) contracts of patronage and
  structures of dependence" (p. 126, folio 109).
- **Excommunication as a stated sanction.** "In the event of a serious dispute, **the master reserves
  the right to revoke fakir privileges and provisions. Excommunication threatens a fakir's removal
  from chains of saintly access as well as from the larger social network of fakirs.** Much of this
  is also what would have been at stake in Murad's case" (p. 131, folio 114).
- **Admission, referee, initiation — a three-stage entry.** "Fakir admission in the lodge was …
  conditional on **the custodian's approval** and involved **rites and ceremonies of formal
  initiation**. New fakirs were regularly sought and recruited. Aspiring fakirs that arrive at the
  lodge first draw the attention of a fakir by offering their services and by being present in fakir
  company. In most cases, **an aspiring fakir must find a fakir referee** in order to be considered
  for initiation into the lodge. **Admission doesn't equal initiation**" (p. 130, folio 113). Once
  admitted, "fakirs are mentored by senior members and are expected to participate in the collective
  upkeep and maintenance of the lodge" (p. 131, folio 114). Initiation happens at "an **annual
  ceremony**" (p. 125, folio 108).
- **Initiation as social death and renaming.** "Initiation marks an important transition **from the
  death of a social subject to the birth of a new talib**, the one who is prepared to seek and
  desire. **The new fakir receives a wardi and with it he is given a new name**" (p. 133, folio 116).
  The self-designation is explained: "fakirs mostly refer to themselves as **talibs** — the Urdu word
  (from Arabic) means **seeker (of knowledge)** but also denotes **craving and yearning**" (p. 129,
  folio 112).
- **The wardi and the fakir's movable estate.** "he had quietly surrendered his **wardi** (lit.
  uniform; fakir costume)", "the long **red tunics** characteristic of fakirs of this particular
  lodge" (p. 125, folio 108). The category is wider than the shirt: "what is referred to as wardi
  here is not restricted to the long red shirt … there were other items like **scarf, rings,
  bracelets, necklaces, and earrings** … **Fakir objects customarily include items like the begging
  bowl and the baton**, material objects and wearables that a fakir possesses" (p. 137, folio 120).
  The lodge's word for the set is "**hal-vaslah** [fakir accessories and objects]" (p. 136, folio
  119).
- **The wardi read as an office, revocable.** Dost: "'when someone commits a mistake, the master,
  according to principle, **takes the wardi back**'" (p. 136, folio 119); he "tried to explain how a
  **policeman on duty has to surrender his gun and his wardi if he is suspended**" (p. 137, folio
  120). Kasmani's gloss: "it was not the first time that fakirs described wardi **in the light of
  duty and privilege**. More important, as per Dost's view, **conferred privileges could also be
  withdrawn**" (p. 137, folio 120).
- **Acquired prowess versus bestowed gift — a stated doctrinal pair.** "Fakir spiritual prowess had
  to be **acquired through practice and action (ʿamal)** and **not bestowed as an irrevocable gift
  (ʿata) of the sayyid pir**" (p. 137, folio 120).
- **Institutional exclusion of women and transgender fakirs, with one documented exception.**
  "Lodges such as Bodlo-kafi are **male-only fakir institutions in Sehwan**" (p. 126, folio 109);
  "affective formations at the lodge are premised on **the exclusion of women and transgender
  fakirs**" (p. 129, folio 112). But: "there was a brief period of time when **two female-presenting
  fakirs were residing at Bodlo**. Their status, as fakirs themselves tried to explain to me, was
  unclear at best, and **their participation did not extend to all aspects of the lodge's daily
  life. They were also housed in a private section of the lodge.** Their exceptional status was
  proof that **maleness was in fact the norm at the lodge if not also a prerequisite for full
  membership**" (p. 130, folio 113). **Neither woman is named and no dates are given for the
  period: (not stated).** The male protagonists had access to patronage structures "that other
  protagonists of this book have not had access to, **mostly due to their genders**" (p. 126, folio
  109).
- **Celibacy is an ethos, not a rule of residence.** "**Celibacy wasn't a condition for residing at
  the lodge, though it was central to its ethos**" (p. 127, folio 110). The chunk's three vernacular
  terms for it: **jatti-satti**, "a term associated with seven celibate saints of Sehwan" (p. 127,
  folio 110) and used "to refer to a person's nonsexual condition as well as unmarried status"
  (p. 135, folio 118); **lung-langot**, "a garment that covers the loins", used "to refer to
  maintaining sexual abstinence and celibacy among fakirs" (p. 134, folio 117); and **lung-band**,
  "literally the sealing of a man's loins, which suggests a refusal of all forms of sexual pleasure"
  (p. 135, folio 118).
- **Abstinence as a term-limited vow.** "**Vows of abstinence … were not always lifelong commitments
  and could also be taken for limited periods of one's life**" (p. 135, folio 118) — Dost "had …
  expressed a desire to marry, that is, once the long fakir process was complete" (p. 135, folio
  118).
- **Women fakirs' abstinence framed as purity within marriage.** "In the case of women fakirs, such
  issues were brought up in their discussion of maintaining **purity and ritual cleanliness**. For
  example, speaking of **discontinuing their sexual relations with their husbands**, women would try
  to establish that **their marital ties did not correlate to those of a husband and wife**"
  (pp. 134–135, folios 117–118).
- **Nightly rhythm of the shrine.** "Things would usually get calmer at the lodge after midnight;
  **the doors of the shrine were closed** and fakirs had the chance to chill **until duties resumed
  in the early hours of the morning**" (p. 139, folio 122).
- **Muharram procession.** The lodge keeps a horse "reserved for **annual processions during the
  commemoration of Muharram**" (p. 133, folio 116).
- **Shrine consultation as the occasion of the scandal.** "She was a young pilgrim visiting from
  Lahore and **Murad a fakir she had consulted at the shrine of Bodlo**. They had exchanged numbers
  and had since been in contact" (p. 125, folio 108). Murad "had contracted a **civil marriage**
  with a young woman of his choice" (p. 125, folio 108); a pilgrim's version was that he married "to
  a girl half his age" (p. 124, folio not stated).
- **The mobile phone as devotional infrastructure's rival.** Kasmani retrospectively reads "the
  countless occasions during my previous fieldwork when Murad would be occupied with his mobile
  phone, chatting for hours at a stretch, especially late at night" (p. 125, folio 108); "on this
  particular **summer night in 2012**, Murad had busied himself on the phone. In hindsight, one

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Bodlo's dismemberment and Lal's overturning of the fortress**, given by the book as legend and
  as the town's topographic story: "Its topography is etched with a spectacular account of Bodlo's
  death. **Legend has it** that his body was **cut into pieces by the ruler of the time and thrown
  all across the neighborhood**. Also, **a furious Lal, on learning what had been done to his
  beloved, turned the fortress upside down**" (pp. 128–129, folios 111–112). **The ruler is not
  named, no date is given, and the fortress is not named beyond its nickname: (not stated)** on all
  three. The landscape is read as the residue: "Today, right under the shadow of the fabled
  'inverted fortress' (ulta qila), the sprawl of Bodlo's shrine and lodge is a **material reminder
  of his body-in-pieces**" (p. 129, folio 112).
- **The saint's injunction to visit Bodlo first**, given as a saying: "'Whoever comes to me must
  first go to Bodlo,' **Lal is believed to have said, or so goes the saying in Sehwan**" (p. 127,
  folio 110). **The book attributes it to belief and local saying, not to any text or authority.**
- **The loving embrace of Lal and Bodlo** as a circulating devotional image (p. 127, folio 110) —
  see under the archive row above.
- **The saint's presence withheld, in an initiate's own words** — the chunk's fullest statement of
  what a karamat would be and the admission that it has not come: "he is not giving his **qurb**,
  his **didar** [audience, viewing], he is not giving his **sign**, he is **not meeting me in a
  dream** so that life may have an assurance … For so long as his qurb is not there, his didar, or
  his **miracles [karamat]**, or some progress, there cannot be didar, there cannot be **certainty
  [yaqin]**" (p. 140, folio 123). And: "**But we do not have his [the saint's] spirituality!**"
  (p. 139, folio 122).
- **Seeing the saint, and its bodily sign**, in Dost's words: "The ones who have seen the saint, who
  have received his audience [didar], **they remain straight** [seedhay; emphasis added]. The ones
  that have seen the saint in a dream are celibate [jatti-satti] fakirs. **If they sleep [soye] with
  a woman they will not be able to sleep with the saint [murshid]**; they will not be able to enjoy
  the comfort of the saint. Because the **Ism-e aʿzam** [the greatest but secret name of Allah] is
  such that **it is lost by being in bed with a woman**… The ones who have met the saint or are
  joined with him [mile huey] lead such lives. **From them emits the fragrance of the saint**"
  (pp. 138–139, folios 121–122). The "[emphasis added]" is Kasmani's own.
- **Loss of occult power as the sanction for sex.** "a fakir who decides to partake in the intimacy
  of a woman is **bound to lose his grasp of the hidden aspects of the world or powers of the
  unrevealed (ghaybi taqat)**" (p. 135, folio 118) — reported as **Dost's** position, which "his
  peers … did not share" (p. 136, folio 119).
- **Murad's own arrival directed by a stranger**: "just when he was about to enter the shrine of
  Lal, **an old bearded fellow had directed him to this place**, instructing him on the custom that
  one ought to make the first offerings to Bodlo" (p. 132, folio 115). **The book does not gloss the
  old man as a saint or an apparition**; it is reported flat, as Murad's telling, and he "duly
  added, 'I had not come to go back — this much I knew!'"

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The chapter's two stated objects.** "A first and overarching interest is to illustrate **how
  fakir bonds and fraternities are established within the homosocial framework of a lodge** … The
  other object of this chapter is **Murad's scandal**. In tracing its impact on the lodge, I point
  to **linkages between sexuality and sacrality, love and marriage in fakir arguments**, or the ways
  in which ideas around sexual intimacy inflect fakir narratives on intimacy with saints" (p. 126,
  folio 109).
- **The chapter's stated insight, and its reversal of the book's own thesis.** "**unstraight
  affordances are queer affects that not only support otherwise ways of being in the world but can
  equally work against intimacy**, especially when the pursuit of saintly objects is contracted on
  pacts of submission and the abandonment of rival intimacies. No less interesting is the insight
  that **failures of saintly intimacy accompany one's return to economies of the heterosexual
  family**" (p. 126, folio 109). Restated at the section's close: "Insofar as Murad's love for a
  young woman also upset his place in the homosociality of the lodge, his decision to marry, to stay
  with the metaphor of fakir lines, **afforded a restraightening of his social horizons**" (p. 127,
  folio 110); and again at p. 138, folio 121: "the abandonment of such intimacies, as we find in
  Murad's case, **constitutes at the same time a return to straight paths, projects, and economies
  of the social**".
- **Marriage as infidelity to the saint, not merely as broken celibacy.** "Seen in this light,
  Murad's decision to marry is **more than a transgression of ideals of celibacy. It involves
  infidelity, a shift in his object of desire, a taking up of intimacy with someone other than the
  saint.** It is a decision that puts his love of the woman above his love for and fidelity to the
  saint" (p. 138, folio 121).
- **Male-male desire as the adhesive of communal formation**, borrowed from Ali A. Mian: it is
  "neither female-male nor female-female desire, but **male-male desire that is channeled into
  communal formation**" (2019, 143), and "**despite the strong prohibition on anal intercourse,
  same-sex desire remains unencumbered** and serves rather as **the affective adhesive that pulls
  men together** through intimate socialization and strengthens devotion to the divine" (p. 129,
  folio 112). Kasmani applies it: "**Such male-to-male axis of desire in Sehwan is enshrined in the
  love between Lal and Bodlo** and lived through fakir homosocial bonds at the lodge" (p. 129, folio
  112).
- **The lodge as a different kind of line from the book's earlier cases.** "**Desiring-seeking at
  the Bodlo-kafi is not a self-brokered line** exclusively involving dreams, visions, or a
  cohabiting spirit like those of Baba, Amma, and Zaheda of the previous chapters but takes place
  **in the company of men and primarily through the intercession of a living spiritual master**"
  (p. 129, folio 112).
- **The celibacy question is declared unsettled, and the author says so twice.** "The question of
  celibacy and sexual abstinence among fakirs in Sehwan is **a hard one to settle**, especially
  given the **history and prevalence of nonnormative sexual behavior among qalandars and be-shar
  mystics or Sufis-without-shariah**" (p. 134, folio 117). "**I wasn't quite able to establish**
  whether ideas of abstinence in fakir accounts referred exclusively to marital and reproductive
  sex, or if they indeed excluded nonnormative forms of sexual practice … Part of the difficulty
  also stemmed from the fact that **most fakirs in Sehwan did not directly engage with questions of
  sexual practice**" (p. 134, folio 117). "**When speaking of it, fakirs regardless of gender often
  relied on veiled metaphors and implicit references**" (p. 135, folio 118).
- **Abstinence from sex ≠ abstinence from pleasure, historically.** "Historical accounts suggest
  that **abstinence from sex was not always abstinence from sexual pleasure** or that notions of
  celibacy were in some contexts **centered around a refusal to engage in reproductive economies of
  family and the society**" (p. 134, folio 117). Ridgeon is cited for the qalandar literature's
  silence: "**even though the literature on qalandars does not discuss celibacy thoroughly, it
  specifies requirements to abandon lust and sexual gratification**. Such absence, he cautions,
  should not mean that qalandars enjoyed free license to engage in sexual acts", with the Haydari
  case: "**the use of iron implements around the genitals** . . . in addition to the general
  lifestyle of poverty, mendicancy and otherworldliness, **militated against marriage and sexual
  relations**" (Ridgeon 2010, 248–49) (p. 134, folio 117).
- **The binary Kasmani names and rejects.** Dost held that "the realm of the celibate fakir was the
  masculine realm … **It was superior to its counterpart, the worldly realm, defined by its feminine
  character**", so Murad "**had bargained for the feminine and therefore lesser world**" (p. 135,
  folio 118). Kasmani's verdict: "**In equating the feminine with the sexual, he was reaffirming an
  easy binary**, which holds that spirituality, intellect, and rationality are attributes of
  maleness and men, or that **women are enormous threats to men's spiritual progress**. By such logic
  and perhaps reaffirmed by the homosocial community of fakirs at the lodge, **sacrality is
  incompatible with (hetero)sexuality**" (pp. 135–136, folios 118–119).
- **The counter-tradition, via Saʿdiyya Shaikh on Ibn ʿArabi.** "**Though prevailing, such views have
  been neither unanimous nor uncontested in Sufi con/texts.**" Shaikh "**takes a radical position**"
  in identifying "a complete interpenetration of spirituality and sexuality" in Ibn ʿArabi (2012,
  186), and figures how "**love between men and women is an inherent aspect of loving God, bringing
  human beings closer to God**" (187). "**Shaikh isn't referring to metaphorical love or reading
  desire in an abstract sense.**" She identifies "a celebration and affirmation of the body and
  sexuality as intrinsic components of human spirituality", to the extent that "**sex provides the
  mystic with the opportunity for one of the most comprehensive experiences of servanthood and
  receptivity**" (189) (p. 136, folio 119). Kasmani's own summation: "**Sexual intimacy thus
  conceived and experienced is not a hindrance but a form of affective knowing**, a self-disclosing
  experience comparable to total annihilation in the Truth", with Mian for "an incredibly erotic
  movement" (2019, 135) (p. 136, folio 119).
- **The fakirs' argument is recorded as unresolved, twice.** "**The debate remained unresolved**"
  (field diary, p. 137, folio 120); "the question whether **Murad left his wardi or was asked to
  surrender it is unsettled here at the kafi**" (pp. 136–137, folios 119–120); "**Dost's reading of
  Murad's situation was neither as generous nor as reconciliatory. His peers, however, did not share
  his uncompromising views**" (p. 136, folio 119); "**Other fakirs remained convinced of Murad's
  return to favor as well as into the folds of the kafi**" (p. 138, folio 121).
- **What the objects argument was actually about.** "the question fakirs were arguing over that
  evening was **whether or not minus his fakir objects Murad was indeed the same fakir**. Also, the
  debate among the junior fakirs rested primarily on **their positions regarding the relationship of
  marriage with fakir life**" (p. 137, folio 120).
- **The fakir as one who has the attributes of the dead.** Murad: "'Somebody who simply wears a red
  wardi [costume or uniform], performs service, **while also raising children, cannot be a fakir!**'"
  (p. 134, folio 117); "'**The fakir is not dead; he has the attributes of the dead.**'" (p. 134,
  folio 117). Kasmani: "**The fakir garment, which Murad often described as shroud-like, was both
  performance and reminder that the fakir is comparable to the dead. Though not being dead per se,
  the fakir is susceptible to sexual desire**, a thing that would come to risk Murad's own fakir
  credentials" (p. 134, folio 117).
- **The break with the past as a fakir institution, not an idiosyncrasy.** "**A break with the past,
  while severe in Murad's case, is not unique to his story. Fakirs are often able to find new
  beginnings in saintly pursuits. The initiation of fakirs into a lodge involves and celebrates such
  discontinuities through ceremony**" (p. 133, folio 116).
- **Dost's hardening is the chapter's measured change.** "**In returning to this conversation a year
  later, I was looking for the dynamism that was eventually lost to certitude in the aftermath of
  Murad's scandal. The malleability of his earlier views stood in sharp contrast to the argument he
  had with his peers just the other night.** Now an initiated fakir at the lodge, Dost had come to
  view his fakir path in **more certain and fixed terms**" (p. 140, folio 123).
- **Chapter three's closing thesis, carried in on p. 123.** Intimacy's public forms: to "establish
  one's closeness with saintly objects and locations as well as to **mandate one's being out of step
  with straight rhythms of the social**. **Failures of intimacy or worsening saintly relations,
  inwardly tormenting as these are, take very public forms**" (p. 123, folio 106). On Zaheda:
  "**there were no lines sanctioned for her as a woman that involved destinations other than the
  home and saints' places**" (p. 123, folio 106); "**to have no lines to follow was to be going
  nowhere, and to be without lines was to be without place**" (p. 123, folio 106); "**Zaheda's
  pursuit of intimacy … would also become the raison d'être of this work**" (p. 123, folio 106).
  "Whether she would in fact **return to the folds of domestic life** is a question that **remains
  unanswered**" (p. 123, folio 106). And on method: "**Orientations of the researcher and the
  researched were indeed distinct**, yet lines of work and our labors toward making those lines
  work, of research and of fakir being, **intersected in these years as much as they parted or ran
  parallel** … though **it is Zaheda who has shown the path**" (p. 123, folio 106).

  "Usman Marwandi" appear nowhere in this chunk.** The mapping rests on three things all internal to
