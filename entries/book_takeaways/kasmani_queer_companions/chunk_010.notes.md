## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The Auqaf statute named, with its year.** "This refers to the West Pakistan Waqf Properties
  Ordinance (1959). For more on auqaf in Pakistan, see Ibad (2019), Philippon (2016), Strothmann
  (2016), Malik (1990). For crossovers of Sufi pilgrimage, shrine markets, and political economy in
  Sehwan, see Delage (2018)." (p. 183, folio 166, intro n. 17)
- **What the Auqaf department says it does.** "According to the department's website, its aims and
  objectives include the management of shrines and mosques, providing pilgrim facilities like
  `com-munity[text-layer?]` [community] kitchens and rest houses. For more on the history and
  development of the Department of Auqaf, see Ibad (2019)." (pp. 188–189, folios 171–172, ch. 1
  n. 25)
- **The revenue mechanism, stated as a claim of the state's.** "The state collects pilgrim donations
  offered to saints at shrines and also generates revenue from waqf properties through work
  contracts, rents, and leases, which it claims to repurpose for the maintenance and reconstruction
  of old shrines and other religious endowments. For a financial review of Auqaf, its budgets and
  finances, see Ibad (2019, chapter 4)." (p. 189, folio 172, ch. 1 n. 26) Note the hedge: "which it
  **claims** to repurpose".
- **Where the money physically goes at Sehwan.** "Monetary offerings to the saint are either collected
  in donation boxes or distributed among shrine workers on duty." (p. 190, folio 173, ch. 2 n. 11)
- **Custodians as shrine-adjacent landlords.** "a quick survey of the markets surrounding the shrine
  revealed that a large number of rented shops in the market adjacent to the shrine are owned by
  various members of the custodian families." (p. 190, folio 173, ch. 2 n. 15)
- **The kafi system, counted.** "Rarely do nonsayyid figures feature in this system. If so, they are
  either nominated by or closely aligned with custodian families. **Only one in a system of fourteen
  kafis of Sehwan is owned and headed by a nonsayyid `sajjadah-­nashin`.**" (p. 191, folio 174, ch. 2
  n. 16)
- **Contested inheritance of the shrine.** "Many fakirs in Sehwan claim that the shrine's original
  heirs were in fact ascetic followers of the saint." (p. 190, folio 173, ch. 2 n. 2) "A former
  association with the shrine and its revenues explains their material `well-­being` and upward social
  circumstances in the present." (p. 190, folio 173, ch. 2 n. 4) "According to Boivin, the rift
  between the two families dates back to the British period (Michel Boivin, email message to author,
  September 1, 2014). For more management of the saint's spiritual authority, see Boivin (2003)."
  (p. 190, folio 173, ch. 2 n. 5) **The two families are not named in the note: (not stated).**
- **A lodge that beat the state in court.** "Its present-day custodians were able to fight a case
  against the state in the courts back in the 1960s, successfully arguing that the shrine of Bodlo
  was in fact a legitimate family inheritance." (p. 192, folio 175, ch. 4 n. 4)
- **Sayyid privilege.** "As descendants of the Prophet, sayyids enjoy historical, material, and
  spiritual privilege unlike other spiritual figures in Sehwan; see chapter 2." (pp. 183–184, folios
  166–167, intro n. 18)
- **Ritual calendar and its erosion, dated.** "The ritual on Friday morning is more elaborate and
  considered auspicious, given the religious significance of the day." (p. 190, folio 173, ch. 2 n. 6)
  "While the ceremony and the participation of kafis remained consistent, the precise manner of
  conducting it cannot be said to be identical… certain services were discontinued; for instance, the
  cleaning and lighting of five lamps (chiraghi) was no longer observable by 2013. During my last
  visit in 2018, the ritual had become a completely private affair." (p. 190, folio 173, ch. 2 n. 10)
- **Women excluded from the ghusl.** "The ghusl or bathing of the saint is considered an intimate

  ch. 2 n. 8)

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The camel that fetched the lodge's food**, given as local legend: "This practice, fakirs of the
  lodge explained, goes back to the time of Lal. According to local legend, Bodlo's `she-­camel`, a
  gift from Lal, would itself leave the lodge each evening to collect food for its fakirs." (p. 193,
  folio 176, ch. 4 n. 13)
- **The killing of the camel and the seal**, also local legend: "Fakirs of Bodlo are exempt from this
  practice. As per local legend, since fakirs of the other lodges had colluded with each other and
  killed Bodlo's camel, Lal ordered that they henceforth be marked with a seal to distinguish them
  from fakirs of Bodlo." (p. 194, folio 177, ch. 4 n. 35) The legend is deferred to the body: "I
  return to the legend in chapter 5 as part of how Lal is remembered in Sehwan." (p. 193, folio 176,
  ch. 4 n. 6)
- **The meeting of the four at Chothanbi**, "believed to be their place of meeting" (p. 188, folio
  171, ch. 1 n. 17).
- **The bride of Lal**, attributed to "some locals": "Citing the figure of `Lal-­kunwar` or the bride
  of Lal, some locals claim that the Hindu custom of ritual marriage with the saint of Sehwan was part
  of the ceremonies of the annual fair up until the `mid-­twentieth` century (see also Boivin 2012b,
  `123 – 26`)." (p. 191, folio 174, ch. 2 n. 28)
- **Koh-Qaf**, "According to local lore… a mountainous setting of the fairies and demons" (p. 194,
  folio 177, ch. 5 n. 2).
- **Spirits that "meet" rather than "mount"** — the fakir/djinn distinction (p. 190, folio 173, ch. 2
  n. 13), with "On spirits and their mediums, see Saniotis (2004)" (p. 190, folio 173, ch. 2 n. 12)
  and "On women and spirits in South Asia, see Bellamy (2008), Callan (2008), Gold (1988)" (p. 184,
  folio 167, intro n. 21).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The coda's three clusters, named.** "The many elements that make up the greater argument of this
  book on saintly intimacy and futurity can be regathered here as three conceptual clusters, namely,
  saintly governance, intimate corporealities, and worlding with saints." (p. 178, folio 161)
- **Saintly governance.** "One of the contentions this book has advanced is that saintly vitality in
  Pakistan thrives on orders of the state." (p. 178, folio 161) "I have pointed to the often
  inseparable dynamics of governing saints and governing with saints." (p. 178, folio 161) "fakir
  futuring amid impaired conditions or against the expected course of things as well as the crises of
  inheritance that are stirred up as a result of intimacy with saints are not just saintly affects but
  are propelled in part by stately infrastructures of the imaginal." (p. 178, folio 161)
- **The state as a limited liberalizer of shrine space — the chunk's most directly citable claim for
  an archive entry.** "I have further elaborated how the state's interest in saints' places or its
  motivations to govern saints have entailed a limited democratization of shrine space. This is
  particularly salient for women's spiritual careers, which, though doubly impaired, both by
  `patri-lineal[text-layer?]` [patrilineal] transmission of saintly authority and an ascribed
  femaleness to their bodies, are to some extent secured by the state's arbitrating role at public
  shrines." (p. 178, folio 161) "Saintly governance more provocatively suggests that as fakirs come
  close to the saintly in Pakistan, they also draw nearer to the state." (p. 178, folio 161)
- **Intimate corporealities and dis/guise.** "a bodiliness I have described in detail through the
  fakir concept of dis/guise (bhes)" (p. 179, folio 162); "it is a public performance of fakir
  dis/guise that sanctions the body's departures from socially inherited objects and situations"
  (p. 179, folio 162). "Saintly bonds, as I have illustrated, even when secured through rites of
  initiation and afforded by spiritual masters, can come undone or show signs of disaffection,
  especially in the face of rival intimacies and competing objects of desire and devotion." (p. 179,
  folio 162)
- **Worlding with saints.** "Coming close to saints affords fakir individuals an elaborate sense of
  time and place, not necessarily deeper or precise knowledge per se but a heightened awareness, a
  growing conscious by way of feeling intimate." (p. 179, folio 162) "such elaborate knowing by way of
  feeling is tied to fakir reputations as persons who know more or better." (pp. 179–180, folios
  162–163)
- **qurb.** "By expanding on the Urdu term qurb, it has described intimacy with saints as affective
  bonding that blooms in the critical interstice of being near a thing and being nearly that thing,
  suggesting in a way that intimacy hangs on notions of critical distance." (p. 177, folio 160) The
  lexical authority is a note: "See Urdu Lughat, http://www.udb.gov.pk/, accessed March 2021."
  (p. 185, folio 168, intro n. 46)
- **What the book refuses to do.** "This book has not invested itself in reparative or recuperative
  labors like scholarship that seeks to anachronistically restore queerness to `non-­Western` histories
  and global South contexts. Furthermore, it has refused a minoritization of both queer and Pakistan
  in epistemological terms." (p. 180, folio 163) "Queer is neither a figure of identity politics in
  this work nor an exclusive reference to my interlocutors' sexualities." (p. 182, folio not stated,
  intro n. 3) "My use of queer and unstraight, each with its own distinct capacities, is not to
  distinguish between what is properly queer or not, sexual or otherwise." (p. 184, folio 167, intro
  n. 27)
- **The disciplinary indictment.** "Debates in the anthropology of Islam are comfortably oblivious to
  conversations in queer theory. On the other side of this equation is queer studies' blind spot for
  histories and figurations of queerness elsewhere, a geopolitical bias that determines why places
  such as Pakistan and settings like religious shrines do not strike us as sites for queer thinking."
  (p. 180, folio 163)
- **Self-quotation as the book's position.** queerness "is not in any way more here than there, more
  now than before; that it is tied to the logic of cities and secularities in a way that it is
  unmappable in religious or `non-­metropolitan` `life-­worlds`" (Kasmani 2019b, 36) (p. 180, folio
  163).
- **The disagreement with Boivin over what Bodlo's site is.** "Michel Boivin holds the view that the
  prototype of dargah (Sufi shrine) better suits what I have here described as the kafi of Bodlo. I
  consider this site a hybrid category. While the shrine of Bodlo has a central role in the economic
  and community life of its lodge, I take the position that both prototypes of fakir space are equally
  present and observable at the site in question." (pp. 192–193, folios 175–176, ch. 4 n. 5)
- **The disagreement with Shahab Ahmed over Sunni sources.** "The term Shiʿi is so gingerly present in
  the book that it doesn't even appear in the index. He notes that adducing non-Sunni examples is not
  antithetical to the project; however, he explains his deliberate choice of Sunni sources in these
  words: 'I have done this simply for the pragmatic reason that I do not want to facilitate the facile
  objection that I am conceptualizing Islam on the basis of marginal or non-representative phenomena'
  (Ahmed 2016, 104)." (p. 186, folio 169, intro n. 53)
- **The disagreement with the piety turn, via Green.** "Nile Green (2012b, 2) has noted that
  privileging of the mystical neglects the social and overlooks Sufi investment in the tangible realm
  of physicality. For comparable critiques on the piety turn in the anthropology of Islam, see
  Mittermaier (2012), Schielke (2010), Soares and Osella (2009)." (p. 186, folio 169, intro n. 56)
- **A disagreement with his own earlier work.** "Some of my earlier work has engaged her notion of
  becoming, from which I have since turned away (see Kasmani 2016b, 2017b)." (p. 186, folio 169,
  intro n. 52)
- **Conjecture explicitly marked as conjecture.** "While its suffix satti, or seven, refers to the
  number of Sehwani saints in a scheme of celibate mystics, jatti, **if one is allowed a conjecture**,
  must stand for an idea of celibacy, even the condition of being virgin. However, as per my
  observation in Sehwan, the word jatti is never used by itself." (p. 193, folio 176, ch. 4 n. 20)
- **Reflexivity about what the field would not tell him.** "Such veiled disclosures may also have to
  do with the fact that they were speaking to a `cis-­male` researcher. Men and transgender persons
  were equally careful with their references." (p. 193, folio 176, ch. 4 n. 19) "I wondered if I was
  indeed witnessing firsthand the remaking of the Bodlo story or whether this account was targeted at
  more immediate concerns, given the changes at the lodge." (p. 194, folio 177, ch. 4 n. 31) "While
  this was a privately conducted ritual that I did not have direct access to in terms of participation,
  my fakir interlocutors at the lodge described the ceremony to me so that I could record it in my
  field diary." (p. 194, folio 177, ch. 4 n. 34)
- **Pronouns as a method decision.** "In rendering Baba's story in English, I adopt the gender-neutral
  pronouns they, them, their to record Baba's distinct subjectivity. When speaking, Baba
  interchangeably relied on feminine and masculine speech registers." (p. 187, folio 170, ch. 1 n. 4)
- **The "not woman" claim, via Salgado.** "In her research with Buddhist nuns of Sri Lanka, Salgado
  (2013) interprets similar 'not woman' claims as female renunciants' understanding of themselves and
  their roles as defying or going beyond gender. Her point, however, is that in doing so, such women
  refuse to be understood through liberal feminist frameworks." (p. 192, folio 175, ch. 3 n. 5)
- **"Ambiguously Islamic" shrines, a category the archive may want.** "In her book The Powerful
  Ephemeral, Carla Bellamy (2011) describes as 'ambiguously Islamic' those Muslim shrines that have
  complex religious histories and involve an interfaith following." (p. 185, folio 168, intro n. 42)
- **Definitions the book fixes in notes.** "Some scholars prefer faqir (from its Arabic root faqr). My
  interlocutors in Sehwan have additionally defined the term in relation to fikr, meaning thought and
  reflection." (p. 182, folio not stated, intro n. 1) "Futurity is distinct from future (temporality)
  in that it refers to the capacity of a thing to advance and endure in forward time." (p. 183, folio
  166, intro n. 13) "`Ahl-­e Hadis` is a religious reform movement that emerged in northern India in
  the `mid-­nineteenth` century. Most Pakistanis identify with the Hanafi school of jurisprudence while
  maintaining an adherence to the Barelvi or Deobandi movements of religious reform in South Asia."
  (p. 183, folio 166, intro n. 12)
- **Where critique of saint veneration circulates.** "Critiques of saint veneration find ordinary
  circuiting through religious sermons, popular literature, television serials, men's reformist
  gatherings, and women's domestic prayer circles, also via social media platforms (see also Malik
  1998)." (p. 183, folio 166, intro n. 10)

## Doubts

- **Four archive ids and no more.** `lal-shahbaz-qalandar`, `shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya`,
  `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` and `shrine-of-jalaluddin-surkh-posh-bukhari-jalaluddin-bukhari`.
  The last three rest on **a single sentence each**, chapter 1 note 17 (p. 188, folio 171), which
  names them only as the other three of four figures with a shared meeting place, with a death year
  and a town. That is a thin but real evidential base: each is named, dated and located, and the note
  says "Their shrines are spread across southern Punjab in Pakistan". **Nothing beyond that sentence
  and the Multan initiation note (p. 184, folio 167) should be attributed to those three rows from
  this chunk.**
- **Name-spelling divergences between the book and the archive, all of which a consolidator will hit.**
  Book "Bahaʾuddin Zakaria" vs archive "Bahauddin Zakariya"; book "`Faridud-din` Ganjshakr" vs archive
  "Baba Fariduddin Mas'ud Ganjshakar"; book "`Pak-­Pattan`" vs archive "Pakpattan"; book "Jalaluddin
  Surkhposh Bukhari" vs archive "Sayyid Jalaluddin Surkh-Posh Bukhari"; book "Uchh" vs archive "Uch
  Sharif". The death years (1267, 1266, 1291) match the archive's exactly in all three cases, which is
  what makes the identifications safe. **No archive spelling has been imported into the quotations
  above.**
- **Hindu deity names: refused, per the standing convention of earlier chunks, and the candidate rows
  named so the refusal is reversible.** Intro n. 37 (p. 185, folio 168) names **Udero Lal**, **Jhule
  Lal**, **Amar Lal**, **Darya Shah**, **Darya Nath**, **Zinda pir**, **Shaykh Tahir**, **(Khwaja)
  Khizr** and **Varuna** as names under which the Indus was worshipped. If the deity ruling reverses,
  the rows at stake are `shrine-at-odero-lal-udero-lal-teerath-asthan` (whose principal figure cell
  reads "Sheikh Tahir / Udero Lal (Jhulelal)" — **two of the book's names at once**, the strongest
  candidate by far), `jhollay-lal-mandir` ("Jhulelal (Uderolal)"),
  `darya-lal-mandir-darya-lal-sankat-mochan-mandir` ("Jhulelal / Daryalal") and `shri-varun-dev-mandir`
  ("Varuna (Varun Dev)"). **No id assigned here.** Note also that the book gives these as names of a
  river cult, not of a shrine or a person, which is an independent reason for caution.
- **Shiva: refused, same convention, rows named.** Shiva is present four times — the "Shivaite sect
  `Pashupa-tas`" and the toponym variants `Shiv-­asthan` / `Shiv-­astano` (p. 185, folio 168, intro
  n. 38), G. M. Syed tracing shrine ritual "to Shivaite forms" (p. 185, folio 168, intro n. 39), the
  "complex and considered relationship between Sufi and Shivaite in Sehwan" (p. 185, folio 168, intro
  n. 41), and above all "the presence of a Shiva lingam close to the saint's tomb" (p. 189, folio 172,
  ch. 1 n. 34). If the deity ruling reverses, the rows whose principal figure is Shiva are
  `amb-temples-amb-sharif`, `bhagnari-mandir`, `katas-raj-temples`, `shahwala-teja-singh-mandir`,
  `shiv-mandir-chiti-ghati`, `shree-ratneshwar-mahadev-temple-karachi`, `umarkot-amarkot-shiv-mandir`
  and `chandragup-baba-chandragup` ("Shiva (associated)"). **None is assigned. The lingam is at
  Sehwan, which is `lal-shahbaz-qalandar`'s site, so the material is already captured under that row
