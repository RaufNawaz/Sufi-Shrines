- **The qalandar as a historical social institution.** "Qalandars were part of an anarchistic ethos
  specific to cultures of the Persian world, and who resisted the institutionalization and elite
  formations of Sufi brotherhoods of the time. Liberated from social custom and often regarded as
  be-­shar (without shariah/law), a defining characteristic of qalandars, as Ahmet Karamustafa (2006)
  notes, was their 'persistent cultural and social iconoclasticism and unconventionalism.' Such figures
  were notorious for transgressive bodily acts, extreme isolation, perpetual wandering, and an
  ostentatious rejection of social-­religious institutions and authorities." (p. 34, folio 17) Ewing on
  the contemporary type: the prototypical qalandar in urban Pakistan "confronts the proper Muslim" and
  "challenges the naturalness of the prevailing social order from within" (p. 34, folio 17, citing

- **A dress practice as social statement**: *kajkulahi*, "the cultural practice of wearing one's hat
  crooked", "an act of social bravado" and "a statement, made deliberately by the individual
  hat-­wearer, and read consciously as such by society, that the person was not concerned to be seen
  to be conforming to the prescribed appearance of moral uprightness and public propriety" (p. 34,
  folio 17, citing Shahab Ahmed 2016).
- **Sufi relational vocabulary.** "In a Sufi constellation, ideas of intimacy transact with notions of
  suhbet and uns. In literary terms, uns refers to feelings of love and affection or the effect of
  witnessing God's beauty… Suhbet on the other hand denotes companionship. It can refer to a saintly
  meeting or an assembly, a discourse, or conversation." (p. 37, folio 20) *Qurb*: "The online resource
  Urdu Lughat describes qurb as closeness in its visible, interiorized, spatial, and temporal
  dimensions." (p. 38, folio 21) "Qurb also denotes access, relation, and rank. And al-­Qarib (the Near
  One in Arabic) is one of the many qualities by which God is known to Muslims" (p. 39, folio 22);
  *aqribaʾ*, "qarib in its plural iteration aqribaʾ means relatives" (p. 40, folio 23); *fana*, "what
  Sufis would call fana, annihilation into the Truth" (p. 39, folio 22). The proverb *lakir ka fakir*,
  "literally, 'fakir of the line'", "conveys a lack of originality, blind faith in one's way of life, a
  sense of predictability in predetermined patterns of action" (p. 31, folio 14).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The book's governing claim about shrine intimacy.** "The book's notion of unstraight affordances
  makes it possible to discuss dis/closures of intimacy, that is, how saintly bonds in bloom reveal or
  foreclose historically operative prospects in the present." (p. 32, folio 15) And: "intimacy has
  outward skins: its affordances texture inward feeling, public life, and historical being." (p. 42,
  folio 25)
- **Affordance, borrowed and redefined.** Gibson "coined the term affordance to name 'the
  complementarity of the animal and the environment' or what a historical environment, for good or
  ill, offers, provides, or furnishes" (p. 32, folio 15, citing Gibson 1979, 127); "Unlike mine,
  Gibson's primary concern is visual perception" (p. 32, folio 15). Reciprocity: "saints are not doing
  the affording on their own, suo moto; rather, these are historical figurations of intimacy either
  prompted or in need of requiting, leaving fakirs, also scholars, to parse a coauthored reality"
  (p. 33, folio 16). "It also means that a failure to recognize an affordance or not heeding it does
  not take away from the opportunity intimacy presents or its capacity to unstraighten ordinary
  futures." (p. 33, folio 16)
- **Fakir lines as consequence-bearing, against the proverb.** "But fakir lines, as the many life
  stories in this book attest, involved a fundamental questioning of their paths, an altering condition
  of being affectively drawn, a carrying on with relations never entirely knowable or routinely
  secure." (p. 31, folio 14) "Fakir pursuits are famously arduous and likewise never without
  consequence, also for the simple fact that they betray inherited lines or extend outside commonplace
  logics of reproduction." (p. 31, folio 14)
- **Deviance as religious method, not apostasy.** Ridgeon's case is that antinomian practice "is not an
  argument for such figures' lack of attachment to Quranic and religious referents insofar as their
  deviant performances served to uphold an ethical relation to the divine" (p. 34, folio 17); the
  qalandar topos stood for open disregard of convention "in the cause of 'true' religious love"
  (p. 34, folio 17, citing Karamustafa 2006, 33). Shahab Ahmed on the crooked hat: "rather, by
  constituting crooked-­hattedness in terms of Islam," the wearer constitutes "Islam in terms of
  crooked-­hattedness" (p. 35, folio 18, citing Ahmed 2016, 204).
- **Kasmani's own position: "beside", not outside.** "The reading I am, however, inclined to pursue is
  that fakir ways of being in the present are enfleshed and affective endurances of social deviance,
  religious protest, and disguised mystical excellence. These tropes have varyingly prevailed in Sufi
  con/texts, practices, and bodies at least since the eleventh century." (p. 35, folio 18) "intimacy
  with saintly figures and the public histories they bear afford fakir individuals a situatedness to
  act from, a posture, so to speak, not so much outside or beyond but beside Islamic norms."
  (p. 35, folio 18)
- **The present differs from the qalandar past.** "It ought to be said that fakir quests in the
  postcolonial present are shaped by conditions distinct from those of their predecessors." (p. 34,
  folio 17)
- **Against reading Sehwan as "ambiguously Islamic".** "to read Sehwan as ambiguously Islamic, as
  Carla Bellamy would have it, underestimates, in my view, believers' capacity to find clearings amid
  sacred density." (p. 36, folio 19) "The ambition of this book either way lies not in how or in what
  terms Sehwan is an Islamic place, rather what its plural orientations, abundant histories, fractured
  heritage, and compound sense of the divine do to prevailing relations, economies, and processes of
  affect in the context of a postcolonial, Muslim national present." (p. 36, folio 19)
- **Religious complexity need not be explained away.** "Religious complexity, however, doesn't always
  warrant that we explain one tradition through the other." (p. 36, folio 19)
- **Shrine attachment as worldliness, not withdrawal.** "attachment to saints and their tombs, otherwise
  emblematic of ascetic self-­mortification or unworlding, is complexly conversant with material
  realities so much that it is a means for becoming worldly in Sehwan… fidelity to saints and their
  places is not always a recoiling from the worldly; instead, fakir labors in Sehwan constitute a
  purposeful coming close to saints." (p. 44, folio 27)
- **A critique of the anthropology of Islam, named against Naveeda Khan.** "in limiting what she calls
  'scenes of striving' to theological disputations and mosque-­related struggles, Khan relegates
  religious becoming to very straight settings that, despite their intra-­Islamic diversity, eventually
  serve to exemplify and uphold a dominant if not weary narrative: Islam as the primary episteme for
  imagining Pakistan. Places like saints' shrines, ever-­present in the Pakistani landscape, find no
  prominence in the scenes she describes; women and transgender individuals are effectively left
  outside concerns of futurity." (p. 43, folio 26)
- **A critique of Sunni-centring scholarship.** "this book doesn't sit straight with the Sunni-­centering
  drive of the anthropology of Islam, whose epistemologies are predominantly served by, if they have
  not also produced, an active minoritization of Islam's other histories and lifeworlds." (p. 43, folio
  26) Against Shahab Ahmed: he "grossly undervalues Shiʿi historical and cultural sources by dismissing
  them as 'demographically minor'" (p. 44, folio 27, citing Ahmed 2016, 104). And against the field:
  "writings on saints' places in South Asia too often fail to accord Shiʿi histories, beyond the
  descriptive, their rightful place in the analysis of shrine lifeworlds — Carla Bellamy's (2011)
  otherwise insightful book is a case in point. In such instances, the category Sufi serves to
  straighten, subsume, even silence irregular, antinormative, largely non-­Sunni pasts through academic
  writing (Kasmani 2017a)." (p. 44, folio 27) — **directly relevant to how archive entries describe
  silsila affiliation.**
- **Saints and shrines as ordinary, not exotic.** "such queer companionships, risqué or troubling as
  these might be, are in fact also ordinary mobilizations conversant with the everyday ubiquity of
  saints and shrines in Pakistan." (p. 40, folio 23)
- **Intimacy as political.** Berlant's "Intimacy builds worlds"; if intimacy "usurps places meant for
  other kinds of relation", then the book points "to the political stakes and futural bearings of
  saintly intimacy… for individuals, society, and the state in Pakistan" (p. 36, folio 19). "saintly
  intimacy, even when it involves a single or dominant figure of bonding, is always already embroiled
  in a historical field of diverse, mediating, or hierarchical relations" (p. 37, folio 20).
- **Becoming a fakir is not a finished state.** "Fakir efforts and anxieties around saintly bonds and
  conditions of intimacy substantiate the position that individuals do not become fakirs in the sense
  that they become a thing and stay that way." (p. 38, folio 21)
- **Qurb as critical distance, not annihilation.** "Qurb helps appreciate how and why fakir pursuits are
  not so much targeted at becoming the object itself, what Sufis would call fana, annihilation into the
  Truth, but rather at the cluster of promises such being critically intimate brings into view…
  Intimacy hangs on critical distance here." (p. 39, folio 22) "intimacy is the name I give to what
  blooms in that indistinct yet critical interstice between being near a thing and being nearly that
  thing." (p. 39, folio 22)
- **Not-knowing as the queer element.** "What is however particularly queer about such affordances… is
  the not-­fully-­knowing the more-­than-­living object one commits to" (p. 39, folio 22).
- **"Worlding with saints."** "This, what we might call worlding with saints, to lean on Kathleen
  Stewart, underscores not just the relational, allied, and interdependent character of fakir pursuits
  and saintly objects, but gestures also at how they make worlds out of such ties." (p. 40, folio 23)
  "It helps figure the felt and enfleshed ways through which Sehwan's disguised histories and diversely
  sacred heritage is intimately mobilized into folds of a postcolonial Muslim present — a historical
  bloom, so to speak." (p. 40, folio 23)
- **Saints have a stake in the world.** "the very intimacy that winds up fakirs with saints in complex
  ethical-­political relations ensures also that saints can have an affective stake in the world."
  (p. 40, folio 23)
- **Method as epistemology: partial lives, assembled.** "While fakir accounts of the present were rich
  in content and dense with metaphor, many found it arduous to speak of their distant pasts in any
  great detail." (p. 48, folio 31) "Challenges around accessing histories have also meant, wherever
  necessary, an assembling of accounts, anecdotes, field notes, and vignettes to form biographical
  semblances requiring both time and repeated, consistent engagements with my interlocutors." (p. 48,
  folio 31) Quoting Sarkar: "oral testimonies are, in the end, stories that people tell about
  themselves" (p. 48, folio 31, citing Sarkar 2012, 593).
- **Positionality.** "urban-­rural disparities, difference in discourse and education, and
  class-­determined character of social experience mean that I am complexly and multiply located
  vis-­à-­vis the field and my interlocutors — possibly a halfie anthropologist." (p. 49, folio 32)
  "the kind of religious emotion I have encountered in Sehwan is quite distinct from my own experience
  of the same faith, an affective distance that I have already described in some detail. It is also
  true that for the earlier part of the fieldwork, I remained estranged from the most quotidian aspects
  of Sehwan's ritual life." (p. 49, folio 32)
