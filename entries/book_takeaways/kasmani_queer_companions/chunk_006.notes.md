## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Why Pakistani women go to shrines, in the book's own list.** "Pakistani women visit shrines for
  all manner of reasons: to make offerings, to sing tributes, to celebrate, to complain, to cry their
  hearts out, to find healing, and sometimes to simply chill on their day off — but equally to mend
  their circumstances, domestic or otherwise, petitioning saints as they remake their worlds, so to
  speak" (p. 105, folio 88). Zaheda's own mother came "to petition the saint regarding a pending
  dispute over a family property" (p. 119, folio 102) — **a property dispute brought to the shrine.**
- **What a woman fakir supplies, and what she does not.** "Fakirs like `Za-`…`heda[text-layer?]`
  [Zaheda] are perfect instruments for better conveying women's desires and wishes and for
  effectively forwarding their pleas to the saint" (pp. 105, 107, folios 88, 90). "Zaheda was not one
  to write amulets but would offer spiritual guidance to shrine visitors, pray over substances like
  water, or bless people over the phone, and in one case had used her saliva as a healing agent"
  (p. 107, folio 90). By 2013 "She had even stopped offering healing or consultations since we had
  last met" (p. 116, folio 99).
- **The courtyard as the enabling institution for women's careers.** "The setting of the courtyard
  offered a measured publicness, a place where women guides like her could interact with unrelated
  people in relative safety and eventually establish a following. Space is indeed key for women's
  religious careers" (p. 107, folio 90). "Zaheda knew that her exchanges with a fakir mentor… would
  draw a lot more suspicion if it were to take place outside the courtyard" (p. 107, folio 90). "The
  courtyard is where Zaheda's ties with the saint were nurtured. It is also where they nearly came
  undone" (p. 109, folio 92).
- **Displacement from the courtyard by administrative rule (2011), and its social cost.** Forced "to
  stay the nights at a guest house nearby, it was a move that proved difficult for her in both social
  and emotional terms. She was increasingly aware how staying the night in town brought new risks to
  her reputation or that conducting herself in public meant opening herself to heightened scrutiny"
  (p. 107, folio 90). Later: "Now, displaced from the courtyard, Zaheda described how she felt
  unwelcome at the shrine: 'I would not even step out from here but even then, I had to go away from
  here. Now, why did I have to leave? Only Lal would know. I don't know it!'" (p. 117, folio 100).
- **Securitization as a Pakistan-wide shrine phenomenon.** "I have already pointed out how the
  character of the courtyard has changed over the years, especially due to an increase in security at
  shrines across Pakistan" (p. 117, folio 100). The concrete measures at Sehwan — entrances pushed
  out, metal detectors, security cameras, police and intelligence officials — are at p. 105, folio 88.
- **Dhamal as a daily, gendered performance.** "hundreds move to the tune of reed and drums every
  evening in the shrine courtyard" (p. 110, folio 93). Zaheda's claim of distinction: "'I have been
  given dhamal like no other; it is just for me'… she was able to endure it without letting her
  chador slip from her head, making a clear separation from countless ordinary women who, every
  evening, are seen to lose their bodily composure and with it, propriety of dress" (p. 110,
  folio 93).
- **Bhes — the fakir guise, defined at length.** "Bhes can be worn, received, or, in its most frequent
  fakir usage, one is in bhes. It refers to a collectivity of attributes that include but are not
  limited to styles of dressing, bodily adornment, and social behavior, spatial practice and
  place-­based relationships, codes of diet and sexual conduct, and manners of sitting and walking as
  well as styles of speech" (p. 108, folio 91). Its loss is described materially: "her signature
  chador replaced by a black shawl and there were no signs of rings or the bedian [shackles] she used
  to wear" (p. 114, folio 97); "She was wearing a light pink shalwar-­kamiz, covered mostly by a big
  black shawl" (p. 115, folio 98).
- **Institutional patronage is gendered.** "In the absence of institutional patronage like that
  available to men across fakir lodges in Sehwan (see chapter 4), women turn to saints at publicly
  administered shrines. As discussed in chapter 2, fakir paths for women are advanced under the
  patronage of the state, but their claims of extraordinariness must be validated through personal
  experience and the embodiment of endurance, and, in particular, their narrativization of dreams and
  visions, pain and suffering, powers and capacities" (p. 110, folio 93). And: women "were also not
  welcome in the all-­male circles of fakir lodges" (p. 122, folio 105).
- **A pilgrimage circuit with an economy of its own.** After Sehwan's three-day fair, fakirs go on to
  Lahut in Balochistan or to shrines in Karachi; Zaheda "had disappeared in the intricate geography of
  countless shrines and saintly places, lost somewhere in the flow of saints' fairs and festivals that
  pop up on this landscape every so often" (p. 114, folio 97). She "had been on her routine tour of
  shrines" for months (p. 113, folio 96). **Remittance as the mechanism that keeps her on the road:**
  Zafar "had wired her some money at the time and was now worried if she had enough" (p. 114,
  folio 97).
- **Loyalty to one saint as a cost-bearing institution.** Moving to Gaji-­Shah "wasn't an easy move
  because switching between shrines, as she herself knew and feared, could cost her pursuit dearly.
  **It is a question of loyalty to saints**" (p. 117, folio 100). "Different saints have different
  things to offer, Zaheda had herself told me, explaining how at one point she was quite close to
  getting her fakir pursuit **authorized by a particular saint** when she decided to divert her focus
  to Sehwan" (p. 117, folio 100) — **that saint and shrine are (not stated)**.
- **Forms of address at the shrine.** "unlike the more common practice of addressing spiritual women
  as amma or mother in South Asia, she was addressed as baji or sister in the shrine-­courtyard. This
  follows the norm of addressing strangers of the opposite sex using a term for a close relative,
  extending a familial frame to a public interaction (Alvi 2013, 180)" (p. 112, folio 95).
- **Celibacy demanded of women fakirs.** "she had been instructed to devote her body exclusively to
  the saint. This was neither new to me nor unexpected for fakirs; other women fakirs I knew had
  spoken of their struggles to keep their bodies clean and pure by refusing sex with their husbands"
  (p. 112, folio 95).
- **Fasting and its bodily cost.** Zafar: "She eats very little. Her stomach has become weak due to
  excessive fasting" and "two years ago she fasted for a whole year" (p. 116, folio 99). Zaheda "had
  been ill for almost two years" (p. 115, folio 98); "her doctors had told her that there was no
  physical illness but it was something to do with her mind" (p. 116, folio 99).
- **Phone and mobility.** "Zaheda, as I recalled, was perhaps the only fakir I knew who at the time
  did not use a mobile phone" (p. 113, folio 96) — against which she "would… bless people over the
  phone" (p. 107, folio 90).
- **Fieldwork chronology as institutional record** (the author's own dates): 2009 (first encounters,
  including "On a November evening in 2009", p. 110, folio 93); 2011 (rule change, p. 107, folio 90);
  2012 (the annual festival and fair; "I had already wrapped up a year of fieldwork in Sehwan",
  p. 114, folio 97); "the night of November 14, 2013" (p. 114, folio 97) and the 2013 farewell
  meeting (p. 118, folio 101); "By 2018, when I last visited Sehwan" (p. 105, folio 88).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The dream of the grandfather's murshid, a prophecy of nothing given** — received "some
  twenty-­five years ago but was revealing to me for the first time" (p. 118, folio 101). Her
  grandfather "did not wish for her to be a fakir" and told her, "Whatever there is, the saint will
  give you. I cannot give you anything," then left it "for his own spiritual master to decide her
  case. 'So, in a dream, he had taken me to his murshid'… 'The murshid of my grandfather said it to my
  face! I was there, my grandfather and the murshid: We will give her nothing. Because if we were to
  give her something, it would cause hard times on her and sickness would come to her. You will get
  nothing at all whichsoever shrine you may go to!'" (p. 118, folio 101).
- **The dream of the sword.** "In a new dream from the saint, she was shown a sword, enough for her to
  interpret the severity of the matter and the costs it might entail" (p. 113, folio 96).
- **Two bisharat from Lal.** "she received a bisharat, that is, a divine sign or inspired word, from
  Lal: 'Leave everything and come to the shrine at this very moment!'"; and on arrival, "another
  message from the saint was to follow: 'You must remain here!'" (p. 120, folio 103).
- **The saint's rope.** "The murshid has stopped me. The saint has put a rope around me. You must go
  at this moment. I will follow once I get permission from the murshid. How could I leave now when the
  murshid hasn't given me permission; he hasn't yet given me a sign" (p. 119, folio 102).
- **Being chosen as the saint's spiritual wife**, held out as a capacity women have and men do not:
  "The fakir woman is distinct, she argued, because her pursuit includes the possibility of being
  chosen by the saint as his spiritual wife, a potentiality that in Zaheda's view was impossible for
  her male counterparts" (p. 111, folio 94).
- **Healing by saliva.** "in one case had used her saliva as a healing agent" (p. 107, folio 90) —
  reported of Zaheda, not of the saint.
- **Saints summon their fakirs.** "In not being invited to Sehwan as often as in the past— fakirs
  believe that saints summon them — she had lost considerable ground, she admitted" (p. 117,
  folio 100); "the two years she did not come was because she was not asked to come by the saint"
  (p. 115, folio 98).
- **The severed line.** "It is indeed the line that is not to be found. The line is cut! There is no
  communication these days" (p. 115, folio 98); "Now it seems that Lal is not even speaking to me. I
  don't see anymore, the thing that used to be between Lal and myself" (p. 116, folio 99).
- **Qurb / qarib — affective closeness as a bodily transformation**, in Zaheda's words: "the one who
  is qarib [affectively close] to the saint, his or her appearance and demeanor, manner of speech,
  lifestyle, diet, every such thing undergoes distinct alterations. S/he will then appear different
  from ordinary men and women" (p. 109, folio 92). And: "Lal has blessed me immensely, way too much. I
  am very close to him!" (p. 112, folio 95).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The chapter's governing claim about women fakirs and public life.** "Fakir women in this book are
  distinct in that their relations with saints afford rare prospects of a public life. In other words,
  what was possible at the shrine, a publicly administered institution of the state, wasn't easily
  imaginable outside its confines" (p. 107, folio 90). The state-run shrine is read as producing "an
  unstraight affordance of saintly intimacy" (p. 107, folio 90).
- **Pemberton's associational model, adopted.** "Women guides in South Asia, as Kelly Pemberton notes,
  conform better to an associational model of spiritual relationship that does not require an
  obligation of allegiance on the part of the follower nor the sanction of formal institutions of
  mystical Islam. Such a pir, she notes, is usually someone who 'has acquired a reputation for
  sanctity' as well as 'forms of "secret" knowledge (such as how to interpret dreams, communicate with
  the unseen world [ghayb] or make amulets) and who can use this knowledge to provide spiritual and
  material services for others' (Pemberton 2006, 62)" (p. 107, folio 90). Kasmani then **departs from
  her** on publicness: "where women have the greatest scope for becoming pirs in practice, if not in
  name, is often outside of the purview of the general public" (2006, 67) — whereas his fakir women
  are public (p. 107, folio 90).
- **Bhes as citational practice.** "Bhes is codified to the extent that it is a citational practice
  that can be customized — a dynamic and always-­in-­the-­making corpus in Heghnar Watenpaugh's (2005,
  547 – 48) cross-­reading with Butler — meaning that fakir doings and dispositions, unique as these
  are in individual instances, cross-reference performances of other fakirs, living or dead, whether
  received in dreams or cited in pictorial and textual representations of saints, ascetics, and
  mystics across Islamic histories and geographies" (pp. 108–109, folios 91–92).
- **Historiality.** "By virtue of such historiality, that is to say, a returning trace of something
  that repeatedly but varyingly exists over time, bhes refers to a dynamic, locally varied, and
  malleable corpus of fakir etiquette whose eventual test lies in the validation it offers to fakirs'
  less-­than-­normative or otherwise ways of being in the world" (p. 109, folio 92).
- **The body as the register of deviance.** Bhes "offers a contemporary illustration of long-­standing
  Islamic-­mystical ways of being beside norms… and confirms 'the historically and culturally specific
  belief that deviant social behavior (however it is defined) manifests in the materiality of the
  body' (Terry and Urla 1995, 2)" (p. 109, folio 92).
- **Bhes works against other women, not only against the male norm.** "that bhes in Zaheda's case
  operates not only in relation to the inherently masculine fakir norm but more critically also in
  contradistinction to other women informs my argument about women's distinctly feminine religious
  careers in Sehwan (Kasmani 2015; see also chapter 2)" (p. 109, folio 92).
- **The chapter's two driving questions.** "How and in what ways is her physical failing to be
  recognized in the shrine field an affective failure of her social orientations and saintly
  affections? Or how, more precisely, does the loss of such dis/guise come to jeopardize her social
  standing as a woman and fakir?" (p. 109, folio 92).
- **The mentor's position on women fakirs, reported verbatim and unendorsed.** "'They [women] are
  incapacitated to undertake such a thing,' he told me. His response was as surprising as it was
  categorical, given that he had agreed to guide a woman in the first place" (p. 111, folio 94). "In
  his view, the presence of women's bodies in whichever shape or form beyond their socially defined
  domestic roles carried serious disorientating risks to public social and moral order" (p. 111,
  folio 94).
- **Zaheda's counter-exegesis of Eve.** "Adam was led to the forbidden fruit, not by the tempting
  force of Eve but instead by her embodiment of God's will. … The only reason for God to create the
  world, according to her, was God's desire to be known. Thus, in Eve's intercessory force lies the
  very creation of this human society; in fact, she is the instrument through which God's desire comes
  to be fulfilled" (p. 111, folio 94). On equality of path with difference of style: "the path for men
  and women is the same in fakiri," but "just as men and women perform distinct but equally important
  functions in society, they do so in fakiri as well"; "the difference, she believed, stemmed from the
  manner and style in which the journey is experienced" (p. 111, folio 94). Her proof is
  experiential — "Had it not been the case, 'the saint wouldn't have made it work so far'" (p. 111,
  folio 94).
- **Ramaswamy on women saints' marital imagery.** "Reading literary verses, she argues that imagery
  for consorting with the divine in the framework of a (heterosexual) marriage comes naturally to
  women saints, who unlike their male counterparts do not have to first take on femininity in relation
  to a male object of worship (Ramaswamy 1996, 30)" (pp. 111–112, folios 94–95). Kasmani applies it:
  Zaheda's spiritual-wife claim "was not a rejection of sex as a definition of marriage, wife, and
  woman but, like Ramaswamy's (1996) twelfth-­century women saints, an embracing of her sexuality
  despite being a mother and a fakir" (p. 112, folio 95).
- **Orientation, disorientation and the change of shrine.** "The change of site, like a change in
  orientations, to lean on Sara Ahmed (2006), meant discontinuing ties with a particular saint and
  letting go of certain orientations as well as the intimacy and futurity those lines would have
  potentially offered. In other terms, it meant an abandonment of certain objects and its accompanying
  cluster of promises (Berlant 2006)" (p. 117, folio 100). And: to follow a line like a fakir is "to
  become invested in that line and also to be committed to 'where' it will take us" (Ahmed 2006, 176)
  (p. 121, folio 104), a capacity "forever courted with moments of orientations' many failures:
  unorientations, or instances and occasions when fakir intimacies discontinue, weaken, or are rivaled
  by other objects of desire" (pp. 121–122, folios 104–105).
- **The loss of bhes destroys the social warrant for being at the shrine.** "Zaheda's motivation to be
  at shrines was now more suspect, as her bhes, or the lack thereof, no longer explained nor supported
  her advanced and unchaperoned presence in a public space. There was nothing about her outward
  appearance that convincingly established her social role as a guide, nor was her sexuality
  neutralized by the guise of women fakirs" (p. 118, folio 101). Kasmani stresses the agency: "the
  event of going unrecognized at the shrine, with which I have opened this chapter, was of Zaheda's
  own choice and making" (p. 116, folio 99), citing her: "putting on the fakir garment makes a fakir
  more visible to the public eye and that is exactly what I do not want" (p. 116, folio 99).
- **Retrospective ordering of a life.** The late-revealed dream "was an explanation in retrospect of
  why she may have not found the desired results she had once foreseen for herself. In other words, it
  was her way of ordering the past, making it meaningful through her engagements with me as well as
  with the conditions of time and place in which this particular telling was taking place (Sarkar
  2012, 585)" (p. 119, folio 102).
- **Cruel optimism.** Zaheda's endurance is "evocative of what Berlant (2006, 22) has named 'cruel
  optimism,' that is to say, 'a relation of attachment to compromised conditions of possibility' even
  when its presence threatens the subject's well-being or 'the continuity of the subject's sense of
  what it means to keep on living on and to look forward to being in the world' despite an apparent
  loss of the object of desire" (p. 120, folio 103).
- **Hope as labour, not disposition** (Thajib on Waria in Aceh): engaging with hope "does not simply
  mean clinging to some abstract promise of happy finality or embodying inner strength or human
  flourishing that is often characterized as 'resilience' but necessitates arduous affective labor"
  (Thajib 2019, 237) (pp. 120–121, folios 103–104).
- **Endurance versus survival** (Simone): endurance depends on "the willingness to suspend the
  familiar and even the counted-­upon in order to engage something unexpected", though such
  "engagement may sometimes simply reiterate a commitment to what already is, where the person decides
  that it is better to stay put with what is familiar" (Simone 2013); Zaheda's endurance is "a means
  of continuing to continue" (p. 121, folio 104).
- **Impasse as embodied knowledge.** "Whether given through a recourse to past dreams, an ongoing
  domestic conflict, or a likely rivalry between saints, impasse here was a bodied form of knowing by
  way of feeling. Neither was continuance part of a plan in that going on or staying put did not
  involve a path of action or a line of futurity that Zaheda could see in its entirety" (p. 121,
  folio 104). Fakirhood is "not so much about the attainment of an object as it was about continuing
  its pursuit"; she "was in the wake — that is, a staying with 'a form of consciousness' (Sharpe 2016,
  15)" (p. 121, folio 104).
- **Loneliness as social failure, not solitude.** "Loneliness, as she had described in this particular
  moment, stemmed from a social failure: the isolating experience that comes with the gradual
  realization that not many around her could comprehend or appreciate her reasons to pursue saintly
  objects, especially when progress was slow if not also unyielding. Being in the fakir line had also
  meant constantly being at odds with what came predetermined for her as wife, mother, and woman"
  (p. 122, folio 105).
- **The husband's authority reasoned theologically by Zaheda herself.** "'When he [the husband] is not
  in agreement, then how will the murshid be convinced? His approval is important — in Islam, in
  tariqat, in shariʿat. If he does not agree, then it will not work. He [the husband] is my owner in a
  way; I am bound. If he does not agree then my avenues will be blocked. There will be obstacles. … I
  had this in my heart since the beginning, that my murshid would persuade him in his own way'"
  (p. 120, folio 103). Contrast her earlier judgement: "'He who doesn't let me follow the wishes of
  the murshid, or if someone wants to belong to Allah and the person doesn't let you do so, in my
  opinion such a person is jahil [ignorant]'" (p. 120, folio 103). **The chunk sets these two
  positions side by side without reconciling them**, calling the later one "a lot more conditional
  than conflictual in this instance" (p. 120, folio 103).
- **The author's methodological candour.** "There wasn't a single major event in Zaheda's description
  of the near past that I could use to reliably explain the palpable change in her condition, although
  she did speak of a protracted illness" (p. 116, folio 99). And on the 2013 reunion: "To ease our
  first conversation in four years, I did not record her, I did not take out my recorder neither did I
  reach out for my notebook" (p. 115, folio 98).
- **The gendered future of the courtyard, stated as the author's impression, not as a finding.** "I
  had the feeling that in a courtyard where Zaheda had once flourished, it was now unimaginable for a
  lone woman to have a place of her own" (p. 105, folio 88).
