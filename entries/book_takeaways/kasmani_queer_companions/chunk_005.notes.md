## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Haziri as a nightly mass ritual.** "Each evening in the white marble courtyard of the shrine,
  women from across the country experience haziri (lit. presence; see fig. 2.3). Responding to the
  sounds of drum and reed, cohabiting spirits are believed to become present during a ritual
  performance, leaving hundreds of women in bouts of exhilaration." (p. 87, folio 70) Family members
  then "help loved ones recover from the exhausting experience of `haz-/iri`" (p. 87, folio 70).
- **Divination sold off the back of it.** "One can also observe how visitors at the shrine approach
  them to seek divination from spirit hosts who are making these passages, already attributing a
  possible line of futurity to such women." (p. 87, folio 70) **No fee or offering is mentioned:
  (not stated).**
- **Fakirs as ritual stewards of haziri.** "It is also for their abilities to discern good from bad
  spirits that fakirs are often summoned to watch over women's passages during haziri." (p. 87,
  folio 70)
- **Two kinds of possession, institutionally distinguished.** "A distinction comes to be drawn between
  `ill-­meaning` possessions that are demonic and commonplace and those which involve cohabiting with
  noble and enabling spirits… one receives or meets a fakir spirit, in contrast to djinns and
  malevolent spirits that mount persons." (p. 87, folio 70, note 13)
- **Hereditary healing transmitted by cup.** The sayyid healer's "gift of healing, as his brother
  explained to me, was divinely bestowed upon their family. It has been transmitted over generations
  and, as was characteristic of local sayyids, was transferred from father to son by means of a
  ceremony that involves symbolically drinking spirituality from a cup (pyala)." (p. 88, folio 71)
- **The two-term vocabulary of authority.** "notions of inherited `ʿata`, the charismatic gift
  available to men like Shah, and `ʿamal`, the acts and exercise of (women) fakirs, are not the same"
  (pp. 89–90, folios 72–73); "`ʿata`, once bestowed, cannot be revoked; it can only be transmitted
  down" (p. 90, folio 73), whereas women "must strive to acquire their capacities through a tenuous
  process that involves individual exercise and sustained action (riyazat). Failure to secure its
  conditions could result in such powers being lost or, worse still, revoked." (p. 90, folio 73)
- **Shrine revenue and the market.** Sayyid prosperity rests on "[a] long history of attachment to
  the shrine and its revenues, social privilege, and official favors spanning centuries" (p. 89,
  folio 72); their sway over "the ancillary resources of the shrine, its related market and economy…
  can hardly be overstated" (p. 89, folio 72, note 15). The courtyard's length stands "between the
  hustle of the bazaar and the fervor of the durbar" (p. 104, folio 87); "tea sellers and vendors of
  all kinds solicited customers" in it (p. 104, folio 87).
- **Kafi lodges as recruitment into a local economy.** A kafi is "the most routine way through which
  a fakir initiate from outside Sehwan is accommodated in local constellations of power, recruited to
  serve the interests of Sehwan's spiritual economies (see chapter 4)" (p. 89, folio 72). **The
  chapter-4 material is outside this chunk.**
- **Shrine committees and annual ceremonies.** Former custodians are "part of shrine committees and
  the organization of annual ceremonies" (p. 89, folio 72). **The ceremonies are not enumerated:
  (not stated).**
- **Nationalization as the pivot of the chapter's political economy.** "while the nationalization of
  shrines has dramatically reduced their roles at the shrine, limiting it to the ceremonial, former
  custodian families continue to hold considerable leverage through other forms of participation"
  (p. 89, folio 72). Against it: "a publicly owned shrine offers fakirs like Amma opportunities for
  `self-­brokered` access to the saint, to operate without hereditary masters, to establish an
  independent following and earn a living as healers and spiritual guides — not a given if it were
  still a privately held heritage in the hands of the sayyid elite" (p. 99, folio 82).
- **Fakiri as a livelihood.** Fakirs "earn a living as healers and spiritual guides" (p. 99,
  folio 82); "Consulting fakirs and pirs in the shrine courtyard was commonplace" (p. 103, folio 86).
  **No sums, rates or income figures anywhere: (not stated).**
- **A spot in the durbar as property.** "one of the ways to also identify a fakir at the shrine of
  Sehwan was to pay attention to the consistency of a fakir's presence at a particular spot"; the
  work of "finding, establishing, and maintaining a regular spot in the durbar" is "arduous and often
  `time-­taking`" (pp. 97–98, folios 80–81). Amma's spot came with a duty: she would "spend the day
  tending to the adjacent grave" (p. 99, folio 82).
- **Purity rules and their suspension.** "In South Asia, menstruating and postpartum women can be
  considered to be in an active state of pollution, attributed with a capacity to defile, which
  prevents them from full participation in many ritual practices across various faiths." (p. 94,
  folio 77) Amma: "After my third child, I sat outside the durbar as I was `na-­pak` [lit. impure;
  unclean]; I begged Lal to rid me of this monthly cycle of impurity. What kind of fakiri would that
  be, if one had to stay away from him [the saint] for a week every month?" (p. 94, folio 77,
  note 21)
- **Celibacy as a saintly requirement.** "Saintly instructions for women initiates to maintain bodily
  purity and integrity were not unheard of. In fact, the requirement to suspend sexual relations was
  a frequent one. Premised on ideals of purity and fidelity of the woman fakir, it was possibly also
  a sign of possessiveness on the part of the saint, though it was often articulated as a demand made
  by the cohabiting spirit." (p. 96, folio 79) Amma "no longer maintained conjugal ties with her
  husband" (p. 96, folio 79).
- **Purdah relaxed for fakirs.** "as much as fakir figures were subject to cultural norms of
  segregation of the sexes, styles of purdah that women were expected to observe did not apply to
  women fakirs in equal measure. Mastani did not cover her head, and Amma interacted with men all
  day. In most cases, women fakirs were expected to drape their heads in public, and their
  interactions with unrelated men were not without scrutiny; however, their fakir roles allowed more
  relaxed styles of covering and eased participation in public contexts." (p. 97, folio 80)
- **Guise as credential.** "During her first months at the shrine, an unknown woman had approached
  her with black clothes, adding how in a dream she was instructed to deliver the garments to her.
  Amma viewed this as an injunction from the saint. The colors, clothes, gems, and accessories all
  came to be customized over time as per the saint's will." (p. 93, folio 76) The chains produced "an
  undeniably Shiʿi aesthetic" (p. 93, folio 76). Chapter three names the practice: "a fakir grammar
  known as `bhes` (also `ves`)", "[l]iterally meaning both guise and disguise" (p. 103, folio 86).
- **Muharram observance at the shrine.** "during the holy commemoration of Muharram, when Amma
  appeared at the shrine with the same chains and shackles" (p. 91, folio 74); "I was supposed to wear
  it on the first day of Muharram" (p. 91, folio 74); "I got them placed at the `ʿalam`, applied
  fragrances to them" (p. 92, folio 75); "I will take these off on the fortieth day" (p. 92,
  folio 75). She was "additionally instructed not to bathe during this period, nor was she allowed a
  change of clothes" (p. 91, folio 74). **No year is printed for this Muharram: (not stated).**
- **Intercession through the vulnerable.** Amma "used the word `bechari` (one who is helpless) to
  describe a woman, though in so doing she had effectively turned a notion of vulnerability into a
  valuable trait… it was almost impossible for the saint to refuse a plea when forwarded by a woman
  fakir" (p. 93, folio 76). Abba added the Sindhi idiom: "the concept of `niyani`, or little sister in
  Sindhi society… among locals, the greatest of conflicts can be resolved if one of the feuding
  parties is able get a niyani to intervene on their behalf. Women fakirs were therefore perfect
  instruments for intercession in his opinion." (p. 94, folio 77)
- **Motherhood as a qualification.** "The commonly adopted and given title `amma` (also `ma` and
  `mata`), in the South Asian context, lends women in godly pursuits the postsexual authority of a
  mother critical to their public circumstances (Pechilis 2012, 123)." (p. 95, folio 78) "Motherhood
  was in fact what paved the way for women's spiritual careers, a futurity disclosed once a woman had
  performed and demonstrated her social worth as a childbearing and `child-­rearing` individual."
  (p. 96, folio 79, note 26)
- **A named change of shrine management.** "This… was an administrative action on the part of the new
  management" (p. 98, folio 81); the order came "not from the Auqaf manager but from a senior police
  official who had been newly posted" (p. 98, folio 81). **Neither official is named: (not stated).**

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Fakir vocation as the saint's own choice**: "Willed by the saint himself, `fakirs-­of-­the-­inside`
  are few and far between." (p. 87, folio 70)
- **The kafi network founded by the saint**: the fourteen lodges are "believed to have been
  established by the saint himself" (p. 89, folio 72) — the book reports the belief, it does not
  endorse it.
- **The saint's punitive power, in Amma's own words**: "It was in the durbar that the saint said, 'I
  will bend your foot!' My foot got sprained for the first time in the durbar. It became so turned
  that I could not walk or do anything… Then a second time, a third time, and then my foot got
  burned." (p. 91, folio 74) After missing the ʿurs instruction: "As the ʿurs passed, my condition
  got serious. I was finished. For two months, I could not perform my duty, neither would I be able
  come to the durbar, nothing!" (p. 91, folio 74) Then: "I came and sought his forgiveness: give me
  health and respect in the name of Sajjad and the pious women. I will be your prisoner once again!"
  (p. 91, folio 74) And on resuming, "Last night I faced difficulty; it also affected my eyesight"
  (p. 91, folio 74).
- **Clothing delivered by dream**: "an unknown woman had approached her with black clothes, adding
  how in a dream she was instructed to deliver the garments to her. Amma viewed this as an injunction
  from the saint." (p. 93, folio 76)
- **Election by the saint**: "He chose me out of so many women here and gave me the guardianship of
  this mosque; I am the first woman to have been given such a duty" (p. 93, folio 76); and Amma "was
  settled in her view that her place in the durbar was willed by the saint and no authority
  superseded his" (p. 99, folio 82).
- **A petition to end menstruation, and its reported granting**: "I begged Lal to rid me of this
  monthly cycle of impurity" (p. 94, folio 77); later, "Amma first told me that she was like me, in
  her words, clean, and thus suggesting she was rid of menstruation" (p. 96, folio 79) and "even if
  she appeared like a woman, she wasn't one in the real sense. 'I am no longer like other women'"
  (p. 94, folio 77). **Kasmani reports the claim; he does not adjudicate it.**
- **The healing gift as divine bestowal on a lineage**: "The gift of healing, as his brother
  explained to me, was divinely bestowed upon their family." (p. 88, folio 71)
- **Vocation by dream and vision**: "Baba-Akram's journey to Sehwan on the basis of dreams and waking
  visions" (p. 90, folio 73).
- **Recognition by spiritual sight**, in Zaheda's words: "People recognize me to a great extent…
  those who are able to recognize, that is, ones who have that sight, those who understand this. . . .
  Then, if I walk two steps, they, to quite an extent, recognize that this person is walking with a
  purpose or is very close [`qarib`] to Lal." (p. 102, folio 85)

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The chapter's governing claim about women fakirs' authority.** What women like Amma and Mastani
  "are set against is not one another but the femaleness ascribed to their bodies and the commonly
  held notion that spiritual authority isn't a thing to acquire but to inherit, reproduced in acts of
  exclusive transmission between men" (pp. 87–88, folios 70–71).
- **Fakir standing is structurally revocable.** "Fakir claims of intercessory virtuosity in Sehwan
  were fragile and revocable, their positions invariably undermined by the social, economic, and
  historical privileges that hereditary masters like Shah enjoyed in Sehwan. In fact, fakirs knew that
  even in instances where their capacities were distinct, their influence couldn't supersede that of
  a sayyid pir." (pp. 88–89, folios 71–72)
- **Sayyid power is not only spiritual.** "Sayyid power in Sehwan wasn't simply a spiritual matter."
  (p. 89, folio 72) Their positions "as pirs but also adjudicators and patrons are deeply grounded in
  Sehwani life" (p. 89, folio 72).
- **Double disadvantage.** "Neither male nor sayyid, figures like Amma and Mastani are therefore
  doubly disadvantaged." (p. 89, folio 72)
- **Exclusion produces innovation.** "An exclusion from dominant tradition means that women must
  innovate with what is available to them." (p. 90, folio 73) Supported by Pechilis on women spiritual
  masters: their distinguishing characteristic "is personal experience both in the sense of
  independent spiritual realization outside of initiation in a lineage (many female gurus are
  `self-­initiated`), as well as a pragmatic orientation that relates experience of the world to
  spiritual knowledge" (2012, 114) (p. 90, folio 73).
- **Possession as a workaround for lineage.** "With meager institutional support or sanction, for many
  women fakirs the key to and the proof of their fakir credentials lies in their embodied experiences
  and accounts of dealing with spirits from the saint." (p. 90, folio 73) Because Sufi transformation
  "typically depends on dyadic relationships of authority and dependence between men (Malamud 1996,
  90)", it "can take multitiered forms when it comes to women" (p. 90, folio 73). The result:
  "women are able to secure an `unstraight` or `gender-­distinct` route to spiritual authority and
  transformation" (p. 90, folio 73), circumventing "the need for a sayyid spiritual master or the
  dependencies that come with life at a fakir commune" (p. 90, folio 73) — **but not escaping
  dependence**: "their progress is all the same affected by their `life-­long` relationship with
  mediating spirits, not least their husbands or family members who assist, accompany, and help make
  meaning of women's passages in the shrine courtyard" (pp. 90–91, folios 73–74).
- **Mediation is doubled, and the male spirit is the licence.** "it is a fakir spirit, always male,
  that furnishes women like Amma's outward claims to be fakirs themselves" (p. 87, folio 70), and
  "messages from the saint are rendered intelligible via the mediating spirit" (p. 87, folio 70,
  note 12), recoverable "only after the performance is over through the explanations of her husband"
  (p. 87, folio 70). With Bellamy: such proclamations "are only meaningful if witnessed and
  rearticulated by someone other than the possessed individual and the possessing spirits" (2008, 40)
  (p. 87, folio 70).
- **Suffering as public capital, and Kasmani's extension of the argument.** Following Harriss (2010,
  170) on chronic ill health among British Pakistani women, "A reputation for endurance… is the very
  leverage that women use to sanction release from everyday roles or increased control" (p. 92,
  folio 75, note 20); with Grima (2004) on Pashtun women, "inasmuch as a performance of suffering can
  be strategic, it is premised on other people's knowledge of one's endurance" (p. 92, folio 75).
  **Kasmani's departure**: "unlike Harriss's and Grima's cases, the extent and viability of her
  performance are not limited to the familial or interpersonal sphere. Her suffering navigates more
  public worlds." (p. 92, folio 75) It is "part of the healing rhetoric that female healers must
  employ in order to highlight their chosenness for, and to sustain their right to, positions of
  spiritual authority, which in the case of their male counterparts is culturally assumed
  (Flueckiger 2003, 267)." (p. 92, folio 75)
- **Not masculinization but other femininity.** "women fakirs' articulation of their gendered selves
  involves a double burden: they must position themselves not only in relation to masculine ideals of
  ascetic practice but also in contradistinction to other women (Kasmani 2015, 2016a)" (p. 94,
  folio 77); "[t]his relation to ordinary women isn't a performance that leans on the fakir subject's
  masculinization; instead, it opens itself to different, multiple, or other femininities" (pp. 94–95,
  folios 77–78). Self-representation as unlike women "is not an overcoming of ascribed femaleness per
  se; it is instead 'the effortful device through which her fakir aspirations are accommodated despite
  her gender' (Kasmani 2016a)" (p. 95, folio 78, note 22). The distinction is "aimed less at I am like
  a man and more at I am not like other women (Kasmani 2015)" (p. 97, folio 80, note 29).
- **The porous female body.** Lamb on India: women are "more open anatomically: things (such as
  menstrual blood, sexual fluids, and children) flow into and out of women's bodies in ways that men
  do not experience" (2005, 219–20) (p. 94, folio 77); "[c]onceived and described as more porous
  compared to men's bodies, women are thus taken to be more exposed to mixes and impurities, also more
  prone to possession" (p. 94, folio 77).
- **Marriage and motherhood as gradated access.** With Alvi (2013, 182) on a Pakistani village,
  "Women have gradated access to the outside world," where "young unmarried teenage girls with no
  social responsibilities are the most restricted"; "elder girls get around more in order to look
  after the livestock", while mothers and mothers-in-law "move freely within the village . . . [and]
  maintain relations between villages through the visits they conduct alone on ritual occasions"
  (p. 95, folio 78, notes 24–25). "Marriage, and by extension motherhood, is therefore seen as a first
  step to social maturity" (p. 95, folio 78), and "women become less constrained by the notion of
  bodily impurity in widowhood and after menopause" (p. 95, folio 78, note 25).
- **The defiance/deviation question, left open between two scholars.** Ramaswamy holds that women's
  "styling of their spiritual careers in such frameworks involves an adherence to patriarchy… and
  therefore in her opinion qualifies not so much as an act of defiance but as one of deviation"
  (p. 96, folio 79, note 28); Pechilis (2012) "has argued that spiritual women, through their
  rejection of sex as a definition of marriage, wife, and woman, refuse on the one hand what it means
  to be a woman on society's terms, destabilizing especially the binary constitution of women in
  opposition to men (Butler 1990, 143–44)" (p. 96, folio 79). **Kasmani does not choose between them**
  — "Either way, refusing a substantial part of what it means to be a woman… does not come easy"
  (p. 96, folio 79).
- **Intimacy with the saint displaces other relations.** Paraphrasing Berlant (1998, 281), "intimacy
  with Lal effectively usurps places originally reserved for other relations. Affective troubles
  follow, since decisions like these are not always met with support; husbands… can resist and
  counteract such moves, causing a slowing down of a fakir's progress." (p. 97, folio 80)
- **An explicit refusal of a liberation reading.** "Such understandings on the part of women fakirs
  also curb a wholesale emancipatory reading of their religious projects." (p. 97, folio 80, note 29)
- **The state as arbiter at saints' places — the chapter's closing thesis.** "Coming close to the
  saint in the setting of the durbar reminds us that the futurity of women's public religious careers
  is circumscribed in a greater contest of authorities and shaped by the state's arbitrating role at
  saint's places." (p. 99, folio 82)
- **The book's central concept, defined in chapter three.** "`qurb` — or intimacy with saints — names
  that critical distance between being near a thing and being nearly that thing, coming affectively
  close does not evidence the attainment of an object but refers rather to one's performative ability
  to sustain conditions of intimacy" (p. 103, folio 86); and, from the author's earlier work, to come
  close is "a contingent and `always-­in-­the-­making` process of saintly orientations, which fell short
  of furnishing individuals with stable charismatic dispositions yet enabled their reputations for
  `other-­worldly` access" (Kasmani 2017b, 81) (p. 97, folio 80).
- **Gendered presumption at the shrine, evidenced by an incident.** A woman asked "`Kya yeh pir
  hain`," meaning, is this (person) a pir (spiritual guide)? — and meant the male anthropologist, not
  Zaheda (p. 103, folio 86). "Such gendered bias proceeds from the common perception, however
  disputable, that most spiritual guides in Sehwan are men." (p. 103, folio 86, note 2)
