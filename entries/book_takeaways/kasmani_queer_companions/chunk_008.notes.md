# kasmani_queer_companions — chunk 008 — pp. 141–158

Continuous ethnographic prose with block-quoted fakir speech, two figure captions and no verse
except one couplet. The chunk **straddles a chapter boundary**. It opens mid-sentence in the tail of
chapter 4, `Love in a Time of Celibacy` — the fragment "3), Dost's trajectory demonstrated a
remarkable shift from the doubting position of an aspiring fakir to one who was firmly grounded as a
talib, a desirer-seeker" (p. 141, folio 124) — runs the section `Love over Loyalty` (p. 141, folio
124) to the end of chapter 4 (p. 146, folio 129), then opens **chapter 5, `Worlding Fakirs, Fairies,
and the Dead`** (p. 147, folio not stated), and carries its section `Two Fakirs and a Graveyard`
(p. 152, folio 135) to the foot of p. 158, where it **breaks off mid-quotation**: "The body is all
Zuljanah, the head is that of my" (p. 158, folio 141).

**Folio: read off each page, not computed.** Seventeen of the eighteen pages print a folio in the
running footer, and every one of them is exactly PDF − 17, so the measured relation holds without
exception in this range: 141→124, 142→125, 143→126, 144→127, 145→128, 146→129, 148→131, 149→132,
150→133, 151→134, 152→135, 153→136, 154→137, 155→138, 156→139, 157→140, 158→141. The order within
the line is as briefed — odd PDF pages print the folio first (`124      Chapter four`,
`132      Chapter five`), even pages last (`Love in a Time of Celibacy        125`,
`Worlding Fakirs, Fairies, and the Dead        141`). **p. 147 is the chapter-5 opener and prints no
folio at all**; it is cited throughout as **(p. 147, folio not stated)** and no value is derived for
it. Note also that the running footer *changes text* at p. 148, from `Love in a Time of Celibacy` to
`Worlding Fakirs, Fairies, and the Dead`, independently confirming the chapter break at p. 147, as
does the endnote numbering, which runs 30–37 through chapter 4 and restarts at 1 and 2 in chapter 5.

**This chunk maps exactly one archive row**, `lal-shahbaz-qalandar`, and the check against all 169
rows is recorded as an explicit negative below along with the near-misses refused. The chunk's most
fully documented shrine — the shrine of Bodlo in Sehwan, with its celibacy doctrine, its
descent-based custodianship and its Auqaf court case — **is not an archive row**, and that is the
single most important thing a consolidator should know about this chunk: its richest institutional
material attaches to a shrine the archive does not carry.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **lal-shahbaz-qalandar** (the book calls him **"Lal"** throughout, and once "Lal, the saint of
  Sehwan"): the chunk **never prints the full name "Lal Shahbaz Qalandar"**, gives **no dates, no
  silsila, no ʿurs, no tomb description and no genealogy** — all **(not stated)**. What it gives is a
  saint functioning as the arbiter of movement, the object of non-human desire, and a figure read as
  a placeholder for ʿAli. See Doubts on the bare short-form name.
  - Arrival and departure are his to grant: "for most of my interlocutors, fakirs or otherwise,
    arriving in and departing from Sehwan was always contingent upon Lal's approval" (p. 150, folio
    133). The author's fakir interlocutors "were quick to remind me how the decision to leave or
    return wasn't mine to make in the first place" (p. 150, folio 133).
  - Two fairies from "the fabled mountains of Qaf (Koh-Qaf)" "had once arrived in Sehwan in their
    amorous pursuit of Lal", in Jamal's narrative (p. 154, folio 137).
  - Identified with ʿAli by some: "Her statement is reflective of what some in Sehwan believe, which
    is that Lal, the saint of Sehwan, is a placeholder for Ali, if not an avatar of Ali himself"
    (p. 156, folio 139). "This correspondence of saintly character is embedded in a landscape of
    imagination that surrounds the figure of Lal, a point to which I return later in the chapter"
    (p. 156, folio 139) — **the return is outside this chunk**.
  - The shrine as a gathering of all orders of being, in Shah-Bibi's words: "Of the shrine of Lal,
    she said, 'There exists an atmosphere where all are present: the ones made of light, made of
    fire, angels — of the entire universe. Friends/allies of Allah, prophets, all of them . . . every
    qutb, ghaws, wali, pir, fakir [categories of saintly figures]. Let me tell you this one thing,
    that this is the main tower!'" (p. 156, folio 139). The bracketed gloss is the book's own;
    **main tower** is her English term, "a reference to telecommunications masts and structures",
    emphasising "the high status of Sehwan as a site of divine reception" (p. 156, folio 139).
  - Sehwan as one of three axial points: "Sehwan, according to her description, was one among three
    vertebrae that hold the earth in place, Karbala and the Hijaz being the other two" (p. 155, folio
    138); the three "are extremely hard fragments of the earth… so hardened by the axial character of
    their function that these necessitate the direct reign of Mawla (a reference to Ali, holiest of
    Shiʿi figures)" (p. 156, folio 139).
  - Wrath inscribed in the landscape: "The nearby hill is laden with miraculous legends associated
    with the saint. The solemn memory of Lal's wrath was etched in its topography, where he was
    believed to have turned on its head what was once an ancient settlement" (p. 156, folio 139).
    The hill is "what locals call the 'inverted city' (ulta sheher) or 'fortress of the infidels'
    (kafir-qila)" (p. 147, folio not stated).
  - The saint's own precinct as contested space, in the chapter's summary of earlier chapters:
    "Amma's dealing with fakir spirits is inseparable from the spatial politics of the saint's
    durbar, in particular her claim to the men's prayer space where she sits every day"; and
    "Zaheda's condition of disorientation involves an ongoing struggle to reground herself in the
    saint's courtyard and beyond the space of the family" (p. 151, folio 134). The book's words for
    the precinct here are **durbar** and **courtyard**.
  - The saint's memory as a field of dispute: the chapter "also engages the many contestations that
    haunt the historical imagination of Sehwan as well as the memory of the saint" (p. 149, folio
    132).
  - Sehwan as a thoroughfare for non-human beings: "Not only was Sehwan of the fakirs a passage for
    winged horses and hybrid creatures, mythic giants and fairies, Shiʿi imams and those befriended
    by Allah (walis) — figures and phenomena Jamal had referred to in the plural as ghaybat — they
    were remarkably, for some, at accessible distance" (p. 150, folio 133).
  - Fakir institutional life in the town generally: "the elite sayyid figures of Sehwan" (p. 143,
    folio 126); "At other lodges in Sehwan, a hot seal is applied on the fakir's arm" (p. 143, folio
    126); "prostration completes a fakir's final act of total submission to his protector lord in
    Sehwan" (p. 144, folio 127); fig. 4.3 is captioned "A fakir pays respects to his sayyid master at
    another kafi in Sehwan, 2012" (p. 144, folio 127).

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

  folio 125). Nur-Shah "portrayed Bodlo anew as a married saintly figure" (pp. 142–143, folios

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Custodianship secured by descent, and an Auqaf court case defeated.** "Nur-Shah's family have long
  maintained that since Bodlo was indeed celibate, as present-day custodians of the shrine, they were
  in fact the direct descendants of his brother. This idea of direct lineage is, more critically, the
  same legal reasoning through which the shrine of Bodlo, despite its economic viability and promise,
  was secured back through a court case when the Pakistani state had sought to take control of the
  shrine under the Department of Auqaf." (p. 142, folio 125) **No date, no court and no case name**:
  all **(not stated)**. This is the chunk's one piece of hard shrine-economy and state-relations
  material and it attaches to a shrine with no archive row.
- **Celibacy as institutional doctrine, and its reversal from the top.** "Moreover, fakir ideals of
  celibacy at the lodge were built upon Bodlo's celibacy. All this, to my utter disbelief, would now
  be put into question, as Nur-Shah portrayed Bodlo anew as a married saintly figure." (pp. 142–143,
  folios 125–126) The custodian was "effectively turning an established discourse on its head while
  casting marriage as acceptable in both Islamic legal and mystical ways" (p. 143, folio 126). The
  author reads it as strategy: "His discourse, I reckoned, was a premeditated response to the recent
  developments, in particular the question of fakir marriages" (p. 142, folio 125).
- **The office of wado-fakir, and who handles the money.** "the lodge's everyday affairs, especially
  the distribution of tasks and the management of the shrine's daily income and fakir expenses, were
  in the hands of another fakir who held the office of wado-fakir, literally big or senior fakir"
  (pp. 141–142, folios 124–125). **Which shrine's daily income is not specified** — see Doubts.
- **Marriage with permission costs rank but not membership; marriage without it costs everything.**
  "the same year that Murad left the lodge for the love of a woman, the wado-fakir established a
  romantic liaison with another woman at the shrine, also a disciple of the custodian. They were
  eventually married. In contrast with Murad's, this match, however, had met the approval of the
  master. And while it meant that the wado-fakir would have to give up his chief role at the lodge,
  settling for a lesser position, he had, unlike Murad, managed to salvage his membership in the
  fakir fraternity." (p. 142, folio 125) Restated at p. 145, folio 128: "The wado-fakir, for instance,
  had married with the approval of the murshid and so, despite the change in rank, he was able to
  retain his wardi and with it a place among fakirs." "There were fathers and married fakirs at the
  lodge, after all" (p. 145, folio 128).
- **Excommunication as an available sanction, not used here.** "Fakir intransigence was rarely
  tolerated, and patrons were known to excommunicate wayward fakirs, although this was not reported
  in Murad's case. It was the giving up of the wardi that had become the shorthand for his story."
  (p. 145, folio 128)
- **Initiation at the kafi, as a death and rebirth.** "As fakirs are initiated at the kafi, they are
  believed to pass through death to a newly born status. This passage is marked by ceremony and
  ritual. These are intimately conducted, private affairs, though some fakirs have described to me
  parts of what takes place." (p. 143, folio 126) The sequence: "the initiate must shave his body
  entirely, upon which he is given a ritual bath by a senior fakir, just as the dead are washed
  before burial. Then dressed in just a loincloth, he is presented to the murshid. He first
  prostrates himself, expressing his complete submission, then, placing one thumb on the other, the
  initiate stands in the audience of his master. He returns born anew, as it were, equipped with a
  red garment, a new name, and a reformed status as a confirmed fakir." (p. 143, folio 126)
- **A branded seal at other lodges.** "At other lodges in Sehwan, a hot seal is applied on the
  fakir's arm. With it, a pact of hierarchy between the master and fakir is also sealed (see fig.
  4.3)." (p. 143, folio 126)
- **Begging as the first act of the new fakir.** "As a first symbolic act, the new fakir is sent to
  beg in the surrounding areas, sealing his status of servitude." (p. 143, folio 126) "As a token of
  this new master-disciple relationship, she notes, the novice is given a secret word or unique
  formula for meditation (Ortis 2020, 317)." (pp. 143–144, folios 126–127)
- **The langot and the wardi as the garments of the office.** Ortis's terms are quoted: the langot is
  "the garment that Murad had once mentioned, the 'sex-cover' as Ortis calls it, or 'a symbol of the
  sexual abstinence [the fakir] is committed to'" (p. 143, folio 126). The **wardi** is the
  shroud-like outer garment whose surrender ends fakir status: "Taking off the shroud-like garment is
  a symbolic occasion of return to straight paths of the social. In departing from the kafi, Murad
  reentered the world of the living, for without a shroud he is less likely to be read as (socially)
  dead." (p. 145, folio 128)
- **The sayyid position on fakir marriage, uniform across informants.** "None of the elite sayyid
  figures of Sehwan I have spoken to over the years have categorically denied or discounted the
  possibility and permissibility of marriage for their fakir initiates. Yet each one of them stressed
  that an engagement with or a pursuing of profane and corporeal pleasures was bound to fundamentally
  affect the capacities and powers of a fakir… Their point was that loyalty to one's murshid and
  complete devotion to a saintly figure precluded the pursuit of other intimacies, rival attachments,
  and competing orientations." (p. 143, folio 126)
- **A generational turnover at the lodge, dated.** "This interview took place at a time when the old
  guard of the lodge was being replaced by somewhat younger fakirs. Dost, for example, was acquiring
  a reputation for speaking on matters of faith, and another young fakir was dealing with new and
  greater responsibilities of managing the administrative affairs of the lodge." (p. 142, folio 125)
  The interview was "during the final days of my stay in 2013" (p. 141, folio 124).
- **Interview conditions set by the custodian.** "As per his directions, the interaction was not
  recorded on a mechanical device. He, however, permitted me to take notes." (p. 142, folio 125)
  Access to the lodge itself ran on his "tacit consent" (p. 141, folio 124).
- **Self-appointed custodianship created out of a vision.** Jamal "had come to unearth the tombs of
  two fairies, now a shrine of which he is a self-appointed custodian" (p. 147, folio not stated);
  the charge is issued in the vision itself — "From this day on, you are the custodian of my home;
  establish my sign [nishan]" (p. 155, folio 138). "Over the years and based on numerous visions like
  these, Jamal has unearthed a great number of lost graves, restoring in a way a large part of the
  site" (p. 154, folio 137). His practical work: "I mark the places of these innocent [people], and I
  water these [graves]" (p. 154, folio 137).
- **A shrine complex accused of swallowing graves.** "when the fairies identified their lost tombs to
  Jamal, they also brought to his attention the hidden graves of many others. These, as Jamal
  explained, had been devoured over the years by the encroachments of the adjacent shrine and fakir
  lodge. As a lone fakir with no means, Jamal was in no position to pick a bone with the powerful
  sayyid patrons of the neighboring complex." (p. 158, folio 141) **The adjacent shrine, lodge and
  patrons are not named**: **(not stated)**.
- **A rival fakir's dwelling, built out from a cordon.** "Propped against its southern boundary wall,
  on slightly raised ground, Bibi had cordoned off an entire section of the graveyard (see fig. 5.2).
  Where there were once wild bushes now stood a two-room mud structure." (p. 152, folio 135) Jamal's
  by contrast is "his spot under the tree" (p. 152, folio 135): "he established a home under a
  eucalyptus tree, where a few blankets on the ground and some earthen pots still mark his presence"
  (p. 154, folio 137), set there "almost ten years ago" (p. 154, folio 137) — **relative only; no
  year**: **(not stated)**.
- **A shrine planned in advance from a dream, down to the fittings.** "she revealed to me how she had
  identified, through another vision, the exact location of her and her husband's future graves… In a
  particular dream, she was shown where the new entrance would be, where precisely the two would be
  buried, and even the space where pilgrims to their graves would gather in the future. So detailed
  was this dreamed layout that she was also able to point out where the water dispenser would be
  placed." (p. 158, folio 141) **Both fakirs "were invested in long-term physical modifications of
  the graveyard"** (p. 158, folio 141).
- **A healing and guidance practice building a following.** "Soon enough, she was drawing a small
  following. I too observed her offering spiritual guidance and praying over materials and substances
  as part of a burgeoning healing repertoire." (p. 156, folio 139)
- **Two custodians in open conflict over one site.** "He was critical of her growing reach, what he
  had come to consider an encroachment on the graveyard. He was also unconvinced of Bibi's claims of
  spiritual expertise, which he attributed to dark, demonic forces as opposed to saintly sources.
  Bibi was no fan of Jamal either. Tensions were soon simmering in the graveyard, each fakir accusing
  the other of false pretense and black magic. In fact, they both confirmed to me on different
  occasions that they had fought verbally as well as through spiritual means." (pp. 156–157, folios
  139–140) "Each accused the other of involving proxy spirits that they both had at their disposal."
  (p. 157, folio 140)
- **A woman fakir's dis/guise (bhes) as a rule-governed regime.** "In order to endure the spirits and
  demons local to the site, Bibi followed a strict regime of prohibitions that determined codes of
  diet, dress, and sexual practice, all part of her fakir dis/guise or bhes. She was always dressed
  in a plain all-white shalwar-kamiz. The masculine cut of her garment was unusual for women to wear.
  Still, she covered her head with a long white drape additionally customized with a colorful
  patchwork cap on top. The many rings on her fingers and a studded necklace accessorized her simple
  outfit to fakir standards." (p. 153, folio 136)
- **Marriage kept as cover, and gender inverted to claim status.** Bibi introduced the old man "as her
  second husband… Though, to stay true to my notes from the day, in the same sitting she also
  described him as her wife, pointing to his daily duties of cooking, cleaning, and caring for her…
  Bibi used this example to bolster her claim that she was indeed a true man in terms of her
  spiritual caliber." (p. 152, folio 135) "like Amma of the mosque, she felt the need to remind me
  how her case was not like that of ordinary women or that her relationship with her husband did not
  involve sex. Conversely, she needed him around in order not to draw suspicion, given her
  more-than-domestic circumstances in the graveyard." (p. 152, folio 135)
- **Fakirs suspected as state informants.** "I had many a time entertained the popular belief that
  fakirs also make for perfect information-gathering agents for the state" (p. 157, folio 140). Bibi
  "claimed to work for a transnational anticorruption task force. She even carried a somewhat
  official-looking id in her pocket, an organization I wasn't able to find a trace of through

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The saint overturning a city, attributed to belief.** "The solemn memory of Lal's wrath was etched
  in its topography, where he was believed to have turned on its head what was once an ancient
  settlement" (p. 156, folio 139) — and the site is locally called "the 'inverted city' (ulta sheher)"
  (p. 147, folio not stated).
- **Fairies in love with the saint.** Two fairies "originally from the fabled mountains of Qaf
  (Koh-Qaf)… had once arrived in Sehwan in their amorous pursuit of Lal", attributed to "Jamal's
  narrative" (p. 154, folio 137).
- **The graveyard of glass, in Jamal's words.** "it is a graveyard made of glass, such that he is able
  to communicate with its inhabitants" (p. 154, folio 137). And: "Once a year, there comes a moon; . . .
  once every year there is a moon such that in the full moon all of them come out of their graves,
  with their shrouds. I do not understand their language [laughs]; I am surprised at what language
  they speak. . . . One says, my home is there and the water [of the river] has destroyed it;
  'retrieve the sign of my home so that I may sleep in peace.'" (p. 154, folio 137) The ellipses and
  brackets are the book's own.
- **The appearance of Jiji-pak, told at length by Jamal.** The shrouded dead stand around him; "I was
  reciting [Quranic verses] but to no effect, so I attempted to run"; then "a light came and stood
  before me. A human being, but appearing as light, illuminated from head to toe, drawing close to
  me… Then to me, it said, 'Don't be scared, fakir! I'm Jiji-pak, the veiled one.'" He answers,
  "'Either you are a hoor [houri; virginal figures in Islamic paradise] or a fairy but even more
  beautiful than a fairy, prettier than hoors you are! But human you are not!'" She replies, "'I'm
  Jiji-pak, the veiled one; no human to this day has held me in sight. The situation has now crossed
  limits and for this reason I beckon you. From this day on, you are the custodian of my home;
  establish my sign [nishan].'" (p. 155, folio 138)
- **Devraj's bargain, refused twice.** "'What are your terms?' He said, 'Drink this blood of the
  buffalo.' I was like . . . oh, he's going to make me drink blood!? I said, 'I won't drink blood.'"
  Then: "'If you do not drink blood,' Devraj continues, 'then chant the name of Lord Krishna.'…
  I said, 'If I were to do om namaste deva, wouldn't I upset my Lord?'" (p. 149, folio 132) **Devraj's
  answer is never given** — "Just when we are curious to hear how Devraj responds, Jamal pauses"
  (p. 149, folio 132) and switches to the return of Jesus: **(not stated)**.
- **A fire-figure singing Hindu devotional song.** "There, inside, is a figure made of fire; it is a
  figure of fire that sings bhajan; bhajan of the Hindus you know!" (p. 148, folio 131); Jamal then
  "sings aloud a Hindu devotional song… he is dancing: dhig, dhig, dhig, dha; he utters musical notes
  as he moves, imitating rather poorly postures of Hindu deities" (p. 148, folio 131).
- **The imminent return of Jesus, announced in hushed tones.** "Now I am certain, hazrat Isa [Jesus]
  is about to return. His time has come. The signs have appeared in the world." (p. 149, folio 132)
- **Beings crossing the sky twice a day, felt in the body.** Bibi's trance — eyes closed, finger
  raised, "a continuous wave of yawns", shivering — "was due to otherworldly beings whose routine
  passage across the Sehwan skies, once at dawn and again at dusk, would affect her in this manner"
  (p. 154, folio 137).
- **A hundred-and-five-year-old living a second cycle.** "she claimed to be 105 years old when one
  would've guessed her to be fifty at the most. She insisted that she is currently living through a
  second cycle and hence appears younger than her cumulative age." (p. 157, folio 140)
- **A husband returned from fifty-five years of wandering.** "the new man in the graveyard was
  supposed to be her original husband, who in this latest version of Bibi's life wasn't dead but had
  in fact returned from a fifty-five-year period of ascetic wandering" (p. 158, folio 141).
- **The moon breaking open, and the horse with a human head** — the vision the chunk ends inside:
  "from within the moonlight emerged a cloud… one of its wings stretched from here to there… And you
  know the Zuljanah [the legendary horse of Hussayn in the battle of Karbala]? The body is all
  Zuljanah, the head is that of my" (p. 158, folio 141). **The sentence does not finish in this
  chunk.**
- **Dreams as the ordinary route to Sehwan.** "Unlike most people who are drawn to it, I was not
  directed to Sehwan in a dream" (p. 150, folio 133); Murad "once insisted that not being able to
  recall a dream is no proof of its nonoccurrence" (p. 150, folio 133). Bibi "had been directed to
  Sehwan and particularly to this site through a series of dreams and visions" (p. 155, folio 138).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The governing claim of the chapter-4 close.** "Fakir disagreements on intimacy opened up a way to
  understand how spirituality is not always in antagonistic relation to sexuality, but rather, the
  two are complexly conversant." (p. 146, folio 129) And: "the story of Murad stands in for the idea
  that saintly bonds, even when secured through rites of initiation and afforded by spiritual
  masters, can come undone or show signs of disaffection, especially in the face of rival intimacies
  and competing objects of desire." (p. 146, folio 129)
- **The offence was not marriage but the taking of an object of desire.** "The problem, it seemed,
  wasn't solely a question of marriage or of abandoning the ideal of celibacy and sexual abstinence,
  as it had first appeared. It was in fact a taking up of an object of desire, which threatened the
  very pact of unquestionable submission that inaugurates fakir life at the lodge." (p. 143, folio
  126) "It is not simply marriage but Murad's defiance that cost him dearly." (p. 145, folio 128)
- **Submission theorised through three borrowed formulations.** Malamud (1994, 434): the murshid–murid
  relation is "intensely personal", and submitting to the murshid's will is a "step in learning to
  surrender to the will of God"; the murid "must turn over 'the governance of his worldly affairs and
  control over his body to the direction of his master'" (p. 145, folio 128). Shahzad Bashir "has
  evocatively described such total voluntary submission to Sufi masters through the corporeal
  metaphor 'corpses in morticians' hands' (2011, 187)" (p. 145, folio 128). Malamud (1996, 96): the
  novice, "comparable with 'newborns who need the suckling of their masters,' accepts the doubly
  gendered role of the murshid as procreator and nurturer, father as well as mother, which according
  to one reading is an infantilizing and feminizing of oneself as the willing and passive receptacle"
  (p. 144, folio 127).
- **A disagreement between the author's sources on the gender of the fakir's servitude.** "Ortis
  (2020, 319), however, suggests that it is the servile condition of the wife that better corresponds
  to the status of the male fakir in relation to his spiritual masters." (p. 144, folio 127) Ortis
  (2020, 317) also reads the initiation acts as symbolising "the fakir's freedom from both his
  virility and pride", the body "first of all rid of its erotic potential by shaving the hair . . .
  and then humiliated by earth put on his head", after which "the fakir 'has neither past nor
  future'" (p. 143, folio 126).
- **Variation across accounts, acknowledged.** "Details may slightly vary across fakir accounts and
  descriptions, though what's consistent is that the spiritual birth of the novice comes with a pact
  of complete submission, uttered in words and acted out in ritual." (p. 144, folio 127)
- **Murad's continuing effect after expulsion.** "Though no longer at the lodge, he remained,
  throughout my time in the field, the most talked-about fakir, his presence most palpable in the
  lives he had affected and touched" (p. 146, folio 129); and on Dost, "Murad had a principal role to
  play in this coming-of-age story. The training and tutelage he offered to Dost had proven to be as
  instrumental as the space his departure created for Dost to flourish on his own." (p. 141, folio
  124)
- **The chapter-5 thesis: worlding with saints.** "this chapter then is also about how fakirs mobilize
  such worlds or turn worldly as a result of their embroilment in human-nonhuman knots, sacred
  enmeshments, and saintly historical affordances, a process I further elaborate as worlding with
  saints." (p. 150, folio 133) Against the renunciatory reading: "fakirs are persons who, in everyday
  parlance, reject the world or renounce the worldly. Their unworlding figures are least likely to be
  read as futural or aspirational. Yet the graveyard tells a different story: fakir ties to the living
  and the worldly are made dense through their affective attachments with its deceased and displaced
  actors." (pp. 149–150, folios 132–133)
- **World defined by borrowing.** Anderson and Harrison (2010, 8): the term world points "to the
  context or background against which particular things take meaning or accrue significance", "a
  mobile but more or less stable ensemble of practices, involvements, relations, capacities,
  tendencies and affordances" (p. 150, folio 133).
- **The unrevealed is the not-yet-revealed, not the imaginary.** "That what counts as unrevealed, even
  hidden, in fakir stories refers not to the absent or the imaginary but the yet-to-be-revealed.
  These are participants who might be differently present in a situation or figurations whose
  apprehension, visibly or otherwise, depends on heightened modes of knowing that fakirs strive for
  or gain through an intimacy with saints." (p. 150, folio 133)
- **Place as material and imaginary at once.** "there exists a correspondence, a slippage if you will,
  between physical contours of a place, its topographic or architectural features, and its
  nonmaterial knowledge, that is to say, its dialogical and visionary potentials" (pp. 150–151, folios
  133–134); Mittermaier (2008, 48) is invoked for the view that understandings of saintly places are
  "incomplete unless they are conceptualized within a notion of space that includes both the material
  and the imaginary" (p. 151, folio 134); and Kasmani et al. (2020) on affect as an "indistinct yet
  critical volume" that "animatingly moves through, between, or alongside other volumes and engages
  its subjects in dialogue across invisible borderlands" (p. 151, folio 134).
- **Thin places and a clearing outside linear time.** Armbrecht (2009, 204) on thin places, "places
  where one's nerve endings are bare", is used "to explore how through strands of feeling and emotion,
  a place such as this graveyard is rendered a diaphanous site of heterotemporal feeling"; and
  Kasmani (2019b, 35): "thin reminds us that there is a clearing outside linear time, that every now
  and then, an opening is created for feeling and knowing, knowing by feeling" (p. 151, folio 134).
- **Fakir storytelling read as historiography.** "Fakir narrations of the graveyard are, in this sense,
  actively and continually historicizing narrations of Sehwan. These are also hauntological citations
  of that which remains submerged or traces of the graveyard's displaced actors and agencies."
  (p. 151, folio 134) With Slaby (2020, 174): "the past . . . lingers on, individually and
  collectively, within the depths of corporeal comportment and within material texture of social,
  institutional, worldly formations" (p. 151, folio 134).
- **Incoherence treated as evidence rather than defect.** "Complex, polyvocal, and incoherent at times
  as Jamal's stories are, his present-day accounts of the graveyard are accounts of Sehwan over time.
  His regular encounters with Hindu gods and Muslim saints, with white souls from the 'time of the
  English,' as he puts it, and with spirits of an ancient world are part of the ways in which deeper
  ecologies and sedimented histories of a place like Sehwan become manifest in everyday forms."
  (p. 149, folio 132)
- **The author's own doubt kept in the text rather than resolved.** "Jamal's reservations about Bibi
  weren't completely unfounded. In all the years that I've known her, I too have at times found her
  stories hard to grapple with, if not baffling. Extraordinary accounts were routine when it came to
  my encounters with fakirs. That wasn't the issue; rather, Bibi, as a matter of fact, was full of
  intrigue." (p. 157, folio 140) And after the husband reversal: "This cast a long shadow of doubt on
  everything I had gathered from her, yet I remained all the more invested, riveted in fact."
  (p. 158, folio 141) "Retrospectively, I have also wondered if the 'sources' that had approved of my
  'case,' as she put it, were at all spiritual" (p. 157, folio 140).
- **The graveyard as the book's final setting.** "In the final of five settings, I turn to a graveyard
  in Sehwan" (p. 146, folio 129); "it sets the last act that stages how grove, shrine, courtyard,
  lodge, and graveyard are intimate settings, not only because fakirs dwell in these sites or their
  lives unfold in these places, but also because these bring fakirs into critical intimacy with
  greater histories and the polysemic imaginations of Sehwan itself" (p. 146, folio 129).
- **Intimacy with saints as a widening of horizon.** "the graveyard offers a heterotemporal view into
  how intimate ties with saints expand fakir social and historical horizons, an unfolding by which
  they come to reappraise their place in the world" (p. 149, folio 132).
