**Succession, endowment and money (all from the Appendix's last page):**

- **Succession restricted to a lineage, and declared perpetual** — "By the blessing of their glance,
  up to (the time of) Shaykh Yahya Madani, the succession to authority [sajjadagi-i mashyakhat] was
  restricted to the descendants of the revered Shaykh Kamal ad-Din (God be pleased with him). Up to
  our day, it is current with his successors, and it shall remain established until the day of
  resurrection" (p. 199, folio not stated). The bracketed Persian term is the book's own.
- **The transfer of authority is material: robe, place, document** — "clothing him with the robe he
  wore, he put him in his own place and gave him a succession document" (p. 199, folio not stated).
- **A quantified return of wealth to a khanqah** — "he brought with him 13,000 tankas of gold, silver,
  and other property"; "Shaykh Nasir ad-Din Mahmud saw 13 camels laden with money and other
  property" and asked "How did you collect so much of the world?" (p. 199, folio not stated).
- **The disposal of it, and the device used** — "He rubbed each tanka coin with ink and gave each one
  to the scholars tied in a knot. He said, 'It is black; accept `it:'[text-layer?] [it."]`. By this
  means he gave most of the wealth to scholars, pious people, and the poor, remaining himself
  detached" (p. 199, folio not stated).
- **A state stipend and the ruling that made it acceptable** — Tatar Khan's "rescript for a daily
  prayer ration of 80 tankas"; the master ruled, "Since the prayer ration `carne[text-layer?] [came]`
  without any request or tale on your part, it is in the category of voluntary gifts; accept
  `it:'[text-layer?] [it."]`" (p. 199, folio not stated). A clean statement of the conditions under which
  Chishti practice accepts government support.
- **Pilgrimage counted** — "he went on pilgrimage to the house of the Ka'ba, and performed seven hajj
  pilgrimages, the pilgrimage to the revered Prophet (prayer and peace be upon him), and the
  pilgrimage to Jerusalem", returning via Khurasan where "sultans came from diverse regions and
  kingdoms ... praising him and bringing many gifts" (p. 199, folio not stated).

**ʿUrs, ziyarat and shrine administration (from the chapter 5 notes):**

- **The book's etymology of *ʿurs*, with its source and a rival sense** — *Fawa'id al-fu'ad* "p. 209,
  gives the etymology of 'urs as 'getting married,' but also mentions another meaning, 'the alighting
  of a caravan at night'" (p. 210, folio 199).
- **Early opposition and early use, dated** — "Jurists such as the Baghdadian Ibn al-Jawzi (d. 1201)

  thirteenth century" (p. 210, folio 199).

  saints' death anniversaries were called birthdays (natalitia) as a sign of rebirth into eternal
  life" (p. 210, folio 199); and "The Maliki jurist Ibn al-Hajj (d. 1336) refers to a candle festival
  for all the saints on 15 Sha'ban" (p. 212, folio 201).
- **The legal status of pilgrimage ritual, in the tradition's own words** — *Adab al-talibin* "fol.
  22b, 24b. The text frequently uses the Arabic phrase bi-la haraj, meaning 'there is no harm or
  crime in it' from an objective legal point of view; the pilgrimage rituals are harmless but not
  required" (p. 212, folio 201).
- **Circumambulation, and tomb-pilgrimage preferred to the hajj** — Goldziher "II:288, on

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The book's date convention, which the archive should note before quoting any date from it** —
  "All dates in this book are given according to the Gregorian calendar or Common Era (C.E.) unless
  otherwise noted; occasionally we employ the Muslim lunar hijri calendar, which begins at 622 C.E."
  (p. 200, folio not stated).
- **The polemical core of chapter 1, stated in its first note** — of Trimingham's *The Sufi Orders in
  Islam*: "Surprisingly, this study has recently been reprinted (Oxford University Press, 1998) in
  unaltered form, with a new preface by John Voll that lauds the book as still providing the standard
  account of Sufi orders. It is our contention that the main historiographical thesis of the book
  needs to be rejected" (p. 201, folio 190).
- **The reason given, with Trimingham's own words turned against him** — he "explicitly states that
  'the decline in the orders is symptomatic of the failure of Muslims to adapt their traditional
  interpretation `oflslam[text-layer?] [of Islam]` for life in a new `dimension''[text-layer?]
  [dimension"]` (pp. 256-57), i.e., the failure of Muslims to become totally Westernized"; Hodgson is
  invoked as "One of the most powerful critiques of the simplistic notion of the 'decline'
  `oflslamic[text-layer?] [of Islamic]` civilization" (p. 201, folio 190).
- **A claim about the medieval subjects' own modernity** — Berger's reversal of Feuerbach, "the only
  reason we can conceptualize God is because he first conceptualized us", is followed by: "This
  `'modern''[text-layer?] ['modern']` argument had already been anticipated by medieval scholars,
  including the subjects of this monograph" (p. 201, folio 190).
- **A working definition of *tazkira*, useful to the archive's own source criticism** — "The Persian
  word tazkira is used for any anthology with a biographical organization; we use it here for
  biographies of Sufi saints, though the term can cover literary and other anthologies" (p. 205,
  folio 194).
- **Named as the standard of the genre** — *Akhbar al-akhyar* is "the foremost biography of medieval
  Indian saints" (p. 207, folio 196).
- **A methodological target named twice** — "Examples of this obsession with beginnings include R. C.
  Zaehner, Hindu and Muslim Mysticism ... and Julian Baldick, Mystical Islam" (p. 205, folio 194).
- **Lineage claims can be engineered to bypass a figure** — "The Naqshbandi lineage goes via Khwaja
  Kalan, a son of Muhammad Baqi Billah, thus bypassing Ahmad Sirhindi" (p. 202, folio 191).
- **A thesis attributed to Rizvi and not adopted** — "The thesis of political rivalry between Chishtis
  and Naqshbandis is maintained by S. A. A. Rizvi" (p. 202, folio 191).
- **A case of textual contamination identified** — of a parallel passage, "The last example appears to
  indicate a case of imperfect literary transmission, as Shah Wali Allah inadvertently inserts
  Chishti practices into the account of the Shattaris" (p. 203, folio 192).
- **Yoga inside a Sufi manual** — a longer version of a zikr "is linked with the lotus position, the
  chief of the 84 yogic postures" (p. 203, folio 192).
- **A comparative collection of forty orders' practices** — "The Clear Fountain on the Forty Paths,
  written by the North `Mrican[text-layer?] [African]` scholar Muhammad al-Sanusi al-Idrisi (d.
  1859); as its name implies, it contains examples of zikr practices from 40 different Sufi orders
  from many Muslim countries" (p. 203, folio 192).
- **A dating claim about *sama'* evidence** — "Most literary evidence for the role of sama' in
  disseminating Sufi beliefs through the Asian subcontinent derives from the Mughal period and later"
  (p. 204, folio 193).
- **A Chishti doctrinal innovation identified by the authors** — two citations "are especially
  significant since they reflect the innovative reinterpretation of tawajud advanced by the Chishti
  theorists on `sama:[text-layer?] [sama']`" (p. 205, folio 194).
- **Zarradi's polemic summarised** — the reference is to "ikhtiyar al-mazhab al-mu'ayyan, i.e.,
  opting for the fixed or rote doctrine, which Zarradi interprets as an approach disparaged by
  Muhammad, even though it was subsequently enthroned by traditionists and legalists, the Sufis alone
  having adhered to the spirit of the Prophetic mandate" (p. 204, folio 193).
- **A critique of an earlier survey for citing without quoting** — of Molé: "Mole's analysis offers
  thematic continuity but not a holistic perspective ... yet he gives no quotations for these
  sources, nor does he compare their contents with those of the works he does cite" (p. 204, folio
  193).
- **A translator's error corrected** — Ibn Battuta's *Travels*, "p. 191, where the translator
  misunderstands this as a visit (ziyarat) to a living person" (p. 211, folio 200).
- **A European scholar's misspelling recorded** — "(here Chishti is misspelled `'Jashti'`)" (p. 209,
  folio 198).
- **The obscurity of a shrine town, quoted from Storey** — Barnawa "is so obscure that it 'is not
  mentioned either in the Mainpuri District Gazetteer or in the Imperial Gazetteer `oflndia[text-layer?]
  [of India]`'" (p. 209, folio 198).
- **A twentieth-century Chishti apologetic identified** — "Ashraf 'Ali Thanvi, described in chapter 6.
  Thanvi defends the Chishti masters in general, and Shaykh `Qytb[text-layer?] [Qutb]` ad-Din in
  particular, from any charge of laxity in complying with Islamic ritual practices" (p. 200, folio
  not stated).
- **Chapter 6's target: the nationalisation of Sufism** — "a two-part film recently produced by the
  Ministry `oflnformation[text-layer?] [of Information]` of the Government of India, entitled The Lamp
  in the Niche. Despite its striking images and pertinent historical information, the film's
  strongest emphasis is on Sufism as an Indian phenomenon" (p. 213, folio 202).
- **A named limit on the "Chishti revival"** — "Two of the foremost scholars of South Asian Islam,
  Eaton and Gilmartin, stress the limits of eighteenth century Chishtiyya, limits either within
  material culture (Eaton) or in the context of a regional power struggle in which not only British
  but also Hindu, Sikh and rival Sufi groups and Muslim reformers, all converge to limit the extent
  and the duration of what is called 'the Chishti revival' (Gilmartin)" (p. 213, folio 202).
- **A critique of "legitimation" as the dominant analytic**, quoted from Sheldon Pollock — "it is
  typical of such science, as the common sense of modernity and capitalism, to reduce one of these
  terms (culture) to the other (power)" (p. 213, folio 202).
- **The authors declare an altered translation** — Digby's translation is used "with some omissions
  and minor corrections" (p. 200, folio not stated), and Colby's "with some modifications" (p. 206,
  folio 195).

## Doubts

- **Both archive mappings rest on a bare author-name and nothing else, and both are recorded under
  the 18 September 2026 ruling.** `data-darbar` is taken from "'Ali Hujwiri" as the author of *Kashf
  al-mahjub* (p. 204, folio 193); `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` from "Shaykh Farid ad-Din" /
  "Shaikh Farid-u'd-Din Ganj-i-Shakar" as a biographical subject (p. 207, folio 196; p. 213, folio
  202). **Neither Lahore's Data Darbar nor Pakpattan is named**, and no death year, tomb, order or
  ʿurs is given for either man in these pages. Nothing here can enter an entry as a fact about a
  shrine; what enters is that Ernst and Lawrence lean on each man's book. **Neither name is ambiguous
  between two archive rows** — `data-darbar` is the only row whose principal figure is al-Hujwiri and
  `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` the only Fariduddin Ganjshakar row — so the doubt is whether an
  author-only mention should be recorded at all, not which row it belongs to.
- **The one Lahore sentence that is not an imprint, written reversibly.** "mentioned the many tombs
  worth visiting in Lahore" (p. 211, folio 200) attaches tombs to the toponym but names **no shrine,
  no saint and no custodian**, so under the interim bare-toponym convention it takes **no id** and
  sits under "Other saints, sites and events". **If that ruling reverses, this sentence still could
  not be assigned to one row**: the index holds more than a dozen Lahore rows (`data-darbar`,
  `bibi-pak-daman`, `madho-lal-hussain`, `shrine-of-mian-mir`, `shah-jamal`, `peer-makki`,
  `shrine-of-mauj-darya-bukhari`, `shrine-of-baba-shah-chiragh` and others), and the book names none
