## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Refusal of land grants, and total almsgiving, as a saint's policy.** "Farid ad-Din later refused
  land grants that were offered to him, and routinely gave away all money and other gifts brought
  to his lodge" (p. 81, folio 67). The consequence is stated as material, not moral: "his family is
  said to have literally faced starvation on numerous occasions" (p. 81, folio 67).
- **Pilgrimage to saints' tombs as an equivalent of the hajj — the chunk's key doctrinal warrant for
  ziyarat.** The early Chishtis "internalized the imagery of the pilgrimage … speaking of the true
  pilgrimage as a journey into the depths of the heart in search of God. They also exalted the
  rites of pilgrimage to the tombs of earlier saints, suggesting that such acts of homage bestowed
  spiritual merit equivalent to the pilgrimage to Mecca and Medina" (p. 81, folio 67).
- **Offerings at the grave as the endowment of the saint's dependants — stated as the saint's own
  provision.** On his deathbed Nizam ad-Din's servants plead, "But we are poor men. After you have
  gone, what will become of us?" and he answers, "The charity that will arrive at my grave will
  suffice for you;'" — and when asked who would divide it, "That man who is able to relinquish his
  own portion" (p. 90, folio 76, with note 23). This is the chunk's single clearest statement that a
  shrine's income is expected to arise and to be distributed.
- **Deathbed liquidation of the estate.** "He instructed his servant, Iqbal: \"If anything of any
  sort remains in this house, it will have to be accounted for on the Day of Judgment. You must
  distribute everything, except the minimum which is necessary for the daily subsistence of the
  dervishes.\" … \"Give it all away and sweep the room clean:'\" — and then "As soon as they cleared
  the storerooms, a host of people gathered and snatched up the goods" (p. 90, folio 76, with note
  23).
- **Succession by regalia, and a numbered limit on successors.** "Only at the death of the teacher,
  and through the inheritance of his mystic regalia, will a successor saint claim to be the greatest
  among equals, the dominant successor (khalifa) among all those designated as successors to a
  particular master" (p. 86, folio 72). Nizam ad-Din "allegedly declared, regarding the numerous
  Sufi cloaks (khirqas) that he had given to disciples, that \"only four actually confer
  successorship; all the others have been khirqas `ofblessing`[text-layer?] [of blessing].\""
  (p. 90, folio 76, with note 24).
- **Tomb-building named as one of three mechanisms of posthumous fame.** "How well did his family
  and followers perpetuate his memory through the construction of an impressive tomb/shrine, through
  the oral dissemination of his teachings and influence, and also (of equal importance) through the
  creation of a literary legacy extolling him to future generations?" (p. 86, folio 72).
- **Custodians named as an interested party in hagiography.** Later hagiographies "reflect inflated
  memories, conscious rewordings, or other distortions because their authors incorporate into the
  saint's biography the expectations of his family and followers, not the least of whom were
  custodians and beneficiaries of his tomb/shrine" (p. 91, folio 77).
- **The lodge as a node in a patronage network, quoted from an unnamed scholar.** "Leading Chishti
  shaykhs dispersed from Shaikh Nizam ad-Din's lodge to all parts of the empire and often enjoyed
  the patronage of provincial rulers (seeking to legitimate themselves both as Muslims and as
  Indians). Conversely many young Indian-born Muslims journeyed from all over India to live in or
  near that shaykh's lodge, later to return to their native lands, where they would establish
  daughter Chishti lodges and enjoy the patronage of local rulers:'" (pp. 83–84, folios 69–70, with
  note 5). **The scholar is named only as "One scholar": (not stated).**
- **Proximity to the city as a condition of remembered sainthood.** "His hospice lay on the outskirts
  of the major city of the Delhi Sultanate; had he lived anywhere else and been the same kind of
  person, it is unlikely that he would have been remembered as more than a minor Sufi saint"
  (p. 90, folio 76); the ideal saint must avoid "worldly people, merchants, soldiers, and government
  officials, including kings" and "yet live in proximity to them (i.e., near a city) and stay in
  touch with worldly people through his lay disciples" (p. 86, folio 72).
- **Refusing kings while living off their courtiers.** "Though Nizam ad-Din refused to see kings, he
  benefited both contemporaneously and retrospectively from the association that his lay disciples
  had with their imperial patrons" (p. 90, folio 76); "it was the elite who helped to establish his
  enduring fame" (p. 90, folio 76).
- **Sama' as an institution with rules of decorum.** Qutb ad-Din died over "the three days of his
  last `sama'` session" (p. 82, folio 68); at "a musical gathering presided over by the shaykh"
  Nizam ad-Din tells Amir Khusraw "You are connected with this world; you are not permitted to raise
  your hands when dancing:'" (p. 91, folio 77, with note 25); Digby's ideal shaykh should show
  ecstasy "linked with a refined sensibility to poetry and music" (p. 85, folio 71).
- **Qawwali as the mechanism of a saint's reputation at a distance.** "Nizam ad-Din, in turn,
  developed a youthful attachment to his future pir when, at the age of twelve, he heard a qawwal
  (musician) sing songs in praise of Farid ad-Din" (p. 80, folio 66).
- **Fasting, iftar and the refusal of food as charity by proxy.** "At the time of breaking of the
  fast (`iftar`), he would eat very little. When food was brought in the early morning, he would
  usually refuse it", with the reason: "Think how many poor people and beggars are suffering hunger
  and deprivation, huddled around the mosques and sleeping in the streets of the city-how can this
  food go down my throat?" (p. 88, folio 74). "For forty days before his death Shaykh Nizam ad-Din
  ate nothing" (p. 90, folio 76, with note 23).
- **Friday congregational prayer enforced by a threat.** "If a person fails to go to Friday prayer
  once, a black dot will appear on his heart; if twice, two black dots; if three times, the whole
  heart will become black:'" (p. 90, folio 76, with note 22).
- **Celibacy as the exception that proves the rule of marriage.** "Nizam ad-Din and Nasir ad-Din were
  the only two to practice voluntary celibacy. Though they did not enjoin celibacy on their
  followers, some chose to follow their example" (p. 80, folio 66); "Most shaykhs, like most
  Muslims, married; but the shaykhs, unlike their spiritual kin, had to maintain a personal regimen
  that with difficulty accommodated the stresses of family life" (p. 81, folio 67).
- **Language division of labour in Indian Sufi devotion.** Practice "did result in the composition of
  prayers, almost always in Arabic, and the recitation of poetry, almost always in Persian, on the
  part of nearly all medieval Indian masha'ikh" (p. 80, folio 66).
- **Naqshbandi exception on deference to the pir.** "In Indian Islam, except for the Naqshbandis,
  most Sufi masters acknowledge their teacher (pir) to be greater than themselves" (p. 86, folio
  72).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **A dream of the Prophet sends Mu'in ad-Din to India** — "it was in a dream that the Prophet
  appeared to him, directing that he go to India" (p. 80, folio 66). Given by the authors as what is
  reported, against their own "we do not know how Mu'in ad-Din became a Sufi".
- **A single pomegranate seed with unstated consequences** — Farid ad-Din "is alleged to have
  consumed a single seed of a pomegranate brought by Jalal ad-Din Tabrizi, with astonishing
  results" (p. 81, folio 67). Marked "alleged" by the authors.
- **Qutb ad-Din's three-day death in sama'**, emerging from ecstasy five times a day to pray (p. 82,
  folio 68).
- **Farid ad-Din describes an unseen man while prostrate in prayer** — the Turk with the chain and
  the earrings, narrated by Nizam ad-Din (p. 82, folio 68, with note 3).
- **Nizam ad-Din in trance** — his disciple found him "sitting perfectly still; not a muscle moved on
  his face. His eyes were wide-open", and afterwards "his eyes rolled as though he were in a drunken
  state" (p. 82, folio 68, with note 2). Attributed to "one of his disciples".
- **Sufi Badhni's limbs separating from his body during prayer** (p. 82, folio 68), given as
  something the bi-shar' masters "are said to experience".
- **Jamal ad-Din Savaji in the graveyard** — he shaved his beard, wore sackcloth, sat "awestruck,
  with his heart and his eyes turned towards the heavens"; the saint found "a gaping face: the eyes
  which had been turned towards the sky were glazed"; "Mawlana Jamal ad-Din melted a piece of lead
  and put it into his mouth. \"Praise be to God!\" he exclaimed; \"it has become like cold water:'";
  and when the 'ulama' charged him with cutting off his beard, "\"Is it a beard you want?\" retorted
  the Mawlana, and he drew his head down into his cloak and brought it out again" (p. 83, folio 69,
  with note 4, from Khayr al-majalis). The authors' own verdict follows: "The details of this story
  may sound excessively miraculous in tone, yet they underscore the tension between the Law and the
  Path" (p. 83, folio 69).
- **Khizr's dream vision naming the two beloved of God** — Nizam ad-Din and 'Abd al-Qadir Jilani are
  "the only saints who have ever attained the state of mahbubiyyat" (p. 84, folio 70, with note 6).
- **Khwaja Hasan reads the Qur'an by its light** — "I see a light in this line that I do not see in
  the other lines `ofwriting`[text-layer?] [of writing]" (p. 88, folio 74, with note 14), told by
  Nizam ad-Din.
- **The mysterious youth at Ghiyaspur, probably Khizr**, who foretells Nizam ad-Din's fame with the
  couplet "That day that you became the moon, you knew not / That you'd be the place to which the
  world looks up" (pp. 88–89, folios 74–75, with note 16).
- **Nizam ad-Din's nightly flight to Mecca** — in Siyar al-awliya' "he is said to have made a nightly
  passage from Delhi to Mecca in order to circumambulate the Ka'ba, implying that he performed the
  canonical pilgrimage, a duty incumbent on Muslims that was otherwise not fulfilled during the
  saint's lifetime" (p. 89, folio 75, with note 18). The authors mark this as a later attribution
  absent from Fawa'id al-fu'ad, where "none are performed or claimed by Shaykh Nizam ad-Din himself"
  (p. 89, folio 75).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The chapter's governing thesis: the Chishti masters resemble gurus in form and oppose them in
  content.** "The earliest Chishti masha'ikh of Hindustan exemplified the normative qualities of the
  Sufi master in both their proximity to and distance from the lifestyle of gurus, or Hindu holy
  men. In their upbringing, initiation, and signs of spiritual success, they functionally paralleled
  the gurus, though contextually the two groups pursued opposite spiritual goals. Moreover, the
  Chishti saints retained Islamic qualities that have no parallel in Hindu devotional life" (p. 80,
  folio 66).
- **The three respects in which Indian Sufism stayed Islamic**, stated as a summary of the preceding
  (pre-chunk) argument: "in three critical respects (conjugal perseverance, adherence to Islamic
  ritual observances, and evidence of scholarly skills), the Sufi masters remained peculiarly,
  distinctively Islamic in India as well as in other medieval non-Arab regions (`'ajam`) of
  Islamdom" (p. 80, folio 66).
- **Each master's turn to the path came from outside himself.** "The major moment that led each of
  them to pursue the mystical path or to change the direction of their life's work was related to
  some external event or person" (p. 80, folio 66) — then evidenced for all five.
- **The relaxation of the hajj is admitted, bounded and explained away.** "For Indian Sufis to be
  assimilated to the guru model, one would expect a relaxation of the mandate to pilgrimage. Such a
  relaxation does, in fact, take place but only temporarily and only among the Chishtis" (p. 81,
  folio 67); compensated by ritual strictness — "in their adherence to ritual and supererogatory
  prayers they were second to none" (p. 81, folio 67).
- **A tension-inducing paradox at the heart of Sufi prayer.** "there is a tension-inducing paradox
  between the demands of five daily prayers and the ecstatic states of a Sufi who is continually at
  prayer, or so close to God as seemingly to be beyond prayer" (pp. 81–82, folios 67–68).
- **Chishti ecstasy is graded against other shaykhs and against yogins.** "The ecstasy normally
  attributed to the prayer and meditation of the Chishti masters is intense but controlled in
  comparison with the experience of other shaykhs as well as `yo gins`[text-layer?] [yogins]"
  (p. 82, folio 68); by contrast the bi-shar' masters "are said to experience shamanistic forms of
  ecstasy, with attendant powers that resemble, if not in detail, in degree, the states of gurus"
  (p. 82, folio 68).
- **Who authored the distortion, and who did not.** "Though the numerous anecdotes and actions later
  attributed to the great saints may involve historical distortions of their actual lives and
  teachings, the indigenous nonelite, who are their probable source, do not challenge or overturn
  the Islamic identity of the masters, which is firmly set forth by the migrant elite both in
  reports of their conversations and in hagiographies" (p. 83, folio 69). **This is the chunk's
  sharpest methodological claim: the Islamisation of the saints is an elite literary act, but the
  popular material does not contradict it.**
- **Hagiography as the fixative of memory.** "It is the patterning of hagiographies, in particular,
  that fixes the memory of Chishti masters, both major and minor, both premodern and modern"
  (p. 83, folio 69); the thing to examine is "the way in which the self-image of the saint and the
  retrospective view of his followers elide, but do not concur, on the nature of the Path" (p. 83,
  folio 69).
- **The selection of three masters, and why.** Nizam ad-Din, Simnani and Zauqi Shah are chosen "to
  accent both continuities and shifts in the literary legacy of the Chishtis" (p. 83, folio 69), and
  together they "permit us to consider saintliness as an index of shifting social and cultural
  norms, from medieval times to the present" (p. 84, folio 70).
- **The "golden age" declared a trope.** "The \"golden age\" of Chishti spirituality is a literary
  trope that should be balanced against what came after, juxtaposing the great saints of the past
  with contemporary Chishti masters" (p. 84, folio 70). Directly relevant to how this archive should
  read shrine self-descriptions.
- **What is Indian about Indian Sufism is called elusive.** "The features of the Sufi master
  presented in Indo-Muslim biographical literature could be replicated elsewhere; what is distinctly
  Indian about them is elusive, especially since the earliest literature is self-consciously Muslim
  and only by circumstance `Indian?`[text-layer?] [Indian.7]" (p. 84, folio 70).
- **Schimmel's twelve recurrent traits**, summarised by the authors (p. 84, folio 70; p. 85, folio
  71, with note 8): an epiphanic moment or ecstatic utterance as "the threshold experience"; "The
  role of the mother in the formative years of education must be stressed"; "claims of early ascetic
  pursuits or the performance of extraordinary feats (karamat)"; "the paradox of family life: While
  not a hermit, the shaykh still remains chaste"; "Each master must perform the canonical pilgrimage
  (hajj) and/or undertake extensive travels to meet other Sufis"; initiation "either by a proper
  master or the mysterious Khizr"; "He must live a long life, often marked by food and conversion
  miracles"; the Prophet "as a prototype of all saintly conduct"; competing qualities "such as
  beauty (jamal) and majesty (jalal)"; "above all, he must exemplify pain (dard) through love
  ('ishq) as the most efficacious and rapid means of attaining perfection (kamal) and/or union
  (`wisan`[text-layer?] [wisal])"; and monitoring "the distinction between drunkenness (sukr) and
  sobriety (sahw) as spiritual states (hal, plural `ahwa/`[text-layer?] [ahwal])".
- **Digby's list, and his card metaphor.** "Detailed study of a mediaeval Sufi shaykh;' he observes,
  \"will often reveal apparent inconsistencies of attitude, character and behavior. Acceptance of a
  Sufi in his lifetime as a great shaykh depended on the recognition that he possessed, to an
  impressive degree, qualities which indicated that he was the special recipient of Divine Grace.
  The balance of these qualities in a single shaykh might vary, just as a winning hand at cards
  might be stronger in some suits than others.\"" (p. 85, folio 71, with note 9). His requirements
  include descent "from the Prophet, his companions, or other noble families of seventh-century
  Arabia (`ashraj`[text-layer?] [ashraf])", connection "with a Sufi silsila (order) of already
  established local prestige", strict orthodoxy, meticulous duties, austerities "of a more and
  sometimes of a less `ortliodox`[text-layer?] [orthodox] character", mastery of texts, karamat
  without "vulgar display", "a reputation for inaccessibility and disliking human society" combined
  with care for disciples, and visible ecstasy refined by poetry and music (p. 85, folio 71).
- **The authors' own synthetic portrait of sainthood as a set of paradoxes** — well-born yet seeking
  a master; educated yet intuitive; dependent on a shaykh yet a saint in his own right; isolated yet
  attending "to `tlie`[text-layer?] [the] needs of his fellow Muslims"; "Married and `tlie` father of
  sons, the accomplished saint must yet be celibate in temperament and disposition"; capable of
  karamat yet suppressing them; prone to ecstasy yet performing his obligatory duties (pp. 85–86,
  folios 71–72). Concluded with Digby's phrase, "striving after different excellences" (p. 86, folio
  72).
- **The two-stage theory of reputation.** "The reputation of a Sufi master largely depended on a
  hagiographical process that may be divided into two parts" — the pivotal life events judged
  against "attitudes, activities, and allegiances esteemed by the various groups composing the elite
  of his time and his region", and posthumous fame built by family, followers, tomb and text (p. 86,
  folio 72).
- **Why spiritual authority cannot be shared.** "It is impossible to have an equal distribution of
  spiritual authority among several Sufi masters, even those joined together in a single order, with
  a common spiritual pedigree and a common spiritual outlook. The followers of one saint, by the
  very nature of their devotion to him, will esteem him not only as the foremost master of his
  generation but also as the greatest Sufi shaykh of all times and all places" (p. 86, folio 72);
  the titles "The axis of the world;' \"the Sultan (king) of the saints;' \"the Beloved of God\"-these
  are more than flowery encomia … they reflect the edge of superiority that devotees of Sufi masters
  ascribe to their shaykh" (p. 86, folio 72).
- **Source criticism of the two Nizam ad-Din texts — the chunk's most transferable methodological
  passage.** The convenient two-stage model is explicitly rejected: "Unfortunately, the material does
  not admit of such a neat segmentation" (p. 91, folio 77). Fawa'id al-fu'ad, "though early and of
  inestimable value, is chronologically limited; it records conversations from only a fifteen-year
  period of the saint's more than eighty years. It is also implicitly compromised at numerous points
  by the compiler's outlook" (p. 91, folio 77). Siyar al-awliya', "though late in composition,
  inflated in style, and perhaps distorted on some points of fact, does offer a complete chronology
  of Nizam ad-Din. It also incorporates evidence from viewpoints other than the author's" (p. 91,
  folio 77) — and, crucially, "by trying to resolve some of the paradoxes implicit in Fawa'id
  al-fu'ad, it actually helps us to see at what points the great saint did not conform to the
  expected norms of behavior" (p. 91, folio 77).
- **Hagiographers flatten paradox, and that is the diagnostic.** "The great saint was the one who
  lived out paradoxes, holding seemingly irreconcilable tensions in a delicate, unresolved balance.
  Lesser saints could not do that. Hagiographers also could not do that" (p. 91, folio 77); Nizam
  ad-Din's hagiographers "tended to make him conform to expected norms of conduct, often by
  flattening or eliminating paradoxes in their retrospective portrait of the deceased master"
  (p. 91, folio 77).
- **The book's one explicit gesture to the present.** "Sainthood may have declined in Delhi since the
  fourteenth century, but the shaykh's depiction of the poor seems eerily contemporary, his motive
  still as valid today as 650 years ago" (p. 88, folio 74).
- **Nizam ad-Din's ambivalence about miracles, stated as a three-part position.** "he espouses their
  full acceptance for prophets, their conditional acceptance for lower grade saints (or disciples),
  and their infrequent disclosure by accomplished Sufis" (p. 89, folio 75).

## Doubts

- **Qutb ad-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki is NOT the archive's `tomb-of-qutbuddin-aibak`, and I have not mapped
  him.** The book's figure is a Chishti shaykh initiated "in Baghdad" who followed Mu'in ad-Din to
  India, visited Multan, had children, and died over a three-day sama' session (p. 80, folio 66;
  p. 81, folio 67; p. 82, folio 68). The archive row's principal figure is "Sultan Qutb ud-Din
  Aibak" (d. 1210), a ruler, in a row categorised "Secular / Memorial" at Anarkali, Lahore. The
  book gives Bakhtiyar Kaki **no death date, no burial place and no royal office: all (not
  stated)**, so nothing here supports a merge, and the shared first element of the name is the only
  resemblance. **No archive row has Qutb ad-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki as principal figure, so he takes no
  id.**
- **"Jalal ad-Din Tabrizi" is not the Uch Sharif Jalal ad-Din, and the two-row question the
  instructions anticipate does not in fact arise in this chunk.** The archive has two candidate rows
  — `shrine-of-jalaluddin-surkh-posh-bukhari-jalaluddin-bukhari` (Sayyid Jalaluddin Surkh-Posh
  Bukhari, d. 1291) and `shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht` (Sayyid Jalaluddin, d. 1384) —
  and both carry the nisba **Bukhari**. The book prints **"Tabrizi"** (p. 81, folio 67), a different
  nisba, and supplies no place, date, order or kinship that would connect him to Uch. **I have
  therefore mapped him to neither row rather than choosing between them.** If a later chunk shows
  Ernst and Lawrence treating Tabrizi and Bukhari as one man, this bullet must be revisited.
- **"Shaykh Jamal Savaji" / "Mawlana Jamal ad-Din Savaji" is not the archive's `shah-jamal`.** The
  archive's Lahore row names "Syed Shah Jamal Uddin Naqvi Bukhari" (d. 1671); the book's man is "the
  alleged founder of Qalandars" with the nisba Savaji, no date and no place of burial (p. 82, folio
  68; p. 83, folio 69). Recorded as a non-match, not as an id. `shrine-of-hafiz-muhammad-jamal-
  multani` is likewise not a candidate.
- **"Iqbal" at p. 90 (folio 76) is Nizam ad-Din's fourteenth-century servant and has nothing to do
  with `mazar-e-iqbal`** (Allama Muhammad Iqbal, d. 1938). Flagged only because a name-match pass
  would catch it. No id assigned.
- **"Mir Khwurd Kirmani" (p. 87, folio 73) is not the archive's Kirmani.** `shergarh` names "Daud
  Bandagi Kirmani" (d. 1575) and `darbar-abul-muali-qadri` names an affiliation with "Syed Dawood
  Bandgi Kirmani"; the book's Kirmani is the hagiographer of Siyar al-awliya', writing "some 30
  years after" Nizam ad-Din's death, i.e. in the fourteenth century. Shared nisba only; no id.
- **"Farid" is not `mithankot-kot-mithan`.** That row's principal figure is Khwaja Ghulam Farid
  (d. 1901). The book's Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar is a companion of Qutb ad-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki and
  the pir of Nizam ad-Din (p. 80, folio 66; p. 87, folio 73) — six centuries earlier — and matches
  `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` ("Baba Fariduddin Mas'ud Ganjshakar"), for which the book's own
  "What can Mas'ud do?" (p. 81, folio 67) is independent corroboration beyond the epithet. **This is
  the one mapping in the chunk I regard as firm, and the Mas'ud line is why.**
- **The `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` mapping is firm on identity but thin on shrine content, and
  the consolidator should know that in advance.** The chunk gives his initiation, his family, his

  "Chishti" and no custodial arrangement: all (not stated).** Nothing about the *shrine* can be
  drawn from these twelve pages.

  his sons acting in it, so a saint is attached. **But the book nowhere says Ajodhan is Pakpattan,
