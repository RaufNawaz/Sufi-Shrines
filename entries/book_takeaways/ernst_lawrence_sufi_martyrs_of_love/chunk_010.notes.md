- **data-darbar**: **the book records a burial inside the shrine precincts and dates it.** "Faruq
  Ahmad died in February 1945, and was buried within the tomb precincts of his patron saint Hujwiri
  (Data Ganj Bakhsh) in Lahore" (p. 138, folio 125). Faruq Ahmad was one of "two brothers named
  Lennard", English converts; the book calls Hujwiri **his patron saint**, which is the only thing

  the Muslim names Faruq Ahmad and Shahid Allah", and "traveled to the Middle East and India in

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Book prices, free distribution and sales figures, stated as a Sufi author's own business.**
  "Nizami also discusses which of his writings sold well, and why. The price increase of certain
  publications is justified, as is the free distribution of others" (p. 131, folio 118). This is the
  chunk's most direct economic statement and it is about publishing, not about a shrine.
- **The loss of state patronage, named as the structural change of the colonial period.** "From
  being a privileged group often supported by state patronage, the Nizami Chishtis had been forced
  to become contenders in a complex society in which they were threatened by powerful opponents
  (British, Hindu, or reformist Muslim) even as they offered new ways to reach mass audiences"
  (p. 131, folio 118). The pressures are itemised as "the demands and challenges of colonial
  authority, secularism, religious politics, and the marketplace of ideas created by print"
  (p. 131, folio 118).
- **Exile as a mode of continued authority.** Imdad Allah "was exiled to Mecca after the abortive
  1857 rebellion against the British (hence his title \"Hajji\")" and "continued to train disciples
  and to inspire the nascent Deobandi movement, from his Arabian exile" (p. 131, folio 118).
- **Lineage documents (*shajara*) as a countable institutional asset, and an inventory of them.**
  "Hajji Imdad Allah had connections through no fewer than eight \"trees\" (*shajarat*) of
  `spirituallineage[text-layer?] [spiritual lineage]`" (p. 132, folio 119). The inventory he gives
  at the end of *Irshad-o-murshid* runs: to the Prophet "first through the Chishtiyya Sabiriyya,
  then through the Silsila-i Chishtiyya Nizamiyya Quddusiyya Darwishiyya and two other Chishti
  sub-branches, the Quddusiyya Nizamiyya Sa'diyya and the Nizamiyya Gisu Daraziyya. He also gives
  four lines of Qadiriyya affiliation, five of `Nasqshbandiyya[text-layer?] [Naqshbandiyya]`
  affiliation, two through the Madariyya Qalandariyya Ajmaliyya, one through the Suhrawardiyya, and
  another through the Kubrawiyya Quddusiyya" (p. 132, folio 119). **The listed lines do not add to
  eight; see Doubts.**
- **Two grades of successor, and the document that records the split.** "According to a biography
  entitled *Imdad al-Mushtaq*, there was disagreement between Hajji Imdad Allah and some of his
  successors. Hajji Imdad Allah had always indicated that he had two kinds of successor: those to
  whom he himself had given spiritual authority as Sufis (*khilafat*) and those to whom he gave
  permission, at their request, to spread the message of religion (*tabligh-i din*). All the first
  kind were loyal to him, and obedient. But of the second kind, those who differed with him included
  famous names like Maulvi Qasim Nanautawi, Maulvi Rashid Ahmad Gangohi, and Maulana Ashraf 'Ali
  Thanvi" (pp. 132–133, folios 119–120). **This is the chunk's fullest account of a succession
  dispute, and it is about authorisation, not about property.**
- **Books destroyed by the successors.** "A Sabiri historian notes that \"the '*ulama*' of Deoband
  did not even examine Hajji Imdad Allah's metaphysical treatises, but consigned them to the
  `flames:'[text-layer?] [flames.\"]`" (p. 133, folio 120).
- **Women barred from *khilafat* and *sajjada*.** Baba Farid's conditional — "If *khilafat* and
  *sajjada* … could be given to a woman" — is read by Thanvi as proof of his legal precision,
  "alluding to the *sunna* that says that a woman can not be given the succession" (p. 135, folio
  122). The chunk contains no counter-instance.
- **A khanqah bureaucratised: the intake form.** Thanvi "retired to his hometown Thana Bhavan, where
  he wrote voluminously, taught, answered letters, and counseled so many visitors that newcomers
  were asked to fill out a form upon arrival" (p. 134, folio 121). The volume of visitors is given
  only as "so many": no number — **(not stated)**.
- **Ghost-authorship in a shaykh's corpus.** "Hundreds of works were attributed to him, although many
  were probably written by his followers" (p. 134, folio 121).
- **The reformist career path, dated.** "After completing the course at Deoband in 1883, he started
  a job as a teacher at Cawnpore. The same year he performed the *hajj* and became a disciple of the
  exiled Hajji Imdad Allah. In his mid-thirties, he retired to his hometown Thana Bhavan" (p. 134,
  folio 121).
- **Music and audition (*sama'*) as the contested practice the reformers argue over.** Thanvi
  "enumerate[s] in the first section several anecdotes on the practice of listening to music and
  poetry (*sama'*)" to show Chishti disapproval (p. 135, folio 122); Chishti masters are "extremely
  scrupulous about performing Islamic ritual duties in minute detail" (p. 135, folio 122). Chapter 7
  lists "polemically inspired adaptations of `sama~[text-layer?] [sama']`" as one of four indices of
  the twentieth-century Sabiri master's role, alongside "the \"rational\" role of miracles; the
  dynamic interplay of physical/spiritual realms; the centrality of vocal *zikr*" (p. 142, folio
  130). Practice and counterpart are then joined: "the health of each depends on two exercises:
  *zikr* and `sama:[text-layer?] [sama'.]` Neither stands alone, each is defined in relation to the
  other" (p. 143, folio 131).
- **Qawwals and ecstasy at a death.** "Shaykh Qutb ad-Din went into spiritual ecstasy (*hal*) while
  listening to *qawwals* singing and jumped ten yards. For three days he remained in this ecstatic
  state, returning to his senses only periodically in order to pray. On the third day he died"
  (p. 135, folio 122).
- **The forty-day inverted *chilla*, and the legal test applied to it.** "the inverted *chilla*, the
  upside-down meditation performed for 40 days" is permissible only "if it is done in order to cure
  some moral defect" (p. 135, folio 122).
- **Initiation by testing a *murid*.** Mu'in ad-Din "asked a potential disciple (*murid*) to recite
  \"there is no god but God, Chishti is the Messenger of God\" (*la ilaha illa Allah, Chishti rasul
  Allah*)"; Thanvi notes "this incident occurred within a gathering of the elite, and its purpose
  was the benefit of testing the disciple (*murid*)" (p. 135, folio 122).
- **Addressing the dead at tombs (*kashf al-qubur*).** Hasan Nizami's "frequent citations of prayers
  and invocations, which he composes before the tombs of departed saints and Prophets. In these
  moments Hasan Nizami claims to speak to them directly as if they are alive in the tomb,
  illustrating `ilie[text-layer?] [the]` practice of \"unveiling the deceased\" (*kashf al-qubur*)"
  (p. 136, folio 123). Thanvi by contrast "seems to focus more on the practice of the Sufis rather
  than their influence after death" (p. 136, folio 123).
- **Offerings at tombs and ʿurs, reported as what critics reduce Sufism to.** "he observed that some
  people mistakenly believe that Sufism consists of offering flowers or shawls at saints' tombs,
  thus reducing Sufism to tomb worship or the celebration of saints' anniversaries" (p. 136, folio
  123). **Flowers and shawls are the only two offering types the chunk names.**
- **Pilgrimage obstructed by the Wahhabi state.** "He spoke out against Wahhabi actions in Arabia
  that prevented Indian pilgrims (including his own teacher) from performing the *hajj* pilgrimage
  properly" (p. 136, folio 123).
- **Riots as a constraint on a shaykh's movements.** "During this time his biography notes major
  Hindu-Muslim riots in Bombay in 1932, 1940, and 1946, because of which he was forced to modify his
  plans" (p. 137, folio 124).
- **A shaykh inside party politics and the Pakistan demand.** "Zauqi himself had become an active
  participant in the Muslim League and the demand for the creation of Pakistan as a Muslim state"
  (p. 137, folio 124); he and Maududi "both felt that reviving Islam through political means
  (Pakistan) was absolutely necessary, but they differed considerably in their interpretation of
  what Islam actually meant" (p. 138, folio 125).
- **Conversion turned outward to Europeans — a stated novelty in Chishti practice.** "While
  instances of conversion by Hindus to Islam had not been unknown in previous Chishti history, it
  had never been a major objective of the Chishti masters … With Zauqi there is a striking shift
  toward viewing the European Christian as the potential convert" (pp. 137–138, folios 124–125).
  "One English sea captain actually converted to Islam upon meeting the ticketless Hajji Imdad Allah
  seeking passage to Mecca in 1857" (p. 138, folio 125) — travel without a fare, stated as fact.
- **Marriage into the master's family as a channel of succession.** "both brothers resided as
  disciples with Zauqi in Ajmer after 1940; indeed, both were married there (Shahid Allah, now
  surnamed Faridi, married Zauqi's daughter Rashid Khatun)" (p. 138, folio 125).
- **Posthumous succession on the Uwaysi pattern, dated to the year.** "In 1955, following the Uwaysi
  pattern that is well established in the Sabiri Chishtiyya, Shahid Allah Faridi was internally given
  the status of full successor to Zauqi Shah some four years after the latter's death" (p. 138, folio
  125). The book adds the social reading: "it must have been considered a profound instance of the
  power of Sufism when a member of the colonizing race would change his religion and become a fully
  qualified Sufi master in the Chishti lineage" (p. 138, folio 125).
- **An annual death-anniversary festival at a Karachi tomb, in the Chishti order's own observance.**
  "He died in 1978, and his tomb in Karachi is the site of an annual death-anniversary festival
  observed by members of the Chishti order in the customary fashion" (p. 138, folio 125). **The book
  gives no date, no month, no attendance figure, no custodian and no expenditure: (not stated).**
- **Colonial education and salaried employment of a future shaykh.** "Born into the family of a
  physician in government service, Zauqi was educated at the Mohammadan Anglo-Oriental College, the
  precursor to Aligarh Muslim University (1893-96), and he served the colonial government for
  several years (1898-1902) in a district in Kashmir. He then worked (1904-1908) at several Urdu and
  English newspapers in Peshawar, Lahore, Hyderabad-Sind, and Karachi, at one time acting as chief
  correspondent reporting the Indian tour of the Prince of Wales. After a series of business
  ventures, he became drawn to Sufism, and was initiated into the Chishtiyya in Lucknow in 1914"
  (p. 139, folio 126). His name was conferred by his master "because of his mystical experience
  (*zauq*) and longing (*shauq*)" (p. 139, folio 126).
- **Periodicals as Sufi infrastructure, with dates and titles.** "he founded a Sufi magazine, *The
  Lights of Holiness* [*Anwar al-Quds*], that was published in Bombay from October 1925 to February
  1927. He continued to publish in newspapers, including some pieces in *Dawn* (Karachi, 1945-1946)
  and a weekly column in *The People's Voice* (1948-1949)" (p. 139, folio 126). The mechanism is
  credited: "As Arthur Buehler has pointed out, Sufis used periodicals to reach a wider public and to
  organize disciples more effectively" (p. 139, folio 126).
- **A lineage's publishing programme across generations.** "Another useful publication of Zauqi's is
  an extensive lexicon of Sufi terminology, which has gone through several editions and revisions by
  Zauqi's successors" (p. 139, folio 126); "Some of these Urdu and English articles have been
  reprinted" (p. 139, folio 126); Shahid Allah's "own Urdu discourses, along with some English
  titles, have been published in Pakistan in recent years" (p. 138, folio 125); in 1949 Zauqi
  "brought out a volume reprinting a selection of the latter's most important
  `writ-ingsY[text-layer?] [writings.47]`" (p. 136, folio 123).
- **Material scarcity in the production of a text.** Zauqi wrote the novel "with enthusiasm, even
  writing on newspaper when he ran out of ordinary paper" (p. 139, folio 126).
- **A tomb with a specialisation, and a visit that produced a book.** The tomb of Qazi Mahmud
  Darya'i "is believed to have the special property of conferring literary gifts on visitors";
  Zauqi's Urdu novel *The Wine and the Cup* was "begun after a visit to Ahmedabad in 1919" (p. 139,
  folio 126).
- **Partition migration of a lineage's centre.** "After the birth of Pakistan, Zauqi soon settled in
  Karachi, but eventually decided to leave on pilgrimage to Mecca with a number of his followers"
  (p. 140, folio 127); on that journey he "visited the tombs of two notable Chishti masters, one from
  the twelfth century, 'Usman Harwani, and one from the nineteenth, Hajji Imdad Allah. Between them
  they encapsulate the great period of the Indian Chishtiyya, when nearly all of the great Chishti
  shaykhs were buried in India" (p. 140, folio 127).
- **Reformers criticise *sama'* and ʿurs but do not break with their masters.** "Even in criticizing
  practices such as *sama'* performances or '*urs* celebrations, reformist shaykhs acknowledged
  loyalty to their own masters as superseding literalist or fundamentalist appeals to \"pure\"
  Islam" (p. 140, folio 127).
- **The communications chain of the Chishti response, listed in order.** "The Chishti response to the
  colonial experience involved the appropriation of new communications media, beginning with print,
  soon to be followed by sound recordings, occasional appearance in government films and on
  television programs, and most recently on Internet sites" (p. 142, folio 130). And: "a large body
  of publications emanating from Sufi orders and shrines, particularly those connected with the
  Chishtiyya" (p. 142, folio 130).
- **A transnational, elite-initiated web presence.** "there is an exuberant Chishti presence on the
  World Wide Web, part of a transnational repackaging of spirituality that has been initiated by
  technologically privileged cosmopolitan elites but is not limited to them" (p. 142, folio 130).
- **What the existing literature foregrounds, named as a distortion.** "The common topics of
  discussion are Sufi shrines and British authorities. New reformist *madrasas* and the Pakistan
  movement are foregrounded, Islam becomes important as public domain rather than private worship,
  and the former is almost always prioritized over the latter" (p. 142, folio 130).
- **Breath-*zikr* as a seven-level discipline.** "*Ziya' al-qulub* is a manual of Sufi devotions that
  focuses on the physical body as a window to its spiritual counterparts" (p. 143, folio 131), with
  "a remarkable stress on *zikr* as the practice of inhaling and exhaling the breath in the
  recitation of the profession of faith" (p. 143, folio 131); "Since there are seven such layers to
  the heart, the recollection by inhaling and exhaling has also been designed with seven levels … Do
  not draw in a single breath without recollection in the appropriate manner" (p. 143, folio 131).
  The tenfold discipline of the same author "culminates in contemplation and annihilation, in which
  you desert all your own assertive power and will, as at the time of your death" (p. 134, folio 121).
- **No Auqaf, no waqf, no land, no revenue, no visitor count, no custodianship litigation, no
  building date and no Pakistani ʿurs date occurs anywhere in pp. 131–143** — all **(not stated)**.
  The only ʿurs-type event named is the Karachi festival at Shahid Allah Faridi's tomb (p. 138, folio
  125) and the generic "celebration of saints' anniversaries" the critics attack (p. 136, folio 123).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Posthumous appearance of masters offered by the authors as one explanation of the Sabiri
  revival** — "the transhistorical phenomenon of spiritual masters who appeared to disciples long
  after their physical death but nevertheless inspired them to a new tradition of spirituality"
  (p. 131, folio 118). Given as an explanation the authors entertain, not as a report of an event.
- **Ibn 'Arabi's two-kind taxonomy of *karamat*, quoted by Thanvi in Urdu translation** —
  "*Karamat* are of two kinds: sensory (*hissi*) and spiritual (`rna 'nawi[text-layer?] [ma'nawi]`).
  Common folk only know sensory *karamat* and think them to be perfection, like predicting (others')
  thoughts, telling about that which is hidden in the past, present and future, causing things to
  disappear, walking on water, and flying through the air and traversing the earth (*tayy-i 'ard*)
  and disappearing from sight and responding to petitions" (p. 133, folio 120). The spiritual kind
  "preserve the norms of Muslim law (*adab-i shari'at*)" and culminate in breath control, which
  "succeeds in searching for the traces of his Lord (*athar-i rabbihi*) in his heart"; "only the
  angels nearest to God and the purified elect of God's people share in these *karamat*" (p. 133,
  folio 120). Attributed by the authors to Ibn 'Arabi via Ashraf 'Ali Thanvi, not asserted.
- **Imdad Allah embraced at the age of three** — "the hagiographical assertion found in some sources
  that at the age of three, Imdad was embraced by Sayyid Ahmad Shahid and given an honorary
  initiation. Yet Imdad Allah himself never mentions this spiritual lineage" (p. 132, folio 119).
  The authors mark it as hagiography and note the subject's own silence.
- **Qutb ad-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki's three-day ecstasy and death** — he "jumped ten yards", remained
  ecstatic three days "returning to his senses only periodically in order to pray", and "On the third
  day he died" (p. 135, folio 122). Reported as Thanvi reports it.
- **Bibi Sharifa's perfection** — "Until her dying breath, she continued to remember God, and was
  considered to be a great perfected saint" (p. 135, folio 122).
- **Mawdud Chishti's prophecy against Khwarazm** — villages there "will be destroyed because their
  inhabitants like music and singing too much"; "in Thanvi's mind, this demonstrates the saint's
  disapproval of music" (p. 135, folio 122).
- **Ibrahim ibn Adham stopping his ears** — "when the early Chishti saint, Ibrahim ibn Adham, heard a
  seductive voice, he blocked his ears" (p. 135, folio 122).
- **'Abd al-Quddus Gangohi's victory over a yogi** — Zauqi argued "that the miracles of yogis are
  fundamentally different from `iliose[text-layer?] [those]` of Sufi saints, citing the example of
  Shaykh 'Abd al-Quddus Gangohi, who triumphed over a yogi with a miracle demonstrating his greater
  purity" (p. 136, folio 123).
- **Two dream-initiations of Zauqi Shah, dated** — "Zauqi had a significant initiatic dream of Hajji
  Imdad Allah in 1924"; and "Zauqi interpreted another dream as initiation with authorization to
  teach from Rashid Ahmad Gangohi" (p. 136, folio 123). He is also "said to have met" Imdad Allah
  "as a child while on his first pilgrimage to Mecca in 1888" (p. 136, folio 123).
- **Healing by recitation of the Fatiha, witnessed by American doctors** — "He describes the
  astonishment of American doctors who witnessed the recovery of patients when the healing power of
  the opening chapter of the `Qur' an[text-layer?] [Qur'an]` was recited at their sickbed" (p. 139,
  folio 126). Reported as Zauqi's anecdote.
- **Miracles at the tomb of Qazi Mahmud Darya'i, and the gift of letters** — Zauqi "experienced what
  he described as several miracles" there; the tomb "is believed to have the special property of
  conferring literary gifts on visitors" (p. 139, folio 126). Both attributions are the book's
  ("what he described as", "is believed to").
- **Posthumous Uwaysi transmission** — Shahid Allah Faridi "was internally given the status of full
  successor to Zauqi Shah some four years after the latter's death" (p. 138, folio 125).
- **Mystical psychology as revealed anatomy** — the hadith quoted in *Ziya' al-qulub*: "Surely in
  the body of Adam is a small knot of flesh, and in this knot is the inner heart, and in this heart
  is a moral core, and in this core is a spirit, and in this spirit is a secret, and in this secret
  is a light, and in this light is 'I' (*ana*)`:'[text-layer?] [.\"]`" (p. 143, folio 131).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The chapter's closing verdict on the Nizami colonial arc.** "The two-century trajectory leading
  from a late Mughal teacher like Shah Kalim Allah to a modern Sufi controversialist (and
  best-selling author) like Hasan Nizami is not all downhill" (p. 131, folio 118); the Chishti
  ideals — "mystical insight, genealogy, Prophetic example, and religious learning" — "still held a
  central position, [but] the context had changed" (p. 131, folio 118).
- **The Sabiriyya poses a different historiographical problem from the Nizamiyya, and the authors say
  why.** "They do not have a three-stage history like the Nizamiyya, but a slow unfolding of a single
  phase of spiritual development. The first part of their story is opaque and leaves no trace in
  major literary sources" (p. 131, folio 118). "Most of what we know of early Sabiris is from later
  biographical dictionaries. What are the motives that impel the authors of these texts to fill in
  the blanks?" (p. 131, folio 118) — a source-critical question the chapter leaves open.
- **Two candidate explanations for the Sabiri flowering.** "One explanation is the transhistorical
  phenomenon of spiritual masters who appeared to disciples long after their physical death …
  Another is the deep engagement of this tradition both with Hindu spiritual practices and with the
  philosophy linked to Ibn 'Arabi" (p. 131, folio 118).
- **Sabiri influence traced outward, with the paradox stated.** "The Sabiriyya had the least impact
  on the Nizamiyya; although ostensibly part of the same order, the Sabiris were merely listed in
  Nizami Chishti biographical works without further elaboration" (p. 132, folio 119); yet "the
  development of Sabiri Chishti influence appears in anti-Sufi reformers, first through the Deoband
  school in the colonial period, and then through the missionary society of the Tablighi Jama'at"
  (p. 132, folio 119).
- **Miracles redefined rather than rejected — the chapter's sharpest formulation.** "Here miracles
  are not renounced; they are redefined. In describing the miracles of Hajji Imdad Allah with this
  passage from Ibn 'Arabi, Ashraf 'Ali is affirming them while denying them, and retrieving them
  while distancing himself from miracles as usually conceived" (p. 133, folio 120). "The spiritual
  miracles become higher and internalized; they do not merely follow the norms of *shari'a*, they
  actually preserve the *shari'a*" (p. 134, folio 121).
- **Thanvi's method named as a double strategy.** "to include quotations and even whole texts of early
  Sufi masters selectively; and then to redirect their original meaning to the apologetic and
  polemical context of nineteenth-century South Asia" (p. 134, folio 121). And: "Thanvi's approach
  is that of a juridical reformer, not a historical critic" (p. 134, folio 121), applying "the
  principle of *husn-i zann*, that is, trying to think the best about something", so that "he never
  utterly rejects even the most outrageous report" (p. 134, folio 121).
- **The authors' judgement that Thanvi does violence to his sources.** "Often his glosses on the
  Chishti *malfuzat* seem to be doing a rhetorical violence or disservice to the intent of the
  original accounts, which do celebrate the charismatic powers of the saints" (p. 136, folio 123);
  "He is defending the Chishtis, but not in conjunction with the modern world" (p. 136, folio 123).
- **Imdad Allah's spiritual vision judged broader than his successors'.** "In other words, Hajji
  Imdad Allah's spiritual vision was broader than that of his Deobandi successors" (p. 133, folio
  120); "His legacy is contested precisely because many people would like to make him be a perfect
  Muslim religious scholar, for whom the Sufi dimension of his persona is either minor or tangential
  or both" (p. 134, folio 121).
- **Imdad Allah as a Naqshbandi-Chishti hybrid who routes around Sirhindi.** "even while Imdad Allah
  claims a remarkable parallel to the Mujaddidi Naqshbandi tradition, he seeks to retain a direct
  link to Shaykh 'Abd al-Quddus, the Sabiri Chishti forebear, and he does so through an alternate
  Naqshbandi line that bypasses Sirhindi and goes through Shah Kalim Allah" (p. 134, folio 121).
- **Zauqi's anti-Orientalism and his account of Sufism's origins.** "He refutes the Orientalist
  `ilieories[text-layer?] [theories]` of the Hindu or Christian origins of Sufism. Sufism is not
  based, he says, on yoga or Persian or Greek sources but on `ilie[text-layer?] [the]` Qur'an and
  *hadith*" (p. 136, folio 123); "While the first part of purity of heart is accessible to
  non-Muslims, attaining the higher part requires the *shari'a*" (p. 136, folio 123).
- **The authors historicise Zauqi's communalism rather than endorsing it.** "Zauqi's sharp
  differentiation of Sufism from Hinduism and other non-Islamic traditions was to some extent a
  product of the polemical atmosphere of Hindu and Muslim communalism in the colonial period"
  (p. 137, folio 124); "The most polemical of Zauqi's remarks on religion need to be understood in
  the context of the highly confrontational politics of the later colonial period" (p. 137, folio
  124); "here he was clearly responding to the anti-Muslim polemics of Christian missionaries and
  Hindu nationalists" (p. 137, folio 124).
- **Zauqi on race, and the authors' flat report of his anti-Semitism.** His pamphlet *New Searchlight
  on the Vedic Aryans* argued "that both the Aryan and Dravidian races were interlopers into India
  from the Middle East" (p. 137, folio 124); "Zauqi also accepted the rhetoric of European
  anti-Semitism, employing it to further the anti-Hindu polemic. He maintained that Hindus and Jews
  were from the same source, with the same customs; one causes trouble in Europe, the other in India.
  The First and Second World Wars were caused by Jewish bankers and armaments dealers, who would also
  cause the third" (pp. 137–138, folios 124–125). The authors then place him: "In adopting this kind
  of hard-edged and ideological emphasis on Muslim supremacy, Zauqi was adopting a position similar
  to that of Maulana Maududi" (p. 138, folio 125).
- **Zauqi's doctrinal differentia between Islam and other mysticisms.** "while many religions offer
  the possibility of annihilation (`jana'[text-layer?] [fana']`), only Islam permits one to go beyond
  that to the abiding in God (*baqa' billah*) that consists of a renewed consciousness of servanthood
  and obligation in the world" (p. 137, folio 124); Christian and Hindu renunciation "is far easier
  than the path of Muhammad, which `in-valves[text-layer?] [involves]` responsibilities like
  marriage" (p. 137, folio 124).
- **Science absorbed rather than refused.** "\"The farther science `progresses;'[text-layer?] [,\"]`
  he asserted, \"the closer it approaches `Sufism:'[text-layer?] [Sufism.\"]`" and "He claimed that
  Einstein's theory of relativity is very close to Sufism" (p. 139, folio 126); "This rhetorical
  capturing of the symbolic dominance of science is one of the ways in which Zauqi articulated the
  position of Sufism in relation to modernity. It is noteworthy that this response required him to
  take the dominant European culture seriously enough to reply to it" (p. 139, folio 126).
- **Zauqi as the marker of a new era.** "His full engagement with the ideology and technology of
  modernity marks a new style of leadership, and a new era for the Chishti order" (p. 140, folio
  127), yet he "remains very much a modern exemplar of the virtues-and tensions-linked to the Delhi
  master of the fourteenth century, Shaykh Nizam ad-Din" (p. 140, folio 127).
- **The three-cycle periodisation, stated with its date ranges.** The Chishti worldview derives "from
  the first cycle (twelfth-fourteenth centuries)" and persists "in the second cycle
  (fourteenth-eighteenth centuries)"; "What is distinctive in the third cycle
  (eighteenth-twenty-first centuries) is the colonial, post-colonial, \"modern\" context of South
  Asian Sufism" (p. 140, folio 127).
- **Lineage, not ideology, is what holds a silsila together — the chapter's thesis sentence.** "The
  Sabiri and Chishti lineages were not divided by ideology; they were formed by the initiatic link,
  and within each lineage there was room for both improvisation of traditional resources and
  accommodations to modernity" (p. 140, folio 127). Hence the closing verdict: "the Chishtiyya of the
  third cycle are not the debased form of a spiritual movement that flowered in the twelfth century,
  but only its most recent advocates" (p. 140, folio 127).
- **Chapter 7 opens by rejecting the classicism–decline–revival model.** "The latest period is not
  only treated as last, but also as least important. It is most often summarized as a
  `\"revival;'[text-layer?] [\"revival,\"]` following a \"decline\" from the original greatness of a
  \"classical\" or \"formative\" period. Our own approach questions the threefold model of
  classicism, decline, and revival" (p. 141, folio not stated). The model's cost is spelt out: "at
  best it can imitate-it can never equal-the glory of past historical epochs", and success "depends
  on institution building and networking, yet rivals limit their long-range potential" (p. 141, folio
  not stated).
- **The Sufis' own criterion, set against the historians'.** "For the Sufis themselves, however, both
  hope and success are measured by other criteria … while people change, the issues do not" (p. 141,
  folio not stated), supported by Hasan Muhammad Chishti's sixteenth-century dictum that "every age
  has the equal possibility of producing-or not producing-saints" (p. 141, folio not stated).
- **A direct counter to Francis Robinson on print.** Robinson "has argued that the introduction of
  printing in nineteenth-century India democratized Islamic religious knowledge, privileging
  reformist Islam while discrediting organized Sufism. Yet a large body of publications emanating
  from Sufi orders and shrines, particularly those connected with the Chishtiyya, suggests that the
  situation is more complex" (p. 142, folio 130). This is the chunk's clearest methodological claim
  about shrine publishing as evidence.
- **A complaint against the existing literature by name.** "Simon Digby does not deal with any figure
  more recent than the early Mughal period. David Gilmartin and Barbara Metcalf, on the other hand,
  deal exclusively with modern figures, but in their studies the Chishtis are subsumed under the
  general category of Sufism, where the Naqshbandis dominate and where reformers are the major
  anti-Sufi representatives of Muslim society" (pp. 141–142, folios not stated and 130); "The same
  tendency can be found in modern Urdu scholarship on these modern Chishti leaders" (p. 142, folio
  130).
- **Politics obscures the real shift, which is in popular culture and media.** "too-close attention
  to political developments obscures the major shift in popular culture and public forms of community
  that affected Sufis as well as reformers during the colonial and postcolonial eras in South Asia"
  (p. 142, folio 130), and the new media "are contributing to new forms of community that reconfigure
  the spiritual practices inherited from the spiritual lineages that connect the Chishtis to their
  past" (p. 142, folio 130).
- **Outer and inner read as dialectic, not dichotomy.** Imdad Allah's distinction "could be seen as a
  move that simply reappropriates the idea of \"outer\" and \"inner\" as dichotomous categories …
  But in fact, he links \"true\" miracles to the correct performance of the meditative practice of
  inhalation and exhalation. Outer and inner are projected not in a dichotomous but in a dialectic
  relationship to each other" (p. 143, folio 131), making nineteenth-century Chishti Sufism "the
  lodestone of piety and hope for all Muslims of North India" (p. 143, folio 131).
- **And yet: nothing new.** "Yet there is nothing new about Hajji Imdad Allah's worldview, for
  underlying both *zikr* and *sama'* is a centuries-old anthropology of ascent/descent,
  microcosm/macrocosm" (p. 143, folio 131); *Ziya' al-qulub* "has equivalents in Hindu yogic
  practice", but "he develops them in a specific Sufi idiom that projects both his loyalty to
  tradition and his own spiritual creativity" (p. 143, folio 131).

## Doubts

- **The `data-darbar` mapping is the firmest in the chunk and needs no author-only ruling**, because
  Hujwiri appears both as an author and as the saint of a named Lahore tomb whose precincts received
  a burial in February 1945 (p. 138, folio 125). The book's "(d. 1072)" agrees with the archive row's
  1072. **What is still missing and must not be supplied from elsewhere:** the shrine's name in
  Urdu, its quarter of Lahore, any custodian, any ʿurs, and the Arabic title of *The Unveiling of the
  Concealed* — all **(not stated)** here.
- **The `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` mapping rests on the name and the lineage role, not on a
  shrine.** The book says nothing in pp. 131–143 about Pakpattan, about his tomb, about his death
