## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Ziyarat ritual as Muhammad Najib describes it, in the book's own summary.** "There is an emphasis
  on determining the exact hour and day of death for commemoration, though this is not indispensable.
  Food and drink also play an important role and are to be offered to the spirits of the dead and
  then distributed on whatever scale the pilgrim can afford" (p. 106, folio 93).
- **Offerings itemised, and the practice said to be current.** "The pilgrim is also urged to offer
  'sweets, roses, and flowers' at the tomb, or a bit of money, and this is still expected of the
  visitor today" (p. 106, folio 93). "Offering food to the spirits of the saints brings good fortune
  in this life, and pilgrims may also present petitions to the saints" (p. 106, folio 93).
- **Music as a Chishti marker.** "Performance of music on these anniversaries is a characteristically
  Chishti practice" (p. 106, folio 93).
- **Ritual sized to the purse, and the doctrine that licenses it.** "one should perform only what can
  be done in accordance with one's ability, especially in case of poverty"; Muhammad Chishti's
  formula is that one should perform it "as much as possible without objection [being attached to it]
  (`bi-la haraj):'[text-layer?] [bi-la haraj)."]`" (p. 106, folio 93), bolstered by "a saying of the
  Prophet Muhammad, that one is to be judged by one's intentions" (p. 106, folio 93).
- **A collective saints' day.** "there is a Muslim equivalent of the Christian All Saints' Day on the
  first Thursday in the month of `Rajah[text-layer?] [Rajab]`, when one may commemorate all the
  saints' festivals at once" (p. 106, folio 93).
- **Circumambulation of the tomb, compared to the hajj.** "Like the hajj, ziyarat calls for
  circumambulation, in this case of the tomb rather than of the Ka'ba"; "Some enthusiastic pilgrims
  actually found ziyarat to be superior to the hajj" (p. 107, folio 94).
- **Adab at the tomb.** "One should especially avoid turning one's back on the saint's tomb or turning
  one's feet disrespectfully in that direction" (p. 107, folio 94); pilgrims "express their love and
  respect for the saints by kissing and touching their eyes to the tombs" (p. 107, folio 94).
- **Prostration as a contested practice, with a named Chishti precedent.** "Prostration, though
  customary in the courts of kings, is technically permissible only before God, as in ritual prayer;
  many jurists draw the inference that prostration before a mortal is therefore idolatrous"; "The
  Chishti master Nizam ad-Din Awliya' was uncomfortable with the practice but permitted it since it
  was an established custom with his predecessors" (pp. 107–108, folios 94–95). Simnani's defence
  distinguishes "worship ( `'iba-dat[text-layer?] ['ibadat]` ) of the person" from "spontaneous
  respect (ta'zim)" (p. 108, folio 95).
- **Architecture: shrines as mosques with funerary functions, with the qibla as the evidence.** "it
  might be more correct to describe Sufi shrines as mosques with funerary functions, since the tombs
  invariably have an orientation to the direction of Mecca, and large mausolea almost always feature
  a qibla niche in the appropriate wall" (p. 108, folio 95); "The tomb was in fact an untypical form
  of architecture in Hindu India, where cremation was the preferred method of disposal of the dead"
  (p. 108, folio 95).
- **Local ritual variation named, at one shrine.** "local variations such as having a fatiha recited
  with a scarf (`chadar`) over one's head while standing at the foot of the tomb upon arrival,
  especially in Ajmer Sharif" (p. 109, folio 96).
- **Qawwali scheduling, and the two named assembly types.** "Ritual space is also generally set aside
  for Thursday evening qawwali music performance. Mahfil-i 'amm (public assembly) and mahfil-i khass
  (private assembly) are musical sessions for distinctive audiences, but in major shrines like Nizam
  ad-Din and Ajmer Sharif, qawwali occurs every day, often at specified intervals during the rituals
  surrounding the maintenance of respect for the tomb" (p. 109, folio 96). "Elite performances of
  qawwali music occur during the day, linking the chilla-gah with distinct personalities" (p. 109,
  folio 96).
- **Chilla-gah as pilgrimage site.** "Pilgrimage also marks the sites of heroic asceticism,
  particularly the location of the 40-day retreat (chilla-gah)" (p. 109, folio 96).
- **The early Chishti ideals about money and courts, stated as ideals that were compromised but not
  abandoned.** "It is sometimes said that later Chishti Sufis compromised major principles of early
  Chishti practice, particularly the notion that one should give away all donations and avoid visits
  to royal courts. However much these ideals were compromised, nevertheless they remained ideals
  throughout" (p. 109, folio 96).
- **Hereditary custodianship as the shrines' chief structural feature, with its title.** "the
  continuation of the shrines under the management of biological descendants of the shaykhs, many of
  whom compete with each other for the privilege and the prestige of being addressed as gaddi nishin,
  'the one who sits on the throne'" (pp. 110–111, folios 97–98); and the judgement that "That
  continuity, whether in the biological or spiritual line, is ultimately a more important guarantor
  of institutional coherence than any single source of outside support" (p. 111, folio 98).
- **Two kinds of descendant, named in Persian and defined.** "the farzandan-i nan (those who claim
  direct biological connection to the saint and therefore maintain his dargah after his 'death'
  through Muslim inheritance law) and the farzandan-i jan (those who see themselves as the saint's
  spiritual descendants, and often are loosely connected to the dargah)" (p. 117, folio 104); "the
  two offspring overlap, and interact, one with the other" (p. 117, folio 104).
- **The Ajmer complex itemised.** It "includes not only the central shrine of Khwaja Sahib … but also
  a vast complex adjacent to the central shrine containing a religious academy (madrasa), a mosque,
  and a burial plot. Other abodes of rest, or 'royal courts' (dargahs), as tombs are called, abut the
  main area of Ajmer Sharif, mostly belonging to Chishtis but also to Qadiris and one to a Shi'i
  martyr" (p. 114, folio 101).
- **Land grants as the origin of the shrine's financial trouble — the chunk's sharpest economic
  claim.** "Had Akbar limited his largesse to the construction of buildings or even direct
  expenditures of charity for the poor or the upkeep of custodian families, long-term problems might
  have been avoided. But instead, he offered land grants for the maintenance of the dargah. They were
  continued by his son and successor, Jahangir, but also other royal visitors (maharajas, rajas, and
  nawabs of princely states), with the result that the positions of volunteer service that dated back
  to the thirteenth century became linked to real estate holdings that yielded and continue to yield
  considerable annual income" (p. 114, folio 101). The conflict began "with the Mughal emperor Akbar
  and were continued by his successors, including Aurangzeb" (p. 114, folio 101).
- **Nazar-o-niyaz defined, and named as the hinge of the whole system.** "namaz, or Islamic ritual
  prayer, which is the main function of all visitors to the shrine, became linked to nazar-o-niyaz,
  monies that are paid to the khuddam, or resident functionaries, by visitors to Ajmer Sharif. The
  same process operates in other shrines. At Ajmer it is simply cast in its broadest, and most
  complex, pattern" (p. 115, folio 102).
- **ʿUrs timing and the peak of income — and the chunk's printed numeral landmine.** "The biggest
  income from offerings comes at the time of the annual 'urs, celebrated during the first six days of
  the month of Rajab (as with all observances in the **356**-day Muslim lunar calendar, this occurs
  nine days earlier each solar year, so it has no fixed date in the Gregorian calendar)" (p. 115,
  folio 102). **The figure "356" is reported exactly as printed and no reading is asserted here**;
  see Doubts.
- **The khadim body counted and described as a tribe.** The successors of the first khadim are "a
  group numbering perhaps 1200 adult males with families, most living close to the shrine" and "have
  been aptly described as a 'tribe'. They are a tribe with many clans, each staking out some claim to
  the vast responsibilities and resources of the shrine complex" (p. 115, folio 102). "Their primary
  task is to oversee and perform the day-to-day rituals and services connected with the shrine. They
  also maintain and modify the various buildings adjacent to the shrine" (p. 115, folio 102).
- **The wikalat-nama: a written contract between pilgrim and custodian, with the khadim's take
  itemised.** "The attendant who supervises serves as an intermediary, or wakil. His relationship
  with pilgrims is formalized through a contract called a wikalat-nama" — "a sort of pledge … by
  which a devotee bound himself, his family members, relatives, clansmen (and in the case of a pir,
  his followers, too) to a particular khadim … by accepting him as his/their wakil at the dargah"
  (p. 115, folio 102). In return "he receives all the nazar-o-niyaz (offerings), whether in cash or
  kind, gold and silver articles, animals, and a one-quarter share of the cauldron food offered by
  the devotee" (p. 115, folio 102).
- **Hindu clients and Hindu land grants at a Muslim shrine.** "This service included Hindus as well as
  Muslims, and it is evident from correspondence preserved at the Ajmer shrine that the traffic in
  Hindu clients, not only in temporary nazar-o-niyaz but also permanent land grants, was substantial"
  (p. 115, folio 102).
- **Colonial and post-colonial state regulation, dated.** "the British Deputy Commissioner took on the
  task of choosing between claimants to the office of chief shrine custodian, `it is or[text-layer?]
  [that is, the]` sajjada nishin. From 1867, the affairs of the shrine were regulated by the Religious
  Endowments Act of 1863" (p. 115, folio 102). "The family that was in charge of the shrine from 1912
  to 1947 migrated to Pakistan at Partition, and the Chief Commissioner then selected a new
  incumbent" (p. 115, folio 102). Appeals "went eventually to the Indian Supreme Court, and resulted
  in the passing of the Dargah Khwaja Sahib Act in 1955, a law designed specifically to regulate the
  affairs of that Sufi shrine. This gave the Dargah Committee the right to appoint as shrine
  administrator the Diwan (a title `meaning"royal treasury''[text-layer?] [meaning "royal treasury"]`),
  and it also opened up all the procedures of the dargah to judicial review" (pp. 115–116,
  folios 102–103). "Particularly vilified, and marginalized, were the hereditary khadims" (p. 116,
  folio 103).
- **Partition, and who stayed.** "While it is true that the chief administrator of the Ajmer shrine
  went to Pakistan, all the khuddam family members stayed in Ajmer" (p. 116, folio 103).
- **A dated income history for the Ajmer khuddam.** "From 1951 to 1971 the remuneration of
  nazar-o-niyaz was very low indeed. Some khuddam complain of eating dust during this period. Then,
  with 1971, things changed for the better, for two reasons: the war for Bangladeshi independence,
  and the recycling of funds from Indians working in the Persian Gulf" (p. 116, folio 103).
- **Present-day state politics diverting shrine infrastructure money.** "The current government of the
  province of Rajasthan, where Ajmer is located, is dominated by the pro-Hindu Bharatiya Janata
  Party. Consequently, funds allotted for much-needed infrastructure improvements in the vicinity of
  the Ajmer shrine have been redirected to the nearby Hindu holy place of Pushkar" (p. 116,
  folio 103).
- **Colonial courts as the new arbiter of succession — Gilmartin's argument as the book reports it.**
  Gilmartin "describes how the custodians of the major Chishti shrines became local figures in a
  larger regional/global contestation of political power and traces how the authority of these
  figures changed due to the mediating control on questions of succession exercised by the British
  court system" (p. 116, folio 103).
- **Akbar's building at Fatehpur Sikri, and the book's verdict on its effect.** "Nor did Akbar's
  construction of Shaykh Salim's tomb within the walled courtyard of the great mosque at Fatehpur
  Sikri promote the spiritual agenda of the Chishti lineage that he represented. By his patronage of
  Salim's tomb, Akbar claimed the pan-Indian role of the Chishtis while simultaneously asserting his
  own authority as imperial patron" (p. 113, folio 100).
- **Charitable trusts as a Mughal office with a Chishti holder.** "The first official in charge of
  charitable trusts under Akbar, 'Abd an-Nabi, had a family lineage directly linked to one of the
  foremost Chishti saints of Babur's period, 'Abd al-Quddus Gangohi (d. 1537)" (p. 113, folio 100).
- **Reformist movements named as present-day threats to shrine practice.** "The reformist threats of
  the Jama'at-i Islami and the Tablighis overlap on this point: Even though both adopt a style of
  leadership that presumes the authority of a Sufi master, they try to annul the traditional order and
  their sites, especially at Nizamuddin in Delhi, perhaps because of its enormous symbolic capital"
  (p. 117, folio 104).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The saint blesses a childless emperor** — Salim Chishti "blessed the childless emperor so that he
  produced an heir", and Akbar chose Fatehpur Sikri by "linking it to the saint who predicted the
  birth of his heirs and successors" (p. 111, folio 98; p. 112, folio 99). Attributed by the authors
  to "the official dynastic accounts" (p. 111, folio 98).
- **The saints are present at their tombs and correct the discourteous** — "otherwise one risks
  offending the saints, who are consciously present and not averse to correcting the offender"
  (p. 107, folio 94). Attributed to Muhammad Najib.
- **The grant of the dominion of all India at Hujwiri's shrine** — Mu'in ad-Din "was granted the
  dominion of all of India, for himself and his successors" at the "station" outside the shrine of
  Hujwiri in Lahore (p. 109, folio 96). Reported by the authors as the significance of the site, with
  no source named in the chunk: **(not stated)**.
- **Simnani on why a corpse's resting place works** — "pilgrimage to tombs increases one's spiritual
  concentration (tawajjuh) through contact with the earthly remains of a saint"; "the place of bodily
  entombment is more closely connected with the spirit than is any other material phenomenon"
  (p. 107, folio 94). At the Prophet's tomb in Medina, "the spirit of the Prophet senses the extra
  effort and hardship of the journey and assists the pilgrim in attaining the full realization of the
  inner meaning of the pilgrimage" (p. 107, folio 94).
- **Transfer of prayer-merit to a sinner in the grave** — "prayers of intercession on behalf of
  deceased sinners who are undergoing preresurrection torments in the grave; one may willingly give
  to another the reward for years of prayer, as is shown by the story of Abu al-Rabi' and his 70,000
  repetitions of the Islamic creed" (pp. 106–107, folios 93–94). Attributed to Muhammad Najib.
- **The tomb yields fayd** — Rashid Ahmad Gangohi "does not deny that spiritual grace
  (`jayd[text-layer?] [fayd]`) emanates from tombs, but he maintains that the common people must not
  be permitted to have access to this practice because of the danger of idolatry" (p. 109, folio 96).
- **Akbar's wounding at Nizam ad-Din's tomb** — "In 1564, during his eighth regnal year, Akbar did
  attempt a pilgrimage to the tomb of Shaykh Nizam ad-Din in Delhi, but he was wounded by an
  assailant. The injury was reportedly minor, yet the incident resonates with symbolic undertones"
  (pp. 111–112, folios 98–99). The symbolism is the authors'; the wounding is from the chronicles.
- **The holy footstone from Mecca** — "The next year when the holy footstone was brought back from
  Mecca, he feigned interest but actually (if we believe Abu al-Fazl) doubted its authenticity"
  (p. 114, folio 101).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Why a calendar of saints was worth compiling in the eighteenth century** — "The internal
  reorganization of the Chishti order was, in any case, combined with highly uncertain political
  conditions. This makes it all the more understandable that a calendar of saints, recording and
  memorializing the religious heroes of the past and present, should have been considered an
  important enterprise" (p. 106, folio 93), against a background where "The eighteenth and nineteenth
  centuries witnessed a considerable growth in the literature of Islamic hagiography and martyrology
  in India" (p. 106, folio 93) and "Sufis like Muhammad Najib still needed the support of powerful
  protectors" (p. 106, folio 93).
- **A generic piety detached from lineage, explained by multiple initiation** — "The expansion of a
  calendar of saints from a strictly Chishti lineage to a comprehensive list of Muslim saints
  suggested a generic piety detached from specific teaching circles, perhaps assisted by the practice
  of multiple initiation" (p. 107, folio 94).
- **Sacred space is made by attention, not intrinsic to the site** — "Sacred space is therefore a
  function of the concentration of those who visit a site, rather than being intrinsic to the place
  itself" (p. 107, folio 94). Drawn from Simnani.
- **Tombs are not a Hindu borrowing — the argument and its two supports.** "Supporters of ziyarat
  pilgrimage reject the suspicion that it is the result of Hindu influence, and they find the Wahhabi
  iconoclasm extreme, pointing to passages in both the Qur'an (e.g., al-Kahf, 18:21) and the hadith
  reports that approve graves as memorials" (p. 108, folio 95); "tombs of Sufi saints are
  developments within the Islamic tradition that do not rely on any Hindu example" (p. 108,
  folio 95).
- **Ziyarat was near-unanimously accepted before the modern period** — "Though some disputed the
  legitimacy of ziyarat, Sufi scholars prior to the modern period almost unanimously accepted it as
  a practice founded on the example of the Prophet Muhammad, and in this view it was thoroughly
  Islamic in intention" (p. 108, folio 95).
- **Mazar read etymologically to make a theological point** — "The ziyarat pilgrimage is not merely a
  journey to a place of burial, but is literally a visit to a living saint: One of the most common
  Persian terms for a saint's shrine is mazar, a place that is visited, indicating that the act of
  personal encounter takes priority over the structure's reliquary function" (p. 108, folio 95).
- **The book's central puzzle about modern reform** — "it is all the more striking that reformist
  Sufis of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries should so thoroughly have accepted the critique of
  pilgrimage advanced by the Wahhabis and their allies. An extraordinary sea change must have taken
  place in nineteenth-century Muslim countries to allow this reversal to proceed so far" (p. 108,
  folio 95), though "it cannot be said that the opponents of tomb pilgrimage have at all succeeded in
  suppressing the practice" (p. 108, folio 95).
- **Stridency as evidence of the reformists' success, and the Deoband/Barelvi divide** — "The
  stridency of the defense of tomb pilgrimage by recent Sufi authorities in South Asia is probably
  the best evidence of the success of the reformist polemic. It may be fairly stated that the chief
  divide in modern South Asian Islam is that between the reformist Deoband school and the devotional
  and pietist Barelvi school, which champions practices that honor the Prophet and the Sufi
  `saintsY[text-layer?] [saints.47]`" (p. 109, folio 96).
- **The chapter's main polemic: against political reductionism.** "Can one reduce these institutions
  to this political relationship?" (p. 109, folio 96); "The approach of political reductionism ignores
  the recurrent references in Sufi literature to spiritual masters, and to the patronage of tombs, as
  a locus of loyalty over centuries of Muslim rule" (p. 110, folio 97); "the political comments by
  Sufi shaykhs constitute a very minor portion of their literary legacy, as Friedmann has shown with
  the letters of Ahmad Sirhindi" (p. 110, folio 97).
- **The self-contradiction the authors find in the instrumental view** — "the instrumental view of
  Sufism as political legitimation becomes enmeshed in self-contradiction, because in practice the
  dynasts either reject Sufism when they do not need its supportive authority, or they support shrine
  administrators whose pliancy to political structures invalidates the spiritual legacy of the Sufi
  masters" (p. 110, folio 97); what underlies it is "an evolutionary-functionalist view of religion,
  in which Sufism becomes significant only as a symbolic capital negotiated between elites" (p. 110,
  folio 97).
- **Named opponents: Eaton and Trimingham.** "In Richard Eaton's view, there were two major strategies
  for the shaykhs vis-a-vis the court: Either co-opt the court or defy it" (p. 109, folio 96);
  "Certainly there are notable instances of shrines that became extensions of the court, but this is
  by no means a universal 'decline' from an earlier ideal, as Trimingham would have it; as numerous
  examples from this book indicate, the later centuries of South Asian Sufi history provide enough
  counterexamples to discredit that theory" (p. 110, folio 97).
- **Against the recluse ideal as a test of Chishti authenticity.** "It would be extreme to insist that
  Chishtis can honor their heritage only by remaining recluses unpolluted by contact with the court.
  To do so would be to project something like early Christian notions of world-rejecting asceticism,
  or the Franciscan ideal of poverty, onto Sufism" (p. 110, folio 97); "the whole tenor of the
  master-disciple relationship is one that insists on fulfilling worldly responsibilities and
  severely limits world renunciation" (p. 110, folio 97).
- **The shrines as a spectrum of sites, not a church/state division.** "Rather than a kind of
  pre-Jeffersonian division of church and state, we are faced with a spectrum of spiritually charged
  spaces or sites. They are the devotional loci for Indian Muslims from several classes, and also for
  Hindus" (p. 110, folio 97), and the significance of the Chishti masha'ikh "is not only political
  and religious but also cultural" (p. 110, folio 97).
- **Akbar's choice of Fatehpur Sikri explained pragmatically, against the court's spiritual account.**
  "Without denying the spiritual motives attributed to the emperor by his minister Abu al-Fazl, we
  can see another, pragmatic motive at work in the choice of the Chishtis for royal patronage: Akbar
  identifies with an illustrious India-specific order, enhancing his own position as a South Asian
  Muslim monarch" (p. 111, folio 98); and the site had to have "such symbolic and legitimating power
  that others would accept the rightness of the emperor's decision" (p. 112, folio 99).
- **Agra against Delhi as the political reason for a third capital** — "It may have been in part due
  to their asymmetry (Delhi having the longer history, Agra the more immediate strategic advantage)
  that Akbar sought another base from which to project his distinctive version of imperial authority"
  (p. 112, folio 99).
- **Akbar abandoned the Chishti connection, and the book dates it** — "he abandoned it by 1585,
  twenty years before the end of his reign" (p. 113, folio 100); "Most telling of all, Akbar did not
  dignify any saint's tomb with his presence after 1580" (p. 114, folio 101); after 1579 "Akbar
  visited `Dellii[text-layer?] [Delhi]` once and spent most of his time at his father Humayun's tomb,
  and when he last visited Delhi in 1585 he did not visit any tomb but Humayun's" (p. 114,
  folio 101). Conclusion: "it is, therefore, impossible to say, as Akbar's apologists have repeatedly
  tried to do, that the greatest Mughal remained faithful to Sufi Muslim exemplars to the end of his
  life" (p. 114, folio 101).
- **The mahzar decree as the turning point** — "in 1577, Abu al-Fazl's father, Shaykh Mubarak, had
  drafted the mahzar decree, which, in Bada'oni's words, 'affirmed the spiritual supremacy of the
  Emperor and his superiority to all religious `functionaries:'[text-layer?] [functionaries."]`"
  (p. 113, folio 100); "By then the Chishti silsila had already lost whatever benefit its
  partisans — whether shrine custodians, living saints, or Hindu/Muslim devotees — may have gained by
  the favor that Akbar had previously showered upon them" (p. 113, folio 100).
- **Source criticism of the two Mughal chronicles.** "Strangely, we find little evidence about the
  Chishtiyya in the chronicle of Abu al-Fazl"; the profiles in the A'in-i Akbari "are condensed,
  formulaic recapitulations of minimal interest" (p. 113, folio 100); "it is in the contemporary
  anti-chronicle Muntakhab at-tawarikh by `Bada' oni[text-layer?] [Bada'oni]`, the alleged defender of
  orthodoxy, that we find many more saints extolled" (p. 113, folio 100).
- **A defence of the khadims against the scholarly consensus, and the book's own counter-reading.**
  "Both native and foreign scholars have chosen to hold the khadims up for scathing review. They are
  accused of mismanagement, corruption, and peculation" (p. 116, folio 103); "The saint can have only
  unworthy followers as keepers of his shrine; the more money they have, the less spiritual they
  become" (p. 116, folio 103). Against this: "What this reading ignores is the extent to which members
  of the khadim community have maintained their role often at personal sacrifice"; where "One scholar
  suggests that they stayed because the commercial gain and social prestige were too great for them to
  leave", the authors answer "More likely, however, they had a commitment to continue, at any cost, in
  the steps of their own ancestors" (p. 116, folio 103). One insider voice is noted: "One descendant of
  a khadim lineage who is now on the history faculty of Aligarh Muslim University is trying to rectify
  the account, but so far his has been a solitary voice" (p. 116, folio 103).
- **A methodological instruction the archive can use directly** — "We should read the testimony of
  living descendants, and custodians, alongside the judgments of prior scholarship that may have
  accepted too readily categories, and also category assumptions, that favor the 'real' Sufi saint
  over the mendacious shrine functionary" (p. 117, folio 104).
- **Internal reform as a live question at the shrines, from the mid-nineteenth century to the
  present** — "It concerns not only the elite-minded reformers but also the khadim community in its
  self-perception of the role that it is supposed to play as custodians and transmitters of the
  saint's legacy" (p. 117, folio 104); the wider attack is "on Sufism as itself in toto a deviation
  from true Islam, insofar as it is linked to cults at saints' tombs, invocation of these saints as
  intercessors, and engagement with music and poetry as media for accessing the Unseen" (p. 117,
  folio 104).
- **The chapter's closing thesis on pilgrimage** — "Pilgrimage to the shrines of Chishti saints is a
  practice that pulls together many contradictory strands of religious identity and political power.
  It is simultaneously defended and attacked for its Islamic credentials, and it is at once dependent
  on state patronage and on popular participation" (p. 117, folio 104).
- **Chapter 6 opens by warning against present-mindedness** — "Assuming an absolute break between past
  and present ignores powerful continuities and the influence of tradition" (p. 118, folio 105);
  colonialism "looms over the history of much of the non-European world like an immense shadow"
  (p. 118, folio 105); the difficulty is "what J. H. Hexter has called 'the disposition to
  present-mindedness'" (p. 118, folio 105). The authors state their own position: "We are still faced
  with the chasm between the perspective of a Sufi author and a modern researcher, who is not a Sufi.
  The chasm cannot be bridged, but it can be constantly kept in mind" (p. 118, folio 105).

  parenthesis's "nine days earlier each solar year" is likewise recorded as printed and not
  adjusted. A human must decide whether either figure is the book's own or the text layer's; the
  numeral is legible in the extraction, so it is not obviously a substitution of the kind
  inventoried below. Nothing in this chunk depends on it except the ʿurs-timing bullet, which should
  not be quoted with a corrected number.
