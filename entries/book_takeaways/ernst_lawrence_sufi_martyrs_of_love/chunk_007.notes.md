## Practices, institutions, economy

- **ʿurs defined, and dated as a term.** "this Muslim calendar lists saints' festivals by the death
  anniversary, called 'urs, literally 'wedding'; this records the date when the saint's soul was
  'wedded;' that is, united with God" (p. 104, folio 91). "It is not clear when the term 'urs first
  came into use, though it was already common among the Chishtis in the early fourteenth century"
  (p. 104, folio 91). Hajji Imdad Allah "traced the term 'urs to a saying of the Prophet Muhammad,
  directed at the saints as they prepare for death: 'Sleep with the sleep of a bridegroom
  `( 'arus)[text-layer?] [(ʿarus)]`'; this saying suggests that the physical death of the saint is
  in fact the moment of joyous reunion with the beloved" (p. 104, folio 91).
- **Why pilgrimage is better at ʿurs than at other times.** "To make a pilgrimage, or ziyarat, to
  the tomb of a saint is considered beneficial at any time, but at the time of the 'urs special
  blessings are available, since Paradise rejoices at the return of that supremely happy moment
  when a human soul is united with God" (p. 104, folio 91).
- **South Asian ʿurs contrasted with Mediterranean mawlid.** "The celebration of saints' death
  anniversaries seems to be peculiar to the Islamic East, since in Mediterranean countries
  celebrations commonly occur on the birthday (mawlid) of the saint" (p. 104, folio 91).
- **The ʿurs calendar as a book, with its physical extent given.** The *Makhzan-i a'ras* (Treasury
  of Death Anniversaries), 1742-43: "The main body of the book is a calendar of saints, which, like
  the Roman Catholic calendars, lists for each day of the year the Sufi saints whose festivals are
  to be celebrated according to the Islamic lunar calendar" (p. 104, folio 91); "the lithographed
  edition of the Makhzan-i a'ras gives the death anniversaries of hundreds of saints in well over
  200 pages. In the 12-page introduction, the author describes the reasons for making pilgrimages to
  the tombs of Sufi saints, and how to perform the requisite ceremonies" (p. 104, folio 91).
- **A shrine holding its own ʿurs manuscript — the chunk's most directly citable institutional
  fact.** "a manuscript in the Nizami-Chishti shrine of Taunsa, compiled at the end of the
  eighteenth century and arranged according to the months of the Muslim lunar calendar (see
  Appendix)" (p. 104, folio 91), and that list "include[s] extra figures outside the Chishti
  lineage, like the first four caliphs" as well as "relatively obscure undated ancillary figures who
  are related by family to the main Chishti shaykhs of this lineage" (p. 104, folio 91).
- **Food offerings, poverty, and the social reach of the calendar.** "References to problems of
  presenting food offerings during times of poverty indicate that the author had in mind the
  religious devotee lacking worldly resources. Yet the rich and powerful were also interested in
  observing the death anniversaries of the saints, to judge from the dedication of the work to a
  powerful noble and patron of Sufism, Anwar ad-Din Khan" (p. 105, folio 92).
- **A royal recension that stripped the scholarship out.** "another recension, compiled by one
  Muhammad Sharif at the request of Tipu Sultan of Mysore (r. 1783-1799), which eliminated the
  scholarly apparatus (including the year of death), thus becoming a purely devotional calendar"
  (p. 105, folio 92).
- **The printed afterlife of the ʿurs calendar.** "today one can still acquire current Urdu almanacs
  printed in Bombay and Lahore that prominently feature the death anniversaries of Sufi saints of
  the Indian subcontinent" (p. 105, folio 92).
- **Shrine endowment and a court-appointed administrator — the book's clearest statement on shrine
  administration.** "The major shrine complexes are more noteworthy because of their textual
  documentation, architectural patronage, and imposing administrative structure. This is especially
  evident in the imperial expansion of Ajmer under the Mughals, and in the creation of a
  court-appointed administrator for the religious endowment of the shrine" (p. 99, folio 86).
- **Royal patronage as building programme.** "Particularly in the Mughal period, rulers made state
  visits and endowed major construction at the site" (p. 99, folio 86); "like many other rulers, the
  Mughals erected extensive monuments at the tombs of Sufi saints, as a sign of their devotion and
  in hope of saintly assistance" (p. 101, folio 88); "The buildings themselves have been
  commemorated in detailed miniature paintings for the court, frequently in the context of royal
  visits" (p. 99, folio 86). Firuz Shah's "opulent refurbishing of the tomb of Nizam ad-Din" is the
  Sultanate instance (p. 99, folio 86).
- **Shrine visiting as modern statecraft.** "Even modern-day politicians feel the need to visit
  Mu'in ad-Din's shrine" (p. 99, folio 86), with the 1980s and late-1996 visits listed above.
- **Women's access, prohibited by decree and still prohibited by custom.** Firuz Shah "complains
  about the large numbers of women traveling by all sorts of conveyance outside of the city of Delhi
  to visit the tombs of saints, where (he believed) they were exposed to the lascivious attentions
  of rogues and profligates. His solution was to issue a royal decree forbidding women to perform
  pilgrimage to these tombs" (p. 98, folio not stated) — and "The very existence of such a
  prohibition is clear evidence of the popularity of the practice of pilgrimage among women"
  (p. 99, folio 86). In the present: Jahanara's meditations inside the tomb "would not be permitted
  in current Chishti practice, according to which women are not allowed to enter the tombs of men
  (nor are men allowed to enter the relatively few women's tombs)" (p. 103, folio 90), her own
  licence coming because "she and her father the emperor were the principal patrons of the tomb"
  (p. 103, folio 90).
- **The rituals of a shrine visit, as performed in 1643 and described as still current.** "at his
  tomb she performed the customary rituals that are still carried out at Sufi shrines around the
  world, including the recitation of prayers and sections of the Qur'an, with the dedication of
  their benefit to the inhabitants of the tomb" (p. 101, folio 88). Jahanara's own list: circling
  the tomb — "Having entered the dome, I went around the light-filled tomb of my master seven times,
  sweeping it with my eyelashes"; going "barefoot, kissing the ground"; applying "the highest
  quality of attar on the perfumed tomb"; laying cloth — "having taken off the rose scarf that I had
  on my head, I placed it on top of the blessed tomb"; lamp-lighting — "I lit a candle to the spirit
  of that revered one" (p. 102, folio 89); and a festival — "One night I performed a wonderful
  birthday and lamp festival for the saint" (p. 102, folio 89).
- **Pilgrimage timed to the anniversary, with dates as printed.** "Her pilgrimage was timed to
  coincide with the death anniversary of the saint" (p. 101, folio 88); "From the 17th of Sha'ban,
  1053, to Friday the 7th of the blessed month of Ramadan [October 31 to November 19, 1643], when I
  entered the building on the shore of Anasagar lake" (p. 102, folio 89); "on Thursday, the fourth
  of the blessed month of Ramadan, I attained the happiness of pilgrimage" (p. 102, folio 89).
- **Building by the patron, used by the pilgrim.** "the mosque where she prayed in Ajmer had been
  recently reconstructed by her father Shah Jahan" (p. 101, folio 88); "Having gone to the marble
  mosque that the great and God-knowing father of this lowly woman erected, I performed ritual
  prayer" (p. 102, folio 89).
- **Initiation from a dead saint, and the ritual gesture that seals it.** "the master who initiated
  her over four centuries after his death (initiation is referred to by the expression 'taking the
  hand,' which is the ritual gesture that seals this relationship)" (p. 101, folio 88); "the easy
  coexistence of dual initiations" with her living master Mulla Shah (p. 101, folio 88).
- **Posthumous succession as a branch practice.** "In accordance with the Uwaysi pattern of
  postmortem initiation well known in the Sabiri branch of the Chishtiyya, a disciple, Shahid Allah,
  received authorization from Zauqi Shah to be his successor, four years after the master's death"
  (p. 96, folio 82).
- **Khanaqah as a maintained residence with dates and places.** Zauqi Shah "maintained a Sufi lodge
  (khanaqah) in Hyderabad (1930-40) and Ajmer (1940-47), traveling frequently to other cities"
  (p. 96, folio 82), and "His maintenance of a Sufi residence did not in any way impede his active
  involvement in society and politics" (p. 96, folio 82). Forty-day retreats: "in 1914 at Ajmer and
  in 1916 at Kalyar, the site of the shrine of 'Ali Sabir" (p. 96, folio 82).
- **Shrine administration as a legislative question in the colonial state.** "He was particularly
  concerned with legislation that governed the administration of the Ajmer shrine" (p. 97, folio not
  stated).
- **Pension and patronage as Sufi economy, twice.** Simnani "at one point … seems to be recommending
  [his teacher] to the court for a pension" (p. 94, folio not stated); Zauqi's father "served the
  colonial government as a physician, receiving a pension when he retired in 1898" (p. 95,
  folio 81).
- **Print, journalism and English as a modern shrine-adjacent economy.** Zauqi Shah "grew up in an
  environment that was already saturated by the new technology of printing" (p. 95, folio 81); his
  studies "included education in English, something that would later make it possible for him to
  pursue a career in journalism and to interact with English disciples and correspondents" (p. 96,
  folio 82); his legacy "is now being expanded both by the activities of his followers in new
  locales (Malaysia, America) and through a new medium, English translation" (p. 97, folio not
  stated), amplified "by the extensive publishing programs of his followers, both in Urdu and,
  increasingly, in English" (p. 97, folio not stated).
- **Samaʿ defended under modern attack, and performed at Mecca.** "Amidst the polemical modern
  controversy over listening to music, he strongly defended this aspect of Chishti practice. He even
  managed, during his final pilgrimage to Mecca in 1950, to arrange for a performance of Sufi music
  in the sacred mosque of Mecca-something that would have been violently opposed by the anti-Sufi
  Sa'udi regime had they known of it" (p. 96, folio 82).
- **Minor shrines as the great unrecorded mass.** "there are countless minor Chishti shrines found

  their own localities, or who have been described in texts though their shrines remain obscure. But
  there are many more whose history will never be known" (p. 100, folio 87).
- **A shrine's healing specialty named.** "the healing specialty of this shrine is to" (p. 94, folio

- **Shrine geography as a typology.** "The tombs that are the goals of pilgrims have widely divergent

  own death, we hear very little" (p. 94, folio not stated).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Fire from heaven consuming a village, attributed to Simnani's own discourses.** "His discourses
  contain a list of 15 miracles that he performed in different localities, frequently to discomfit
  his critics; in one case, negative comments spread about him after his arrival in a village in
  Gujarat and prompted him to summon fire from heaven, consuming the entire village" (p. 94, folio
  not stated). Ernst and Lawrence's own verdict follows: "This kind of miracle story legitimizes the
  authority of the saint through supernatural power of divine origin. It may be 'true;' but it
  betrays the institutionalizing form of the hagiographical impulse" (p. 94, folio not stated).
- **A doubter converted over a name.** "Simnani's conversion of the doubting 'Ali Qalandar seems to
  serve a confirmatory function in his subsequent hagiography. 'Ali Qalandar is converted because he
  had made the mistake of challenging Simnani about his titular name, Jahangir ('the one who holds
  the world')" (p. 94, folio not stated).
- **Born a saint, against his own doctrine.** "According to the late hagiography Khazinat
  `al-asfiya:[text-layer?] [al-asfiya']` Simnani's birth and early childhood were not important,
  except that one should note that he 'was born a saint'-this in spite of the fact that Simnani
  himself says in Lata'if-i Ashrafi that sainthood is something that needs to be acquired" (p. 92,
  folio 78).
- **Prodigious childhood learning, attributed to the same late hagiography.** "by the age of seven
  he had memorized the `Qur' an[text-layer?] [Qur'an]` according to seven different methods of
  recitation, and by the age of fourteen he had mastered all the rational and traditional sciences"
  (p. 92, folio 78).
- **A transhistorical apparition as the cause of an initiation.** "It was due to the appearance of
  Khizr to him after seven years of continuous spiritual discipline, and above all, his immersion in
  cycles of chants (azkar), that he began the spiritual odyssey … Thus it was a transhistorical
  apparition that led him to join the Chishti order" (p. 93, folio 79).
- **The jinn of Kichchaucha and the blank film — reported as Schimmel's own conclusion.** "she tried
  to take a picture of the women in ecstasy next to Simnani's tomb, but the film when developed
  remained blank" (p. 94, folio not stated); "the conclusion seemed inescapable to Professor
  Schimmel: The jinn, or spirits inhabiting the possessed, had also been responsible for frustrating
  her documentary quest" (p. 95, folio 81).
- **Battle advice from a saint to a conqueror, with the outcome withheld.** "When Qiran as-Sa'dayn
  ('the Conjunction of Felicities;' an epithet based on Timur's horoscope) asked Simnani how to win
  battles, the latter told him to recite a passage from the Qur'an (Sura 58, al-Mujadala); we are
  not told if the world-conqueror employed this technique, or with what results" (p. 94, folio not
  stated).
- **Dream initiation by a saint dead seven centuries.** Zauqi Shah's "dreams and spiritual
  experiences at Chishti shrines, including an encounter with Khizr and a dream initiation by Mu'in
  ad-Din Chishti in 1920" (p. 96, folio 82); and his own restraint — "Zauqi Shah believed in the
  reality of miracles, but they did not play a prominent role in his own life. Most of the
  extraordinary events that he experienced were internal (dreams, spiritual encounters)" (p. 96,
  folio 82), though "he occasionally revealed an intuitive knowledge of the spiritual states of
  others" (p. 96, folio 82).
- **A spirit issuing pilgrimage orders.** At Ajmer "the spirit of Mu'in ad-Din Chishti gave him
  instruction in meditation and ordered him to visit the nearby tomb of Hamid ad-Din in Nagaur"
  (p. 98, folio not stated).
- **A city's survival credited to a saint.** Delhi's "continued survival as dependent on his
  intercessory powers", attributed to "even non-Muslim writers" (p. 92, folio 78).
- **Jahanara's own ecstasy at the tomb, in her words.** "a marvelous spiritual state and mystical
  experience befell this annihilated one, which cannot rightly be written. From extreme longing I
  became astonished, and I do not know what I said or did" (p. 102, folio 89).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **What separates a major Chishti master from a minor one — the chapter's organising question.**
  "What separates minor from major? It may be the absence of either a literary tradition, or a tomb
  cult, or both, but it also may be that the circle of influence, at least in respect to major
  saints, seems less broadly inclusive and brightly remembered" (p. 92, folio 78). The answer is
  applied: Simnani has a vast literary tradition but no wide tomb cult — "What is also missing in
  the case of Sayyid Ashraf is a tomb cult that sustains more than local interest" (p. 94, folio not
  stated); Zauqi Shah has neither, because "there is no possibility of a tomb cult, since he is
  buried outside Mecca" (p. 97, folio not stated). **Directly relevant to how this archive decides
  which shrines matter.**
- **Hagiography read as institution-building rather than as record.** The fire-from-heaven story "may
  be 'true;' but it betrays the institutionalizing form of the hagiographical impulse" (p. 94, folio
  not stated); the conversion story "seems to serve a confirmatory function in his subsequent
  hagiography" (p. 94, folio not stated).
- **Argument from silence, used twice.** "Another unusual feature of Simnani's life story is the
  lack of any account of his conduct in society. A saint's social conduct is usually considered
  important in his hagiography, so its absence from Simnani's is all the more surprising" (p. 93,
  folio 79); "His attitude to family life is also, uncharacteristically, given little attention"
  (p. 93, folio 79).
- **Modern sainthood must be tested against the same paradoxes as premodern.** "Since Sufism and
  sainthood should not be limited to a remote golden age, we must consider modern saints in terms of
  the same paradoxes that confronted the archetypal model, Nizam ad-Din. New constraints must be
  recognized, including the inversion of traditional sources of authority by the imposition of
  non-Muslim foreign rule in British India" (p. 95, folio 81).
- **The biographical process as a tool that keeps working after death.** "The biographical process
  continues to be a critical tool in the ongoing fashioning of identities after the death of the
  saint" (p. 97, folio not stated); "the major difference in the modern period is that a host of new
  economic, technological, and cultural factors are added to the mix" (p. 97, folio not stated).
- **The Wahhabi dating of the anti-shrine shift, tied to a specific Chishti "cycle".** "The major
  shift in Chishti devotionalism to tomb cults dates not from the second but from the third cycle of
  the Indian Chishtiyya. It occurred during the colonial period, in particular due to the rise of
  the Wahhabis of Sa'udi Arabia in the nineteenth century" (p. 103, folio 90), and criticism
  "mushroomed into an unprecedented denunciation in the nineteenth century, led by a branch of
  reformist Chishti scholars linked to the reformist school at Deoband in North India" (p. 99,
  folio 86).
- **The "Hindu survival" explanation of ziyarat refused, without denying Hindu practice.** "Protestant
  British civil servants and modern Muslim reformers alike have often seen in this ritual the
  insidious influence of Indian paganism … but a closer look reveals that the case is not so simple.
  Hindu practices undoubtedly occur at some Muslim shrines. The presence of Hindu practices at a
  Muslim shrine cannot, however, explain centuries of participation of educated Sufi masters in
  pilgrimage, for they found ziyarat pilgrimage to be an authentic expression of Islamic piety,
  `Qur' anic[text-layer?] [Qur'anic]` in spirit and firmly based on the model of the Prophet
  Muhammad" (pp. 103–104, folios 90–91). **A load-bearing argument for this archive's Muslim-shrine
  entries.**
- **A confession of the chapter's own limits.** "What follows here is therefore an account of the
  most accessible aspect of Sufi shrine pilgrimage, that is, the intersection of ritual practice and
  social history that is made possible by the literate tradition of Indo-Persian Sufi writing and
  the `patronage ofTimurid[text-layer?] [patronage of Timurid]` dynasts. The full story is much
  bigger, but even this brief glimpse suggests how important these major Chishti shrines were to
  their devotees" (p. 100, folio 87).
- **The near-total absence of women from Chishti hagiography, stated as a rule with one exception.**
  "While one might expect to find accounts of their piety in the biographical literature, the
  opposite is the case: No hagiography sets forth a major saintly woman on the same par as the
  Chishti exemplars discussed elsewhere. Instead, pious Muslim women are noted in the literature
  only as part of the groups seeking advice and solace from the major masters" (p. 99, folio 86);
  Jahanara's account "draws attention to the difficulty of eliciting the role of women in the
  practice of Sufism through historical documentation alone" (p. 103, folio 90); "this is part of
  the famous problem of the 'invisibility' of Muslim women, which is slowly being illuminated
  through the efforts of many scholars" (p. 103, folio 90).
- **Class as the condition of a woman's literary survival.** "Although relatively few non-elite
  Muslim women left well-attested literary legacies, among upper-class women there was much freer
  access to education as well as the possibility of acting as a patron for religious and cultural
  activities" (p. 100, folio 87).
- **Simnani's value to the historian stated as a source argument.** His *Lata'if-i Ashrafi* "is shorn
  of any oral, extemporaneous qualities; it is a highly complex literary and historical composition,
  with tremendous value for reconstructing precisely those aspects of the Chishtiyya that are not
  touched upon in the informal discussions chronicled in either the original records of
  conversations or later hagiographies" (p. 92, folio 78); "The writings of Simnani in fact
  constitute a vast repository of early Indian and Persian Sufi tradition, often with surprising
  variations on standard themes" (p. 92, folio 78).
- **Social position of the calendar's compilers inferred, and flagged as inference.** "Neither
  Muhammad Najib nor Sharaf ad-Din Nahrawali appears among the list `ofNizam[text-layer?] [of
  Nizam]` ad-Din Awrangabadi's chief disciples, nor do they mention his successor Fakhr ad-Din
  Dihlawi, so it may be assumed that they were probably minor government functionaries and lay
  disciples rather than full-time Sufis" (p. 105, folio 92).
- **Codification read as a response to political disorder.** "The times during which Muhammad Najib
  wrote were troubled ones, when, it may be supposed, the codification of religious traditions might
  serve as a source of order amid political chaos. Mughal India in the eighteenth century was
  undergoing a difficult decentralization, and its territory was disputed by Afghans, Marathas, and
  Sikhs" (p. 105, folio 92).
- **The practices in the calendar are old, not the compiler's invention.** "The practices described
  by Muhammad Najib, based on these early texts that he quotes at length, are thus the product of
  centuries of tradition" (p. 105, folio 92); "The Makhzan-i `a'raswas[text-layer?] [a'ras was]` not
  a novelty, but was based on earlier calendars of saints and a number of other literary sources"
  (p. 104, folio 91).
- **ziyarat set against hajj at the top of the chapter.** "It is well known that in the Islamic
  tradition the hajj pilgrimage to Mecca is of paramount importance, but local and sectarian forms
  of pilgrimage to tombs (Arabic ziyara, Persian ziyarat) are also widely practiced. Pilgrimage to
  the tombs of the Shi'i martyrs is an important feature of Shi'i piety, and across the Islamic
  world, from Morocco to Chinese Turkestan, the tombs of the saints are the resort of Muslims of
  many varying backgrounds" (p. 98, folio not stated).
- **The Deobandi/Sabiri contest over a single ancestor.** "we see how the legacy of Hajji Imdad
  Allah could also be claimed by Sabiri Chishtis, even though they rejected the scripturalist
  reformism of the Deobandis, who claimed Imdad Allah as their founder and exemplar" (p. 95,
  folio 81).
