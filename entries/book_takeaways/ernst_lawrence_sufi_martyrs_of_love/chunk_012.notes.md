## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Tomb construction at Ajmer in three stages, the chunk's only building history** — "The first tomb
  for Mu'in ad-Din was made of wood, and then they built above it a stone canopy. In other words,
  they left the first tomb in its place, erecting a lofty, noble canopy over it" (pp. 161–162, folios
  149–150); then "The first person to add to the tomb edifice of Mu'in ad-Din was Khwaja Husayn
  Nagori. Afterward, some of the kings of Mandu added the doorway and hospice" (p. 162, folio 150).
  **No date is given for any of the three stages: (not stated).**
- **Credit from a neighbourhood grocer as a saint's household economy, with a ceiling on it** — "There
  was a certain grocer in his neighborhood who in the early years of his career always used to give
  Qutb ad-Din a dam [`l/40th[text-layer?]` [1/40th] of a rupee] on credit. The saint instructed the
  grocer not to lend him any more than 300 dirhams. As charitable offerings [futuh] began to arrive,
  he also made use of them" (p. 165, folio 153). A second, differing ceiling follows immediately:
  "Shaykh Nizam ad-Din Awliya' that Shaykh Mu'in ad-Din had permitted Shaykh Qutb ad-Din to borrow up
  to 500 dirhams. When he became a perfected saint, however, he ceased to live on credit" (p. 165,
  folio 153). **300 against 500 — see Doubts.**
- **futuh as the mechanism by which a hospice is funded, named twice** — "As charitable offerings
  [futuh] began to arrive, he also made use of them" (p. 165, folio 153); and at Ghiyaspur, "The
  noble and the lowly—every class of people turned to him, and the doors of futuh (charity) were
  opened and the world benefited from the outpourings of his generosity and kindness" (p. 172, folio
  160). This is the chunk's clearest statement of shrine-and-hospice economy.
- **The miraculous loaf, and how the flow of charity stops when its social cause is exposed** — a loaf
  "appeared under his prayer mat" and "He distributed the loaf among his entire household"; the
  grocer's wife was sent "to gather information in the women's quarters of the Shaykh's household",
  and "from that time on the cake ceased to appear" (p. 165, folio 153).
- **Succession by written document and by transferred objects, itemised** — Mu'in ad-Din ordered 'Ali
  Sanjari: "Write a document indicating that Qutb ad-Din should go to Delhi since we have given him
  the authority to succeed us and Delhi should be his place of work"; then "He placed a turban and a
  cap on the head of this faqir, and the staff of Khwaja 'Usman Harwani in my hand, and the patched
  cloak [khirqa] on my back. He also gave me the Holy Qur'an, the prayer carpet and the wooden shoes.
  'This is a trust from the Prophet; he noted, 'which has been handed down by my master (i.e., the
  pre-Indian Chishti shaykhs)'" (p. 166, folio 154).
- **The reception of a new successor by a city's religious establishment** — "Then I went to Delhi and
  settled there. All of the sudurs and imams [i.e., religious functionaries] of the city came to do
  me homage" (p. 166, folio 154).
- **A hospice that is still standing when the source writes** — "In Delhi, by an intimation from the
  Unseen World, Nizam ad-Din settled in Ghiyaspur, where to the present day his hospice is located"
  (p. 172, folio 160).
- **Refusal of food with the urban poor as the stated reason** — past "eighty years of age… fasting
  continuously", he answered a servant's plea: "Think how many poor people and faqirs [spiritual
  mendicants] are suffering hunger and deprivation, huddled around the mosques, sleeping in the
  streets of the city—how can this food go down my throat?" (p. 172, folio 160).
- **Gift-giving at an audience, and what happens to the gifts** — visitors "each brought some gift";
  a sceptical scholar reasoned "All these gifts will be put together in front of the Shaykh… and a
  servant will come to pick them up", and offered a twist of paper holding road dust, which the
  Shaykh had collected with the rest "since it is a special collyrium for my eyes" (p. 173, folio 161).
- **Refusal of state business, twice, and the threat to leave the city** — 'Ala ad-Din Khilji sent a
  letter of state by Khizr Khan, "since the prince was a disciple of the Shaykh"; "the Shaykh left it
  unopened", called for the Surat al-Fatiha and said "What has the work of dervishes to do with the
  work of kings? I am a dervish and I have chosen a corner of the city in which to busy myself with
  prayers for the king and for all Muslims. If the king says anything further to me, I shall shift to
  another place:'" (p. 174, folio 162); and on the proposed royal visit, "The house of this humble
  person has two doors. If the king enters by one, I will leave by the other:'" (p. 174, folio 162).
- **sama' regulated: qawwals yes, instruments no** — "I have prohibited the use of musical instruments
  and indulgence in other forbidden practices during `sama~[text-layer?]` [sama'.] What they have
  done is not good" (p. 174, folio 162); the qawwal Abu Bakr is a professional reciter travelling
  between Multan, Ajodhan and Delhi (p. 171, folio 159); a qawwal recites at 'Ali Sanjari's house
  (p. 165, folio 153); and Shihab ad-Din "summoned qawwals and arranged a room for the gathering"
  (p. 174, folio 162).
- **Hindu pilgrimage at Pushkar, described with hostility by the source** — "The Hindus worship that
  reservoir. Every year for six days at the time of the passing of Scorpio they congregate and bathe
  there, wasting both their lives and their `childrens'` lives in futile religious rituals. Those
  among them who believe in Judgment Day claim that Judgment will begin at this same reservoir"
  (p. 164, folio 152). A dated annual congregation — the only calendrical gathering in the chunk, and
  it is not an ʿurs.
- **Conquest framed as the condition of the order's establishment** — "Since that time Islam has
  prevailed in this region and the root of unbelief and corruption has been eradicated" (p. 161,
  folio 149).
- **An office of religious endowments named** — "Shams al-Mulk, the chief of religious endowments of
  Delhi", under whom Nizam ad-Din finished his studies (p. 171, folio 159).
- **Nightly durud counted** — "Every night before resting he recited 3000 invocations [durud] in
  praise of the Prophet" (p. 165, folio 153).
- **The inverted forty-day fast as a technique, with its mechanics** — "the inverted 40-day fast"
  [chilla-i ma'kus]… "For 40 days they would suspend him from a branch of the tree that overhung the
  well every night. At daybreak they would pull him out" (p. 168, folio 156).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

All of the following are reported by 'Abd al-Haqq in *Akhbar al-akhyar* as translated here, not
asserted by Ernst and Lawrence.

- **Pithaura Rai's fall foretold and claimed** — after Pithaura mocked him ("This man… has come here
  but what does he do except sit and converse with the Unseen?"), Mu'in ad-Din said "We have caught
  Pithaura Rai alive, and delivered him (to his enemy):'", and "Soon thereafter" Pithaura was "taken
  captive by Mu'izz ad-Din" (p. 161, folio 149).
- **The inscription on the dead saint's forehead** — "After his death, on the forehead of Mu'in ad-Din
  appeared this inscription: \"The lover of God has died in the love of God.\"" (p. 161, folio 149).
- **The loaf under the prayer mat** — "it happened by the grace of God that a loaf of bread appeared
  under his prayer mat" (p. 165, folio 153).
- **The Prophet's complaint about missed durud** — "Give my greetings to Bakhtiyar Kaki and tell him
  that my nightly gift from him has not arrived these past three nights:'" (p. 165, folio 153).
- **Death by a sung line** — the couplet "so moved Khwaja Qutb ad-Din that for four days and four
  nights he remained ecstatic, continually longing to hear this line repeated. On the fifth night he
  died" (p. 166, folio 154).
- **Foreknowledge of his own death** — "why has God brought me here? God wishes me to die here. After
  a few days I will go on a journey" (p. 166, folio 154), matched by the report that "twenty days
  after I had left Ajmer, Khwaja Mu'in ad-Din had surrendered his soul to the mercy of God:'"
  (p. 166, folio 154).
- **Pebbles turned to sugar** — "Stretching out his blessed hand to earth, he picked up some pebbles
  and put them in his mouth. Those pebbles turned to sugar on his tongue. When the same miracle had
  occurred three times, he realized that the sugar was a gift from God", and Qutb ad-Din confirmed
  "Go, and you will be sweet like sugar:'" (p. 168, folio 156).
- **Sugar to salt and back** — "\"This is not sugar; it is salt;' replied the merchant. \"Then let it
  be salt;' quipped the saint"; the bags were found full of salt, and on the merchant's apology "The
  Shaykh so ordered, and it became sugar" (p. 168, folio 156).
- **Food rejected by the body because its source was impure** — bread from "a drunkard" broken after a
  three-day fast "did not sit well on his stomach. He vomited", and Qutb ad-Din explained "God has
  shown His concern for you so that the food did not remain in your stomach" (p. 168, folio 156).
- **Detection of borrowed salt by inner light** — "the Shaykh, by his inner light, perceived what had
  occurred" (p. 167, folio 155).
- **A lamb fathered by a wolf** — "A wolf had impregnated that sheep, and the newborn lamb lay on the
  ground, contentedly gazing at that `wolfl[text-layer?]` [wolf!]", taken as the sign that Nagaur was
  fit for settlement (p. 164, folio 152).
- **The handsome youth from the Unseen who fixed Nizam ad-Din at Ghiyaspur** — "a very handsome and
  delicate youth appeared before me", recited a couplet, counselled "True benefit comes from remaining
  in the midst of people while constantly remembering God", refused food, "drank a little water and
  left:'" (p. 172, folio 160).
- **The dream book** — "I saw a book in which was written, 'So long as you are able, provide comfort
  to the ear of the believer, for that is the place where lordship is manifested.' And again, 'On the
  Day of Resurrection, purity of heart will not be for sale in the market place.'" (p. 173, folio 161).
- **The master's rebuke from beyond the grave** — Farid ad-Din appears in Nizam ad-Din's dream over
  the turned-away dervish; on waking the Shaykh set a standing rule: "when you consider awaking me,
  ask yourself two questions: One, has evening come? and two, has a guest come?" (p. 173, folio 161).
- **Weekly meeting at the Ka'ba** — Shaykh Mohun's claim "that every Friday evening I see him at the
  Ka'ba", which distressed Nizam ad-Din rather than pleasing him (pp. 173–174, folios 161–162).
- **Insensibility to bereavement while absorbed** — "Even if one of his children happened to die, he
  would not be aware of what had happened till some time later" (p. 165, folio 153).
- **Miracle-working defined as the gnostic's highest degree** — "The highest degree for the gnostic,
  with respect to love, is that if someone requests something of him, he provides it through the
  power of saintly miracles" (p. 163, folio 151).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The appendix's source and method, stated by Ernst and Lawrence in their own voice** — the material
  comes from "a single, heretofore untranslated tazkira", whose author shows "no bias toward any one
  order or viewpoint", though "'Abd al-Haqq accents the pivotal role of the Chishtiyya, at least for
  the first cycle and arguably for every cycle thereafter"; the book "is structured by generations and
  it recounts the lives of great sufi masters from the twelfth to the late sixteenth century"
  (p. 161, folio 149). Endnote markers 1 and 2 are attached to this paragraph and to the first
  biography (p. 161, folio 149).
- **Nizam ad-Din ranked first by the translators** — "Many of the biographies relate to disciples of
  Nizam ad-Din, arguably the foremost Chishti master of the premodern period" (p. 161, folio 149);
  the headnote to his life repeats it, "the foremost of the Chishti exemplars" (p. 170, folio 158).
- **The translators' reading of what 'Abd al-Haqq is doing with Mu'in ad-Din: geography** — "[The
  strongest accent `of'[text-layer?]` [of ʿ]Abd al-Haqq is on location. Where Mu'in ad-Din came from,
  where he settled and where Ajmer was in relation to `Nagaur-all[text-layer?]` [Nagaur—all] occupy
  the imagination of Shaykh 'Abd al-Haqq" (p. 161, folio 149).
- **A translators' caveat on the authenticity of the sayings, worth quoting whenever the aphorisms are
  cited** — "The collection of `aphorisms-which[text-layer?]` [aphorisms—which] may or may not have
  been uttered by the `saint-establish[text-layer?]` [saint—establish] a pervasive tone for the entire
  Chishti discipline" (p. 161, folio 149).
- **The translators' one-line characterisation of Qutb ad-Din** — "Shaykh Qutb ad-Din stands next in
  rank after Shaykh Mu'in ad-Din. He is chiefly remembered for his ecstatic and contemplative life.
  Although prone to intoxication, he did not neglect his observance of the canonical prayers"
  (p. 165, folio 153).
- **The translators on Nizam ad-Din: the argument is about place and reach** — "The major difference is
  the accent on location and influence: he was the spiritual master of Delhi, and from Delhi, his
  blessings influenced all of India" (p. 170, folio 158), which the source text then echoes: "The
  country of Hindustan is full of the effects of his blessings" (p. 171, folio 159).
- **A theological gloss the compiler adds in his own voice** — after the saying that a disciple's sins
  go unrecorded for twenty years, "The recorder of these lines attests that some of the early saints
  also spoke such words", followed by the later Sufis' position that once repentance is evident "the
  recording of sins… is impossible since by nature such a person can commit no sin" (p. 162, folio
  150). The compiler is visibly arguing with his own material.

## Doubts

- **The `shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya` mapping rests on a bare name plus a travelling qawwal.** The
  book writes only "Shaykh Baha ad-Din", with no nisba, no Zakariya, no tomb, no death date and no
  order (p. 171, folio 159); the archive row names its principal figure "Bahauddin Zakariya" of
  Multan City. The link is that the qawwal "came from Multan" and had recited "in sama' before Shaykh
  Baha ad-Din" before travelling on to Ajodhan (p. 171, folio 159) — the Multan connection is the
  qawwal's, and the book never says the hospice with the grain-grinding slave girls is at Multan.
  **Under the 18 September 2026 ruling a principal figure takes the row's id on a bare-name mention,
  so the id is recorded, but the identification is mine and not the book's.** No second archive row
  is a candidate: `shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya` is the only row whose principal figure is a Baha
  ad-Din.
- **The `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` mapping is secure on the name and epithet but gives the row
  no location.** The book's "Shaykh Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar" (p. 167, folio 155), his personal
  name "Mas'ud" (p. 168, folio 156) and the epithet explained twice (p. 168, folio 156) match the
  row's "Baba Fariduddin Mas'ud Ganjshakar". But the book seats him at **Ajodhan** and never writes
  Pakpattan (p. 167, folio 155): the row's location is **(not stated)** by this chunk, and nothing
  here says where he is buried.
- **The three death years in this chunk do not sit easily together and I have not reconciled them.**
  Mu'in ad-Din dies "on the sixth of `Rajah[text-layer?]` [Rajab], A.H. 633 (A.D. March 15, 1236)"
  (p. 161, folio 149); his successor Qutb ad-Din dies "on 14 Rabi' I, 633 A.H." — the same Hijri year
  (p. 166, folio 154); and Farid ad-Din, who is Qutb ad-Din's successor and whose disciple Nizam
  ad-Din came to him at twenty and outlived him by a long career, dies "on 5 Muharram 634 A.H. at the
  venerable age of 95" (p. 169, folio 157) — one year after both. The narrative in between requires a
  much longer span: Qutb ad-Din learns of Mu'in ad-Din's death twenty days after leaving Ajmer
  (p. 166, folio 154), Nizam ad-Din visits Ajodhan three times during Farid's lifetime (p. 171, folio
  159), and Nizam ad-Din is later past eighty and dealing with 'Ala ad-Din Khilji (pp. 172, 174,
  folios 160, 162). **All three dates print as clean arabic digits with no I-for-1 or o-for-0
  substitution, so no emended reading is offered for any of them**; the only damage inside a date is
  the month name "Rajah". A human must decide whether 634 is the book's error, the tazkira's, or a
  text-layer digit fault that happens to look clean. **Do not convert any of these to or from the
  Common Era.** Note also that shrine_index.tsv records `figure_died 1266` for
  `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` and the book gives no Common Era equivalent at all: the two
  numbers are recorded side by side here and not reconciled.
- **Name collision to be kept apart: the book's "Khwaja Qutb ad-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki Ushi" (p. 165,
  folio 153) is NOT the archive's `tomb-of-qutbuddin-aibak`**, whose principal figure is "Sultan Qutb
  ud-Din Aibak" of Anarkali, Lahore. The book's Qutb ad-Din is a Chishti successor buried near Delhi;
  no id was taken for him. Flagged because "Qutb ad-Din" recurs on nine of these fourteen pages.
- **Name collision to be kept apart: "Shaykh al-Islam Jalal ad-Din (Tabrizi)" (p. 169, folio 157) is
  not obviously the archive's `shrine-of-jalaluddin-surkh-posh-bukhari-jalaluddin-bukhari`**, whose principal figure is
  "Sayyid Jalaluddin Surkh-Posh Bukhari" of Uch Sharif. The book's own parenthesis marks its figure as
  Tabrizi, and the two nisbas differ, so no id was taken. **Named here rather than decided**, per the
  rule on names that could match a row.
- **Bare toponyms, written reversibly under the interim convention.** (a) **Ucch** — "the well of the
  Hajj mosque `ofUcch[text-layer?]` [of Ucch]" (p. 168, folio 156) carries a mosque, a well and a
  saint's forty-day practice, but no shrine, tomb, ʿurs or custodian, so it takes no id. **If the
  ruling reverses, no single row follows from it**: the archive has five Uch Sharif rows
  (`shrine-of-jalaluddin-surkh-posh-bukhari-jalaluddin-bukhari`, `shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht`,
  `tomb-of-baha-al-halim-uch-sharif`, `tomb-of-javindi-bibi`, `tomb-of-ustad-nuriya`) and the book
  names none of their figures, so the mention would have to be split or dropped, not assigned.

  migration stage with nothing attached. **If the ruling reverses it still resolves to no single
  row**: the archive has well over a dozen Lahore rows and this sentence distinguishes none of them.
