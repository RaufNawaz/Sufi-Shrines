## Practices, institutions, economy

- **A twentieth-century qawwali manual is quoted at length, and it is the chunk's richest material
  on the economy of shrine performance.** The book introduces it: "So important, in fact, was the
  probity of performers that it became the topic for a listing of qualifications for sama' in a
  twentieth-century manual intended for the use of qawwali singers" (p. 59, folio 45). **The manual
  is not named, its author is not named and its date is not given: (not stated).** It is printed
  under the head `THE MANNERS OF THE MusiCIAN[text-layer?] [MUSICIAN]` (p. 59, folio 45).
- **Payment of singers, stated as a rule and as a class insult.** "First, the singer should not be
  greedy. When the singer is reciting, he should be able to proceed without calculation. Whatever he
  receives he should regard as a blessing, and accept cheerfully" (p. 59, folio 45); "Nor should one
  first of all settle on the amount of payment, in the style of courtesans. This is highly
  objectionable, and it is in conflict with the honor of the singer. This is the custom of
  courtesans and low-caste musical performers, who first set the price for performing or singing,
  not even setting foot outside their house until the payment is made" (p. 59, folio 45).
- **Travel expenses are the one permitted charge, and the liability is the host's.** "But if the
  singer is invited out, it is no problem if he asks for travel expenses, rather it is the duty of
  the one who invites him to send adequate travel expenses in advance" (p. 59, folio 45).
- **Ritual and bodily discipline required of the performer.** "he should be bound by the rules of
  fasting and ritual prayer. While singing he sits in a state of ritual purity, dressed in clean and
  perfumed clothes, which are an external means toward spiritual blessings" (p. 59, folio 45); "One
  should avoid listening to the performance of a singer who indulges in intoxicants; otherwise,
  constriction and scattering will result instead of expansion and opening up" (p. 59, folio 45).
- **The performer must read the assembly and repeat on demand.** "With his gaze on the listeners,
  seeing which verses exert their power over which people, if one person goes into ecstasy from a
  particular verse, it is essential for him to repeat that verse until that person settles down from
  ecstasy" (p. 60, folio 46); "How many moments have been constricted and thoughts destroyed because
  of an ignorant singer!" (p. 60, folio 46).
- **Patronage is explicitly deprecated.** "Fourth, the enunciation and attention of the singer
  should be directed towards the people of taste and ecstasy, not towards the rich and powerful
  because of greedy hopes" (p. 60, folio 46).
- **The programme order of a sitting is fixed.** "it is necessary to consider the order of
  presentation of subjects. One begins with praise of God and adoration of the Prophet. At the end,
  one recites the unity of God. It is difficult to set other moods after reciting the unity of God"
  (p. 60, folio 46).
- **Indian musical technique is named inside the Sufi rule.** "He performs the words along with
  melody, thinking of the musical modes (raga, ragini) and their rhythms" (p. 60, folio 46). Also
  prohibited: "it is objectionable for a singer to engage in vocal exercises or showing off his art"
  (p. 60, folio 46).
- **A decline narrative about performers, stated by the manual as fact.** "The singer was generally
  learned, spiritually sensitive, and stylistically expert… But since at the present time, the
  singer is generally illiterate and is excluded from the company of pure souls, therefore I will
  mention a number of necessary manners for singers" (p. 59, folio 45), with the rule: "All
  authoritative opinion agrees that one cannot be a singer without being in the company of Sufis"
  (p. 59, folio 45).
- **Samaʿ and zikr treated as a single institutional problem.** "This last directive makes clear how
  thin the line is between zikr and `sama~[text-layer?] [samaʿ.]` Together they have been both a
  challenge and an opportunity for Chishti adepts in every generation" (p. 60, folio 46).
- **Musical assemblies as a named institution.** *majalis-i sama'* is glossed "(musical gatherings)"
  (p. 58, folio 44), and Simnani's third part gives "guidelines for the conduct of sama', including
  observations about the concurrence of 'time, place, and brethren' as well as grounds for
  permitting the use of musical instruments" (pp. 56–57, folios 42–43).
- **The legal apparatus behind permissibility.** Simnani's first part derives proofs "from Qur'anic
  verses and Prophetic traditions as well as from the decisions of jurists and the deeds of 'masters
  of `guid-ance;'[text-layer?] [guidance,']` i.e., early Muslim leaders" (p. 56, folio 42); he "cites
  the opinions of notable hadith scholars and the decisions of Hanafi jurists", which "support the
  permissibility of not only sama' but empathetic ecstasy for those who otherwise discharge their
  mandated Islamic `duties.7 3[text-layer?] [duties.73]`" (p. 57, folio 43).
- **Dancing enters the practice through Simnani.** "Simnani linked it to dancing (raqs)" (p. 58,
  folio 44); the Indian shaykhs "experienced empathetic ecstasy and danced" (p. 57, folio 43).
- **Order rivalry and the datable end of consensus.** The Chishti essayists "are closer to Ahmad
  Ghazali on the issue of `sam a'[text-layer?] [samaʿ]` than are their Sultanate rivals, the
  Suhrawardiyya, even though the latter were Ahmad Ghazali's avowed spiritual successors" (p. 58,
  folio 44); Chishti attitudes influenced "later Sufi orders such as the Qadiriyya and Firdawsiyya"
  (p. 59, folio 45); and "among Indian Muslims the wholesale rejection of sama' was not voiced —
  either as a theoretical desideratum or a practical requirement — till the advent of the
  Mujaddidiyya Naqshbandiyya in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries" (p. 59,
  folio 45).
- **Shrine custodianship producing hagiography, which is the chunk's only shrine-office material.**
  The second account of Shah Mina was "written in the nineteenth century by a custodian of the
  shrine of Shah `Mirra[text-layer?] [Mina]`, Irtida 'Ali Khan", and it "uses every trope of
  hagiography and every imaginable religious source of authority to provide an overwhelming set of
  credentials for the saint"; against it, "The one `by'Abd[text-layer?] [by 'Abd]` al-Haqq is very
  brief; Irtida 'Ali Khan's is much more developed, and also more ornate" (p. 65, folio 51). The
  custodian's office is not otherwise described and the shrine is not located beyond Lucknow:
  **(not stated)**.
- **A living publishing economy of hagiography in Pakistan and India.** "Even nowadays, after the
  partition of colonial India … an aggressive retrieval of memory has led to numerous translations
  of old hagiographies from Persian into Urdu in both Pakistan and India" (p. 61, folio 47); popular
  lore about Shah Mina is "preserved in currently available prints" (p. 66, folio 52).
- **Genealogy as the transmissible asset of a Sufi household.** "Pride of descent is strong among
  Sufi masters, who carry in their heads long and complicated genealogies" (p. 61, folio 47);
  "Genealogical authority within Islam goes back to the pre-Muslim era, and to Arab clan kinship
  practices. Modified in referents, it persists as a major index of both identity and legitimacy"
  (p. 61, folio 47).
- **Succession by adoption rather than blood, narrated as an institutional act.** Qiwam ad-Din tells
  the dervish Qutb ad-Din: "I want you to become a son, your name will be Muhammad Mina, and you
  will receive the decree of favor in place of my own `child:'[text-layer?] [child.']` In this way
  Shaykh Mina came into existence" (p. 66, folio 52).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **A saint's word kills his own son** — after the son relapsed "on the edges of the region of
  Nadirughar", the Shaykh said, "I do not wish that that failure should ever come into my presence
  `again:'[text-layer?] [again.']` From that day the boy became ill, and died" (p. 66, folio 52).
  Given by the book as 'Abd al-Haqq's narration, not as its own.
- **The infant's first day at school** — "It is transmitted that on the first day that they sent him
  to school, upon the mention of `''A, B ...\"[text-layer?] ['A, B …']` he spoke of spiritual wisdom
  and divine realities, so that those present were astonished" (p. 66, folio 52). Attributed by the
  formula "It is transmitted".
- **The miracle contest of the tiger and the moving wall** — "Popular lore has made Shah
  `Mirra[text-layer?] [Mina]` into a miracle worker whose exploits form part of a widely repeated
  pattern of saintly charisma, in which a local saint, riding on a tiger and wielding a poisonous
  snake like a whip, is defeated by the superior holiness of a saint who rides on a moving wall"
  (p. 66, folio 52); in the prints, Shah Mina sits on the wall and the tiger-rider "is none other
  than Shah Madar" (p. 66, folio 52). Attributed to "popular lore", and expressly **not** to the
  literary hagiographies: "the only remaining trace of this story is the wall on which Shah Mirra
  performs his rigorous asceticism" (p. 66, folio 52).
- **The prayer carpet and the fiery disciple** — Shah Madar "is supposed to have sent him a disciple
  named Atish-Pargala ('Compass of Fire') with a prayer carpet" (p. 65, folio 51). The book's
  hedge — "is supposed to have" — is kept.
- **A hadith legitimating bodily agitation, quoted by Mas'ud Bakk** — "To legitimate his accent on
  hizza, he quotes a hadith in which the Prophet Muhammad declares to his personal attendant:
  `'0[text-layer?] ['O]` Mu'awiya, no one experiences my kindness who is not agitated at hearing
  mention of the Beloved.'" (p. 55, folio 41).
- **A hadith making hostility to a saint an act of war against the Prophet**, quoted by Simnani as
  Ahmad Ghazali had quoted it — "He who is hostile to a saint of mine has come forth against me in
  `warfare:'[text-layer?] [warfare.']" (p. 57, folio 43).
- **Saints who died in samaʿ, treated as a class** — Simnani "re-creates the condition and lauds the
  example of saints who died in sama', from Zu'l-Nun Misri and Shibli to Qutb ad-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki
  and Ruzbihan Baqli Shirazi" (p. 57, folio 43); the book's own title phrase is attached to them —
  the Chishti writers extended their authority to "their own saints, especially those who were
  martyrs of love because of their attraction to verse chanted in musical assemblies" (p. 58,
  folio 44).
- **The deafness argument** — "since none of them was ever deaf, they all, by their reception of
  revelation, attest to the legitimacy of sama' ('hearing')" (p. 55, folio 41). Given as Bakk's own
  ingenuity, not as a miracle report.
- **The planetary ascent of hearing** — after the listener's "musical sensitivities have risen to
  the orbit of Venus (the third of the seven planets in an ascending scale), there is no longer an
  experience of self", and through the remaining four orbits one "hears the word of God
  `tlirough[text-layer?] [through]` God without letters or `voice:'[text-layer?] [voice.']`"
  (p. 55, folio 41).
- **Magic attributed to a saint without comment** — Simnani "dabbled in magic as well as mysticism"
  (p. 56, folio 42); Dara Shikuh is "Fascinated with the miraculous" yet "nonetheless takes account
  of real events" (p. 64, folio 50).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The chapter's thesis: one technical term separates Indian from non-Indian theory.** "empathetic
  ecstasy becomes the pivotal technical term in distinguishing between non-Indian and Indian
  theorists' approach to sama:`[text-layer?] [samaʿ.]`" — for the Chishtis it "is not just optional
  but indispensable to the entire experience of sama:`[text-layer?] [samaʿ]`" and "ought to be
  emphasized as much as momentary ecstasy and durative ecstasy, even though the latter are
  technically 'higher' spiritual states" (p. 54, folio 40).
- **The irony the authors press: the innovation is built out of an ambivalent source.** "How strange
  that the inspiration or justification for this new approach to sama' comes from one of the most
  ambivalent advocates of musical assemblies, Qushayri!" (p. 54, folio 40); what Nizam ad-Din does
  "is conflate the two texts, pairing each listener with an appropriate level of experience"
  (p. 54, folio 40).
- **A five-point summation of the Chishti practice of samaʿ and its legacy** (pp. 58–59, folios
  44–45), given expressly as such: "There are five major dimensions to the Chishti practice of sama'
  and its legacy" (p. 58, folio 44). (1) Indian theory "is related to non-Indian antecedents" and
  "follows the Sufi penchant for triadic progressions" (p. 58, folio 44). (2) Non-Indian writers
  often "express their personal antipathy to the self-indulgence (rather than divine inspiration) to
  which they feel immature listeners are prone" (p. 58, folio 44). (3) The Chishtis are nearer Ahmad
  Ghazali than the Suhrawardiyya are, and are "less defensive": "Instead of destroying the arguments
  of their opponents, as he had done, they describe the benefits" (p. 58, folio 44). (4) "at the
  theoretical level, the sole but significant innovation of Indian writers is their stress on
  empathetic ecstasy as a normative, essential aspect of `sama~[text-layer?] [samaʿ.]`" (p. 58,
  folio 44). (5) "the legacy of the Chishtis was cumulative. Each generation built on the experience
  and referred to the statements of past generations of renowned saints" (p. 58, folio 44).
- **Development within the Chishti camp was not uniform, and the divergence is named.** "Bakk, for
  example, subsumed empathetic ecstasy (as well as momentary ecstasy and durative ecstasy) under a
  new category, agitation (hizza), while Simnani linked it to dancing (raqs). However, both
  positions were consistent with the Chishti advocacy of sama' as the pride or adornment of their
  devotion to inner — and sometimes also outer — poverty" (p. 58, folio 44).
- **Bakk's scheme of hizza, mapped onto the body.** "That which appears in the body, according to
  Bakk, is empathetic ecstasy; in the heart, momentary ecstasy; in the spirit, durative ecstasy. The
  distinction cannot be pressed, though, since each of the three anticipates and/or is anticipated
  by the other two" (p. 56, folio 42); the five-stage chain is "resolving, entering, witnessing,
  finding, and being extinguished (qusud, wurud, shuhud, wujud, and khumud), also from Qushayri", so
  that "finding lordship `( wujud-i rububiyya)[text-layer?] [(wujud-i rububiyya)]` causes the
  extinction of humanity (khumud-i bashariyya)" (p. 56, folio 42) — "a dazzling display of mystical
  logic" (p. 56, folio 42).
- **Bakk's chapters close in verse, and the authors note the device.** The thirteenth chapter ends
  "as do all of Bakk's chapters and most of his subchapters in *Mir'at al- 'arifin*, with a verse (in
  this case, a quatrain) of his own composition" (p. 56, folio 42); the quatrain is printed in roman
  transliteration — "Anku bi-sama' dar tawajud ayad / Wajd-ash zi-khoda ru-yi bi-dil binmayad. / Pas
  wajd su-yi wujud-i mahbub kashad / Anja chu rasad na-mord ba khwud ayad." — with the translation
  "Whoever in sama' anticipates ecstasy / Ecstasy appears in his heart from God. / Ecstasy draws him
  toward the realm of the Beloved, / He arrives there but does not die; he enters
  `himsel£.7°[text-layer?] [himself.70]`" (p. 56, folio 42).
- **Simnani's classification of listeners is Indian in source, not Qushayrian.** He gives "a
  three-part classification `oflisteners[text-layer?] [of listeners]`, though not on the model of
  Zarradi or Bakk", following Jamal ad-Din Hansawi: "(1) those who forsake this world and seek the
  next; (2) those who forsake the next world and seek this; and (3) those who forsake both worlds
  and seek only God" (p. 57, folio 43); the third is preferred, "but neither of the other two is to
  be excluded from musical assemblies according to Simnani" (p. 57, folio 43).
- **Chapter 3's opening claim: hagiography is a native category, not an imposed one.** "We talk about
  genealogies because Sufi authors themselves do" (p. 61, folio 47 — printed with a broken drop cap,
  `W ecause / talk about genealogies because Sufi authors themselves do. They do so be- / cause`;
  see Doubts); and "Genealogies serialized become biographies, and in the hands of Sufi authors,
  biographies often become hagiographies — the lives of holy exemplars" (p. 61, folio 47).
- **A defence of the "old styles of reasoning".** The retrieval of hagiography is "a postmodern,
  premodern move that many 'moderns' find odd, if not objectionable" (p. 61, folio 47); Sufi masters
  and followers "continue to accept what are now regarded, in a post-Galilean and post-Cartesian
  worldview, as old styles of reasoning", preferring "'textual exegesis, cosmic analogies and above
  all appeals to authority, scriptural and hierarchical [and also] genealogical, which is to say
  `time-sanctioned:'[text-layer?] [time-sanctioned.']`" (p. 61, folio 47, with note 2).
- **A quoted proposition about genealogy and time.** "A noted scholar once remarked that 'the
  significance of genealogies, according to which the legitimacy of a particular clan or chief [or
  brotherhood] is authenticated, points to the perpetual continuity of the past in the present.'"
  (p. 61, folio 47, with note 3). **The scholar is not named in the chunk: (not stated).**
- **The methodological core: an insider/outsider tension that must not be collapsed.** "while we
  need to be able to appreciate the stories of the saints, we should distance ourselves from them by
  comparison and analysis. The tension between our outsider point of view and the insider's vision
  provides one of the most important dynamics… Flattening out either one of these poles results in
  either advocacy or reductionism" (p. 62, folio 48).
- **A charge against the existing literature, directly relevant to this archive's source criticism.**
  "more Persian Sufi `hagiographicalliterature[text-layer?] [hagiographical literature]` was
  produced in India than in all of Persia and Central Asia combined"; "By far the majority of
  English-language writing on the history of Sufism in India is of a biographical or historical
  character. But the spell of hagiography … is so strong that few writers have been able to escape
  its influence altogether… most of the supposedly scholarly literature on eminent Sufis ends up
  adopting the same rhetorical style of presentation employed by devotees" (p. 62, folio 48).
- **Origins versus beginnings, taken from Marc Bloch and then turned against him.** "Marc Bloch once
  observed that religious history has been muddled by the confusion between origins and beginnings.
  Origins reflect the ideal vision of a new moment, while beginnings are the record of its actual
  occurrence. Bloch wanted to emphasize beginnings and downplay origins. With respect to Sufism,
  neither beginnings nor origins can be ignored" (p. 62, folio 48); "The history of Chishti Sufism
  demands … attention to both its historical beginnings and its transhistorical origins" (p. 63,
  folio 49).
- **Two bad scholarly approaches named and rejected.** One "takes historical data in general and
  Sufi biographies in particular as test cases for rules or methods", seeking "to winnow the few
  pellets of truth lying beneath all the accumulated dross of legend and superstition concocted by
  overzealous hagiographers"; the other is "to excavate and then array massive chunks of obscure
  information about little-known saints, on the assumption that once their story has been told 'in
  their own `words;'[text-layer?] [words,']` the message of their quest for Truth will be
  self-evident" (p. 62, folio 48). Both serve "a narrowly positivist agenda, in which only
  scientifically determined facts are allowed entry" (p. 62, folio 48).
- **The standard of a good question, quoted.** "'a fact is a pertinent fact only with respect to a
  frame of reference involving questions that we pose to the past …. It is the ability to pose the
  "right" questions that distinguishes productive `scholarship:'[text-layer?] [scholarship.']`"
  (p. 62, folio 48, with note 8).
- **The typology that organises chapter 3: two strands of Indo-Persian hagiography.** The choice is
  "to limit oneself to Sufi exemplars or to include all pious Muslim 'heroes'" (p. 63, folio 49);
  the first strand is "The classical tradition of Sulami/Ansari/Jami, which examines only Sufi
  saints and Sufi poets", continued by Shaykh Jamali and 'Abd al-Haqq (p. 64, folio 50); the second
  "attracted some major hagiographers in the high period of Indo-Persian culture, particularly
  during the reign of the great Mughals", above all Dara Shikuh (p. 64, folio 50).
- **The supporting evidence is a long quotation from Wadad al-Qadi** on how al-Sulami's *Tabaqat
  al-sufiyya* "[displays] a certain desire for 'historicizing' Sufism" while Abu Nu'aym al-Isfahani's
  *Hilyat al-awliya* changes "the situation… completely", so that "the biography of the Companion
  'Umar ibn al-Khattab stands side by side in the book with that of the Follower al-Hasan al-Basri,
  the jurist al-Shafi'i, and the Sufi al-Junayd" (p. 63, folio 49, with note 9).
- **The verdict on Dara Shikuh: historical scruple in the service of sectarian self-authorisation.**
  His "apparent concern for historical accuracy in *Safinat `al-awliya;[text-layer?] [al-awliya',]`*
  like his appeal to Muslim 'heroes' from the seventh and eighth centuries, is a mask for his
  overriding goal: not only to affirm 'Abd al-Qadir Jilani as the foremost Sufi exemplar and the
  Qadiriyya as the paramount Sufi brotherhood, but also to undergird his own authority vis-a-vis
  rival claims to Qadiri spirituality" (pp. 64–65, folios 50–51); "The significance of the Islamic
  past for Dara Shikuh is functional: It affirms his status as a Qadiri adept" (p. 65, folio 51),
  and even Rumi and 'Ala' ad-Dawla Simnani are "accorded only half a page" whose "sole purpose … is
  to provide a backdrop for the stage onto which he places as central exhibit the Qadiriyya"
  (p. 65, folio 51).
- **Dara's own method, quoted from Perwaiz Hayat** — "'he did not accept the age of Salman or the
  Prophet Muhammad cited in the traditional accounts. He narrates different sources, but accepts
  that account which for him seems to be nearer to historical fact. He was also interested in
  providing as complete an account of the awliya [saints] as possible: he tried his best to furnish
  birth dates, death dates and the places of the tombs of every wali [saint].'" (p. 64, folio 50,
  with note 13). **Directly useful as a statement of what a seventeenth-century source does and does
  not record about tombs.**
- **The single-saint hagiography as a third form, and the argument it makes through lineage.** "The
  lens of hagiography can also be turned in the opposite direction. Instead of attempting to capture
  much or all of history, certain hagiographers focused on a single saint, whose importance was then
  clarified by his linkage to one or more lineages" (p. 65, folio 51); the two rival accounts of
  Shah Mina are "one dealing with him as 'just another Chishti master' and the other presenting him
  as the foremost paragon of spirituality in his age" (p. 65, folio 51).
- **Suppression of a miracle read as an argument about literacy and charisma.** "Martin van
  Bruinessen suggests that this story initially represented the triumph of literate and scripturally
  based religion over raw antinomian charisma, although in later versions the conflict is
  transformed into a cooperation projecting the compatibility of diverse forms of spirituality. This
  interpretation would seem to be borne out by the nearly complete suppression of the miracle contest
  from the literary accounts" (p. 66, folio 52, with note 15).
