## Practices, institutions, economy

- **ʿUrs as a multi-day annual festival, with a named ritual and a money object.** At Pakpattan
  "cowrie shells (once used as the smallest units of currency) are thrown over the crowd" (p. 18,
  folio 3); the festival is the saint's "'marriage' with God" (p. 18, folio 3). The Chishti style of
  life itself includes that "They celebrate the death anniversaries of their masters" (p. 21, folio 6),
  and the appendix is built round "A Chishti Calendar of Saints' Death Anniversaries" (p. 8, folio not
  stated).
- **A named, dated, photographed ʿurs in Pakistan** — "the 2001 'urs festival of Capt. Wahid Bakhsh
  (Allahabad, Pakistan)" with qawwali singers (p. 10, folio not stated).
- **Feeding the poor as a defining Chishti practice — the chunk's nearest thing to langar.** "They aim
  at poverty and denial, and they keep company with the poor and beggars, giving them food… They
  themselves wash the hands of the stranger, and themselves provide fire and food to the poor. They
  never give the rich man a place higher than the poor man, and their feasts are very bountiful"
  (p. 21, folio 6). The word *langar* is **(not stated)**.
- **The begging bowl, permitted under duress and marked as a break with precedent** (p. 20, folio 5,
  with note 4).
- **Land grants and rural gentrification of a saintly family.** "Descendants of a leading Chishti
  master in South India, for instance, accepted land grants that in effect made them rural gentry"
  (p. 20, folio 5, with note 3). The master is not named: **(not stated)**.
- **Rented property as a saint's income of last resort** — Shah Kalim Allah "had to forgo dependence
  on donations and rent out property in order to recover his health" (p. 20, folio 5, with note 5).
- **The doctrine on kings, offerings and land, quoted as eight couplets.** "Be a darwish and sit in
  solitude; do not ask for food from anyone"; "Never seek kindness and generosity from kings; / When
  you take a village or land, you will fall down in front of a door"; "Do not follow the King's
  employment"; and the countervailing rule, "Do not go to Kings uncalled; but if they summon you, go
  instantly; / Obey their commands; know that this category is obligatory", obedience in a just royal
  demand being worth "more than sixty years of private worship" (p. 19, folio 4, with note 2).
- **Patronage framed as the structural problem.** "In premodern India, the symbol of power-social,
  economic, and political-was the king, or sultan. The sultan was not only a ruler but also a patron of
  art, artists, and religious specialists. For the Chishtis, unlike other Sufis, avoidance of the
  sultan meant avoiding the corruption of the soul by earthly power" (p. 19, folio 4).
- **Succession: spiritual, not hereditary, in the first cycle.** "None of the initial five Chishti
  masters of North India was succeeded by a blood relative; rather, they chose as their principal
  successor one who had worked with them on the path of abstinence and prayer, meditation and
  listening" (p. 21, folio 6). Against this, Trimingham's claim that "The hereditary succession of
  authority is both product and cause of a 'deeper spiritual `malaise;'` [`malaise,`]" (p. 25, folio
  not stated).
- **Tomb cults as the mechanism of access, stated as the book's own position.** The great ones "are
  linked through tombs, or tomb cults, places where the great ones were 'married' to God and where
  others can find access to God" (p. 17, folio 2); "Since the great ones live, their tombs and tomb
  cults amplify but also modify the reception of biographies, and we devote chapter 5 to certain
  Chishti tombs and the practices of pilgrimage" (p. 23, folio 8).
- **State sponsorship of tombs, reported as Trimingham's decline thesis and rejected.** "once orders
  became linked to saints' tombs and the latter became state-sponsored centers of devotion, pure
  mysticism surrendered to popular, mass religiosity" (p. 25, folio not stated).
- **Sama' and zikr as the order's institutional signature.** The Chishtis "followed a single ritual
  activity more zealously than did any other brotherhood in the subcontinent. That practice was sama',
  evoking the divine presence through song or listening to music. `Sarna'` [`Sama'`] cannot be
  separated from meditation through recollection (zikr) of the Divine Names" (p. 20, folio 5); it
  "manages to accommodate the practice of sama' to the rigors of a male community that also observes
  the full range of Muslim obligations" (p. 21, folio 6) — **the community is named as male**.
- **The khanqah as a built institution in a town.** "The style of life (of Chishti masters) is to
  build a house in a city or town and call the people away from vanity toward God" (p. 21, folio 6).
  No building date, patron or cost anywhere in the chunk: **(not stated)**.

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Qutb ad-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki dies of a three-day sama', reviving five times for prayer** — attributed
  to the tradition ("as the Sufis say"): "Though in the death throes of his passion for God, Qutb
  ad-Din 'revived' momentarily at five points during the final days of his life. Each time, after
  completing his obligatory prayers, he went back into the semicomatose state of ecstasy created by
  this Persian couplet sung in a session of mystical music (sama') until at last, as the Sufis say, he
  surrendered his soul to God" (p. 21, folio 6, with note 8). The book calls this "the most famous
  incident of musically inspired ecstasy in the history of Chishti Sufism" and the source of its title
  (pp. 20–21, folios 5–6).
- **Farid ad-Din's ecstasy at Nizam ad-Din's attainment, re-enacted annually with cowries** — the
  saint "went into ecstasy, on realizing that Nizam ad-Din Awliya' had just attained… the nearly
  unprecedented state of becoming the beloved of God Himself" (p. 18, folio 3).
- **The forty-day inverted meditation, told as one of the "Stories of his rigid ascetic practice"**
  about Shaykh Farid ad-Din of Pakpattan (p. 20, folio 5).
- **Sama' as a cosmological descent, in Nizam ad-Din's own teaching.** "It is he alone, al-Wajid, who
  induces ecstasy ( wajd), when the devotee listens to music performed under the correct conditions.
  Then music ceases to be mere notes or words. It becomes an unfolding of the cosmos from its highest
  sphere, 'the angelic sphere' of the spirits, to the lowest sphere, the human heart and the bodily
  limbs" (p. 20, folio 5); the quoted teaching sets out "celestial lights, mystical states, and
  physical effects" descending on "the spirit, the heart, or the bodily limbs" (p. 20, folio 5, with
  note 6).
- **The hadith of five generations, quoted by Nizam ad-Din, and his weeping.** "'After me, from my
  community ( umma), there will be five generations. Each generation will live for forty years…'";
  and "After these 200 years, if someone gave birth to a puppy, that was better than giving birth to a
  human child. (Tears filled his eyes, as he continued.)" (p. 22, folio 7, with note 12). Attributed by
  the authors to Nizam ad-Din's recorded discourse and immediately reinterpreted as goading, not
  prophecy (p. 22, folio 7).
- **The soul destroyed and resurrected, as the definition of the experience.** "It is the experience of
  remembering God so intensely that the soul is destroyed and resurrected" (p. 17, folio 2).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Subject, and the two stated differences from the existing literature.** "The subject of this book
  is the Chishti Sufi order" (p. 16, folio not stated); "First, we use many texts produced by the
  Chishtis and their supporters that have been previously ignored or slighted. Second, we deliberately
  abandon the numbingly circular arguments, so prevalent in the literature, about a privileged 'golden
  age' of Sufism in general and the Chishti order in particular" (p. 16, folio not stated).
- **The thesis, stated in one sentence, with a five-cycle periodization.** "we advance a new historical
  periodization of five 'cycles' in the development of the Chishtis. Our thesis is that a Sufi order
  such as the Chishtiyya is more than a parasitical legitimation of power or a nostalgic reverence for
  bygone saints; it is instead a complex of spiritual practice, historical memory, and ethical models,
  which continues to evolve from its medieval Islamic origins in response to the political,
  ideological, and technological transformations of the contemporary world" (p. 16, folio not stated).
  One cycle is used in passing: Shah Kalim Allah belongs to "the third cycle of the Indian Chishtiyya"
  (p. 20, folio 5).
- **The book's problematic is its two bibliographies, and the asymmetry between them.** European-language
  scholarship is "focused largely on the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, a period
  that-paradoxically-is almost entirely lacking in contemporary documentation and is known only from
  later texts", while the Persian works "expand and multiply in number and geographical extent right
  up to the end of the nineteenth century", "and Urdu continues to be an important medium for Chishti
  Sufism today" (p. 16, folio not stated); the result is "this complete asymmetry between Western
  scholarship and the Chishti literary tradition… symptomatic of a historiographical disconnect, which
  this book will attempt to repair" (p. 17, folio 2). South Asian scholarship is placed on the Western
  side: it "is mostly written according to Western Orientalist methods" (p. 16, folio not stated).
- **What the book is not.** "It is a methodological inquiry into Sufism that focuses on one particular
  regional Islamic mystical movement… It is not a documentary history in the usual sense. It is a
  stocktaking that also includes a projection of agendas for research. It is not a treatment of Sufism
  as a subset of either Islamic orthodoxy or heresy" (p. 17, folio 2).
- **Definition: experience plus memory, and the refusal of the origin question.** "It is both an
  experience and a memory"; "For the devotee, however, the origin of Chishti Sufism is less important
  than its experience"; and "the experience is not limited to one period or one group of heroes"
  (p. 17, folio 2).
- **Audiences beyond Islam, listed in ascending distance** — Muslim pilgrims at Pakpattan; Sikhs, who
  took Farid's Panjabi verses into the Guru Granth Sahib; Hindus at Ajmer; Inayat Khan's Western
  followers; and finally "popular music derived from Chishti practice that has reached large audiences
  who are often unaware of the great ones or their Islamic, South Asian, and Chishti background"
  (pp. 17–19, folios 2–4). Even anti-Sufi reformists "have connections with the Chishtiyya" (p. 19,
  folio 4).
- **Two distinguishing dimensions, named as the book's organising pair.** "The first concerns the
  ethical relations of the great ones to institutional power, and the second is the distinctive
  modality of their spiritual practices, expanding remembrance (zikr) to include versified song
  (sama')" (p. 19, folio 4).
- **First corrective: decline is a rhetoric, not a diagnosis.** "we avoid taking the rhetoric of
  decline as the criterion of Sufi viability. We distinguish between a shortfall in pursuit of ideals,
  which happens in every generation, and a decline in Sufism as a spiritual option from one generation
  to the next. Because Sufi masters were prone to be their own most severe critics, others reading
  their self-corrections have taken them to be historical diagnoses auguring decay and decline"
  (p. 22, folio 7); the conclusion: "The 'golden age' historiography of Sufism is more related to
  internal imperatives of modernity, including those of Orientalist scholarship, than to the
  self-interpretation of Sufi theorists" (p. 22, folio 7).
- **Second corrective: present-mindedness.** Defined as "the one-sided assumption that the European
  Enlightenment is the final judgment on civilization, whether couched as premodern, modern, or
  postmodern" (p. 22, folio 7); it "assumes the superiority of reason and the attainment of
  objectivity… It denies the validity of Sufism and also the possibility of a Sufi response to Western
  norms and values" (p. 23, folio 8).
- **The Sufi counter-claim, given in a living Chishti's words and endorsed as a refutation of the
  "influence" argument.** "Sufism and science are striving for the same destination… Sufism has found
  the answers and invites the scientists to come and have that `knowledge:>13` [`knowledge." 13`]";
  "Far from being dependent on or derivative of Neoplatonism, Christianity, Buddhism, or Hindu yoga,
  Sufism is an original, comprehensive, and hence authentic form of knowledge. Here we find an
  aggressive assertion of indigenous knowledge" (p. 23, folio 8, with note 13).
- **The order defined as regional, then as transnational and transcreedal.** "The Chishtiyya is not a
  pan-Islamic order that happens to have an Indo-Muslim branch. The Chishti order stands out, and
  stands apart, as the major brotherhood to be identified solely with the subcontinent and its
  multiple regional communities"; yet "the Chishtiyya is neither exclusively Indian nor Islamic. The
  Chishtiyya is at once transnational and transcreedal. And it is this tension, bordering on a
  contradiction, that we will explore in the pages that follow" (p. 23, folio 8).
- **The chapter plan, in the authors' own words** — chapter 1 interrogates "the notion of a Sufi order
  or brotherhood"; chapter 2 takes "the distinctive Chishti approach to remembrance through meditation
  and remembrance through listening to music"; chapter 3 gives "an extensive analysis of spiritual
  genealogy"; chapter 4 "a critical profile of the biographical process itself"; chapter 5 "certain
  Chishti tombs and the practices of pilgrimage"; chapter 6 "the bifurcation of the Chishtiyya itself
  into two prominent branches, the Nizamiyya and the Sabiriyya, a bifurcation intensified during the
  period of British colonial rule"; chapter 7 technology and Inayat Khan (pp. 23–24, folios 8–9). The
  appendix translates "extensive excerpts from one tazkira" and "illustrate[s] the historical emergence
  of both minor and major masters" (p. 24, folio 9).
- **Sources named as a class: the tazkira.** "we draw on several of the principal Sufi biographical
  memoirs (tazkiras) and manuals of spiritual practice employed by the Chishtis" (p. 23, folio 8);
  and, opening chapter 1, "Many sources remain unstudied or undervalued, none more so than the
  biographical compendia known as tazkiras" (p. 25, folio not stated).
- **Chapter 1's opening admission.** "We don't understand them, or at least we haven't figured out how
  to understand them as historical developments. Despite the abundance of texts about Sufi orders,
  their place in the emergence of Islamic civilization remains unclear" (p. 25, folio not stated).
- **The critique of Trimingham, named and dated in three stages.** *The Sufi Orders in Islam*
  "enunciates a threefold theory of the development of Sufism that has more than a passing resemblance
  to the tripartite schemes that litter the landscape of Western historiography
  (ancient-medieval-modern)": a first period "from the ninth century on", tariqa "ways" "beginning
  around the twelfth century", and ta'ifas or organizations "around the fifteenth century" marking
  "the full institutionalization of Sufism" (p. 25, folio not stated, with note 1). The verdict: "The
  valuable information collected in his compendium is marred by a theory of classicism and decline"
  (p. 25, folio not stated); "Trimingham's observations contain a modern and strongly Protestant
  attitude" and "The notion of historical decline becomes a rhetorical strategy for projecting personal
  values" (pp. 25–26, folio not stated / 12).
- **Decline as an unlikely consensus across hostile camps.** "Until recently most Orientalists, secular
  modernists, and fundamentalists have all found their own reasons for asserting the decline of Islam.
  How else to explain that much of the Muslim world has been colonized?" (p. 26, folio 12); the
  position is given at length in Isma'il R. al Faruqi's words, where `tasawwufbecame` [`tasawwuf
  became`] "a disease" that "ruined the health of Muslim society during half a millennium from the
  fall of Baghdad to the Tatars in 655/1257 to the rise of the Wahhabiyyah, the first anti-Sufi reform
  movement, in 1159/1757" (p. 26, folio 12, with note 3).
- **Decline as colonial self-justification.** "During the colonial period the notion of the decline of
  Muslim nations was especially attractive to the self-image of Europeans: It provided a noble
  justification for conquest and empire; it supported the 'civilizing mission' of the West (also known
  as 'the white man's burden')" (p. 26, folio 12, with note 4).
- **Hodgson offered as the alternative.** "another historian, Marshall Hodgson, has questioned the
  whole notion of decline… The rise of the West, in his view, was not an unassisted triumph of one
  group over another… but rather a convergence of disparate historical circumstances"; his "Great
  Western Transmutation" "takes as axiomatic that other civilizations in other periods and parts of the
  globe could also experience `greatness:'` [`greatness."`]" (p. 26, folio 12). The chunk breaks off
  mid-sentence at the foot of p. 26: "the so-called decline of Islam is not due to internal moral
  failure or to a flawed systemic view of the universe but" (p. 26, folio 12).
- **The blurb states the same thesis from outside** — Ernst and Lawrence "challenge the 'myth of
  decline' as it has been adopted by historians and chroniclers both within and outside of the Chishti
  tradition, and allow us to see that the notion of a lost golden age is a trope within the tradition
  itself, always moving to a new generation that sees itself as the generation of decline" (p. 3, folio
  not stated).
- **Method of composition, stated as a claim about the text's authority.** "at this point there is not
  a single page that does not bear the marks of both authors" (p. 14, folio not stated); Marcia
  Hermansen's "unpublished research materials on colonial Chishti masters" are summarized in chapter 6
  by permission (p. 14, folio not stated).
