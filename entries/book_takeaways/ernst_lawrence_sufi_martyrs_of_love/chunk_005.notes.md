## Practices, institutions, economy

- **A death-anniversary feast with food distribution, described in passing as routine** — the saint
  "was busy dividing the food from the celebration of the anniversary of the death of the axis of
  the masters, Nasir ad-Din Chiragh-i Dihli. From the bread soaked in butter and sugar that happened
  to be at the assembly, he sent me one round piece" (p. 70, folio 56). The distributed food is then
  the vehicle of a cure; the chunk gives no numbers, no cost and no donor: **(not stated)**.
- **Death dates preserved without death years, expressly so that a saint can be commemorated** —
  "Shadowy figures from the generations after the most prominent founders are not supplied with a
  death year at all, but are known only by a death date so that they may be ritu- ally celebrated"
  (p. 78, folio 64). The chunk's own lineage list shows the practice: "7. Huzayfa al-Mar'ashi (18
  Shawwal: no year), 8. Hubayrah al-Basri (18 Shawwal: no year), 9. Mumshad 'Alu Dinawari (14
  Muharram: no year), 10. Abu Ishaq Shami Chishti (14 Rabi' II: no year)" (p. 77, folio 63).
  **Directly useful to the archive's ʿurs-dating problem.**
- **Tomb-visiting as a formal course of spiritual discipline, with an itinerary** — Delhi, then
  Ajmer, then Pakpattan, then Lucknow, then back to the living master at Taunsa (p. 71, folio 57).
  A documented nineteenth-century linkage of Pakpattan and Taunsa in one circuit.
- **A shrine named as a pilgrimage destination crossing the Sufi/lay divide** — "His shrine is in the
  region of Lucknow, the pilgrimage des- tination of both Sufis and common folk" (p. 70, folio 56).
- **Shrine incumbency as an office held by an author** — 'Abd ar-Rahman "is also the incumbent of a
  shrine in Awadh" (p. 73, folio 59). The book does not name the shrine or the office:
  **(not stated)**.
- **The cloak of succession (khirqa) as the instrument of authorisation, given twice over** — Shaykh
  Sarang "gave him the cloak of succes- sorship to wear, and he also gave him permission to work
  (i.e., take disciples of his own)" (p. 69, folio 55); Shah Mirra in turn "honored two people with
  the gift of the cloak of succession. One of them was the revered master Shaykh Sa'd. The other was
  Shaykh Qutb ad-Din, the son of his brother" (p. 70, folio 56) — i.e. succession split between a
  disciple and a nephew.
- **A relic passing by descent, and explicitly still held** — Shah Madar's instruction, "give a
  woolen prayer carpet to Shaykh Mina as a gift from me"; "The prayer carpet remains in the
  possession of the descendants of the revered master Shaykh Ilah-diya" (p. 68, folio 54). The
  nephew-successor "also was the owner of the carpet" (p. 70, folio 56).
- **Petitioning, prayers and written amulets as the saint's transaction with the public** — the Qazi
  "wrote out all their petitions" for the needy of Lucknow (p. 68, folio 54); the saint "helped all
  [the needy], saying prayers and writing amulets for them" (p. 68, folio 54). One petition is
  refused outright (p. 68, folio 54).
- **Initiation as a necessity extending past death, transacted through cap and written lineage** —
  the dead man "needed a cap and a [spiritual]lineage[text-layer?] [[spiritual] lineage]"; the saint
  "gave them the cap off of his blessed head" and "also wrote out a lineage, that they placed on the
  man's chest" (p. 70, folio 56).
- **Named ascetic disciplines** — the "inverted prayer (namaz-i ma'kus)" (p. 68, folio 54); the
  "40-day retreat", deliberately restarted so that "his carnal soul did not become proud with
  finishing it" (p. 68, folio 54); wooden sandals and journeys of "eleven or twelve leagues in order
  to visit his master" (p. 69, folio 55); sleeping on gravel, on thorns, on a high wall, and
  wetting shirt and cap in winter (pp. 67–68, folios 53–54); constant ablution, and the doctrine
  "that food which is eaten with ablutions becomes active in the stomach praising God" (p. 69,
  folio 55).
- **The four traits by which the book characterises both groups of five Chishti masters** —
  "solitude (zuhd), discipline (riyazat), music (sama'), and a taste for God (zawq)" (p. 78,
  folio 64). The book calls them "the same traits" while listing four.
- **Marriage against poverty as the structural strain in Indian Sufi practice** — "it is incumbent on
  Muslim males to marry, to produce children, and to care for a family. That obligation is hard to
  reconcile with the mandate to poverty, or at least restricted income, that characterized early
  Sufism" (p. 79, folio 65).
- **Pilgrimage as a problem of capital, stated in economic terms** — "the overland journey from
  Hindus- tan to the Arabian peninsula" cannot "be undertaken unless there is a freedom of time and
  sur- plus of capital that only the well-to-do elite of Indo-Muslim society enjoyed" (p. 79,
  folio 65). **The chunk's one explicit economic claim.**
- **A taxonomy of orders used as an institutional map** — "the 14 ancient fam- ilies, of whom 4
  families are primordial: the Zaydiyya, the 'Iyaziyya, the Adhamiyya, the Hubayriyya" plus the
  Chishtiyya as "the 9th"; the remaining nine "the 'Ajamiyya, the Tayfuriyya, the Karkhiyya, the
  Saqatiyya, the Junaydiyya, the Kazaruniyya, the Tusiyya, the Suhrawardiyya, and the Firdawsiyya
  (Kubrawiyya)" (p. 77, folio 63; p. 78, folio 64); and thirteen "later" orders, "the Qadiriyya, the
  Yasawiyya, the Naqshbandiyya, the Nuriyya, the Khadrawiyya, the Shattariyya, the Hasaniyya
  Bukhariyya, the Zahiriyya, the Ansariyya, the Safawiyya, the 'Aydarusiyya, the Qalandariyya, and
  the Madariyya" (p. 78, folio 64).
- **Manuscript economy of the sources** — Mir'at al-asrar "has never generated a fraction of the
  interest di- rected to Safinat al-awliya'; the Persian text has never been published, though a
  good Urdu translation is available" (p. 72, folio 58).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

All are given as Irtida 'Ali Khan's report in **Fawa'id-i Sa'diyya**, mostly on Shaykh Sa'd's
authority, unless noted; the book frames them as hagiography, not as fact (p. 71, folio 57).

- Zikr and Qur'an recitation were heard from his mother's belly during her pregnancy (p. 67,
  folio 53).
- As an infant he would not drink milk by day in the month of fasting, nor from his mother at all
  "unless she had performed ablutions"; his mother found him at night "under his leopard skin in
  prostration" (p. 67, folio 53).
- Shaykh Qiwam ad-Din prophesied the birth: "In the house of my brother Shaykh Qutb ad- Din a baby
  will be born, who will be the candle of our household" (p. 67, folio 53).
- At two or three he summoned sparrows, which "came and alighted facing the revered master, and they
  were unable to fly away until he gave them permission to leave" (p. 67, folio 53).
- At five, asked to say "B", he answered "Why should I be squint-eyed?", and "the teacher and those
  others present lost their senses" (p. 67, folio 53).
- He was recognised qutb at twelve by Shah Badi' ad-Din Madar through Qazi Shihab ad-Din, "even
  though that revered one is still young, only twelve or thirteen years old" (p. 68, folio 54).
- He refused a cure for a petitioner's son: "no matter how much I requested it from the divine court,
  it did no good. His life has been thus decreed", and recited a dohra [Hindi verse] (p. 68,
  folio 54).
- A barber "became drunk on wine, and showered the Shaykh with abuse"; the Shaykh "responded to him
  with complete courtesy, offering his apologies" and recited a quatrain of blessing on his enemies
  (p. 69, folio 55).
- **The restless head.** A traveller died but "his head kept on moving, never quieting down" until
  the saint's cap and a written lineage were placed on him, "As soon as the cap arrived on the head
  of the dead man it stopped moving and became still" (p. 70, folio 56).
- Shaykh Sa'd, thrown from his horse into the flooded Mawhan river, thought of the saint and "saw him
  present by my side. He threw me up to the surface of the water" (p. 70, folio 56).
- Shaykh Sa'd's "burning fever" was cured by eating one round piece of the bread distributed at
  Chiragh-i Dihli's death anniversary (p. 70, folio 56). The narrator closes: "there are other
  miracles and powers of his, such that they exceed the range of what can be written" (p. 70,
  folio 56).
- **A visionary meeting after death**, credited to Muhammad Ghaws (d. 969/1562) and reported by 'Abd
  ar-Rahman: a dream encounter with Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar "in one of the highest celestial
  realms" (p. 75, folio 61).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The chapter's method is source comparison, and the authors state the pay-off of it** — Irtida
  'Ali Khan's account differs "in tone and content from the Akhbar al-akhyar narrative, even in
  matters such as the meaning of the saint's name", the common elements being only the adoption
  story, the schoolroom miracle and "the brief reference to the wall as the site of his ascetic
  practice" (pp. 70–71, folios 56–57).
- **Hagiographic motifs are traced to Qur'anic models.** The death of Qiwam ad-Din's original son
  "directly parallels the mur- der of the mischievous son of believers by Khizr in the Qur'an
  (18:74, 80-81)"; the bird miracles are "reminiscent of those ascribed to Jesus in the Qur'an"
  (p. 71, folio 57).
- **The thesis of the Shah Mirra section: confirmation is the theme.** "It is doubtless the same
  logic of necessity that dictated the nu- merous confirmations of Shah Mirra's authority, and it is
  these confirmations that constitute the main theme of this hagiographical account" (p. 71,
  folio 57); the restless-head story "seems to indicate that spiritual initiation is a necessity
  beyond the grave, in death as well as in life" (p. 71, folio 57).
- **A single saint as an institutional junction** — Shah Mirra "becomes the locus of a distinctive
  institutional line of spirituality, combining Chishti, Suhrawardi, and Madari spiritual
  filiations", and "Neither Sa'd ad-Din nor Qiwam ad-Din would be remembered, however, were it not
  for Shah Mirra" (p. 71, folio 57). He "himself becomes a new anchor for subsequent generations of
  Chishti adepts" (p. 71, folio 57).
- **Regional attachment explains textual production** — "It is the latter saint's close connection to
  Lucknow that led a subsequent Lakhnawi notable … to produce the Fawa'id-i Sa'diyya" (p. 71,
  folio 57).
- **Inclusive versus partisan hagiography as the chapter's organising contrast** — "While ha-
  giographies like 'Abd al-Haqq's and Dara Shikuh's attempt to be inclusive of multiple traditions,
  others prioritize one silsila, in this case the Chishtiyya" (p. 72, folio 58).
- **The "14 families" as the dominant taxonomy, and its partial fictiveness** — of the five traced to
  Ibn Zayd "only the Chishtis are historical"; of the Habibi nine "only the last four represent
  concrete historical lineages" (p. 72, folio 58); "In later Indo-Persian hagiographies, the
  paradigm of 'the 14 families' was the dominant taxonomy for understanding the ramification of Sufi
  or- ders" (p. 72, folio 58).
- **Generational reckoning is shown to be chronologically incoherent, and the incoherence is argued
  to be beside the point.** "The arbitrariness of these two clusters is evident"; the disparities are
  "historically erroneous" yet "do not detract from 'Abd ar-Rahman's primary pur- pose: to retell the
  saga of Persian/Indo-Persian Sufism as a single drama shaped by the Unseen" (p. 73, folio 59). The
  Sabiri line "is beset with chronological difficulties that cloud its initial years", including a
  successor who would have been "named successor, by the age of fourteen" (p. 74, folio 60). The
  chapter's conclusion generalises it: such gaps "would perplex any conven- tional historian. But
  these criticisms do not undermine the importance of genealogy for the Chishtis, because what
  matters to the engaged inquirer is the ritual invocation of heroes" (p. 78, folio 64).
- **The Chishtiyya as the order defined by exclusive South Asian attachment** — the first Indian
  cycle is "the period when the Chishti order was introduced into India and began to establish
  itself as the sole brotherhood linked exclusively with South Asia. From this point onward, the
  Chishtis become the linch- pin of Sufism, and all other Sufis are viewed from their perspective"
  (p. 73, folio 59).
- **The qutb of each generation as a device that ranks without isolating** — "While each generation
  is marked by a qutb, he is situated among, not apart from, other Sufi masters: Though he stands at
  their head, they add to his preemi- nence" (p. 75, folio 61); the "double purpose" is to show "how
  vital the connection to a Persian Sufi tradition was for all Sabiri Chishtis while at the same time
  conferring the highest spiritual rank on a handful of obscure saints, most of whom lived and toiled
  and died in northern India" (p. 75, folio 61).
- **Localisation as the central historical process, in a formulation the authors quote and adopt** —
  "There was no single story of ancestry but many stories with a varying cast of ancestors"; the
  legacy "was contin- ually reshaped as both an extension and a replacement of Persian antecedents,
  and not only in each period, but also in each region and in each order"; 'Abd ar-Rahman's
  "paramount need was 'to create a local sacred geography for Indian Islam'" (p. 76, folio 62).
  **This is the chapter's governing claim and the one most directly transferable to a Pakistani
  shrine archive.**
- **Persian as a deliberate bridge language** — 'Abd ar-Rahman "like Akbar and also like Dara Shikuh,
  wanted to make Persian the bridge language between a legally based Islamic world beyond India and
  an Indian domain privileging local resources of myth, mir- acle, and magic" (p. 75, folio 61); his
  worldview "is more a kaleidoscope than a coherent system of thought. Praxis reigns over theory,
  anecdotes and poetry over metaphysical treatises" (p. 75, folio 61).
- **A documented internal controversy over the Chishti chain's origin** — Shah Wali Allah found the
  Hasan al-Basri link "historically implausible" because "Hasan al-Basri's dates were 642-728, while
  'Ali ruled as caliph from 656 to 661" (p. 76, folio 62); 'Abd al-Haqq and Fakhr ad-Din both
  defended it via Suyuti (p. 76, folio 62). The authors decline to adjudicate: "The outcome of this
  debate is not so decisive for our purposes as its existence" (p. 76, folio 62).
- **Source stratigraphy stated explicitly, across three periods** — "Lata'if-i Ashrafi is a Nizami
  Chishti source from the Sul- tanate period, followed two centuries later by Mir' at al-asrar, a
  Sabiri Chishti source dating from Mughal India. Both are then confirmed in the late colonial
  period by Anwar al-'arifin" — the pattern "remarkably consistent, only the spiritual claims are
  inflated over the centuries" (p. 77, folio 63).
- **Hagiography as a genre that sets expectations rather than records** — it "is also important for
  setting ex- pectations of what is and is not the prerequisite character sketch for Indo-Muslim
  sainthood", and "none of them appears de novo, either factually or fictionally" (p. 77, folio 63).
- **Chapter 4's opening thesis** — "The first Chishti shaykhs to establish their order in Hindustan
  (northern India) made a decisive shift from their Central Asian bearings, despite the continued
  appeal of that tradition for future generations" (p. 79, folio 65).
- **The masters distinguished from the ʿulama by inner meaning** — masters "were expected to know the
  essential or inner meaning of whatever question was raised or issue answered, while the scholars,
  that is, the 'ulama: could provide an answer that satisfied only the formal requirements of the
  situation" (p. 79, folio 65).
- **A contrastive claim about Hindus that a user of this archive should weigh.** The marriage/poverty
  and hajj paradoxes "are uniquely Muslim paradoxes with which Indian Sufis had to wrestle and that
  were un- known to their Hindu counterparts, because they did not exist for Hindus" (p. 79,
  folio 65). Asserted without evidence in this chunk; the archive's Hindu and Nanakpanthi rows make
  it a claim worth flagging rather than citing.
