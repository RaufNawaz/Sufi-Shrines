## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Sufi initiation is not exclusive, and the authors say so against a common assumption** — "It is
  often assumed in addition that membership in a Sufi order was exclusive. That is not in fact the
  case. Unlike the Christian monastic orders, which were divided by firm lines of authority and
  sacrament, Sufi orders frequently could overlap one another" (p. 42, folio 28).
- **Initiation rite, described stage by stage** — chapter 2 of *The Order of Hearts* "describes a
  three-day fast with numerous prayers in preparation for this event. After promising to uphold the
  `shari'a` and to love God, the disciple shakes hands with the master and receives instruction in
  the zikr formulas that are appropriate for his or her spiritual state. The disciple repeats each
  formula three times in order to memorize it" (p. 43, folio 29).
- **Restricted circulation of the manual** — "Awrangabadi urges the reader to use the treatise only
  with permission from a qualified guide, and asks that it be concealed from the eyes of the
  unworthy" (p. 43, folio 29).
- **The Chishti zikr timetable and its counts, quoted from Awrangabadi** — "Always they sit from the
  end of the night until morning, and after dawn prayer they always recite the vocal Chishti zikr
  7,000 times. Likewise from noon to mid-afternoon prayer, from mid-afternoon prayer to sunset
  prayer, and from sunset prayer to evening prayer, the lovers are bound to the circle of zikr. After
  the mastery of longings, most pass the entire night in zikr, most reaching 30,000 zikr; 20,000 zikr
  or 18,000 zikr is their usual custom" (p. 45, folio 31).
- **The chunk's one explicitly economic claim** — "The time demands of this practice also clearly
  prevented serious Chishtis from having regular jobs" (p. 45, folio 31).
- **Court and military patronage shaping public practice** — because Central Asians "notably in the
  court and the military … are almost always attached to the Naqshbandi order", Kalim Allah judges
  "it will be necessary in some public contexts to downplay distinctive Chishti practices and
  highlight instead those that are common" (p. 42, folio 28).
- **A graded curriculum of divine names in five levels** — three fundamental names (Hearing, Seeing,
  Knowing); then five more (Lasting, Standing, Present, Looking, Witnessing) "making for a total of
  eight"; then "twelve more names: Holy, Loving, Living, Subsistent, Outer, Inner, Forgiving, Mild,
  Light, Guide, Renewer, Eternal"; then "others of the 99 names of God or isolated phrases from the
  discourses of the master"; then "additional names beyond the 99 include superlative forms"
  (p. 45, folio 31).
- **Other orders' techniques recorded inside a Chishti manual** — a Qadiri sequence of names,
  variations "of from 1 to 12 beats", "an extensive description of a Qalandari zikr", "The Shattari
  method of reciting 'Allah'", and, in "an appendix to an account of 40 forms of meditation
  (`muraqaba`) in the lengthy chapter 18 … a four-page description `ofNaqshbandi[text-layer?] [of
  Naqshbandi]` techniques of instruction" (p. 46, folio 32).
- **Samaʿ is congregational, not private, and the authors define it as such** — "Verses were chanted
  in a corporate setting, in the presence of a group of like-minded men"; it "ought to be defined as
  'hearing chanted verse (with or without accompanying instruments) in the company of others also
  seeking to participate in the dynamic dialogue between a human lover and the Divine Beloved'"
  (p. 49, folio 35).
- **Access is graded: samaʿ is withheld from beginners** — "proponents of the second approach argued
  that sama' should not be made available to beginners, for they might be led to experience sensual
  delight instead of spiritual catharsis" (p. 49, folio 35).
- **Samaʿ as an institutional boundary marker** — "It separated the Chishti saints from the
  Suhrawardiyya, their major mystical rivals in the Sultanate era `oflndian[text-layer?] [of Indian]`
  Islam, and also opposed them to the `'ularna:[text-layer?] ['ulamā',]` those too comfortable
  spokesmen for official, i.e., government sanctioned, Islam" (p. 50, folio 36).
- **Elite control of the practice, and the resulting silence in the record** — "the popular,
  nonelite, mass sentiment in favor of `sarna'[text-layer?] [samāʿ]` fell outside the scope of their
  inquiry: Popularization suggested vulgarization, and for the Chishti theorists, as for most of the
  Indo-Muslim elite, vulgarization of any mystical institution … was firmly resisted. Hence, we find
  but a few, random references to the popular dissemination of `sarna:[text-layer?] [samāʿ.]`"
  (p. 50, folio 36).
- **The three genres of the samaʿ literature, useful for source criticism** — "independent essays on
  `sarna'`; chapters on `sarna'` that appear in biographical accounts of saints (`tazkiras`) or books
  devoted to theological inquiry; and anecdotal references to `sarna'` in the
  `rnalfuzat[text-layer?] [malfuzat]`, or recorded conversations, of major saints" (p. 50, folio 36).
- **Rules of the samaʿ assembly** — no female vocal accompaniment; a beautiful male voice; the reed
  pipe forbidden and drum and tambourine permitted; inspired verse "not only permitted but also
  encouraged for Sufis of all stages"; and "`sarna'` is to be conducted in a suitable place"
  (p. 51, folio 37).
- **A three-rank typology of listeners tied to three grades of ecstasy** — "the uninitiated listener
  (`rnutasarnrni'[text-layer?] [mutasammiʿ]`), the mature listener (`rnustarni'[text-layer?]
  [mustamiʿ]`) and the perfected listener (`sarni'[text-layer?] [sāmiʿ]`)", hearing respectively
  "through the moment (`waqt`)", "through the state (`hal`)" and "through the direct agency of God
  (`al-Haqq`)", their characteristics being "empathetic ecstasy", "momentary ecstasy" and "durative
  ecstasy" (p. 51, folio 37).
- **Vernacular instruction is licensed** — "It is right if one instructs the non-Arab disciple with
  expressions in Hindi or Persian or whatever he understands" (p. 47, folio 33).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- The joining of the breaths brings one to "the meeting-place of the two oceans" (`majma'
  al-bahrayn`; Qur'an 18:60) and "the station of the 'water of life'", where "one becomes a spiritual
  being and enters the world of flight and journeying", attains "long life", meets "the immortal
  prophet Khizr" and "becomes the emblem of sainthood" — given as the effects Awrangabadi's text
  describes (p. 44, folio 30).
- The visualization of 'Abd al-Qadir Jilani, taught by Nizam ad-Din and reported by Nasir ad-Din
  Chiragh-i Dihli, is said to be still in use: "which up to today they do in Chisht and in the
  Qadiriyya order" (p. 45, folio 31).
- Gisu Daraz reports from Nasir ad-Din "a method of causing spirits to be unveiled by zikr" (p. 45,
  folio 31).
- Prolonged breath-held recitation produces bodily signs: "until heat appears internally, and a
  spiritual state is born" (p. 45, folio 31).
- The "unstruck sound" is heard "when one recites the 99 names of God while holding the fingers in
  the ears" (p. 46, folio 32).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Overlapping initiations refute a political reading of Sufi orders** — "it is difficult to regard
  the constitution of Sufi orders and sainthood as a zero-sum competition, which a purely political
  analysis would suggest. It is in fact this wide collection of techniques that makes Sufism a
  cumulative tradition rather than a series of isolated and private experiences" (p. 42, folio 28).
- **The "decline" narrative is qualified rather than endorsed** — "it may be something of an
  exaggeration to regard the Chishti order as moribund or in decline between the fifteenth and
  eighteenth centuries; it may be more useful instead to consider Kalim Allah and Awrangabadi as two
  prominent examples among many who strove to give life to the tradition" (p. 42, folio 28).
- **Against reading master–disciple correspondence politically** — "It would be easy to read the
  attitude of Kalim Allah and Awrangabadi as a purely political struggle between Sufi orders, but
  that would be a drastic oversimplification of the issues of religious practice and the
  master-disciple relationship" (p. 43, folio 29).
- **The four-part reading frame for *The Order of Hearts*** — it contains general meditations; "a
  series of particular exercises associated with masters of the Chishti lineage"; "practices
  associated with other orders, which nevertheless form part of the Chishti repertoire"; and
  "explicit references to the Indic background and to yogic practices" (p. 43, folio 29).
- **The manual is laconic and written for insiders, which the authors treat as evidence of
  specialization** — "some of the details remain obscure in a text that is laconic and obviously
  aimed at insiders. Part of the density of the text is simply the specialization that has gone on in
  the Chishti order" (p. 45, folio 31).
- **Yoga is absorbed, not merely borrowed** — "Awrangabadi, like many Sufi commentators, fits yogic
  techniques into an Islamic framework that supplies the intentions and ultimate meaning that yogis
  may have been unaware of" (p. 46, folio 32).
- **The Chishti–Naqshbandi contrast is stated as the chapter's comparative test** — "Again and again,
  the Chishti author emphasizes vocal zikr as the standard form of mystical exercise, while the
  Naqshbandis with equal fervor insist on performing the silent zikr. Moreover, Wali Allah shows not
  the slightest interest in languages other than Arabic, and he even regards the word 'Allah' as
  beyond any human tongue" (p. 47, folio 33).
- **A textual-dependence hypothesis, left open** — the overlapping discussions "occur in nearly
  identical forms, which suggests either that Wali Allah used Awrangabadi's text, or that they both
  relied on a common source" (p. 47, folio 33).
- **The authors' reading of Awrangabadi's motive, which is the section's conclusion** — he noted
  other orders' practices "not with a view to completing a tradition that is deficient, nor as a
  borrowing from competitors, but rather to give a comprehensive and practically useful overview of a
  common heritage"; "Continuities in meditative practice help inform the structure of a Sufi order,
  but they do not constitute fixed boundaries" (pp. 47–48, folios 33–34).
- **Samaʿ restated as a problem of separation and union** — "Its fundamental precondition was
  separation. For without separation there could be no love, and yet separation was also to be
  overcome", elaborated into "`wahdat al-wujud` (the unity of existence; all is the One; the One is
  all) and `wahdat al-shuhud` (the unity of witness; all is from the One but is not the One
  `)_41[text-layer?] [). note 41]`" (p. 48, folio 34).
- **Three possible valuations of samaʿ, of which the debate concerns only the last two** — exclusion;
  acceptance "as a penultimate stage on the mystical ladder"; or "the top rung on the ladder, itself
  the ultimate mystical experience when properly pursued" (p. 48, folio 34).
- **The book's explicit counter-thesis to Mole** — "Contrary to Mole, we argue that in the Indian
  environment, from the period of the Delhi Sultanate through the Mughal era into the
  postindependence era, `sarna'` retained a unique significance as the integrating *modus operandi*
  of the Chishti order" (p. 50, folio 36).
- **A large claim about the Chishtis' place in South Asian Islam** — "The Chishtis, as the largest
  and most important mystical order among South Asian Muslims in the thirteenth and fourteenth
  centuries, forged the first authentic specimen of South Asian or Indian Islam"; samaʿ "became, if
  not the monopoly of the Chishtiyya, the preeminent symbol crystallizing their position" (p. 50,
  folio 36).
- **Hujwiri is explicitly excluded from the Indian line despite Lahore** (p. 52, folio 38) — see his
  bullets above; this is the chunk's sharpest statement bearing on `data-darbar`.
- **Zarradi's systematization is what makes the Chishti contribution "distinctly Indian"** (p. 52,
  folio 38).
- **Why the classical theorists endured** — "The major Sufi theorists have been honored and their
  works remembered long after their own time precisely because they were able to summarize numerous
  perspectives rather than advocate only one view (their own)" (p. 52, folio 38).
- **The homoerotic possibility inside the samaʿ dyad, given as the reason for restricting access** —
  "the dyad could be (and sometimes was) construed as the love of two human beings for one another,
  whether that of a man for a woman or a man for a boy or occasionally a man for another man"
  (p. 49, folio 35).
- **The Suhrawardis' reserve presented as a puzzle** — "The Suhrawardis, despite their inclusive,
  synthesizing minds, felt constrained—perhaps by their own experience, perhaps by the dictates of
  community consensus—to suggest that `sama'` was a limited and potentially dangerous experience"
  (p. 53, folio 39).

## Doubts

- **The `data-darbar` mapping is secure on identity but gives the archive no shrine material.** The
  book's "Hujwiri (d. ca. 1071)" who "died and was buried in Lahore" (p. 52, folio 38) is the row's
  principal figure beyond reasonable doubt — no second archive row has a Hujwiri — but **the archive
  row records the figure's death as 1072 while the book prints "d. ca. 1071"**. Keep the book's
  wording; a human must decide which stands in an entry, and must not silently harmonise them.
  Nothing in pp. 42–53 names Data Darbar, Ganj Bakhsh, the *Kashf al-Mahjub*, an ʿurs, a tomb or a
  custodian, so nothing here can enter the entry except Hujwiri as samaʿ theorist and Lahore burial.
- **The `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` mapping rests on a bare name plus an epithet.** The book
  gives "Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar" (p. 45, folio 31) and "the famous Chishti saint Farid ad-Din"
  (p. 47, folio 33) and **no death year, no Pakpattan, no shrine and no lineage position**. The name
  and the order together are distinctive enough that no other archive row competes, but the evidence
  is a name, and the consolidator should weigh it as such.
- **The book names the same man two ways within two pages, and possibly three.** "Sufi Hamid ad-Din
  Nagauri (d. 1274)" (p. 50, folio 36) becomes "his Chishti predecessor Hamid ad-Din Suwali" (p. 51,
  folio 37) and then "the farmer-saint Hamid ad-Din" (p. 52, folio 38). The chunk never says these
  are one person; the argument assumes it. **Do not resolve this from outside the text.** Neither
  name is an archive row.
- **"Zarradi" appears without introduction.** At p. 50 (folio 36) the author of *Usul as-sama'* is
  described only as "a disciple of the foremost Chishti saint of Delhi, Nizam ad-Din Awliya'"; at
  p. 51 (folio 37) the name "Zarradi's *Usul as-sarna'*" is used as if already given. Either the
  naming falls outside this chunk or the book introduces him abruptly. No date and no place are given
  for him anywhere here: **(not stated)**.
- **The approximate section map is wrong at the chunk's head and this note supersedes it.** ch. 2
  cannot open at p. 43, because p. 43's first line continues p. 42's sentence and the chapter header
  is already running (p. 42, folio 28; p. 43, folio 29). Where ch. 2 begins is outside this chunk.
- **Bare toponyms, written reversibly under the interim convention.** Chisht (p. 45, folio 31),
  Central Asia, Delhi, Arabia and Baghdad ("Baghdadian") carry no shrine, tomb, saint, ʿurs or
  custodian and take no id; **no archive row corresponds to any of them even if the ruling
  reverses**. **Lahore is the one case that would change.** Here it is not bare — it is where the
  book says Hujwiri "died and was buried" (p. 52, folio 38) — so it is recorded in the
  `data-darbar` bullet on the strength of the person, not the place. **If the bare-toponym ruling
  reverses and a place-name alone could take a row, "Lahore" on its own would be undecidable among
  the archive's many Lahore rows and should still not be mapped.**
- **Hinglaj does not appear in this chunk and no id was invented.** No Hindu deity appears either, so
  the open question about deities as principal figures does not arise here.
- **No archive row other than the two above arises.** The Qalandari order is named (p. 46, folio 32)
  but Lal Shahbaz Qalandar is not; the Suhrawardiyya is named (p. 50, folio 36) but Bahauddin
  Zakariya, Shah Rukn-e-Alam and the Uch Bukharis are not; the Shattaris, Kubrawis, Kazaruniyya,
  Shadhilis, Madyanis and 'Aydarusis are named as orders only (p. 47, folio 33). **An order name is
  not a person and takes no id.**
