## Practices, institutions, economy

- **ʿurs defined, and named as one of two controversial topics** — Hajji Imdad Allah's *Faysala-i
  haft mas'ala (Resolution of Seven Questions)* is "a short treatise… and only in the third question
  does he take up the controversial topics of death anniversaries of saints ( 'urs) and listening to
  music (sama')" (p. 145, folio 133).
- **A death-anniversary observance described by the man who performs it — the chunk's single fullest
  account of ʿurs practice, and it is an ʿurs without sama'.** "My practice in this matter is as
  follows. Every year at the time of the passing of the blessed spirit of my master and guide, I
  perform a virtuous act. First there is Qur'an recitation, then sometimes if time permits, a
  birthday poem in honor of the Prophet Muhammad is recited, food is prepared for those present, and
  the virtue of these deeds along with other things is dedicated to the shaykh. My custom has been
  that no sama' ever occurs, whether with voice alone or with instruments." (p. 145, folio 133).
  **The master is not named in the passage and no date or month is given: (not stated)** (p. 145,
  folio 133).
- **Conditions on sama' as Hajji Imdad Allah states them** — "if the permissible conditions are
  present and negative characteristics are removed, then it is permitted; otherwise, it is not
  permitted" (p. 145, folio 133); and on manners, "there is consensus as to the necessity of proper
  manners (adab), even though at this time in most gatherings these are nonexistent or nearly so"
  (p. 145, folio 133).
- **A living ʿurs with qawwali, photographed in 2001** — "7.2. Qawwali singers at the 2001 'urs
  festival of Capt. Wahid Bakhsh (Allahabad, Pakistan) (Photograph by Robert Rozehnal)" (p. 147,
  folio 135). **No attendance figure, offering, langar or custodian is given: (not stated)**
  (p. 147, folio 135).
- **State control of an ʿurs, named** — "Government manipulation, as in Pakistan's control of the
  'urs of Baba Farid" (p. 157, folio 145). **This is the only Auqaf-type fact in the chunk and the
  book does not use the word Auqaf: (not stated)** (p. 157, folio 145).
- **State broadcasting declined to promote Sufi practice in both countries** — "in both Pakistan and
  India the state-operated radio and television did not promote the spiritual practices of either
  the Chishtiyya or other orders, with the exception of documentaries produced by foreigners or the
  Government of India film cited earlier" (p. 155, folio 143).
- **The recording industry as the first mass market for shrine music** — "In the early part of the
  century, the British Gramophone Company of India monopolized the recording and distribution of
  qawwali music, under the catchall category of \"Urdu Muslim `Islamic:'[text-layer?] [Islamic.\"]`"
  (p. 149, folio 137); "Then `witl![text-layer?] [with]` the advent of film in the 1930s, live
  qawwali performances were also introduced, especially in the Bombay productions from the 1950s
  on." (p. 149, folio 137); "especially from `tl!e[text-layer?] [the]` 1970s on, more and more
  qawwali recordings featured textual phrases in Arabic, appealing to the re-Islamized sensibilities
  of Pakistan expatriate workers and overseas Muslims." (p. 149, folio 137).
- **Post-independence Indian state policy on qawwali** — "The ideology of postindependence India was
  to use qawwali to promote religious tolerance, creating \"a musical style that blended distinctive
  features of qawwali `witl![text-layer?] [with]` general traits `oflndian[text-layer?] [of Indian]`
  popular `music:'[text-layer?] [music.\"]`" (p. 149, folio 137).
- **A French state subsidy for qawwali's European audience** — "A series of state-of-the-art
  recordings from the French Ministry of Culture has made qawwali available to discriminating
  European listeners" (pp. 150–151, folios 138–139).
- **Printing, and who paid for it** — "indications are that in the principal locations for the
  introduction of printing technology on a mass basis in the nineteenth century (Cairo, Istanbul,
  Tehran, and Delhi), the main patrons of publication, aside from governments, were Sufi orders."
  (p. 149, folio 137). The scale effect is given concretely for Ibn 'Arabi: "a work that had been
  represented by at most a hundred manuscripts (and those difficult of access) was easily available
  at a corner bookstore in a thousand copies" (p. 149, folio 137).
- **Sufi leaders as publishers marketing to their own disciples** — "the way Sufi leaders could
  focus on marketing to their disciples through the publication of serials, a topic that is only
  beginning to be explored." (p. 150, folio 138).
- **The printed shajara: initiation turned into a commodity with a blank** — "Typically, initiation
  into a Sufi order in previous times involved the disciple learning by heart and then transcribing
  by hand the family \"tree\" (shajara) of the Sufi lineage, inscribing his own name at the end of a
  line traced back to the Prophet Muhammad. Now… the publisher makes available a large stack of
  ready-made lineage documents, with a blank for the would-be initiate and the master to inscribe
  their own names." (p. 150, folio 138).
- **A printed ʿurs calendar sold with the shajara — directly relevant to this archive's anniversary
  data.** "On a facing page, a grid forms a calendar of saints' death anniversaries, with some

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Hajji Imdad Allah's words to a Mevlevi dervish are called prophetic.** After "politely declining
  the offer to hear the flute performance, he referred the Mevlevi dervish to one of his disciples,
  Mawlavi Muhammad Husayn Ilahabadi, noting that the dervish would find an appreciative connoisseur
  of music in him. These were prophetic words" (pp. 147–148, folios 135–136). Attributed by the
  authors to an account Capt. Wahid Bakhsh "salvaged from one of Ashraf 'Ali's works" (p. 147, folio
  135).
- **Death in sama', the chunk's central marvel, told twice.** Muhammad Husayn Ilahabadi "would die
  during a musical session in Ajmer, while listening to a qawwal recite Persian verses by 'Abd
  al-Quddus Gangohi" (p. 148, folio 136); Ashraf 'Ali's own telling is that "Overwhelmed by hearing
  the ghazal by 'Abd al-Quddus, he prostrated himself in prayer and \"gave up his
  `spirit:'[text-layer?] [spirit.\"]`" (p. 148, folio 136). Attributed to *Khumkhana-i batin*
  (p. 148, folio 136).
- **Wahid Bakhsh's gloss on that death** — "Muhammad Husayn Ilahabadi had entered into absolute
  annihilation in God—\"he flew so high that he had no power to come `back:'[text-layer?]
  [back.\"]`" (p. 148, folio 136).
- **A saint's absorption in a single ghazal on a train** — "Such was the power of the singer's voice
  that Ashraf 'Ali could not even tell whether the train was still moving or had stopped. But after
  one ghazal he withdrew his permission." (p. 148, folio 136). Attributed to Ashraf 'Ali's own
  *Khumkhana-i batin* (p. 148, folio 136).
- **Saints who died in sama' as a recognised class** — Simnani "recreates the condition and lauds the
  example of those saints who died in `sama:\"[text-layer?] [sama'.\"]`" (p. 148, folio 136).
- **The authors' own verdict on marvels in this lineage** — "Miracle mongering is not the outcome of
  Hajji Imdad Allah's legacy, but neither is prohibition of `sama~[text-layer?] [sama'.]`" (p. 152,
  folio 140).
- **The station reached at the end of zikr, in Hajji Imdad Allah's words** — the seeker "will
  actually become transformed from head to toe into light. He will attain the level of \"through me
  he sees, through me he hears.\"" (p. 144, folio 132).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The zikr ladder of *Ziya' al-qulub*, quoted at length as the chapter's anchor.** The seeker
  moves from "no God" through "except God" at the level of the heart, to the spirit where the
  recitation is "Allah" and "the `a!-[text-layer?] [al-]` preceding the name \"Allah'' no longer
  remains. Only the pronominal `syllable\" hu\"[text-layer?] [syllable \"hu\"]` is allowed to
  persist." (p. 144, folio 132), then to the secret where "the one recollected (Allah) becomes the
  very self (khwud). This is the meaning of annihilation in annihilation (`jana' dar
  fana'[text-layer?] [fana' dar fana']`)." (p. 144, folio 132), ending in shari'a-bound guidance:
  "the seeker should remain occupied with worship in the strict sense and preserve the dignity and
  commandment of the shari'a" and "His own sainthood and mastery will stay sound and valid."
  (p. 145, folio 133).
- **The authors' claim that Hajji Imdad Allah has absorbed the yogic inheritance without citing it.**
  "No reference is made here to antecedent Sabiri masters and their devotion to a similar kind of
  `breath control! zikr[text-layer?] [breath control/zikr]`"; "Hajji Imdad Allah has so internalized
  the psychology of the heart that he no longer needs to refer to any external Hindu analogues"; the
  manual "seems to be one with that of Nizam ad-Din Awrangabadi and Shah Wali Allah, yet the flavor
  is more fully and freely Indian than either of theirs." (p. 145, folio 133).
- **Imdad Allah's position on sama' read as a deliberate middle** — he "rejects the practice of sama'
  with musical instruments, but he stops short of rejecting music altogether. Indeed, he provides a
  back-door confirmation of its most extreme ecstatic practitioners (ahl-i hal)" (p. 146, folio 134);
  and, for his disciples, the treatise "can be seen as his way of identifying with the Chishti past
  while still distancing himself from the problematic nature of then current musical practice"
  (p. 145, folio 133). "Unlike those of his disciples who became leaders of the Deoband madrasa,
  Hajji Imdad Allah did not unconditionally reject sama' as a practice." (p. 147, folio 135).
- **The double tension the chapter is built on** — "Bracketed together, these two Chishti exemplars
  reveal a double tension, one between South Asian Islam and British Christian Orientalism, and a
  second between anti-Sufi reformers and the defenders of Sufi practices as integral to Islam."
  (p. 146, folio 134).
- **Wahid Bakhsh's book structure, given chapter by chapter** — *Mushahada-i haqq* is "initially
  framed by chapters entitled \"The Need for Islam in a Misguided `World;'[text-layer?] [World,\"]`
  \"The Conception of God in `Islam;'[text-layer?] [Islam,\"]` and \"The Nature of Humanity\"; then
  follows a chapter on \"Objections to Sufism\" that responds primarily to orientalist attempts to
  find extra-Islamic origins for Sufism", the key chapter being "\"Traveling to God, or the Path for
  Attaining the Goal of `Life:'[text-layer?] [Life.\"]`" (p. 146, folio 134); it "still retains core
  elements from Hajji Imdad Allah's manual Ziya' al-qulub, i.e., the description of the three
  principal methods of meditation, the zikr of affirmation and negation in the profession of faith,
  and the six \"subtle centers.\"" (p. 146, folio 134).
- **The book's key contrast between the two men: sama' moves from ancillary to central.** "He
  explicates sama' as the culmination of spiritual discipline, which alone can produce the result of
  annihilation (fana') in God. It is no longer ancillary; it is central." (p. 147, folio 135);
  against this, Hajji Imdad Allah "stops short of affirming sama' as the completion of meditative
  practice" (p. 148, folio 136).
- **Wahid Bakhsh's method described as recruitment of his own opponents.** "Instead of explicitly
  disagreeing with either Hajji Imdad Allah or major disciples of his like Ashraf 'Ali Thanvi, he
  instead recalls anecdotes from their own writings where they actually approved of sama' or listened
  to it themselves." (p. 148, folio 136); "his method of recalling his contemporaries as \"secret\"
  defenders of sama' harks back to the experience of the fifteenth-century Chishti saint Sayyid
  Ashraf Jahangir Simnani (d. 1428)" (p. 148, folio 136).
- **The counter-evidence the authors themselves put on record** — "it would appear that the
  nineteenth-century Sabiri Chishti master was at best reticent, at worst ambivalent about the
  practice of sama', and it is not surprising that biographies of Hajji Imdad Allah such as Kamalat-i
  Imdadiyya give no account whatever of his practicing `sam a:[text-layer?] [sama'.]`" (p. 147, folio
  135); the explanation offered is temperamental, not doctrinal — "he was, as he himself admits,
  unmusical. This personal idiosyncrasy was for him not a matter of principle, however." (p. 147,
  folio 135).
- **The chapter's thesis sentence on zikr and sama' together** — "sama' and zikr together—one
  inextricably reinforcing and extending the other, both working always and everywhere together—zikr
  and sama', sama' and zikr remain the core spiritual methods of the Chishtis, from the Sultanate
  period to the postcolonial era." (p. 148, folio 136).
- **Reformism as identity-splitting** — "The critique of sama' by reformist Chishtis illustrates the
  extent to which neo-scriptural tendencies can create conflicting identities, as Islam and Sufism
  are differently construed, `witl![text-layer?] [with]` both overlap and contradiction, in the
  nineteenth and twentieth centuries." (pp. 148–149, folios 136–137).
- **Two tangents of modernising technology, the chapter's organising distinction** — "We
  differentiate two tangents of modernizing technology: One is instrumental and the other is
  cognitive." (p. 149, folio 137); the cognitive shift "may be labeled \"diffusion into a broad based
  spiritual aesthetic that is neither specifically Sufi nor generically `Muslim:'[text-layer?]
  [Muslim.\"]`" (p. 150, folio 138).
- **Regula Qureshi is the authority for the audience shift** — she "has explored how
  `tl!e[text-layer?] [the]` very devotion to sama' was changed by a shift in audience; it came to
  include non-Sufis as well as Sufis, non-Muslims as well as Muslims." (p. 149, folio 137).
- **Print as dilution or as hybridity — the authors put both sides and decline to settle.** "How
  could a printed shajara chart substitute for the vibrant presence, and the needed direction, of a
  living spiritual master?" (p. 150, folio 138); against which, "printed shajaras, mass deputizations,
  and now qawwali recordings indicate is that Muslims in both Pakistan and India practice a form of
  cultural hybridity that eludes the usual indices of political intrigue, regional conflict, and
  global power. The colonial period remains important, but the dimensions of its importance have to
  be extended." (p. 150, folio 138).
- **The timing claim about publicity and attack** — "this publicization of Sufism occurred at
  precisely the time when Sufism was becoming an abstract subject, separated from Islam in orientalist
  writings, and condemned by reformists as a non-Islamic innovation." (p. 149, folio 137).
- **Wahid Bakhsh's science apologetic, and the authors' distance from it** — "Science as an outcome is
  seen as the perfection of Sufism, even when scientists ignore \"the spiritual origins of their
  `discipline:'[text-layer?] [discipline.\"]`" (pp. 151–152, folios 139–140); "The real science they
  are discussing is not astrophysics or chaos theory; it is science of the inner self" (p. 152, folio
  140); "Even if one dismisses this approach as bad science or rampant apologetics, one cannot ignore
  its core Chishti reflex: to reaffirm sama', music, as the ultimate experience of humanity." (p. 152,
  folio 140).
- **The refusal of a meta-narrative** — "There is no single narrative of the past 150 years of Chishti
  Sufism, nor should we expect to find one. While this book has engaged with numerous master
  narratives, it is not proposing a meta-narrative to supplant them. What results… is not a historical
  certainty but an interpretive ambiguity" (p. 152, folio 140); "they have clearly lost the
  single-order identity that underscored their prominent influence in earlier eras" (p. 152, folio
  140).
- **The Inayat Khan problem stated as a question about Chishti identity** — "The central question he
  raises is about Chishti identity: How does one become a Sufi, and a Chishti, in a non-Muslim
  society?" (p. 154, folio 142); "Though there is little doubt about Inayat Khan's own Islamic
  observance and loyalty, there is a question about the Islamic identity of those participants in
  various Sufi organizations linked to him." (p. 154, folio 142); "Inayat Khan made the momentous
  decision to present Sufism to Europeans and Americans as a spiritual path that was not tied to
  Islam." (p. 154, folio 142); the consequence, "Islam both as symbol and as religious system is
  conspicuously downplayed in the multiple groups that derive their authority from Inayat Khan."
  (p. 154, folio 142). His universalism rests on "the Qur'anic dictum (2:136) that no distinction is
  to be made between the messages revealed by God to the different prophets" (p. 154, folio 142).
- **"Four-school Sufism", quoted from an unnamed disciple** — Inayat Khan "studied Qur'an, hadith, the
  literature of the Persian mystics, and underwent a course of training in four
  `schools:'[text-layer?] [schools.\"]`", the four "generally taken to be the Chishti, Suhrawardi,
  Qadiri, and Naqshbandi orders" (p. 154, folio 142); a disciple's claim that "to identify Inayat Khan
  solely with the Chishtiyya is in itself a `mistake:'[text-layer?] [mistake.\"]`" (p. 155, folio
  143); and "our Chishti lineage or tariqat is only one part of four school Sufism, whose founders and
  precursors knew each other in tenth-century [sic] Baghdad" — **the "[sic]" is the book's own**
  (p. 155, folio 143).
- **An authorial conflict of interest, disclosed in the text** — Zia Inayat Khan's "doctoral research
  on the Chishti masters of Gujarat during the first cycle of the Indian Chishtiyya (for which both
  the present writers are advisers)" (p. 155, folio 143).
- **David Hollinger and Castells frame the cyber section** — Hollinger "has argued that the
  twenty-first century will be marked by `self-ascription-individuals[text-layer?]
  [self-ascription—individuals]` drawing on multiple sources in constructing their own identity"
  (p. 155, folio 143); "How well do they move through what Castells calls the gradients of modern
  communication, from printed texts to radio to television to movies and now to the Net?" (p. 155,
  folio 143); "we project that the future will resemble what Castells calls the information age, with
  multiple sites but no fixed center." (p. 156, folio 144).
- **Indianness as an irreducible of Chishti identity** — "it is impossible to reduce the importance of
  Indianness for the Chishtiyya. Traditionally, Indianness has been expressed through links to the
  physical places of Hindustan, whether through teachings of living masters or tombs of bygone
  masters, but always acknowledging that the network of Chishtiyya passes through India." (p. 156,
  folio 144).
- **The book's own limit test for Chishti identity** — "While it may be possible for some to downplay
  Islam and remain a Sufi, one cannot be a Chishti Sufi and ignore sama' and zikr. They are the
  irreducible core of Chishti devotionalism, whatever the epoch, whatever the place, whatever the
  aesthetic." (p. 157, folio 145).
- **Diaspora alliances and their condition** — "a link of diasporic Chishtis to other Sufi and Muslim
  groups, such as Rumi devotees in the United States and Barelvis in the United Kingdom. This takes
  place wherever such groups form minority communities vis-a-vis a majority community that is neither
  Muslim nor Sufi in its dominant outlook. These ties vary, depending on the degree of hostility from
  anti-Sufi, pro-Wahhabi groups." (p. 157, folio 145).
- **The title phrase reappears as the chapter's last argument** — "The challenge of Chishti Sufism,
  then, remains the self-sacrifice whose very possibility is demonstrated by those martyrs of love who
  have gone before." (p. 158, folio 146).

## Doubts

- **`mithankot-kot-mithan` is mapped on a bare name, and the book's death year disagrees with the
  archive's by ten years.** The book prints "Ghulam Farid (d. 1911)" (p. 146, folio 134); the archive
  row's `figure_died` cell reads 1901 and its `principal_figure` cell reads "Khwaja Ghulam Farid".
  The book gives no "Khwaja", no Mithankot, no Kot Mithan and no shrine, only "another great
  nineteenth-century Chishti". **A human must decide whether the book's Ghulam Farid is the archive's
  before either year enters an entry — and the 1911/1901 discrepancy must be carried forward either
  way.** No second archive row is a candidate: `mithankot-kot-mithan` is the only row whose principal
  figure is a Ghulam Farid.
- **"Baba Farid" is used in this chunk for two different people, and only one of them is the archive's
  saint.** The ʿurs sentence (p. 157, folio 145) and the Muharram 5 calendar entry (p. 159, folio 147)
  belong to `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar`; **"the Web site of Baba Farid, a contemporary poet and
  representative of the Hazrat Nizamuddin Spiritual Foundation" (p. 156, folio 144) is a living
  twenty-first-century person and was deliberately not mapped.** A consolidator merging on the string
  "Baba Farid" would conflate them.
- **The archive's death year for Fariduddin Ganjshakar and the book's differ by a year.** The book
  prints "(d. 664/1265)" (p. 159, folio 147); the archive row's `figure_died` cell reads 1266. Kept
  side by side, not reconciled.
- **`mazar-e-iqbal` is mapped under the 18 September 2026 ruling on the bare-name evidence the ruling
  names.** Iqbal appears only as a poet whose sung poetry qawwali overshadowed (p. 149, folio 137) and
  as the author of a ghazal for Nizam ad-Din Awliya' (pp. 157–158, folios 145–146). **The book gives
  him no tomb, no Lahore, no death year and no shrine of his own: nothing about the mazar itself can
  enter an entry from this chunk.** The ghazal's translator is also unnamed, so the English wording
  quoted above is of unknown authorship and should not be attributed to Iqbal as his own words.
- **Hazrat Inayat Khan matches none of the archive's three "Inayat" rows, and no id was chosen.** The
  candidates are `dargah-roza-sufi-shah-inayat-shaheed` (Sufi Shah Inayat Shaheed, Jhok Sharif,
  d. 1718), `shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri` (Shah Inayat Qadiri, Lahore, c. 1728) and
  `ganj-e-inayat-sarkar` (Pir Muhammad Inayat Ahmad Naqshbandi Mujaddidi, d. 2011). The book's man is
  a Chishti Nizami of the Gujarat–Hyderabad musician family who died in Delhi on "February 5, 1927"
  and whose tomb stands in the Nizam ad-Din Dargah, Delhi (pp. 153–154, folios 141–142) — wrong
  country, wrong date and wrong silsila for all three. **Flagged, not chosen**, as this run was
  instructed. The same non-mapping applies to Pir Vilayat Khan, "Pir Vilagat Khan" and Zia Inayat Khan
  (pp. 155–156, folios 143–144).
- **Name collision that must not be resolved by string match: "Qutb ad-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki" is NOT the
  archive's `tomb-of-qutbuddin-aibak`.** The calendar entry reads "Rabi' I / 4. Qutb ad-Din Bakhtiyar
  Kaki (d. 633/1235)" in a list of "important saints of the Chishti lineage" (p. 159, folio 147); the
  archive row's principal figure is "Sultan Qutb ud-Din Aibak" (d. 1210), a ruler, buried in Lahore.
  Different person, different date, different kind of figure. **No id taken.**
- **Second name collision, also left unmapped: "Abu al-Fath" is not certainly the archive's Shah
  Rukn-e-Alam.** The calendar gives "Sha'ban / 16. Abu al-Fath (d. 858/1454)" with no further

  Rukn-e-Alam (Rukn-ud-Din Abul Fath)" with `figure_died` 1335. The years are 119 years apart and the
  book supplies no nisba, so **no id was taken** — but the string overlap is exactly the kind a
  consolidator could mis-merge, so it is recorded here.
- **Internal contradiction inside this chunk: Siraj bin Kamal ad-Din 'Allama is dated twice and the
  two dates disagree.** The lineage of Inayat Khan gives "Siraj bin Kamal ad-Din 'Allama (d. 1368)"
