## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Succession as a documented office: the khilafat-nama.** Nizam ad-Din "chose the leading disciples
  from a list of 32 candidates. He drew up their diplomas (khilafat-namas) according to a strict
  procedure that Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar employed" (p. 194, folio 182). The book fixes the number
  of successors from the shape of the source rather than from a statement: "Although in that passage
  Mir Khwurd did not indicate the exact number of khalifas, his organization of the chapter on Nizam
  ad-Din's khalifas makes it clear that there were ten" (p. 194, folio 182, with note 24).
- **A succession conferred by donated clothing and by silence.** Iqbal, "the manager of the dargah,
  brought him a cap and a shirt that had been part of a donation. The saint blessed them, and in his
  presence, Iqbal put them on the Maulana and said, 'Now you are indeed a `successor:'[text-layer?]
  [successor."]` Shaykh Nizam ad-Din said nothing, but his silence was assumed to be an indication of
  his approval" (p. 191, folio 179). Useful to the archive as a case of investiture performed by a
  shrine manager, out of the offerings, rather than by the saint.
- **The initiatic regalia enumerated.** Burhan ad-Din "described himself as having received from
  Nizam ad-Din Awliya' the 'essential' hat of initiation, as opposed to the hat of ordinary
  discipleship" (p. 192, folio 180, with note 10); on the road "from Delhi to Daulatabad, Burhan
  ad-Din Gharib had a cot carried alongside him, in which was the staff of Nizam ad-Din, one of the
  primary insignia of `succession.U[text-layer?] [succession.11]`" (p. 192, folio 180); and "When
  Burhan ad-Din Gharib was on his deathbed, he called for Nizam ad-Din's rosary" (p. 192, folio 180,
  with note 12). Late hagiographers "proceeded to enumerate the sacred Chishti regalia with which he
  was entrusted" (p. 194, folio 182, with note 30).
- **Territorial jurisdiction (wilayat) granted as a formal act.** Nizam ad-Din "pronounced the
  Qur'anic passage 'Today I have perfected your religion and completed my bounty to you' (Qur'an
  5:3), in reference to Burhan ad-Din Gharib's spiritual perfection, when he gave the latter dominion
  `( wilayat)[text-layer?] [(wilayat)]` over the Deccan" (p. 192, folio 180, with note 15).
- **Malfuzat as a dated genre, with a place and a decade.** "the malfuzat texts recorded by Burhan
  ad-Din's own disciples in Khuldabad in the 1330s" (p. 192, folio 180); within them "He stated in
  1333 that he had followed the path of Nizam ad-Din Awliya' for 40 years (a number symbolic of
  perfection), and only on four occasions had he been forced to ask directly for the latter's
  `help?[text-layer?] [help.7]`" (p. 192, folio 180).
- **Sama' as a contested practice defended by a disciple.** Burhan ad-Din "mentioned how he
  vigorously defended Nizam ad-Din Awliya' and the practice of `sama'against[text-layer?]
  [samaʿ against]` hostile criticism" (p. 192, folio 180, with note 6). Mir Khwurd's portrait
  describes the performance itself: "In `sama'this[text-layer?] [samaʿ this]` saint was completely
  extreme, experienced much ecstasy, and said the prayers of lovers. He had a distinctive style in
  dancing, so that the companions of this saint were called 'Burhanis' among the lovers" (p. 190,
  folio 178, with note 3).
- **The carpet as a marker of rank, and the charge that hangs on it.** "Old age had weakened the
  already frail health of the Maulana, so he folded a blanket on which he sat in his house", but his
  accusers reported "that Maulana Burhan ad-Din was sitting on a carpet like a big Shaykh and
  dispensing favors in the manner of the great saints" (p. 191, folio 179). Nur ad-Din's master is
  praised in the same idiom: "he did not sit on a carpet. He used to say, 'Truly to "sit on the
  carpet" is this: whoever (sits on the carpet) looks neither to the right or the left'" (p. 196,
  folio 184).
- **Expulsion from a master's assembly as a public sanction, and intercession as its remedy.** Iqbal
  told the Maulana "The Shaykh has ordered you to leave this place immediately and not return", after
  which "The people of the city came to console him" and Amir Khusrau interceded twice (p. 191,
  folio 179).
- **Khanqah service as graded manual labour.** Nur ad-Din "performed all the duties required for the
  faqirs of his father's khanqah, from laundering clothes to heating water. His first duty was to
  clean the latrine" (p. 195, folio 183); "Shaykh Nur for eight years carried wood" (p. 196,
  folio 184); and at the slippery watering place "For four years he did this task. At the times of
  festivals he would carry water; many of his former fellow students would also carry jugs on their
  heads, and the people of Bengal found this very amusing" (p. 196, folio 184).
- **A silsila's own count of the stages of the path, reduced twice over.** "The shaykhs of old had set
  99 stages for the completion of the spiritual quest (this corresponded to the number of the names
  of God). The shaykhs of our silsila have fixed on 15 stages, of which this faqir has selected 3"
  (p. 196, folio 184).
- **The jama'at khana as the place of hospitality, and alms given to an abusive guest.** "They then
  went to sit in the jama'at khana", the man refused the ground as unclean and the food as "the flesh
  of the pig", and "The Shaykh called for some money. The man took it, then left" (p. 197, folio 185).
- **Prostration and foot-kissing before a living shaykh, reported as a burden.** "God almighty has led
  so many people to be captivated by me. Some of them prostrate themselves before me, some of them
  grab and kiss my feet" (p. 196, folio 184). Mir Khwurd reports the same gesture of himself: "The
  author many times had the happiness of kissing the feet of that saint" (p. 190, folio 178).
- **A ritual practice explained: the handshake after the salaam.** Asked "Why do the shaykhs
  customarily shake hands after the salaam at the end of the obligatory prayer?", the Shaykh answered
  that the worshipper "leaves himself to go on an inward journey. When he says the salaam, it is
  because he has come back to himself, and hence must shake hands with his friends" (p. 197,
  folio 185).
- **Travel by palanquin, and the saint's letters as a circulated corpus.** "the Sheikh left the
  khanqah and went riding in a palanquin" (p. 196, folio 184); and "Shaykh Nur Qutb-i 'Alam also
  wrote a collection `ofletters[text-layer?] [of letters]` that are extremely sweet and eloquent, in
  the idiom of people who are sensitive to the love of God" (p. 197, folio 185).
- **Sufi masters positioned against sultans, in cities but off-centre.** "The Chishti masters
  identified themselves with the expanding Muslim polity as a counterweight to sultans, in particular
  the Ilyas Shahi dynasts" (p. 195, folio 183); "The first five Chishti masters were uniformly urban
  in their orientation, but always on the margin of power, not at its center" (p. 195, folio 183).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The saint's foreknowledge of a disciple's face.** "When Mahmud came to Daulatabad, Burhan ad-Din
  Gharib without being told described the personal appearance of the saint of the south" (p. 195,
  folio 183, with note 32). Attributed by the book to the Deccan tradition of Burhan ad-Din's own
  followers.
- **A mysterious saint's direction, with a comparison.** Mahmud of Lajwara was told to seek Burhan
  ad-Din: "He has recently arrived in `Daulatabad;'[text-layer?] [Daulatabad,"]` said the man, "and
  compared with others he is like the sun compared with the moon" (p. 195, folio 183).
- **A prediction of another man's master.** Gisu Daraz "recalled hearing Burhan ad-Din Gharib predict
  that Gisu Daraz would obtain spiritual guidance from Chiragh-i Dihli" (p. 193, folio 181).
  Attributed by the book to Gisu Daraz's own recollection.
- **The naming of the epithet as a prophecy fulfilled.** "He is indeed poor now, but the whole world
  will come to know him" (p. 192, folio 180, with note 13) — reported by Burhan ad-Din's disciples.
- **Election as a status conferred in eternity, taught in the anonymous discourse.** "In eternity He
  knew a certain soul to be worthy of nearness and favors and He created him according to what He
  knew about `him:'[text-layer?] [him."]` … And in eternity He knew another soul to be suited for
  distance and torture" (p. 189, folio 177).
- **Absolute predestination stated in a proverb.** "Without His intention or His choice not a single
  leaf can drop from a tree nor can a blade of grass spring up `form[text-layer?] [from]` the ground"
  (p. 188, folio 176).
- **A voice from the unseen.** "In the midst of his agitation, a voice came to him: 'Leave off
  hypocrisy. Be a lover! Be mad!'" (p. 196, folio 184). Given by the book as what "They also report".

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The chunk's governing problem is stated as a question about sources, not about the saint.** "What
  are we to make of the discrepancies between these accounts of the status of Burhan ad-Din Gharib?"
  (p. 193, folio 181). The answer offered is factional: "The negative evaluations of Burhan ad-Din
  Gharib's position come primarily from the followers of Chiragh-i Dihli, who viewed the latter as
  having succeeded to the authority `ofNizam[text-layer?] [of Nizam]` ad-Din" (pp. 193–194,
  folios 181–182).
- **A stated method: cross-reading competing biographers rather than choosing between them.**
  "To bring out the contradictory perspectives that emerge from competing biographers, we have to
  flesh out his portrait from a number of other sources" (p. 190, folio 178); and the conclusion,
  "For the historian, it is not necessary to decide which is the 'correct' version" (p. 195,
  folio 183). Directly useful to this archive's own source criticism.
- **The central historiographical claim: hagiographic reputation is an artefact of lineage
  competition.** "The discrepancies between the different evaluations of Burhan ad-Din Gharib's
  status, then, derive from competing historiographies within the Chishti order" (p. 195,
  folio 183); "Each khalifa of Nizam ad-Din was surrounded by disciples who necessarily tended to see
  their master as the one supreme successor to Nizam ad-Din's authority" (p. 194, folio 182); and the
  verdict on the casualty, "their followers in the next generation could not help playing the game of
  one-upmanship, and Burhan ad-Din's biographical profile became a casualty of that contest"
  (p. 194, folio 182).
- **The evidence for the negative case is weighed and found partisan.** Mir Khwurd "had closed his
  account of `Bur han[text-layer?] [Burhan]` ad-Din Gharib with the barest mention of the latter's
  sojourn there", and "basically omitted or downplayed any information that would indicate that
  Burhan ad-Din might in fact be one of the chief disciples of Nizam ad-Din" (pp. 192–193,
  folios 180–181).
- **Gisu Daraz's motive is named explicitly.** "Gisu Daraz was concerned to praise Burhan ad-Din
  Gharib faintly and to preserve the prestige of Chiragh-i Dihli (and by extension Gisu Daraz) as the
  true successor of Nizam ad-Din" (p. 193, folio 181).
- **A source's chronology corrected against itself.** Of Gisu Daraz's oldest biography the authors
  interject "(that event actually took place four years later, in 1329)", and of the anecdote in
  which the child leads Burhan ad-Din to the meditating Chiragh-i Dihli they interject "(who would
  have been less than four years old at the time)" (p. 193, folio 181). Both are the book's own
  corrections of its source.
- **The explicit counter-evidence: the Deccan malfuzat.** "The lesser-known Deccan tradition deriving
  from Burhan ad-Din Gharib's followers shows an entirely different picture. It is his critics who
  are refuted, while Burhan ad-Din is extolled" (p. 194, folio 182); and on the succession, Burhan
  ad-Din's own account "contrasted with Mir Khwurd's description; instead of using the silence of an
  ailing saint to approve his own nomination as successor, Burhan ad-Din Gharib specified the precise
  oral instructions that Nizam ad-Din gave him when authorizing him to teach" (p. 194, folio 182,
  with note 28).
- **The succession-by-silence story is placed in a class of rumour.** "Mir Khwurd's account of this
  event seems to belong to the class of rumors regarding Nizam ad-Din's feebleness in the month
  before his death, which some had used to question the credentials of certain khalifas. Mir Khwurd
  himself had hastened to dismiss these rumors, arguing that Nizam ad-Din was still quite vigorous at
  the time of the selection of the ten khalifas" (p. 194, folio 182, with note 29).
- **A claim about why one line of transmission came to dominate.** "Due to the continued prominence
  of the Delhi branch of the Chishtis, and by dint of repetition in later biographical texts, this
  picture of the relationship between the two has come to dominate the later Chishti perspective"
  (p. 195, folio 183).
- **The verdict on 'Abd al-Haqq as an author with a doctrine.** "in considering the legacy of 'Abd
  al-Haqq, one must surmise that he was driven by the concept of undivided transmission of authority.
  Despite Nizam ad-Din's clear unwillingness to designate a single successor, later interpreters,
  including 'Abd al-Haqq, could not resist the temptation to draw a single North Indian line of
  transmission back from Nasir ad-Din to Nizam ad-Din and, ultimately, to the Prophet Muhammad"
  (p. 195, folio 183). The same suspicion opens the Nur ad-Din section: "one wonders how much of it,
  too, was compressed by the centrist perspective of the author" (p. 195, folio 183).
- **The stated editorial purpose of the Nur ad-Din excerpt.** "The chief point of this excerpt is to
  illustrate the teachings of the shaykh with reference to his letters, paralleling similar summaries
  of Chishti principles found in the accounts of Mu'in ad-Din and Hamid ad-Din Suwali. It is also
  crucial to recall the importance of his connection to the court through his elder brother"
  (p. 195, folio 183).
- **A thesis about Chishti urban politics, stated against the obvious reading.** "Seen from the
  perspective of the Chishti masters, their urban location was not meant for the exercise of backdoor
  political and public influence, but rather to resist the effort to accord undue power and authority
  to earthly instruments-whether political or military, social or economic" (p. 195, folio 183).
- **The court's view of the Sufi life, recovered from one detail and marked as thin evidence.** "For
  the Bengali master Nur Qutb-i 'Alam, the brief reference to his elder brother's view of the
  spiritual life is as intriguing as it is elusive. For the elder brother, immersed in life at the
  court, anything less than the advancement of the political goals of the Ilyas Shahi dynasts was a
  waste of time and talent! To be a Sufi, and to ignore the benefit of seeking other employment, was
  a waste!" (p. 195, folio 183).
- **A saint's reputation carried by his own bracketed obscurity.** Of Kamal ad-Din the authors note
  that "this Gujarat-based lineage was not well known outside western India for several centuries"
  and survived only through the later fame of its descendants (p. 198, folio 186). The bracketed
  headnote is the authors' editorial voice, distinguished typographically from the translated text.

## Doubts

- **The `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` mapping rests on two bare reference mentions inside another
  saint's biography, and on nothing else.** The book writes **"Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar"**; the
  archive row names its principal figure "Baba Fariduddin Mas'ud Ganjshakar" of Pakpattan, died 1266,
  Chishti. In pp. 187–198 the book gives **no** death date, no location, no tomb, no order name and
  no saying of his; the spelling differs from the archive's. Under the 18 September 2026 ruling a
  principal figure takes the row's id on a reference-only mention, so the id is recorded, but a human
  should confirm that the book's Farid is the archive's before either fact enters an entry. **No
  second archive row is a candidate** — no other row's principal figure is a Farid — so the doubt is
  whether this is the archive's saint at all, not which of two rows he is.
- **"Iqbal (the manager of the dargah)" is NOT `mazar-e-iqbal` and must never be mapped to it.** The
  Iqbal of p. 191 (folio 179) is a fourteenth-century shrine servant at Ghiyaspur who fetches a cap
  and shirt and delivers an expulsion order; the archive's `mazar-e-iqbal` row is Allama Muhammad
  Iqbal, died 1938, Lahore. The collision is on a bare given name only. Recorded here so a later
  worker does not make the match.
- **Standing near-misses checked and all absent from this chunk.** Qutb ad-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki, Jalal
  ad-Din Bukhari and Jamal al-Din Multani do **not** appear anywhere in pp. 187–198, so none of the
  three rejected matches arises. Hinglaj does not appear, and no id was invented for it. **No
  Pakistani place-name of any kind occurs in the twelve pages**, so the bare-toponym convention
  produces no reversible entry for any archive row here: Delhi, Deogir, Burhanpur, Khuldabad,
  Daulatabad, Ghiyaspur, the Deccan, Bengal, Pandua, Awadh, Ma'bar, Gujarat and Mecca would take no
  row even if the ruling reverses, because no archive row lies in any of them.

  translated question-and-answer discourse whose author is named nowhere in pp. 187–190 (p. 187,
  folio 175 — p. 190, folio 178); the attribution lies in an earlier chunk. Every teaching quoted
