## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Shrine succession as the political test case of the period, and the authors' refusal to let it
  stand alone.** "If we were to follow only the dictates of po- / litical history and the criteria of
  social science, the test case for the Chishti Nizami Su- / laymaniyya lineage in the colonial period
  might seem to be the battles over shrines in / the Punjab" (p. 124, folio 111). The Taunsa conflict
  is then given in full — two branches of succession from Shaykh Sulayman, "one of them linked to his
  grand- / son Allah Bakhsh (d. 1901), the other to Mihr 'Ali Shah (d. 1937)" — and attributed to
  three causes at once: "British manipulation and also / the rivalry of local Sikhs, Hindus, and
  Muslim reformers" (p. 124, folio 111). **This is the chunk's one explicit statement of colonial
  state involvement in a Pakistani shrine's internal succession.**
- **Succession rivalry at Jalalpur Sharif, and the two forms it took.** "the rivalry between
  descendants of Haydar 'Ali Shah over how best to represent his / legacy, with one group founding a
  political party named Hizb Allah, but enjoying a / narrow band of influence. In contrast, the
  disciples of Haydar 'Ali Shah focused their / hagiographical accounts of him on his charismatic
  personality and miraculous influ- / ence" (p. 125, folio 112). Descent-group politics against
  hagiography, in one shrine.
- **Endowed teaching institutions as the instrument of consolidation.** Shaykh Sulayman "consolidated
  the order by stressing learning / through establishment of institutions: not only the academy called
  Dar al- 'Ulum at / Taunsa on the upper Indus, but also other religious schools throughout the
  Punjab" (p. 121, folio 108).
- **Free instruction and lodging, at scale, in the southern Punjab.** Muhammad 'Aqil and Gul Muhammad
  Ahmadpuri "founded religious schools in the southern Punjab that / provided free instruction and
  lodging to hundreds of students" (pp. 121–122, folios 108–109). **The book gives no endowment,
  revenue source, land or donor for these schools: (not stated).**
- **A lodge library retained as an institutional asset.** "the library built up and retained at his
  principal lodge in Taunsa / on the upper Indus" (p. 121, folio 108).
- **Alms: a rule of total redistribution, with two named exceptions, one of them a wage.** "these later
  Chishtis / made it a principle always to distribute the alms they received, except under special /
  circumstances (such as personal illness for Shah Kalim Allah, or the desire to pay the / qawwals on
  Friday night for Shaykh Nizam ad-Din Awrangabadi)" (p. 123, folio 110). **Paying the qawwals on
  Friday night is the chunk's only named recurring shrine expenditure.**
- **Avoidance of the court as a fiscal-political discipline.** "Shah Kalim Allah and his successors
  stressed the importance of avoiding the court, in / his case the emperor Farrukhsiyar (r. 1713-19)"
  (p. 123, folio 110), while Nizam ad-Din Awrangabadi "had to be careful with Aurangzeb himself
  (apparently in the 1670s)" (p. 123, folio 110). The authors read both as "their deliberate attempt
  to maintain the continuity with classic Chishti prin- / ciples from the first cycle" (p. 123, folio
  110).
- **Hereditary succession, broken and then partly restored.** "The Gujarat Chishti lineage over eight
  generations had a / hereditary aspect, but with Kalim Allah there is a breaking away from the
  principle of / hereditary succession" (p. 122, folio 109).
- **Recentralization of an order as an institutional achievement, and its political counterpoint.**
  "K. A. Nizami argues that from a structural point of view, the decisive achievement / of Kalim Allah
  was to recentralize the Chishti order, which had been operating on a / regionally dispersed basis
  since the beginning of the second cycle" (p. 122, folio 109), and "this recentralization … coincided
  with the decentralization of the Mughal em- / pire" (p. 122, folio 109). The authors go further:
  "It may have even been the decline of central political au- / thority in Delhi that made possible
  the establishment of independent institutions of / spiritual and religious instruction by the
  successors to Kalim Allah" (pp. 122–123, folios 109–110).
- **Multiple initiation as a spreading institutional practice.** Kalim Allah's master "also initiated
  him into the Qadiri, Suhrawardi, Naqsh- / bandi, and Shattari orders, illustrating how common the
  practice of multiple initia- / tions was becoming" (p. 122, folio 109).
- **Shrine atmosphere as the folk theory of suborder difference, sourced to a khadim.** According to
  Liaqat Moini, "himself a member of the khadim family attached to Ajmer / Sharif", "Up to the present
  / day, the aspect at the chief Sabiri shrine of Kalyar Sharif is one of divine majesty / (jalal),
  complementing while also competing with the ambience of divine beauty / (jamal) at Ajmer Sharif"
  (p. 120, folio 107).
- **Hereditary attendants and their privilege of the sanctuary at night.** "the much ma- / ligned
  hereditary attendants; one of their privileges is to have a place inside the sanc- / tuary to pass
  the entire evening, either in prayer or sleep, and many still do so today" (p. 125, folio 112).
- **A shrine's resident-family system, named.** Of the Nizam ad-Din shrine: "Quite a lot of attention
  is given to material that is rele- / vant to the Nizam ad-Din shrine and its system of resident
  families. Nizami's family / is called 'Nabiragan' (grandchildren), one of the four traditional
  custodial families / descended from the children of a pir brother of the saint" (p. 130, folio 117).
- **Two named lines of descent, blood and spirit, as the vocabulary of shrine claim-making.** "One,
  through blood descent, is called 'the children by bread' / (farzandan-i nan)"; "The other line of
  influence is / traced through spiritual affiliation. It is called 'the children by the soul'
  (farzandan- / i jan)" (p. 126, folio 113). The second is the line through which the attendants at
  Nizam ad-Din's Delhi tomb "trace themselves" (p. 126, folio 113).
- **ʿUrs attendance as a measure of a suborder's cohesion.** Nizami criticises "the fact that those in
  the Nizami line do not all / attend the `'ursofNizam[text-layer?] ['urs of Nizam]` ad-Din, whereas
  the Sabiri Chishti shaykhs all attend the 'urs / in Kalyar in the company of their disciples"
  (p. 130, folio 117).
- **An organisation of sajjada-nashins, its date, its patrons and its four aims — the chunk's densest
  institutional passage.** "In 1908 he gathered young / hereditary shrine custodians (sajjadas), and
  some famous patrons such as Azad and / Shibli Nu'mani, to form a group called the Halqa-i Masha'ikh
  (or 'Circle of Mas- / ters'), in order to reform institutional Sufism in India and support its
  authentic / teachings. Their aims were to spread and preserve Sufism, to unite the Sufi shaykhs /
  within one body, to reform customs at 'urs festivals and khanaqahs so as to eliminate / practices
  outside of the shari'a and tariqa, and to protect the political rights of Sufi / shaykhs" (p. 127,
  folio 114).
- **The cost of that reform programme, up to violence.** "it con- / tained enough similarity to the
  reform program of the Deoband school that Nizami / faced criticism and opposition. Those who feared
  any talk of unnecessary customs / and corruption at the shrines even declared him an enemy of Islam
  and Sufism. In / 1928 someone shot at him, but killed one of his relatives instead" (p. 127, folio
  114).
- **Colonial surveillance of a Sufi organisation, and its lifting by a named official.** "Hasan Nizami
  also met with pan-Islamic activist circles, and for this he became / the subject of intense
  surveillance and harassment on the part of the British author- / ities. Meetings of the Halqa-i
  Masha'ikh became impossible, and it was only in 1917 / that this surveillance was lifted by the
  commissioner of Delhi, Hayley. One of / Nizami's writings, a guidebook to Delhi, is dedicated to
  Hayley and seems to be de- / signed to assuage British suspicions as to his loyalty" (p. 127, folio
  114).
- **A British officer as patron and murid.** "His earliest patron was a / British general named Dixon,
  who seems to have become one of his first murids" (p. 129, folio 116).
- **The reformist attack on shrine practice itself, and where it ended.** The Deoband move "entailed a
  reevaluation and critique of parts of the Chishti legacy, especially in mat- / ters of practice such
  as tomb pilgrimage and listening to music" (p. 120, folio 107); with the Tablighi Jama'at
  "reformism amounted to a sublimation and / simplification of Sufi piety. In the end, the Tablighis
  rejected institutional Sufism al- / together" (p. 120, folio 107).
- **Defence of zikr and sama' against public criticism.** "Shah / Kalim Allah was sensitive to public
  criticism of Chishti practices of zikr and sama', / but he defended their practice with tempered
  arguments" (p. 122, folio 109). The two are named as the order's distinguishing practices: "there
  are particular practices of / sama' and zikr that characterize the Chishtiyya" (p. 121, folio 108).
- **A shrine built by overseas remittance, and where it stands.** "Then a successful disciple from
  South / Africa committed funds to expand the tomb into the structure that now sits on Tara- / garb
  Hill, on the climb up to the disputed tomb of Miran Husayn Khing Sawar" (p. 125, folio 112).
- **Colonial infrastructure as the enabling condition of shrine networking.** "because of the dispersal
  of labor / and the immigration of Indians to other parts of the British empire, Chishti mis- /
  sionary activity (tabligh) extended within and beyond India" (p. 124, folio 111); "the increased
  travel and communica- / tion, fostered by the colonial infrastructure made possible a new degree of
  geograph- / ical extension and networking of the Chishtiyya" (p. 125, folio 112). South African
  Chishti practice "connected back to the center of Chishti Sufism in India, the / shrine of Mu'in
  ad-Din Chishti in Ajmer" (p. 125, folio 112).
- **Precedence disputes at a major shrine, dated and named.** "One of the major disputes in early 1997
  concerned two visitors who were not / given special treatment: Shaykh Husaina Wajid, prime minister
  of Bangladesh, and / Shaykh 'AbdAllah, head of state of Kashmir. Both wanted private time at the
  saint's / tomb, but other devotees did not want to absent themselves from the shrines for the /
  convenience of these public figures" (p. 125, folio 112).
- **Print capitalism named as the transforming technology of the order.** Hasan Nizami "began to
  employ new forms `ofliterary[text-layer?] [of literary]` communi- / cation adapted to the mass
  audience of print capitalism" (p. 119, folio 106). The Delhi shrine becomes "the cen- / ter of a
  network established through print media, with the Urdu periodical Munadi / [The Crier] serving to
  highlight the achievements of Nizamiyya Chishtis of all peri- / ods" (p. 126, folio 113); the same
  periodical carried "a special / issue … dedicated to Chishtis in South Africa" (p. 125, folio 112),
  and a critic recalls "He also edited a weekly, / Munadi, which contained his personal diary and
  other writings" (p. 129, folio 116).
- **The cash economy of a Sufi writer, stated bluntly by a hostile critic.** Muhammad Sadiq "ranks him
  with Rashid ul-Khairi (1868-1936) as one of those / rare Urdu `WTiters[text-layer?] [writers]` who
  has been able to live off the income from his writings. Sadiq / claims that Nizami rose from being
  an itinerant bookseller to being a millionaire" (p. 129, folio 116), and that some of the hundred-odd
  books "were written by hacks em- / ployed for the purpose. These he corrected and improved and gave
  out as his own" (p. 129, folio 116).
- **Book prices and sales recorded inside the books.** "a long section discusses his various writings
  in chronologi- / cal order, why he composed them, how well they have sold, and their current prices
  / and availability" (p. 130, folio 117); "In addition to adver- / tisements at the back of books,
  there are references to books within the text" (p. 130, folio 117).
- **Initiation by post, and a disciple count with a date.** "many of his dis- / ciples have never met
  him and have pledged their allegiance by post (he is said to have / initiated 60,000 disciples by
  1919)" (p. 130, folio 117). In 1951 he addressed his "hundreds of thousands of disciples" and
  "hundreds of successors" (p. 128, folio 115).
- **Ritual permission granted in print.** "He gave / his readers permission in print to perform Sufi
  practices such as the recitation of the / famous talismanic Arabic prayer Hizb al-Bahr" (p. 130,
  folio 117).
- **A forty-day retreat as the occasion of a book.** "his work 'The Education of the Wife' is said to
  have been composed / during a 40-day spiritual retreat" (p. 129, folio 116), composed as "20 lessons"
  covering "religious, family and / economic matters, but ending with topics of contemporary political
  urgency such as / British rule, the home rule movement" (p. 129, folio 116).
- **Prostration before the pir as a defended practice.** A late statement is "attached to his work on
  the permissibility of performing / prostration before one's spiritual guide" (p. 128, folio 115).
- **Meditation manuals as the continuous institutional core.** "the persistence of meditation manu- /
  als and compilations" is offered as the proof of unbroken spiritual training "throughout all of the
  / three cycles of Chishti history" (p. 126, folio 113).
- **Uwaysi initiation and yoga as the Sabiri institutional signature.** The Sabiris "achieved renown
  through a series of masters whose explo- / sive power frequently was attained by transhistorical
  Uwaysi initiations, initiations / that were also modulated by contact with Indian yoga techniques
  and Sufi meta- / physical doctrine" (p. 119, folio 106).
- **Colonial-era madrasa education as a biographical norm.** Hasan Nizami "was given a traditional
  educa- / tion in the Nizam ad-Din basti and later at Rashid Ahmad's madrasa in Gangoh for / one and
  a half years" (p. 127, folio 114); and the authors note that "in the new secular colleges there was
  increasing interest in the great / Chishti saints of the past, like Mu'in ad-Din Chishti", with
  English-educated Muslims "searching for such scholars and shaykhs who could cooperate with them"
  and newspapers giving "their prominent sup- / port to the objectives of the Halqa-i Masha'ikh"
  (p. 127, folio 114). Nizami himself "supported 'English' education for boys and girls" (p. 129,
  folio 116).
- **No ʿurs at any Pakistani shrine, no langar, no offerings box, no Auqaf, no land grant, no revenue
  figure and no building date appear in this chunk: all (not stated).** The only ʿurs named are those
  of Nizam ad-Din in Delhi and of Kalyar, both in India (p. 130, folio 117); the only building work is
  the Ajmer tomb expansion (p. 125, folio 112) and, obliquely, Kalim Allah's family's part in the Taj
  Mahal (p. 122, folio 109).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **A dream visitation by Nizam ad-Din Awliya', attributed to Hasan Nizami's own autobiography.** He
  describes "finding / his spiritual guide through a dream visitation by Nizam ad-Din Awliya', which
  con- / vinced him to undertake self-reform as well as a trip on foot (partially) to Pakpattan"
  (p. 127, folio 114).
- **A spiritual sign from Baba Farid, given as Hasan Nizami's own belief.** "At the age of 24, of his
  own will and believing he had a spiritual sign from Baba / Farid, he became a murid of Mihr 'Ali
  Shah" (p. 127, folio 114).
- **Miraculous influence as the disciples' chosen register at Jalalpur Sharif.** "the disciples of
  Haydar 'Ali Shah focused their / hagiographical accounts of him on his charismatic personality and
  miraculous influ- / ence" (p. 125, folio 112) — reported by the authors as Gilmartin's finding about
  the hagiographies, not asserted.
- **A warrior turned into a martyr of another sect over time.** "the disputed tomb of Miran Husayn
  Khing Sawar, a leg- / endary Sunni Muslim warrior who was turned into a Shi'i martyr over the course
  of / time" (p. 125, folio 112).
- **Transhistorical (Uwaysi) initiation, reported as the Sabiris' own claim.** "masters whose explo- /
  sive power frequently was attained by transhistorical Uwaysi initiations" (p. 119, folio 106).
- **Napoleon's epigrams read as Sufi sayings.** "The French / Dervish [Fransisi Darvish], excerpts
  anecdotes from a life of Napoleon Bonaparte that / had been translated into Urdu, treating
  Napoleon's epigrams as Sufi malfuzat and / proof of his spiritual wisdom and insights" (p. 129,
  folio 116).
- **A modern saint's identification with living statesmen, in his own words.** Following Rumi's verses
  on the mineral, vegetal, human and angelic realms, Nizami portrays his identification "with
  successive / spheres of existence, concluding with the experiences of various prophets, as well as /
  contemporary figures such as Kaiser Wilhelm, Hindenburg, King George, and Lloyd / George. 'This
  month I can be seen and heard in the voices of Sir Hamilton Grant and / General 'Ali Ahmad Khan,' he
  once observed" (p. 130, folio 117).
- **A parable of the Hindu under God's protection, from Nizami's Nizami Bansuri.** "in / a story in
  which a coarse Muslim shopkeeper claims that the Hindu is from a sub- / servient religious class and
  under his protection. The saint later corrects him, saying / that all are under the protection of
  God" (p. 128, folio 115).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The opening claim is epistemological, about the historian and not the saint.** "We remain
  present-minded, even when we resist present- / mindedness. We investigate the past as a social datum
  filtered through our own / present. We enjoy no secure frames of reference; we possess no
  uncontestable or in- / controvertible facts" (p. 119, folio 106).
- **Three nation-states are named as the unacknowledged filters of the scholarship.** The texts "are
  shaped, sometimes explicitly, more often unconsciously, by the existence of / three modern
  nation-states: Bangladesh, India and Pakistan. Each has its own ideol- / ogy of survival and filters
  the past according to priorities matching that ideology" (p. 119, folio 106). The two national
  biases are then named specifically: "Pakistanis, for instance, channel much scholarship into proving
  either the inevitabil- / ity or the avoidability of Pakistan, while Indian scholars frequently see
  only the link / to Hindu bhakti devotion as confirming Sufis, especially Chishtis, in a variant mode
  / of specifically Indic spirituality", with "an underlying partisanship fa- / voring one Sufi group
  (e.g., Naqshbandis over all for many Pakistanis) or one sub- / group over other subgroups (Nizami
  over Sabiri Chishtis for many Indian scholars)" (p. 119, folio 106).
- **Western scholarship is charged with a functionalist prejudgment.** "There is, for instance, a
  schol- / arly approach that concentrates on showing the limits of all South Asian Sufism, be- / cause
  it is tied either to political developments or material limits … they depend for their va- / lidity
  upon functionalist assumptions about the relationship between religion and / politics, a
  relationship in which the overriding importance of political legitimation / reduces other aspects of
  religion to a secondary position" (p. 119, folio 106).
- **The method: take the historiography from the subjects.** "We would like to highlight a different
  aspect of the recent history of South Asian / Sufism, taking as our point of departure a
  historiography developed by our subjects / themselves. The narratives by which Chishtis explained
  their own history in the pe- / riod of British dominance distinguish two principal sublineages, the
  Nizamiyya and / the Sabiriyya" (p. 119, folio 106).
- **Nizami and Sabiri, characterised in one paragraph each.** The Nizamis "came to prominence in the
  eighteenth century, principally in / Delhi, the Deccan, and Punjab, through masters who were related
  to but in tension / with local political leaders", combining "a material and political base" with
  "spiritual training in the / meditative techniques of the Chishti tradition" (p. 119, folio 106); the
  Sabiris "vaulted from obscure origins to prominence in / the Mughal period" and after 1857 "took the
  lead in reasserting Islamic scriptural and legal norms, / particularly through the foundation of the
  Deoband seminary" (pp. 119–120, folios 106–107).
- **The Chishtis' own explanation is contrasted with the authors'.** "Thus the Chishti's own perception
  of the difference between their suborders / rests on the spiritual character of pivotal figures,
  while our approach stresses the / complexity of historical contexts and institutional structures"
  (p. 120, folio 107).
- **Lineage redefined as a resource, not a chart.** "What is required, above all, is attention to
  lineages as more than family / trees; they also become resources for mobilization and engagement in
  the present" (p. 120, folio 107).
- **The polemic against the "decline and fall" model, which is the chunk's governing argument.** The
  devaluing view holds that "latter-day Sufism is a sim- / ulacrum, a pale imitation, of the previous
  days of glory" and that "late Mughal/early modern Indian spiritual / masters reflect a historical
  devolution, marked by institutional sclerosis, in which / the high-water mark of Sufism actually
  precedes the rise of the Sufi orders (turuq) / in the twelfth century" (p. 120, folio 107); its
  successors are said to have "thought narrowly in terms of securing local / terrain, material gain,
  and political access" (pp. 120–121, folios 107–108). Against it: "While there is a neat symmetry and
  / progression to this view of Islamic spirituality, it reduces Sufism to a process of in- / escapable
  decline that ignores the agency of individual masters and also the persis- / tence of ideals that
  link one period to the other" (p. 121, folio 108).
- **The book's five-part periodization, stated as a deliberate counter-design.** "Our own five-part
  periodization of / the history of the Chishtis, with two archaic pre-Indian periods and three cycles
  in / India, is deliberately formulated to avoid the 'decline and fall' model and illustrate / the
  continuities in changing contexts" (p. 121, folio 108). The third cycle is dated
  "(eighteenth-twenty-first cen- / turies)" (p. 121, folio 108) and the second "(fourteenth-eighteenth
  / centuries)" (p. 123, folio 110); Kalim Allah "remains the pivotal figure who inaugurates the third
  / cycle of the Indian Chishtiyya" (p. 123, folio 110).
- **The four shared ideals, which the chapter then tracks one by one.** "it shares with other orders
  the fol- / lowing four ideals: the primacy of mystical insight ( 'irfan), the invocation of a sacred
  / lineage (`nasi[text-layer?]`), the recollection of the model behavior of the Prophet Muhammad /
  (sunna), and the advocacy of Muslim learning ( 'ilm)" (p. 121, folio 108).
- **Modernity as reconfiguration of one ideal rather than loss of all.** "While devotion to the example
  of the Prophet (sunna) may not have wavered, / this period has definitely seen the reconfiguration
  of the other ideal of the Chishtis, / Muslim learning ('ilm), through new concepts of education and
  communication" (p. 126, folio 113).
- **A direct claim that the Mughal and the Chishti trajectories do not track each other.** "While
  politics is never separate from religion, the shift in political fortunes for the / Mughals did not
  mirror or cause the shift in fortunes of the Nizamiyya Chishtiyya" (p. 122, folio 109); the decline
  narrative after Aurangzeb's death "in 1707" and the sackings of Delhi by "Nadir Shah (1738-39) and
  Ahmad Shah Durrani (1757)" is one "often linked with a moral and religious decline among the Muslims
  of / North India", which the authors decline to draw "so starkly" (p. 122, folio 109). "It is only in
  / modern times that the trope of decline has become pervasive" (p. 122, folio 109).
- **Empire as hostile to spiritual creativity.** "As we saw with / Akbar, a powerful empire centered on
  a charismatic ruler had little interest in foster- / ing spiritual creativity on the part of others"
  (p. 123, folio 110).
- **The authors turn their own parallel against themselves — the chunk's sharpest methodological
  move.** Having set out the five-master parallel between the thirteenth–fourteenth and the
  eighteenth–nineteenth centuries (p. 124, folio 111), they write: "The difficulty with this evident
  similarity over a five-century span is that it is / too neat. It derives from a revision of the
  master account of the Chishtiyya, called / Takmila-i siyar al-awliya'" (p. 124, folio 111), and
  ascribe the shaping to its author: "What needs to be stressed is the / influence on Gul Muhammad
  Ahmadpuri's account-conscious but more likely / subconscious-of the exemplary masters from the first
  cycle … They offered a model in his subconscious too pervasive to be erased or / modified greatly in
  his own updating of the story of the Nizami Chishtiyya" (p. 124, folio 111).
- **Sources disagree about who mattered, and the authors make that the point.** "even within the
  lineage of Shah Kalim Allah there was disagreement about the / relative importance of particular
  masters" (p. 123, folio 110); the "massive nine- / teenth-century Punjab-based biographical
  collection Khazinat al-asfiya" gives Yahya Madani, Kalim Allah and Nizam ad-Din Awrangabadi "hardly
  … more than cursory treatment" (p. 123, folio 110). The conclusion: "for Sufi masters, as for their
  sublineages, it is / historical context, not abstract dyads, that is determinative. Judgments of
  greatness / and decline interfere with our ability to grasp the detailed significance and character
  / of either individual Sufi masters or their family traditions" (p. 123, folio 110).
- **Hasan Nizami read as a colonial-modern religious subject.** "In all these gestures / Hasan Nizami
  was responding to the atmosphere of inter-religious controversy and / polemic fostered by the
  colonial government with its support of Christian mission- / aries, and to this extent his concept of
  religious identity absorbed the modern Euro- / pean paradigms" (p. 128, folio 115). "Nizami was
  unusual in his appeal to secular virtues of the colonial realm, such as / general education and
  public morality" (p. 128, folio 115). "Although Hasan Nizami's religious views place him squarely in
  the colonial pe- / riod, it is probably in his prolific writing and publishing that he best
  exemplifies the / spirit of the age" (p. 128, folio 115).
- **Hermansen's study is named as the authors' source for the Delhi shrine argument.** "we / draw upon
  Marcia Hermansen's detailed and insightful study of the Chishti leaders / who made the central urban
  location of this shrine into the linchpin for Nizamiyya / Chishti identity, extending beyond the
  tumult of Independence into the dawn of the / twenty-first century" (p. 126, folio 113), including
  Nizami's double role "as both a generic Indian holy man (sadhu) and an inclusive Sufi pir / (with
  non-Muslim disciples, including Baha'is and Jews)" (p. 126, folio 113).
- **The "children by bread / children by the soul" distinction is presented as a gap in an existing
  book.** "The book includes an introduction by Khwaja Hasan Sani / Nizami, but what is omitted, and
  needs clarification, is the concept of two lines of ge- / nealogical influence" (p. 126, folio 113);
  the authors add an aside on their own uncertainty: "though the Am- / roha connection needs to be
  traced more carefully" (p. 126, folio 113).

## Doubts

- **Muhammad 'Aqil is dated twice, differently, and both are kept.** "Muhammad 'Aqil (d. 1814)"
  (p. 121, folio 108) against "his master was Muhammad 'Aqil (d. 1813)" (p. 124, folio 111). The book
  does not acknowledge the discrepancy. **Not reconciled (RULE 2).** He is not an archive row, so the
  contradiction affects only the Taunsa row's succession narrative.
- **The hijri year 1142 is attached to two successive masters with different common-era years.** "Kalim
