## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Deathbed liquidation of a khanqah's goods, and the reason given for it.** Nizam ad-Din to his
  servant Iqbal: "If anything of any sort remains in this house, it will have to be accounted for on
  the Day of Judgment. You must distribute everything, except the minimum that is nec-essary for the
  daily subsistence of the dervishes." He then withdrew even that exception — "These are the effects
  of a dead man-why should they be preserved? Give it all away and sweep the room clean." (p. 175,
  folio 163). The outcome is reported without comment: "As soon as they cleared the storerooms, a
  host of people gathered and snatched up the goods." (p. 175, folio 163).
- **The tomb as the successor institution's income, stated in one sentence.** The servants ask "We
  are poor men. After you have gone, what will become of us?" and are told: "The charity that will
  arrive at my grave will suffice for you" (p. 175, folio 163). The distribution rule is given too:
  asked who will divide it, he answers "That man who is willing to relinquish his own portion"
  (p. 175, folio 163). **This is the chunk's central economics-of-shrines passage** — grave-offering
  income is anticipated by the saint himself and made the endowment of his dependents.
- **Khirqa economics: four successorship cloaks against an unnumbered many of blessing.** "Of the
  cloaks that this humble person has bestowed … only four have been given with the intention of
  conferring spiritual successorship; the others have been cloaks of blessing" (p. 176, folio 164).
  The chunk does **not** name the four: **(not stated)**.
- **Rules on the master's cloak and relics as inheritable property.** "you must not give away the
  cloak you receive from your Shaykh. Though it is permissible to wash it, it is better not to wash
  it. Concerning the blessed objects that the disciple has received from the master, if he decides
  to have them buried with himself, that is proper. If, on the other hand, he decrees that they are
  to be given to some worthy descendant, that also is proper" (p. 176, folio 164). Burial and
  hereditary transfer are set out as the two licit destinations — directly relevant to how shrine
  relics enter a sajjada-nashin family.
- **Exclusive allegiance as a house rule of one khanqah.** "In this hospice such a thing does not
  occur: One's allegiance remains to the first spiritual master, even if he should be a very
  ordinary person" (p. 176, folio 164). The asymmetry is then stated: "The retention of discipleship
  rests with the disciple, not with the Shaykh." (p. 176, folio 164).
- **Two rulings on samaʿ, given a generation apart and not identical.** Nizam ad-Din reports a saint's
  ruling that "Sarna'`[text-layer?] [Sama']` is neither absolutely permitted nor absolutely
  forbidden" and that "sama', with instruments, is prohibited" (pp. 175–176, folios 163–164), and in
  practice "allowed neither musical instruments nor hand-clapping in his assembly" (p. 178, folio
  166). Nasir ad-Din goes further in method though not in outcome — he walked out of a gathering
  where "a woman singing and playing a tune on a drum" was heard, saying "This activity is contrary
  to Prophetic practice [sunna].''`[text-layer?] [.\"]`" and demanding "some supporting evidence
  from Qur'an or hadith" (p. 178, folio 166); his formal answer is "Musical instruments are not
  permitted in Sufi as-semblies … As to sama' itself, there is a controversy. In the opinion of the
  'ulama, under certain conditions it is permissible to its devotees; but using musical instruments
  in Sufi assemblies is absolutely forbidden" (p. 179, folio 167). Instruments named in the question
  put to him: "the drum or reed-pipe or rebeck" (p. 178, folio 166). Nizam ad-Din backed his
  successor's walk-out — "Nasir ad-Din has spoken the truth" (p. 178, folio 166).
- **Ecstatic dance recorded despite the instrument ban.** At a Sufi assembly Nasir ad-Din "was so
  moved by the following quatrain that he became manifestly enraptured and began to dance" (p. 179,
  folio 167); on another occasion a couplet left him "overcome with ecstasy" (p. 179, folio 167).
  The term khanqah appears here for the shaykh's establishment (p. 179, folio 167).
- **A saint's smallholding as his whole livelihood.** Hamid ad-Din Nagauri "owned a piece of land
  that was two rope lengths in area. He tilled and planted it with his own hands, and from this he
  made a living." (p. 184, folio 172). Set directly against the wealthy shaykhs of his letter to
  Baha ad-Din Zakariyya on the same page.
- **Royal gifts as traps, and a technique for neutralising them.** Muhammad ibn Tughluq "sent a gift
  of food in gold pots" intending "only to cause trouble" — refusal or acceptance both punishable;
  the Shaykh "took a little portion from the golden dish, placed it on his palm and then ate from
  his own hand" (p. 178, folio 166).
- **The Chishti rule of not writing books, and the counter-case.** Nizam ad-Din: "I have not written
  any book because neither Shaykh al-Islarn`[text-layer?] [al-Islam]` Farid ad-Din nor Shaykh
  al-Islam Qutb ad-Din nor any of the Chishti saints before them wrote any book." (p. 178, folio
  166). The editors' head-note makes Hamid ad-Din Nagauri "a counterexample because, unlike his
  esteemed predecessor Mu'in ad-Din, and his co-khalifa Qutb ad-Din, Hamid ad-Din wrote, and he
  wrote extensively." (p. 183, folio 171).
- **Manuscript transmission inside the order.** "Shaykh Hamid ad-Din made a summary of his writings,
  Usul at-tariqa, which he copied in his own hand. The author of Siyar al-Awliya' extracted his own
  information about the teachings of the Shaykh from a copy of the Usul at-tariqa written by Sultan
  al-masha'ikh (Shaykh Nizam ad-Din)." (p. 184, folio 172). A three-hand chain of custody, useful for
  this archive's source criticism.
- **Dervish dress explained as amputation-by-substitution.** Cut sleeves stand for cut hands so that
  he "can neither stretch out his hand to beg, nor close it to grasp"; the shirt is cut short and the
  head shaved because "When the dervish enters the Sufi Way, his head should be cut off, because on
  this path, the first step is to risk one's head" (pp. 181–182, folios 169–170).
- **Public prayer for rain, performed and unsuccessful.** "The Shaykh came out to pray for rain. The
  people also prayed to God and offered time-sanctioned invocations, and still no rains came. They
  returned to their homes." (p. 179, folio 167). A rare notice of a saint's public rite **failing**.
- **Pastoral charge over a city, and against withdrawal from it.** Nizam ad-Din's answer to Nasir
  ad-Din's request for a hermitage: "You ought to remain among the people, take upon yourself the
  burden of their hardships and calamities, and give them in return as much as you can provide."
  (p. 177, folio 165).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Miracles deliberately demoted, in Nizam ad-Din's own reported words** — "we find a hundred stages
  of spir-itual advancement. Ninety-seven of them pertain to disclosing secrets and perform-ing
  miracles. If the traveler remains content with these 97, how can he reach the last 3? One ought to
  see saintly miracles in their proper perspective" (p. 176, folio 164). Attributed to Nizam ad-Din
  as a saying.
- **Nasir ad-Din's refusal to rely on karamat** — "He also said that one must suffer the anxiety of
  faith and not rely on miracles." (p. 179, folio 167), placed immediately before the failed
  rain-prayer.
- **Understanding the speech of a crow**, told by Nasir ad-Din of his own youth and reported by Gisu
  Daraz — "In (the courtyard of) the mosque there was a dry shrub on which sat a crow, and whatever
  that crow said (in his cawing) I understood" (p. 180, folio 168).
- **A death following the Shaykh's dismissal** — Maulana Mughis, summoned and told "record all the
  foolishness you see here", "never returned to the khanqah (and died shortly thereafter.)" (p. 179,
  folio 167). The book reports it in parentheses and draws no causal moral.
- **The gold-vessel deliverance** — "Thus did he frustrate the Sultan's evil intentions." (p. 178,
  folio 166). Reported with "It is recorded that".
- **Forty days without food before death, and doubled prayers** — "For 40 days before his death Nizam
  ad-Din ate nothing … He would perform every prayer twice and add, 'I am going, I am going.'"
  (p. 175, folio 163).
- **Hamid ad-Din's claim of primacy among Indian Muslims** — "'I was the first child who was born
  into a Muslim family after the conquest of Delhi'" (p. 184, folio 172), and "He was among the
  earliest saints of India" (p. 184, folio 172). Given as his own old-age saying.
- **The wish-granting assembly at which a title was conferred** — Mu'in ad-Din: "Everyone who wishes
  something let him speak and the gates of Divine favor will be opened"; Hamid ad-Din's reply,
  "'Wishing' is not for the creature", wins him the title "The one who leaves this world and also
  dispenses with the world beyond is the King of those who leave (everything except God)" (p. 184,
  folio 172).
- **An answered prayer substituting for a shaykh's unanswered letter** — "This humble man wept before
  God … God accepted my prayer, and His answer appeared in the following manner" (p. 184, folio 172).
- **Sufis arraigned among thieves at the Judgment** — "'You have worn the clothes of men, but you
  have not done a man's work: In the end through the intercession of their spiritual masters they
  will be saved" (p. 176, folio 164). Intercession by the pir is stated as the mechanism of rescue.
- **Abu Hurayra seeing "my Lord" in the street of Medina** — "'I saw my Lord walking down the main
  street of Medina. He had a reddish nimbus about his head, and upon his feet were fine shoes!'",

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The Appendix's bracketed head-notes are the authors' editorial voice, and they periodise the
  order.** Nasir ad-Din "brings the experience of his masters to completion, while also reformulating
  that experience in terms that are characteristically his own" (p. 177, folio 165); Hamid ad-Din's
  writings "expound for the first time in prose what is elsewhere evident only in fragmentary maxims,
  or topically diffuse conversations" and "charts the Chishti view toward worldliness, which is at
  the heart of Chishti practice till the present day" (p. 183, folio 171). The triad "Farid ad-Din,
  Nizam ad-Din, and Nasir ad-Din … complete the first cycle of the Chishtiyya" (p. 183, folio 171).
- **A five-master canon is implied, not stated.** Nasir ad-Din is "less renowned than his four
  predecessors" (p. 177, folio 165), which with the part-heading makes five MAJOR CHISHTI MASTERS.
  The book does not enumerate the five in this chunk: **(not stated)**.
- **An explicit thesis about regional expansion, with a source-criticism caveat attached.** "Regional
  masters were also important for the development of the Chishti order. Four particular examples
  stand out … They are also distinctive in terms of the biographical process that developed around
  them. In the cases of Shaykh Hamid ad-Din and Shaykh Nur ad-Din, Akhbar al-akhyar remains an
  authoritative source, but for Shaykh Burhan ad-Din and Shaykh Kamal ad-Din, we can gauge the
  profile of their lives and the importance of their legacies only by consulting additional sources."
  (p. 183, folio 171). Ernst and Lawrence grade their own sources by figure.
- **A fourteenth-century saint performing source criticism on a malfuzat, reported approvingly.**
  Nasir ad-Din on the sayings ascribed to Usman Haruni: "Moreover these are not his sayings. I have
  also seen a copy of this work, in which many things are said that are not in keeping with the
  character of the saint." (p. 178, folio 166) — a pseudepigraphy judgement made on internal
  evidence, and directly usable for this archive's handling of malfuzat.
- **A graded theory of spiritual regression with seven named stages.** "(1) Turning away, (2)
  Veiling, (3) Multiplying the veil, (4) Wasting the excess, (5) Wasting the basis, (6) Hardening,
  and (7) Hatred" (p. 175, folio 163), expounded through the lover-beloved analogy at pp. 175–176,
  folios 163–164, including the confiscation of "excess merit that has accrued to him from all his
  supererogatory devotions" (p. 175, folio 163).
- **The 'Awarif al-ma'arif triad glossed at length.** "The beginner is master of the moment, the
  intermediate is master of spiritual states, and the consummate one is master of the (heart's)
  breath" (p. 180, folio 168), expounded through p. 180, folio 168 and closed with "divine gifts are
  a result of human effort" (p. 180, folio 168).
- **An explicit comparison of Sufi breath-counting with yogic practice.** "one interpretation of the
  statement 'The consummate one is he who is the master of the breath' is that he counts breaths, and
  the yogins, though they call the breaths siddhis, engage in the same practice of counting" (p. 181,
  folio 169). The chunk's only Indic-religions comparison and a notable one for an archive covering
  both Muslim and Hindu sites.
- **A grammatical argument doing theological work.** On `fi` versus `li` in Q 9.60: "In the
  preposition fi there is an intensity of unity that does not exist in li. Pi`[text-layer?] [Fi]` has
  a connotation of'capac-ity`[text-layer?] [of 'capacity]` for containing" — because manumission "is
  like giving life to a dead man", the Qur'an uses `fi` of slaves and `li` of other alms (p. 182,
  folio 170).
- **Struggle for reward versus struggle in love, ranked.** "either because of the hope of heaven and
  the fear of hell or for the love of God. In the former instance, it is a struggle towards God; in
  the latter a struggle in God. The latter is more demanding than the former, but also more complete"
  (p. 182, folio 170), citing "[Q 22. 78]`[text-layer?] [Q 22.78]`".
- **Two readings of the Mi'raj hadith, the second demoting "Lord" to "master".** "that by 'the most
  beautiful', the Prophet was referring to himself" (p. 182, folio 170); "The second possible
  explanation is that by 'my Lord' was meant 'my master'" (p. 183, folio 171).
- **Hamid ad-Din's threefold classification of the elect**, keyed to Q 35.32: "[1] the wretched
  [ ma'dhuran], [2] the grateful [mashkuran], and [3] the vanishing ones [faniyan]" (p. 185, folio
  173), with Abu Bakr, 'Ali Murtada, Uways Qarani and Salman the Persian as the examples of the third
  (p. 185, folio 173).
- **Sainthood defined as negation, and the negation declared unnegatable.** "our kingdom is
  sainthood, and sainthood is a matter of negation rather than affirmation. With respect to
  affirmation, negation is possible, but there can be no negation of negation." (p. 186, folio 174).
- **Secrecy as the condition of spiritual wealth, in an explicitly economic image.** "The breasts of
  Sufis are treasure houses of secrets: As long as a treasure is hidden, its stores remain full, but
  when its stores begin to flow out through a breach, the treasure becomes ruined." (p. 186, folio
  174).
- **A dialectic of seeking resolved by refusing both horns.** "Do not seek and do not give up
  seeking. For God is not in any di-rection toward which you might proceed … He is not immanent, so
  that you might aspire to reach Him by prayers and tears. Nor is he far so that you might hope to
  become near to Him." (p. 186, folio 174), the affirmation being "You should deny yourself and your
  own attributes." (p. 186, folio 174).

## Doubts

- **The `shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya` mapping rests on a bare name and should be treated as
  provisional.** The book writes only "Shaykh Baha ad-Din Zakariyya" (p. 184, folio 172) and gives
  him **no place, no date, no tomb and no order**; Multan is not named anywhere in pp. 175–186. The
  archive row names its principal figure "Bahauddin Zakariya" of Multan, d. 1267, Suhrawardi — none
  of which the chunk supplies. The 18 September 2026 ruling puts the id on the row even for a
  reference-only mention, so it is recorded, but **the identification is mine, not the book's**. No
  second archive row competes: `shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya` is the only Baha ad-Din Zakariyya in
  shrine_index.tsv, so the doubt is whether the book's figure is the archive's, not which row.
- **The `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` mapping is better evidenced but still placeless.** The
  epithet "Ganj-i Shakar" is printed twice (p. 177, folio 165; p. 185, folio 173) and matches the
  row's "Baba Fariduddin Mas'ud Ganjshakar", so the person is secure. **But the chunk gives no
  Pakpattan, no tomb, no death year and no ʿurs**: nothing about the *shrine* can enter an entry from
  here, only facts about the man and his relics. Again only one row is a candidate.
- **Ajodhan, held reversibly under the interim bare-toponym convention.** "I met in Ajodhan a scholar"
  (p. 177, folio 165) attaches no shrine, tomb, ʿurs or custodian to the name, so it takes no id and
  sits under "Other saints, sites and events". **If the bare-toponym ruling reverses, the row it
  would take is `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar`**, since the sentence places Nizam ad-Din there
  while "in the service of my own noble Shaykh, Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar" (p. 177, folio 165).
  **The book does not say Ajodhan is Pakpattan, does not say it is in Pakistan, and does not say
  Farid ad-Din's khanqah stood there** — that equation is general knowledge and is deliberately left
  out (RULE 2). A human should decide whether the equation may be made at consolidation.
- **Qutb ad-Din: the standing near-miss, re-confirmed as a near-miss.** The chunk names "Shaykh
  al-Islam Qutb ad-Din" (p. 178, folio 166) and "Khwaja Qutb ad-Din" (pp. 183–184, folios 171–172) as
  a Chishti master and Mu'in ad-Din's khalifa. **He is not `tomb-of-qutbuddin-aibak`**, whose
  principal figure is Sultan Qutb ud-Din Aibak, a ruler. No id was written for him. The book supplies
  neither "Bakhtiyar" nor "Kaki" nor any tomb here, so nothing distinguishes him further:
  **(not stated)**.
- **No Jalal ad-Din Bukhari, no Jamal al-Din Multani and no Hinglaj occurs in this chunk**, so the
  two other standing ambiguities and the empty-id row are not engaged and no id was invented.
- **Whether the archive should hold a Nizam ad-Din row at all is a question this chunk raises but
  cannot answer.** He is the most fully documented figure in pp. 175–186 — an exact death date, a
  burial description, a deathbed estate settlement and a named grave-offering economy — and
  `shrine_index.tsv` has no row for him, his tomb being in Delhi. Recorded under "Other saints" as
  the convention requires; flagged because the material is unusually rich and would be lost if no row
  ever exists.
- **Section-map contradiction, restated for the consolidator.** The run's map puts this whole range
  under `MAJOR CHISHTI MASTERS`; the book prints **`REGIONAL CHISHTI MASTERS`** at p. 183 (folio 171)
  and everything after it belongs to that part. The Appendix boundary itself (p. 159) is not
