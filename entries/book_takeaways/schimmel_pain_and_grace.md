## What this book is

A paired scholarly study of two eighteenth-century Muslim mystical writers — **Khwaja Mir Dard of
Delhi (1721–1785)** and **Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit (1689–1752)** — by a historian of religions, in
English, with the whole of Part II (pp. 151–262, with a concluding comparative run at 263–269)
devoted to Shah Abdul Latif and his *Risalo*. Schimmel's own statement of why the pair belong
together is the book's title: "The title of these studies, 'Pain and Grace', alludes to the names of
the two mystics: Dard, 'Pain', and Abdul Latif, 'The Servant of the Gracious', as we may translate
it" (p. 2, folio xii). Her method is avowedly textual and avowedly restrained — "I have tried to rely
largely upon the poets' own words" (p. 2, folio xii) — and she states her source base as asymmetric
in Latif's favour: "In his case a much greater amount of material in both Sindhi and English was
available for the study of his poetry than is the case with Mir Dard who, strangely enough, has never
attracted a scholar in the West" (p. 2, folio xii).

For this archive the book is overwhelmingly a **Sindh** book, and its centre of gravity is a single
row. Sixteen archive ids are attached; `bhit-bhit-shah` carries 133 of the bullets and the other
fifteen share the rest. Its value is **not** shrine ethnography: Schimmel visited Bhit Shah and
describes the mausoleum, its tiles, its ʿurs month and its Thursday-night singing in one paragraph
(p. 151, folio not stated), and that paragraph is nearly the whole of the book's physical shrine
material for any site. What it supplies instead is (i) the textual and doctrinal substance behind
three Sindhi shrine figures — Shah Abdul Latif, Shah Abdul Karim of Bulri, Abdur Rahim Girhori —
(ii) a dated political narrative of a *khanqah destroyed by the state* at Jhok in 1717–18, (iii) an
eighteenth-century critique of shrine economy in Dard's and Shah Waliullah's own words ("sellers of
miracles", "shopkeeper saints", p. 21, folio 17), and (iv) two load-bearing **negatives** about
`bhit-bhit-shah`: that the poet is reported to have had no initiatic chain at all, and that the
*Risalo* names no Sufi order and no Sindhi sanctuary whatever (pp. 190–191).

### bhit-bhit-shah — Bhit (Bhit Shah)

The book's whole second part. The running form is **Shah Abdul Latif**; the book also uses "Shah Abdul
Latif of Bhit" (p. 24, folio 20), "Shah Latif", "Shah", "Latif", "Shah Sahib" (p. 153, folio 153),
"the mystic of Bhit" (p. 195, folio 195), and, in Burton's quoted words, "Shah Bhitai" (p. 152,
folio 152). The index prints him as `"Abdul Latif Bhita?i[OCR?], Shah` (p. 300, folio not stated) —
**and gives him no headword of his own in the main alphabetical run**, only the parenthetical
attribution on some twenty-eight `Sur` headwords and the place entry **"Bhit Shah 25, 151, 195"**
(p. 301, folio 301). The index's own map of the treatment is `"Abdul Latif Bhita?i[OCR?], Shah —X, 20,
21, 22, 24, 25-27, 138n, 151-262, 263-69"` (p. 300, folio not stated) — **treat those numerals as
OCR-suspect and do not reproduce the run as clean**.

- **Saint**
  - **Dates — given three times and never varied: "(1689-1752)".** On the part-title leaf,
    `SHAH ABDUL LATIF OF BHIT / (1689-1752)` (p. 150, folio not stated); in the Foreword, "the greatest
    mystical poet of the Lower Indus Valley, Shah Abdul Latif (1689-1752)" (p. 2, folio xii); and in
    the body, "It is Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit (1689-1752) who died ten years before Shah Waliullah left
    this valley of tears" (p. 24, folio 20). **The book states them flatly, with no "not established
    with complete certainty" qualification.** **No day, no month and no hijri date of birth or death
    are given anywhere** — **(not stated)**. The chapter text itself (pp. 151–262) never repeats a
    birth or death year.
  - **A second, independent date for the death: a chronogram on the tomb, and the book does not
    convert it.** "One of the Persian chronograms composed at his death and inscribed at the northern
    side of the dome says: / When he became intoxicated by union from the goblet of 'Come back'
    (Sura 89/17). / The hidden inspirer said: 'The year of his journey was Ridwan-i Haqq,' (God's
    satisfaction) = 1165" (pp. 29–30, folios 25–26), with "Other chronogramms in Qani', Lc."[OCR?]
    [l.c.] "p. 428 f." (p. 30, folio 26, footnote 14). **"1165" is given as the chronogram's value and
    is nowhere equated to 1752 by the book** — see Doubts.
  - **Family and descent — father named, mother's line explicitly uncertain.** "Shah Abdul Latif was
    born into a family of mystics who lived near Hala; his father, Shah Habib, was a descendant of one
    of the first noted poets in his mother tongue, Shah Abdul Karim of Bulrri"[OCR?] [Bulri] (p. 29,
    folio 25). **The father is named only "Shah Habib"; no death date, burial place or shrine for him**
    — **(not stated)**. On the mother: "about his mother's family the traditions are not unanimous.
    Yet, it is said that he (like Mir Dard) was a sayyid, his lineage going back to the seventh
    zmam Misa al-Kazim"[OCR?] [imam Musa al-Kazim] (p. 29, folio 25) — **"it is said that" is the
    book's own hedge**. Elsewhere: "This man from a sayyid and Sufi family" (p. 151, folio not stated)
    and "Both mystical writers come from Sayyid families; hence, they are particularly fond of their
    ancestors, the Prophet and the caliph Ali" (p. 268, folio 268). **"sayyid" is the only affiliation
    term used of him in the entire book.**
  - **Silsila — THE LOAD-BEARING NEGATIVE, and the single most important page in this book for this
    row.** "It is said that Shah Latif was an wwazsi[OCR?] [uwaisi] mystic, e.g., that he had no formal
    spiritual master, and hence did not belong to any of the acknowledged chains of initiation"
    (p. 191, folio 191). **Schimmel reports this as tradition ("It is said"), not as her own finding,
    and immediately qualifies it**: "Yet, he quotes with approval an Arabic saying about the necessity
    of the mystical guide: 'Who walks on the Path without shaikh?' (Dési[OCR?] [Desi] II 3) and
    continues his instructions to Sassui with the famous Sufi saying: 'Who has no shaikh, his shaikh is
    Satan' (id. II 4)" (p. 191, folio 191). **The row's silsila cell is empty; this page is direct
    evidence for why, and should be cited rather than filled.** No pir, no bayʿa and no order are given
    for him anywhere in the book — **(not stated)**.
  - **Sectarian position, reported and not attested.** "he is related to have called himself 'something
    between Sunni and Shii Islam;' that means he continued in the tradition of the
    tash-ayyu' hasan"[OCR?] [tashayyu' hasan] ", the loving attitude towards Muhammad's family"
    (pp. 268–269, folios 268–269). **The technical term is the book's; the poet is not quoted
    directly.**
  - **Three years with wandering Yogis, twice told and hedged the second time.** "Shah Abdul Latif
    spent three years with a group of yogis, wandering through the country, up to the sacred mountain
    Hinglaj in Balochistan" (p. 29, folio 25), footnoted to "M. Jotwani has given as many biographical
    details as are available in his book, 'Shah Abdul Latif', New Delhi 1975" (p. 29, folio 25,
    footnote 13). And later: "Shah Abdul Latif had spent some time—probably three years—in the company
    of wandering Yogis, and it is said that he visited with them many of the places mentioned in the
    Risalo. That may or may not be true—but the deep impression which the Yogis left in his heart
    cannot be denied" (p. 219, folio 219). **"probably three years" is Schimmel's own estimate; the
    itinerary claim is reported as hearsay and left undecided. No dates, no companions** — **(not
    stated)**.
  - **Settlement at Bhit, death, childlessness — all in one sentence.** "Later Latif settled in Bhit,
    where he died without issue in 1752, leaving behind him the collection of thirty Sindhi poems that
    has become known as the Risal6"[OCR?] [Risalo] (p. 29, folio 25). **No foundation of the village
    is described and no year is given for the settlement** — **(not stated)**. **"died without issue"
    is the book's phrase and is the only statement in it bearing on a hereditary succession at this
    shrine.**
  - **The illiteracy claim, named to its source and rejected.** "Mir Ali Shir Qani' claims in his two
    major historical works that the mystical poet was ummi, 'illiterate;' but that is impossible to
    accept when one enters into the complicated web of thoughts displayed in the poems, and decorated
    with Arabic quotations, or allusion to Persian mystical poetry" (p. 29, folio 25). **Schimmel's
    rejection is her own, argued from the text of the poems.** The same claim recurs as legend: "Many
    legends tell that a saint was illiterate and knew only the alif: that is said about the Turkish
    folk poet Yunus Emre, the Panjabi poet Bullhe Shah, and our Abdul Latif as well" (p. 208,
    folio 208).
  - **Musicianship, with an instrument invented.** "Shah Latif was an excellent musician, who even
    invented a new type of tambura (drone instrument), and whose blending of traditional Indian raga
    and Sindhi folk tunes enhances the beauty of his verses which ought to be sung, not analyzed, to
    enjoy their full charm" (p. 29, folio 25). Also: his music, "though indebted to the classical
    Indian tradition, utilizes the folk songs of his home province" (p. 263, folio not stated).
  - **A quarrel with a Kalhoro prince, and its repair — the only event of his life the book gives
    besides the yogi years.** Rumi's Mathnawi "was loved and recited at the Mughal court in Delhi, and
    served the Kalhoro prince to gain back the sympathies of Shah Abdul Latif" (p. 165, folio 165).
    **Schimmel does not name the prince here, give a year, or say what the estrangement was** — all
    **(not stated)**.
  - **A relation to Shah Inayat of Jhok, given as a probability.** "It is possible that Shah Abdul
    Latif alluded to his fate in some of his verses; in any case he has quoted a Persian line usually
    connected with Shah Inayat" (p. 26, folio 22); the line is quoted at "he quotes in Persian a line
    that is usually connected with the fate of Shah Inayat of Jhok (exec. 1718): / The head is
    sacrificed at the Friend's foot, / which is in order (Sohn. II)" (p. 228, folio 228).
  - **Relations to two other archive figures, both stated as relative chronology only** — Girhori is
    "Shah Latif's younger contemporary" (p. 201, folio 201) and "Shah's younger contemporary" (p. 261,
    folio 261), and "A verse by him expresses also his admiration for Shah Abdul Latif, whom he may
    have met when still a child" (p. 28, folio 24); Sachal Sarmast is "his younger contemporary"
    (p. 184, folio 184) and "met him when still a boy" (p. 30, folio 26); Muhammad Zaman Lanwari is
    "his younger compatriot" (p. 208, folio 208).
  - **His unnamed friend and servant.** "A little joke about Shah Latif's friend and servant forms the
    last chapter of this Sur; he, though 'stinking', 'a glutton', 'ugly,'—to mention only a few of his
    epithets—becomes purified in the rose-water-like company of the master" (p. 160, folio 160).

- **Shrine / Darbar**
  - **The burial, located three ways in one sentence.** "Shah Abdul Latif is buried in a beautiful
    mausoleum in Bhit Shah near Hala, north east of Hyderabad/Sind" (p. 151, folio not stated).

  - **An "adjacent graveyard" is named and not described, and nobody buried in it is named** — **(not
    stated)** (p. 151, folio not stated).
  - **A dome carrying Persian chronograms on its northern side** (pp. 29–30, folios 25–26, quoted

  - **A generic Sindhi saint's tomb, described but not named or located** — "the blue and white tiles

- **Practices & Events**
  - **The ʿurs: a month, a crowd that is not counted, and a building put up for it.** "To accomodate
    at least part of the innumerable visitors who flock to Bhit Shah in the days of the "urs[OCR?]
    [ʿurs], when the memory of the mystic's death is celebrated in the month of Safar, a guest house
    has recently been built. A cultural center is being developed" (p. 151, folio not stated).
    **"the month of Safar" is the only calendar anchor; no day, no duration and no visitor figure are
    given — the crowd is only "innumerable"** — **(not stated)**. **The ʿurs runs over "days", not a
    day.** **Who built the guest house and who is developing the cultural centre are both in the
    passive and are (not stated); "recently" and "is being developed" date them only against the
    book's own present (1976).**
  - **The weekly observance: day, hour, performers, place.** "Every Thursday evening, till late at
    night, the few dervishes who still continue the tradition of Shah Abdul Latif sing his poetry
    inside the compound of the mausoleum" (p. 151, folio not stated). **Note "the few dervishes who
    still continue the tradition": the book records the practice as surviving and diminished in the
    same breath.** Their number, names, standing and support are all **(not stated)**. **This is the
    book's one statement of living, routine practice at any archive shrine.**
  - **Recitation practice a century and a quarter earlier, from Burton.** "The learned praise it for
    its beauty, and are fond of hearing it recited to the sound of the guitar. Even the unlearned
    generally know select portions by heart, and take the trouble to become acquainted with their
    meaning" (p. 152, folio 152, quoting "R. Burton, 'Sind and the races ...,' p. 203"). **"the sound
    of the guitar" is Burton's word as printed; the Sindhi instrument is not named** — **(not
    stated)**.
  - **The Risalo as sung, with a performance rule attached to one Sur.** "for the Rrsalo"[OCR?]
    [Risalo] "is meant to be sung, not to be theologically and grammatically dissected" (p. 162,
    folio 162); "Sur Yaman Kalyan is a derivation from Kalyan, and should be sung in the evening; it
    is meant to bring peace to the soul" (p. 195, folio 195). Each Sur "is named according to the
    musical mode in which it is sung, and which points to the contents" (p. 154, folio 154).
  - **Instruments named in his verse** — "all veins have become a rabab (Kal. II 6, Ripa I 21)"
    (p. 166, folio 166); "the fiddles of the Yogis, those 'golden instruments'" in "Ramakali (1 19ff)"
    and "the murli, the snake charmer's instrument" (p. 166, folio 166); "Those, whose body is a
    rosary, the soul a bead of the rosary, the heart a tanboura" (p. 194, folio 194).
  - **Transmission by labour rather than by assembly — the book's own account of how the poetry
    circulated.** "They could sing Shah Abdul Latif's poetry when ploughing or going out for fishing,
    picking cotton or spinning it, and thus the central concepts of Islamic mysticism—Love of God and
    the Prophet, and trust in the eternal divine wisdom—became part and parcel of their daily lives"
    (p. 269, folio 269). **Four named occupations and a mechanism that is neither shrine nor book.**
  - **EXPLICIT NEGATIVES for this row, stated across the whole book: no mela, no langar, no offering
    or nazrana, no sajjada nashin or gaddi nashin, no custodian or caretaker family, no Auqaf or any

- **Legends & Miracles**
  - **The existence of a legendary corpus is asserted and not one legend is given.** "Contrary to Mir
    Dard, Shah Latif did not leave any notes about his life or the development of his mystical
    theories; besides a few dry facts we know about him merely some legends of more or less authentic
    character. But his poetry is enough to make us acquainted with his personality" (p. 151, folio not
    stated). **No karama of Shah Abdul Latif is told anywhere in this book** — **(not stated)**.
  - The one legend attached to him by name is the illiterate-saint legend, shared with Yunus Emre and
    Bullhe Shah (p. 208, folio 208, quoted above), which Schimmel reports and does not endorse.
- **Significance**
  - **The book's superlatives, three of them.** "the greatest mystical poet of the Lower Indus Valley"
    (p. 2, folio xii); "the greatest mystical poet in the Sindhi language" (p. 24, folio 20); "Shah
    Abdul Latif's poetry is the first apex of a long literary development, the beginnings of which we
    cannot properly distinguish" (p. 161, folio 161).
  - **The reception history, and why this row must be handled carefully — the most citable statement
    in the book about the political appropriation of a shrine saint.** A leading Pakistani scholar,
    quoted at length: "Shah was the crown of the Sufis, Shah was a folk poet, Shah was the master of
    ragas, Shah was a patriot, Shah was a congressman, Shah was a Muslim Leaguer, Shah was Rumi, Shah
    was Goethe, Shah has produced Hir, Shah has made alive Marui—in short, Shah is the medicine for
    every illness, Shah is the remedy for every pain. That means, the same thing that has been done to
    Ghalib and Iqbal we have done to Shah Sahib" (p. 153, folio 153, footnote 3 citing "HL Rashdi[OCR?]
    [H. … Rashdi], 'Shah kafia j6 mijid na ahe,'[OCR?] in: Mehran jin maupin[OCR?], p. 218.").
    Schimmel's own generalisation of it: "there is always the danger that great poetry will be
    interpreted by every reader according to his own feelings, and also according to certain
    opportunist or even political tendencies; and since contradictions cannot be excluded in the verse
    of a great poet, let alone a religious, or mystically minded, poet, everyone finds in him what he
    has sought" (p. 153, folio 153).
  - **The second load-bearing negative: the Risalo names no order and no sanctuary.** "None of the
    great tarigas[OCR?] [tariqas] is mentioned, nor any of the sanctuaries to which the inhabitants of
    Sind flocked at many occasions" (p. 190, folio not stated). **The subordinate clause is itself
    evidence that flocking to sanctuaries was normal in the poet's Sind: the author asserts the
    practice while denying its presence in the poems.**
  - **The third: he founded no order and preached no community.** "He certainly did not call his
    listeners to rebellion against the outward forces, or to the formation of a closely knit mystical
    community. His mysticism is individualistic, and has as its center only one story: that of the
    return of the loving soul to her Lord" (p. 187, folio 187). **Taken with the uwaisi statement
    (p. 191) and the no-tariqas statement (p. 190), these three passages make one consistent argument:
    the Risalo is not an institutional document.**
  - **The pir in the poetry, and the limit of it.** "All the way through the chapters of the
    Risdlé[OCR?] [Risalo] the importance of the mystical leader is implicitly understood… Only in a
    few places the mystical guide is mentioned overtly as a conditio sine qua non for the seeker, and
    in Sarirag he assumes the figure of the pilot who leads the boat safely through the ocean of this
    world so that its precious cargo cannot be plundered. However, as much as the necessity of the Pir
    is understood, Shah Latif never indulges in deliberations about his veneration or the duties of
    the initiate towards him: only in poetical allusions he introduces his listeners to the mysteries
    of the Path" (p. 187, folio 187). **Against this stands the Satguru passage — "the true mystical
    guide, the Satguru, as Shah Latif calls him, is he 'who has the dla Allah,'[OCR?] [illa Allah]"
    (p. 223, folio 223) — and Schimmel's own note of surprise that "Shah Latif, in this connection,
    does not mention the necessity of a mystical guide" (p. 214, folio 214). The book does not
    reconcile the three.**
  - **The book's answer to the syncretism question, given as its chapter's last sentence.** "We may sum
    up the Islamic contents of the Risalo, which, it scems to me, are undeniably strong, by saying
    that the God of the Rusald"[OCR?] [Risalo] " is a living and loving God; not a philosophical or
    mystical principle, but rather the Creator, the Merciful and Maj-estic, the First and the Last,
    and always the Lord as revealed through the Quran" (p. 262, folio 262). The chapter opens by
    naming the reading it opposes: "The way Shah Abdul Latif unites his descriptions of Sufis and
    Yogis by explaining the wondrous life of the Hindu ascetics with Quranic terminology has induced
    many of his Hindu admirers and interpreters to regard his poetry as a perfect blending of the two
    religious traditions of India" (p. 236, folio not stated).
  - **But the Hindu material is inside the canonical text, and the book says so plainly.** "How much
    Shah Latif has islamized his Yogis becomes clear from verse 32, where he praises the
    Shiva-followers 'whose leader is Ali.'" (p. 221, folio 221); "The application of Quranic words to
    the Hindu ascetics is quite surprising, and worth quoting in full" (p. 224, folio 224); "Shah
    Abdul Latif, as did his Panjabi contemporaries and his successors in Sind, accepted the Hindus who
    were striving on the way to perfection as true pilgrims in the eternal Light" (p. 267, folio 267);
    "Pre-Islamic ideas are natural in literature that draws upon Hindu tales; thus Sassui is sometimes
    called a banbhan (Abri I 10), a 'Brahmin girl,' or is praised because she is becoming a yogini
    (Abri III 10); the heroines also speak of the funeral pyre (thus Sam. I 8)" (p. 164, folio 164).
    **His Hindu and yogic sympathies are placed inside the chapter's argument about conversion:
    "Shah Abdul Latif's admiration for the true Yogis belongs to this group of phenomena, and fits
    very well into the religious situation in the country" (p. 29, folio 25), the "group of phenomena"
    being Hindus who "became devoted disciples of Muslim saints" (p. 28, folio 24).**
  - **His audience straddles both communities, in his own reported verse.** "You are a liar in
    infidelity—don't call yourself an infidel: / You are not at alla[OCR?] [at all a] Hindu, the
    brahmin's thread does not fit you, / The caste-mark is for those, who are true in their paganism
    (Asa IV 14, 15)" (p. 191, folio 191), glossed "whether Muslim or Hindu, monotheist or idol
    worshipper, man has to be sincere and not to make claims to sanctity which he cannot realize"
    (p. 191, folio 191).
  - **The typology that closes the book.** He "stands before us as the representative of that type of
    the rural mystical tradition in which his contemporaries in the Panjab and, to a certain extent,
    in the Pashto speaking areas were rooted" (p. 267, folio 267); the pairing with Dard is "the
    urban, sophisticated, and highly intelligent trend" against "the rural, simple and unassuming
    Sufism" (p. 269, folio 269). And a claim about what his verse does *not* register: "Shah Abdul
    Latif apparently had no problems of this kind" — i.e. no crisis of Muslim self-identification
    (p. 267, folio 267).
  - **Endurance, stated as a duration.** "his poetry would certainly not have attracted the Sindhi
    speaking people for more than two centuries, had he only dwelt upon highly mystical topics"
    (p. 168, folio 168); one verse, "Thou makest the leaf drown in the stream, and makest the stone
    swimming ("Dahar II 19")", has "become almost proverbial in Sindhi" and the lines "have consoled
    many pious souls in the afflictions of life and the years of poverty and misery that came over the
    country time and again, and which form the title of several contemporary short stories" (p. 241,
    folio 241).

## Cross-cutting material

*Merged from `Practices, institutions, economy`, the two `Arguments and interpretations` headings and
the book's bibliography and apparatus. Almost all of the institutional density in this book belongs to
Mir Dard's Naqshbandi house in Delhi, which has no archive row; it is kept here in full because it is
the corpus's best eighteenth-century description of how a saintly household was actually run, and
because the two reformers' attacks on shrine economy are directly usable. Where a chunk states an
explicit negative, that negative is recorded, not padded.*

### The eighteenth century as the book frames it

- **The founding complaint, in the book's first sentence.** "Eighteenth-century Islam is something
  that is usually neglected by the historians of religion. Histories of Sufism in general end with the
  thirteenth, at the best the `"fitteenth"[OCR?]` [fifteenth] century; the follow-ing period is,
  then, shortly classified as decadent." (p. 1, folio not stated) The counter-thesis: "mystical
  thought and poetry flourished during the eighteenth century from Morocco to Islamic India" (p. 1,
  folio not stated), and "Without studying the works of the mystical writers during the pre-modern
  period it will be difficult to appreciate the developments that set in, during the following
  century, for the rejuvenation and reform of Islam" (p. 1, folio not stated).
- **Catastrophe produces religious literature — the book's organising claim, offered as a
  speculation.** "Thus, the 18th century, politically perhaps the most saddening phase of Indo-Muslim
  civilization, proves to be the most fertile period in terms of religious literature—very similar to
  the situatio.. in the 13th century, when the larger part of the Islamic Empire was devastated by the
  Mongol hordes… it is as though a strange balance of power produces such effects" (pp. 30–31,
  folios 26–27). **Applied to Sind explicitly**: "thus in Sind, too, the 18th century can be called
  the most important period for the formation of mystical poetry and prose" (pp. 30–31,
  folios 26–27).
- **Mysticism as the survival mechanism of an afflicted population.** "the mystical way was, for most
  of them, the one source of strength which enabled them to survive during the afflictions which were
  showered upon Northern India in the 18th century" (p. 12, folio 8). And the shrine-side version of
  the same claim: the poetry could be "sung by the large masses who would flock to the shrines of the
  saints, hoping for material and spiritual help in this time of deepest depression" (p. 17, folio 13)
  — **"material and spiritual help" is the book's own pairing.**
- **The two regions the book treats, named and justified in advance.** "two areas can be singled out
  as particularly interesting in the history of mystical poetry in the Subcontinent. They are, as is
  to be expected, the capital Delhi with its long history, and the Lower Indus Valley" (pp. 17–18,
  folios 13–14).
- **Sind as a continuous religious centre since 711.** "the Arabs conquered Sind, the southern part of
  present day Paki-stan, in 711, and this province never ceased to be a center of Islamic studies and
  a seat of Islamic mysticism" (p. 6, folio 2). **No site is attached** — **(not stated)**.
- **Hard numbers on the century's violence.** Nadir Shah "invaded the country and eventually loot-ed
  Delhi in 1739; about 30,000 people were killed. The once splendid city never fully recovered from
  this blow" (p. 7, folio 3); "Delhi was looted once more in 1757, this time by the Afghan 'friends;'"
  and "the third battle of Panipat 1761, Jan. 6" (p. 51, folio 49); and a famine, dated dually, that
  "swept away something like a third to a half of the rural population" of Delhi territory "about
  1196/1782" (p. 88, folio 86). **The quotation's source for the famine figure is not printed on the
  page** — **(not stated)**.

### The reformist attack on shrine economy — the most directly usable material in the book

- **Pilgrimage to tombs declared worse than murder, with the shrines named and the ruling
  self-limited.** Shah Waliullah "would have liked to declare as infidels those who perform
  pilgrimages to sacred places: 'That is a sin, greater than murder and adultery.'" (p. 21, folio 17);
  the footnote gives the full text: "All those who go to Ajmer or to the tomb of Salar Mas'
  ud"[OCR?] "or similar places for something they want from there: that is a sin, greater than murder
  or adultery. Is he not like someone who worships handmade things, or like those who called Allat and
  al-Uzza? But I cannot declare them clearly as infidels because there is no text from the lawgiver in
  this peculiar case." (p. 21, folio 17, footnote 4). **Note the transactional framing — people go
  "for something they want from there" — and the limit the reformer sets on his own ruling.**
- **The commercial vocabulary of the attack, from two reformers independently.** False Sufis are
  "karamat furushan, 'sellers of miracles'" and "the 'highwaymen and robbers of religion,'" in
  Waliullah's words, while "his colleague Mir Dard uses the expression 'shopkeeper saints' for these
  characters, whom he even calls 'pig-natured.'" (p. 21, folio 17) Dard again: "Dervishdom does not
  consist of astrology and geomancy (K 447), but many of the contemporary shaikhs are nothing but
  'religious shopkeepers' (K 445)." (p. 76, folio 74) And: "no shop-keeper-shaikh could explain the
  reality with this correctness" (p. 87, folio 85). **Three market metaphors for religious authority,
  all pejorative, all in the 1770s–80s.**
- **Waliullah's wider indictment, which names the fief economy.** He attacks "the grandees who could
  not care less when wine shops, gambling dens or brothels are installed in the realm of their fiefs;
  soldiers who shave their beards and think only of amusements; artisans who indulge in drinking,
  adul-tery, and visits to allegedly sacred places" (p. 20, folio 16); and "he heavily attacks those
  Sufis who talk about 'what they think is truth,' seduce people by miracle mongering" (p. 20,
  folio 16). **"visits to allegedly sacred places" and "miracle mongering" are the two shrine
  practices condemned; no site is named** — **(not stated)**.
- **All three Delhi reformers refused the name "Sufi".** "All the three alike stressed the importance
  of the unmitigated Divine Law as the center of Muslim life; all the three attacked philosophy and
  fake Sufism, and refused to be called 'Sufis,' a term that had deteriorated during their days even
  more than before" (p. 24, folio 20). **A finding worth carrying into any entry about
  eighteenth-century shrine criticism.** And a stated negative about them: "He never mentions any of
  them by name in his writings, nor do they mention him or his father" (p. 24, folio 20).
- **Sufi books as a restricted good.** Waliullah's zeal "leads him to even regard the Sufi books,
  'which may have a wonderful effect on the elect, to be deadly poison for the ordinary people,'"
  (p. 21, folio 17). **A stated two-tier readership.**
- **Miracles affirmed and ordered hidden.** "Miracles will occur to the wayfarer; they are real and
  true, but / miracles are the menstruation of men (K 449), / for the friends of a king will never
  divulge the secrets he entrusts to them." (p. 76, folio 74)
- **Tashayyukh named as a recognised abuse** — "the dangerous mode of tashayyukh, i.e., to behave like
  a self-made mystical leader which he blames often in others" (p. 63, folio 61).
- **Muharram as a contested observance, in three places at once.** Makhdum Muhammad Hashim "attacks
  the Muharram celebrations, which were celebrated on Makli Hill" (p. 27, folio 23); Mazhar Janjanan
  "was killed when ridiculing a Muharram procession" at the age of 82 in 1781 (p. 22, folio 18); and
  "Earlier in that century, Shah Waliullah too had protested against the celebrations of the Ashura
  Day" (p. 22, folio 18). **A named observance at a named burial hill, a reformer killed at a
  procession, and a third reformer's protest — the same target in Sind and in Delhi.**
- **Reform with princely backing.** Makhdum Hashim fought "with the support of the Kalhora princes
  relentlessly … against the innovations that had spoiled Islam in his days" (p. 26, folio 22).
- **The ban on samaʿ and the jizya, both routed through a Sufi office.** Muhammad Maʿsum "is
  considered to be responsible for Aurangzeb's re-imposure of the jizya on the Hindus and to have been
  instrumental in forbidding music and samd'"[OCR?] [sama'] (p. 36, folio 34). **State regulation of
  shrine practice and of a discriminatory tax, both attributed to a mystical office-holder's
  influence.** Restated: "During Aurangzeb's reign, which lasted nearly half a century (1658-1707),
  music and fine arts were restricted" (p. 11, folio 7).

### Shrine practice actually observed or asserted

- **Votive cloth on trees at Sindhi shrines — the book's single most usable institutional statement,
  and it is asserted as current.** "We watch the wife of the seafaring merchant who binds pieces of
  cloth on sacred trees and pours fragrant otto and oil on the waves of the Indus to secure her
  husband's safe return (Sam. V 2): still in our day the trees near Sindhi shrines are decorated with
  many pieces of cloth, preferably red, in the shape of little flags or tiny cradles, which are
  attached there for the sake of a vow, as it is usual almost everywhere in the Islamic world"
  (p. 168, folio 168). **A conditional vow, a physical ex-voto with its colour and two named shapes, a
  river offering of "otto and oil", and an explicit claim of continuity from the poet's century to the
  author's own. No shrine is named and no custodian is identified** — **(not stated)**.
- **Animal sacrifice at shrines, asserted in general terms.** "all lovers are sacrificed like animals
  slaughtered at the festival of pilgrimage", and "who would not think of the Blut-und-Wunden poetry
  of the Christian Church, if one does not prefer to turn to sacrifices at Indian temples and
  shrines?" (p. 182, folio 182). **The festival is not named and no temple or shrine is named** —
  **(not stated)**.
- **Tomb dust as a diplomatic payment — the book's one recorded shrine transaction.** Divan Gidumal,
  "the minister of the Kalhoro princes", offered Ahmad Shah Abdali "two bags which, as he said, /
  contain the most valuable of Sind's gifts; they contain the holy dust from the tombs of numerous
  saints and Pirs of Sind." (p. 25, folio 21)
- **Sayyid status as a present-day asset, in a parenthesis.** "Mir Dard's greatest source of pride was
  the fact that he was a sayyid from both sides. (Even today Indian sayyid families may consider
  themselves as absolutely superior to any other human being and will tell the visitor how fortunate
  he is to touch the threshold of a descendant of the holy Prophet.)" (p. 40, folio 38) Alongside it,
  Sorley quoted on the "'almost incredible veneration for sayyids as such and a great accretion of
  superstitious practices in the belief of the multitude'" (p. 28, folio 24).
- **Baraka surviving the decay of charisma — the book's general model of a shrine family.** "This
  happened, of course, when the leadership of an order became hereditary, although in these cases the
  original strength of the mystical experience is usually lost in the sequence of a few generations,
  as can be witnessed in many 'saintly' families. Still, the baraka, the blessing power, of the
  founder saint is venerated by the masses even in a late descendant." (p. 265, folio 265)
  **Charisma decays; the veneration, and therefore the income, do not. No family, shrine or place is
  named to it** — **(not stated)**; the index gives "baraka = =265" (p. 301, folio 301) as its only
  locus.
- **Initiation from a dead saint's spirit, treated as valid — the doctrinal mechanism by which a tomb
  confers authority without a living custodian.** "the mystical introduction into the Path without the
  guidance of a living master: the initiation through the spirit of a deceased saint or through the
  Prophetsaint Khidr was considered valid in most Sufi circles" (p. 26, folio 22); Mazhar too "claimed
  also to be initiated, partly in wwais?"[OCR?] [uwaisi] "succession, into the other orders" (p. 22,
  folio 18). **Record this for the custodianship line of enquiry, and against the uwaisi statement
  about Shah Abdul Latif at p. 191.**
- **Tomb-visiting defended doctrinally, with the liturgy, the atonement function and a frankly
  hierarchical social justification.** "tombs are so to speak the footprint of those who have gone the
  way of annihilation" (p. 102, folio 100); "it is a sign of love that people should visit the tombs
  of their deceased family members, and recite there the fatiha and other efficient prayers so that
  God may also grant them a good end thanks to this good deed. For such a visit is an atonement for
  sins, and people may become more thoughtful about their own end: big people would become more tender
  and gracious toward the small, and the small would understand that their happiness lies in serving
  the great people (A 247)" (p. 102, folio 100). **Grave-visiting as a school of deference.**
- **Tomb upkeep and death-anniversary gatherings prescribed as duties — the book's ʿurs material in
  the abstract.** "The faithful should also look after the tombs and keep them well and urge others to
  visit the tombs of the great, i.e., of the pious and saints, and they should gather to remember the
  anniversary of their death" (p. 102, folio 100). **No shrine is named, no date is given, the word
  ʿurs is not used, and no custodian is identified** — all **(not stated)**.
- **Intercession by the entombed dead as a reciprocal obligation, with a sanction for neglect.** "Only
  those without insight do not consider such visits necessary … How could they, then, hope to find the
  intercession of those whom they have neglected? (S 117)" (p. 102, folio 100); in verse, "The one who
  has no living heart / Does not come to our tomb ... (P 39)" and "You must pass by the tomb of us,
  the strangers— / As long as we were alive, we passed through your street" (pp. 102–103,
  folios 100–101).
- **A tomb-lamp practice assumed as the normal case.** "Even if the destroying Time does not light a
  candle at the tomb of those great men, still, every morning and evening … a confidence burns in the
  grief of those pure people" (p. 104, folio 102, from S 80). The same practice appears in the German
  appendix: "Genug ist, wenn auf meinem Grabe manchmal An Kerzen-Statt ein freundlich Herz entbrennte!"
  ("it is enough if on my grave sometimes, in place of candles, a friendly heart should burn") (p. 276,
  folio 276).
- **Threshold-kissing and barefoot circulation**, the second as a general statement about Indian
  shrines: those "who visited it and kissed its threshold were refreshed even during the hottest days
  of summer" (p. 54, folio 52); "Everyone who has visited Indian shrines in summer at high noon,
  walking barefoot through the precincts will gratefully acknowledge Muhammad Nasir's kindness towards
  his disciples" (p. 54, folio 52). **Schimmel records her own visit: "I visited the tomb in September
  1975" (p. 54, folio 52, footnote).**
- **Sacralised kingship, and a rain rite outside the town.** "Nowhere else in the Risalo ts[OCR?] [is]
  the character of the king as representative of God expressed as clearly as in Kamdd[OCR?] [Kamod],
  where the Quranic words about Allah are applied to him, whose throne is kibrvyd[OCR?] [kibriya],
  'Divine Grandeur'" (p. 175, folio 175); and "In order to implore God's help in times of drought a
  special ritual prayer, the salat al-tstisqa"[OCR?] [salat al-istisqa] ", was performed by the
  community outside the town" (p. 257, folio 257). **The only congregational rite described anywhere
  in the book, and it is not shrine-based.**

### The khanqah as a going concern — Mir Dard's Delhi house

*No archive row. Recorded in full because it is the book's only sustained account of the working
institution a shrine grows out of.*

- **A princess endows a khanqah complex, itemised room by room.** "the princess built a nice compound
  for them, comprising of houses, a mosque, a meditation room and a large space for musical
  gatherings, the meetings for the celebration of ʿurs, and gatherings of poets. This compound in
  Chélun ka kucha remained in the hand of Dard's family for a long time; its last traces disappeared
  after partition." (p. 46, folio 44) **A named patron, a built complex with four functional spaces, a
  stated ʿurs function, hereditary family possession, and a terminal date given only as "after
  partition".**
- **A monthly commemorative samaʿ for the founder, with a day, a size, a timetable and an etiquette.**
  "Mir Dard, therefore, arranged for the memory of his father a samaʿ -meeting on every second and
  26thday[OCR?] [26th day] of each month, to which thousands of pious listeners flocked, enjoying the
  nightly music, kneeling as etiquette would have it, on both knees in perfect quietude. (F 46)."
  (p. 57, folio 55) **Two fixed days a month, a crowd figure, a night timetable and a prescribed
  posture. The figure is Firaq's, and Firaq is discounted three times elsewhere in the book.**
- **A monthly poets' assembly in a named mosque, on a fixed day, and the right to convene it
  transmitted by licence rather than inheritance.** "In this mosque Muhammad Nasir Andalib, faithful to
  Shah Gulshan's instructions, used to arrange a mushdʿira (meeting of poets) on the 15th day of every
  month, his son accompanying him" (pp. 50–51, folios 48–49); "After Andalib's death, the organization
  of mushaʿiras was disrupted and Mir asked his friend Dard for the permission to continue the
  tradition, which permission was granted" (p. 51, folio 49). **An explicit licensing act by the head
  of the order.**
- **Music inside the house, and the fact that it was contested inside the order.** "the musical
  sessions in his house were famous" (p. 57, folio 55); "music and samaʿ are prohibited as religious
  expressions by the Mujaddidi school" (p. 55, folio 53); "some of his pious colleagues did not approve
  of his inclination to music" (p. 55, folio 53). **An internal regulatory conflict over ritual
  practice, dated to "the early 1770's" (p. 56, folio 54).** Schimmel's historical explanation of the
  Mujaddidi position: "One can understand this sober attitude of the 'orthodox' tariga better when
  remembering how the rulers of Delhi after Aurangzeb, especially Muhammad Shah, took to music" (p. 55,
  folio 53). **A reigning emperor attended**: Shah Alam II "used to attend some of the meetings in
  Dard's house" (p. 71, folio 69) and Dard "later did not mind the presence of Shah Alam II in his
  musical sessions" (p. 97, folio 95). **No gift, grant or payment accompanies the attendance** —
  **(not stated)**.
- **Refusal of princely patronage, stated as a prohibition from God — the book's single most important
  sentence for the economics of religion.** "my Lord did not allow me to go to the doors of the princes
  and the sultan, and I belong to the 'benchers' (ahli suffa) of poverty which was the pride of our
  Prophet" (p. 97, folio 95). Beside it, an explicit discipline against soliciting income: "so that the
  spider of negligence does not weave the warp and woof of the thought of expecting livelihood in your
  mind, and the fly of greed never sits on the gracious table of inward collection (jam' iyat)"
  (p. 98, folio 96).
- **The disciple is told to work rather than to depend.** "Perfect trust in God, tawakkul, is basic for
  the disciple, but if he experiences mental discomfort during his attempt to rely absolutely upon
  God's gifts, he had better earn his livelihood and follow the Prophet's advice: / Bind your camel's
  knees notwithstanding your trust in God (K 241, 243)" (pp. 74–75, folios 72–73).
- **Succession, stated three times and never as a rite.** "After Muhammad Nasir's death, Mir Dard
  became his successor as the leader of the Muhammadan Path" (p. 55, folio 53); "From the day Mir Dard
  succeeded his father he had been deeply involved in writing books" (p. 62, folio 60); "Mir Alam, who
  lived for a long time in Bengal, became leader of the convent after his uncle Athar" (p. 93,
  folio 91) — **brother-then-son, not father-to-son**. **No installation rite, no turban, no gaddi and
  no contest is described** — **(not stated)**.
- **A hereditary-succession warrant argued as doctrine, and this is the passage an economics-of-shrines
  entry should quote.** "real vicegerency of the Prophet is conditioned by proximity of time—the time
  of the khulafa? ar-rashidin[OCR?] was finished … However, for the saints such a proximity of time is
  not necessary. He himself is the first and true vicegerent of his father" (p. 80, folio 78); and, as
  a general law, "the father is the vicegerent of the Prophet, ... especially when the father is a
  sincere Muhammadan and also a spiritual guide, for thanks to the grace of God a rotten and unworthy
  son never comes to such an excellent father" (pp. 92–93, folios 90–91). **The last clause is a
  hereditary-succession warrant in one sentence: an excellent shaikh cannot produce an unworthy heir.**
  Against it, Schimmel's general model of decay at p. 265 (folio 265), above.
- **Investiture regalia, itemised in Dard's own Arabic.** "he inclined on his invented throne called
  al-mi' yar[OCR?] [al-miʿyār], and 'Orna-ment of the prayer-niche and the pulpit,' and put on his
  God-be-stowed crown and lifted his Muhammadan banner" (pp. 81–82, folios 79–80). **Schimmel admits
  she cannot place them: "it is diffi-cult to discern where the throne and the crown belong" (p. 83,
  folio 81).**
- **A relic with a family monopoly on its blessing use.** The cane "an-Nasiri" with the founder's name
  in "an artistic tughrd" "was for a long while in the family", and "this peculiar type of tughraé was
  used by the members of the family for the sake of blessing (M 93 f)" (p. 49, folio 47).
- **A household naming rule extended to the servants, with a stated efficacy.** "every member of his
  house should bear a name connected with 'Nasir' for the sake of blessing; thus, even the servants
  were called Nasir Quli or Nasir Bakhsh. It was believed that by this sacred name the members of the
  family would always remain victorious and successful; every letter which began with the formula Huwa
  an-Nasir, 'He is the Victorious' was supposed to yield a positive result for the writer (F 92)."
  (p. 64, folio 62)
- **Recruitment events, three of them, each with a named mechanism.** A grief-recital in the
  vernacular: Andalib "told this story in Hindi language during three nights"; "all those who listened
  to it were deeply impressed and started crying and weeping"; "many of them entered the tariga
  Muhammadtyya" (p. 46, folio 44). A miracle: after the rescue in Bengal, "most of the travellers
  immediately entered the Muhammadan Path (K 146)!" (p. 54, folio 52). And a textual entry condition:
  disciples "who have studied his and his father's books" are counted as family (p. 94, folio 92) —
  **book-reading is the entry condition, and no number is given** — **(not stated)**.
- **Mass initiation of kin as a single graded transaction.** "I took all my children and the family of
  the Qibla of Both Worlds under my protection… and I brought all my family and children and the
  relatives of the Qibla of Both Worlds according to their different ranks and took them till
  resurrection un-der his protection" (p. 94, folio 92). **"according to their different ranks" — the
  family is graded, and the grant runs "till resurrection".**
- **Sohbat as the working mechanism, and books as its licensed substitute.** "sohbat, the conversation
  with the master, which guarantees not only a perpetual control of the disciple's progress but the
  constant flow of spiritual energy from the shatkh[OCR?] [shaikh] to the murid… In rare cases the
  perusal of the master's books can be substituted for oral sohbat (K 499; cf. 431)." (p. 75,
  folio 73) And "To read somebody's books is like keeping company with him" (p. 111, folio 109).
- **Complete surrender of the murid, in the classical formula.** "the murid would be like the corpse in
  the hand of the washerman"; Schimmel's verdict: "Dard underlines the importance of the pir more than
  his predecessors" (p. 72, folio 70). And the test of membership: "everybody who venerates the pir and
  has faith is my soul and the beloved of my heart (N 117)" (p. 73, folio 71).
- **The office of shaikh described as scarce and prophetic.** "the office of shatkh[OCR?] [shaikh] is
  vicegerency of the prophecy, and thus not available to every saint (K 451)" (p. 72, folio 70); and
  "Direct illumination is impossible—either it happens after the initiation or the mystic, after his
  first experience, seeks a master to guide him further (K 638)" (p. 72, folio 70), on the principle
  "God's custom is to bring spiritual bounty from the living to the living" (p. 72, folio 70).
- **The duties of a convent servant, listed by the servant himself** — sweeping "the court of the
  'Qibla of both worlds'", "the instruction and education of brethren and children", "completion of
  assemblies and invitations of people", and "showing the people the way" (p. 50, folio 48).
- **A family graveyard, unroofed, with four generations in it.** "his father's tomb had no roof or dome
  so as to be exposed to the weather" (p. 54, folio 52); "besides Andalib, Mir Dard, his son Alam and
  his brother Athar are buried there; two small :ombs in the modest enclosure have no names" (p. 54,
  folio 52, footnote). **No enclosure date, builder or custodian** — **(not stated)**. Dard "was
  buried close to his father in the place near the Turkoman Gate in Delhi, called the Baghiche-yi Mir
  Dard" (p. 104, folio 102), and the grave is "in what is now a slum area. Few people know the place,
  fewer visit it" (p. 33, folio not stated), where visitors go "to recite a fatiha".
- **Daily tomb attendance as a year-long discipline.** Dard "for about a year, used to spend all day,
  from before the morning prayer till after the night prayer, at his father's tomb, without taking with
  him any friend or child" (p. 88, folio 86) — and these visits "were the only interruption of his
  daily routine" (p. 89, folio 87).
- **Feeding guests in the mosque at the household's expense — the closest thing in the book to a langar
  economy.** "a plate, dreamt of for days, was carried away by the father to feed the guests in the
  mosque instead of his family ..." (p. 96, folio 94). **The word langar does not appear anywhere in
  the book** — **(not stated)**.
- **Quantified devotional labour, and an order's deputies counted.** Muhammad Zubair "is credited with
  having uttered the profession of faith 'There is no God but God' 24,000 times during the 12 hours of
  the day; further, he repeated the name of God 15,000 times a day, retaining his breath according to
  the traditional Naqshbandi fashion" (p. 36, folio 34); Mazhar "remarks that one should pray not less
  than 60 rak'as (prayer units) during 24 hours; that is more than three times the prescribed number"
  (p. 23, folio 19); and of Mazhar's "tariqa, the Shamsiyya Mazhariyya … not less than 49 of his
  khalifas are known by name" (p. 23, folio 19). **The only countable measure of an order's
  institutional reach in the book.**
- **The office of qayyum as a claim to universal jurisdiction, with its basic text still in
  manuscript.** "'is the dignitary upon whom the whole order of existence depends… He is the vicar of
  God on earth.'" Even "the gutb"[OCR?] [qutb] "is under his rule, nay, is his servant" (p. 36,
  folio 34, footnoted to "J. Subhan, 'Sufism,' and S. M. Ikram, 'Radi Kauthar,'"[OCR?] and to "Ihsan's
  Raudat al-qayyumiyya … the ms. is found in the Library of the Asiatic Society of Bengal"). **Its
  political and ceremonial face**: Pir Muhammad Zubair "was surrounded by large crowds when he rode
  through Delhi in pompous style, and how the princes spread velvet and brocade under his feet when he
  entered the Lal Qila" (p. 37, folio 35) — **recorded with the author's own caveat, "as usual, greatly
  exaggerated".**

### Book production, schooling and the market in religious speech

- **Poetry for hire refused, and the refusal itemised.** "I have not been drowned and consumed myself
  in this work with careful observances, and have never praised or satirized anybody, and have not said
  anything according to the order of a person ..." (p. 115, folio 113). **Three refusals: panegyric,
  satire, and composition to commission.**
- **The rule that makes poetry licit, with "beggary" as the book's own word for the alternative.**
  "Poetry is not such a perfection that a human being should make it his profession… under the
  condition that it is not conditioned by searching gifts and running about from place to place, and
  that one does not utter panegyrics and satires for the sake of worldly reward, for then it is a sort
  of beggary and implies greed and meanness of the lower soul ..." (p. 115, folio 113).
- **The patronage economy of poetry described as a system, with a patron-set curriculum.** The poet
  "must live in the society—even though this society be, as mostly for an Oriental poet, the
  environment of feudal circles, of kings and wealthy amirs, who could pay for his praise, or pay off
  his satires"; "he was as much a member of the society as any artisan and craftsman, and poetry was
  indeed… considered an art and profession which could be studied like anything else"; the training
  ran "from astrology (to cast his master's horoscope in ingenious verses) to medicine" (p. 106,
  folio 104). **Praise and satire are both priced; the poet is placed with the artisans.**
- **A stratified market in religious song, and a learned shaikh's contempt for its lower end.** "the
  passage shows also how proud Dard was of his high education and art, and how little he thought of
  the numerous mystics of low birth who lived all around the country and attracted people with their
  simple songs" (p. 123, folio 121). **Competition for an audience between a learned urban shaikh and
  unlettered rural singers, stated in the author's own words**; and the doctrine that ranks them: "at
  least the inspiration of saints with knowledge and practice in poetry will be more true and better by
  degrees than the inspirations of unscholarly and unpoetical saints" (p. 123, folio 121).
- **Out-migration of cultural labour on economic grounds.** Delhi's "misfortunes and afflictions which
  caused most of the poets, musicians and scholars to wander East in search of livelihood" (p. 55,
  folio 53); "free also from the greed which caused most of his contemporary poets to leave Delhi for a
  safer place where they could better sell their poetry" (p. 98, folio 96). **Dard is the stated
  exception; he "never left Delhi" (p. 55, folio 53).**
- **A teaching institution run from a private house, dated.** "In his house the theoretical foundations
  of Urdu poetry were established in the mid 18th century" (p. 126, folio 124); "he alludes here to the
  poetical instruction which he gave throughout his life to a large number of Urdu poets" (p. 116,
  folio 114).
- **A merit economy extending to copyists and readers.** "on Doomsday, the ink of the writers of such
  noble books will be equal to the blood of the martyrs"; "the writers and those who copy the books
  full of blessings and the readers and those who look into them are also comprised in the blessing and
  take part in the heavenly reward" (pp. 113–114, folios 111–112). **Four grades of participant —
  author, copyist, reader, browser — drawing on one stock of merit.** With it, a named constraint from
  the scribal market: "the copyists and writers of his time do not like to write long books" (p. 69,
  folio 67).
- **Religious publishing and schooling in Sind, with named books and a named press.** "Makhdum Hashim's
  Sindhi Tafsir Hashimi was usually the first book given to children in the Quran schools, and belongs
  to the first Sindhi books ever printed" (p. 27, folio 23); Makhdum Hashim produced "more than 300
  works" (p. 26, folio 22); devotion to the Prophet "was taught in orthodox works like Makhdum
  Muhammad Hashim's Qut al-‘dshiqin[OCR?] [Qut al-ʿashiqin], 'Nurture of the Lovers,' and was imbibed
  by the children who learned even in lullabies something about the lofty qualities of Muhammad"
  (pp. 253–254, folios 253–254). **A named schoolbook and a named domestic genre.** And in Delhi: works
  of Dard's "were published by the Matba'a Ansari from 1309/1891 onwards … the first edition was
  prepared in 1847 at the request of Dr. Alois Sprenger" (p. 33, folio not stated) — **a colonial
  officer commissioning the first edition of a Sufi's poetry.**
- **The madrasa syllabus named by its three books, and the Sindhi–Panjabi tradition of mocking it.**
  "Sindhi and Panjabi mystical poets have regarded the three books mentioned in its first line as
  symbols of useless bookishness" (p. 202, folio 202); the Kafiya was the poem "according to which
  children in Indian madrasas were instructed in Arabic" (p. 202, folio 202). **A named curriculum, a
  named clientele (children), and a documented tradition of ridiculing both.**
- **Two dated destructions of manuscripts.** The autograph of the Nala "was destroyed during the Mutiny
  1857" (p. 49, folio 47); Girhori's books "destroyed during the Afghan raids in Upper Sind in 1781"
  (p. 28, folio 24).
- **A nineteenth-century Sindhi manuscript in named private hands, carrying a hadith variant recorded
  nowhere else.** "the version of the famous tradition which I found in a 19th century manuscript of
  Sindhi mystical poetry… 'Who knows himself through `fand`[OCR?] [fana], "annihilation," knows his
  Lord through `bagd`[OCR?] [baqa], "remaining in him."' This elegant formulation may have been common
  among the Sindhi Sufis" (p. 212, folio 212, footnote 23, which gives the owner and place: "Ali
  Laghari, Tajpur, Sind"). **Provenance for a text the archive cannot find elsewhere.**
- **Four competing textual authorities over the Risalo, set against one another** — the Trumpp edition,
  the Hyderabad edition, "the commentator" and the Urdu translation, and "The editor", whose reading of
  `la-kuft`[OCR?] Schimmel corrects: "The editor explains the expression as `farqé`[OCR?] [farqi],
  'distinction, separation;' but it really means 'kufic'" (p. 201, folio 201; further at pp. 195, 200,
  202, 203, 206, 210). **Jotwani's translation is contested too** (p. 200, folio 200, footnote 9).

### Sindhi religious and literary institutions

- **Sufis as the makers of the literary vernacular, and the samaʿ assembly as the setting, dated.**
  "It seems that in Sind, as in other parts of the Muslim world, the Sufis are responsible for the
  development of the spoken language into a literary one" (p. 161, folio 161); the first Sindhi verses
  were used "during the sessions of mystical concerts when music and dancing were in full swing",
  "shortly after 1530" (p. 161, folio 161). **A dated institutional practice producing a literary
  consequence.** The same claim in general terms: "the mystics had played a decisive role in developing
  the language, since they needed a medium for conveying their message to the large masses" (p. 15,
  folio 11).
- **Two silsilas dated into Sind.** "a Sufi of the Qadiri order which was introduced in the Indus
  Valley in the late 15th century" (p. 162, folio 162) — **the book's only statement of a silsila's
  arrival in Sind**; and the Naqshbandiyya "also spread in other parts of India, for instance in the
  Lower Indus Valley" (p. 11, folio 7), **with no khanqah, shrine, family or date** — **(not
  stated)**.
- **Mathnawi recitation as an organised devotional activity across orders, with a market metaphor.**
  "In Sind there were many pious who, by their recitation of the Mathnawi, 'kept the marketplace of
  union warm', and Rumi's work was quoted by stern Naqshbandis as much as by members of the more
  emotional orders" (p. 165, folio 165). And a manuscript used as princely diplomacy: the Mathnawi
  "served the Kalhoro prince to gain back the sympathies of Shah Abdul Latif" (p. 165, folio 165).
- **A ritual-musical overlap between the Risalo and Sikh scripture, at the level of raga.** The
  Risalo's "poetical form is reminiscent of many of Kabir's poems, and of the poetry of the Adit
  Granth"[OCR?] [Adi Granth]". A number of ragas used in the Risdlo"[OCR?] "are also used in the Adi
  Granth" (p. 154, folio 154, footnote 6).
- **A dated eighteenth-century festival calendar, with music and mystical dance, at a burial hill with
  no archive row.** Qani's "Maklindma"[OCR?] [Maklinama] gives "his notes about the festivities, the
  sessions of music and mystical dance, as they were held there in constant succession", from which
  "many of the buildings which are no longer existent, can be reconstructed from his remarks" (p. 27,
  folio 23). **See `## Figures and sites not in the archive` — Makli Hill is the richest shrine
  material in the book and has no row.**
- **Caste and social stratum, stated as fact about Sind, not only about the poems.** "The Mohana
  traditionally belonged to the lowest social strata in Sind, and their women were noted for beauty and
  not too strict morals" (p. 175, folio 175); Nuri belongs "to the low gandri caste" (p. 175,
  folio 175); "the `kalal` or wine-distiller is considered, in parts of Indo-Pakistan, a member of the
  most despised caste" (p. 199, folio 199). **All three stated in the present tense of 1976.**
- **Conversion asserted as a rate without a magnitude.** Qani' on Makhdum Hashim: "In his time appeared
  so to speak a renovation of Islam, and every month some Hindus found salvation from the meanness of
  infidelity thanks to his laudable striving." (p. 27, folio 23, footnoted to
  "Qani', Maqdlat ash-shu" ard"[OCR?] ", ed. H. Rashdi, p. 841 f.")
- **Hindu participation in Muslim shrine religion, stated four ways and once contradicted.** "a number
  of Hindus became devoted disciples of Muslim saints and did not hesitate to compose religious poetry
  in honor of Muslim saints and even of the Prophet of Islam" (p. 28, folio 24); Shaikh Tahir / Lal
  Udero "is venerated by members of both communities" (p. 28, folio 24); Shah Inayat's death "has
  inspired many Sufi poets, Hindus and Muslims alike" (pp. 25–26, folios 21–22); and "the Hindu
  Bedraban"[OCR?] "Khushgu" is named among Shah Gulshan's Delhi disciples (p. 38, folio 36). Against
  these, Mazhar "did not consider the Hindus as sheer mushrikin" (p. 23, folio 19), while Girhori died
  attacking "the Shiva idol in Hathungo" (p. 28, folio 24). **Keep the two sides side by side.**
- **Schimmel's definition of Sufi "tolerance" — a qualified one that should be quoted with its
  qualification.** "The 'tolerance' of Islamic mysticism consists of its embracing all religions under
  the crown of that final revelation which was granted to Muhammad." (p. 10, folio 6)
- **A named craft and trade world, from the Risalo's vocabulary** — "seafaring, fishing, milking,
  churning, spinning and hunting" (p. 164, folio 164), plus "the spinning of cotton, one of the most
  important occupations of women" (p. 160, folio 160), camel breeding (p. 155, folio 155), buffalo
  grazing (p. 156, folio 156), pottery and the Indus boat trade (p. 171, folio 171); the lacquer-work
  swinging beds that "are still a specialty of Sind" (p. 168, folio 168); and the Ceylon trade, "from
  which the lucky merchants may return with pearls and spices. But, alas! today only a few faithful
  dealers in jewels are found, and most of them offer cheap beads instead of precious pearls" (p. 155,
  folio 155).
- **Grain prices, hoarders and the poor, inside the poetry.** "he prays that there may be plentiful
  rain so that the grain becomes cheaper, and the people happier; but he curses the greedy hoarding
  merchants who are unhappy when the poor are eased (IV 16)"[OCR?] (p. 258, folio 258). **Schimmel
  marks it as unusual: "It is touching to see, and very rare in the history of Islamic mystical
  poetry, that a poet tenderly mentions the poor villagers who have been waiting for the rainy season"
  (p. 258, folio 258).** The German appendix carries the same prayer: "Mach billiger das Getreide, die
  trockenen Tiimpel[OCR?] hilf fiillen[OCR?]" ("make the grain cheaper, help fill the dry pools")
  (p. 286, folio 286).
- **Renunciation of property as the entry condition to the ascetic life, itemised.** "Sie lassen Felder
  und Tennen" ("they leave fields and threshing-floors"), "Sie rollen die Biicher[OCR?] zusammen"
  ("they roll up the books"), "Geld and Gut[OCR?] sie verlassen" ("they leave money and goods"), "Er
  lasst das bunte Gewand" ("he leaves the coloured garment") (pp. 288–289, folios 288–289). **Land,
  books, money, goods and clothing.** And in the Sindhi: the Kaparis "'who have no greed, tam' , for
  food, ta' am (7)'" and "8. Dying from hunger they ask not alms from anyone" (pp. 233–234,
  folios 233–234) — **the ascetics are described as outside any alms or offering economy.**
- **The Sufi cap as an affiliation badge, and a Sindhi text telling the disciple to burn it.** "The
  Sufi cap, often mentioned as the distinctive mark of the mystic, and varying in shape and color
  according to the affiliation of the Sufi, was quite early regarded by the advanced mystic with some
  mistrust" (p. 203, folio 203); "You wish to be called Sufi! To the Sufis (something) like this is not
  fitting, Cut the headgear, take and cast it in fire!" (p. 203, folio 203, v. 6).
- **The body replacing the built place of worship — repeated four times and worth taking as an
  institutional claim.** "The body a mosque, the mind a cell" (p. 215, folio 215); "4. Those, whose
  knees are a prayer niche, their body is a mosque, / Making the heart the place of the qibla, they
  have made circumambulation around the body" (p. 226, folio 226); "5. The friend's face ts[OCR?] the
  mihrab, the whole world a mosque" (p. 226, folio 226). **Circumambulation and qibla are relocated
  from a building to a body.** With it, an office declared dispensable: "24. Put into your interior a
  mufti (ze. keep your conscience awake), / so that you do not need the Qadi" (p. 219, folio 219).
- **Genealogy explicitly denied to the ascetics — a pointed absence the book does not draw out.** "They
  have no genealogy, neither father nor mother nor family tree (13)" (p. 234, folio 234). **Set against
  the descent-based authority of the Sindhi shrine families.**
- **An eighteenth-century Sindhi proverb, localised by the author without a source.** "we may see in
  this verse an application of a current Arabic saying which was very popular in 18th century Sind:
  al-bala' lil-wala? ka l-lahab lt? dh-dhahab[OCR?], 'Affliction is for saintliness the same as are
  flames for the gold'" (p. 222, folio 222). **"very popular in 18th century Sind" is Schimmel's
  assertion and carries no source.**

### Method, and the book's own warnings

- **Do not read the poetry as topography.** "Shah's geographical remarks should not always be taken at
  face value" (p. 256, folio 256). **A direct caution against using Risalo place names as itinerary
  evidence, and it bears on every Hinglaj, Kech, Malir and Bhambhore line above.**
- **Hagiography adds what was not there.** "the later biographies of the saints as composed by many a
  pious soul approach the problems only from the outside and tend to add much unhistorical material in
  order to wrap the venerated master's personality in a gown richly embroidered with legends and
  miracles" (p. 264, folio 264). **Her own practice matches it: Firaq is discounted three times in
  three pages — "as usual, greatly exaggerated" (p. 37, folio 35), "in his usual romantic style"
  (p. 35, folio 33), "embellishes the story with touching details" (p. 42, folio 40) — while being
  accepted on specific points; and Qani' is rejected on the illiteracy claim while being called "one
  of the most important writers in the Persian language in India during the 18th century" (p. 27,
  folio 23).**
- **A miracle refuted on chronological grounds, as a model.** "The story that Gulshan did not accept
  Muhammad Nasir as disciple in mysticism but only in poetry because Nasir was superior to him due to
  his famous vision of Imam Hasan is historically incorrect, for Shah Gulshan died in 1728, and
  Muhammad Nasir's vision took place only in the mid-thirties (cf. F 34 f.)" (p. 37, folio 35).
- **A miracle given a natural explanation in a footnote.** Of the temperate tomb: "The ground is clay,
  and the location is situated ona little hill-that may account for its 'coolness'." (p. 54, folio 52,
  footnote).
- **Mystical autobiography as a rare genre — directly relevant to the archive's source problem.**
  "Mystical autobiographies of this extent are rare in Islam. To gain a picture of a saint's
  development we have to rely in the early period upon the scattered sayings of the Sufis as they were
  collected in more or less correct form by their followers" (p. 264, folio 264).
- **The malfuzat as social and economic evidence.** "The disciples wrote down the
  `"malfiizat"[OCR?]` [malfuzat], the words and sayings of their masters. They allow us some insight
  into the spiritual, and also the social life of the Middle Ages, and complete thus the outlook of
  the official chronicles" (p. 8, folio 4).
- **Spiritual authority becoming temporal power — the one sentence an economics-of-religion entry can
  use directly.** "in later times, the borderline between the highly influential pir and the feudal
  lord was sometimes blurred, for spiritual influence not rarely resulted in political power."
  (pp. 8–9, folios 4–5)
- **Colonial-era hostility to Sindhi shrine culture, preserved verbatim.** Trumpp's introduction calls
  the Sorathi tale a picture of "Hinduism in its complete decomposition and deepest moral decline" and
  "a picture of crime and absolute obtusity, nothing else", and claims "how much even the strong
  monotheistic deism of Islam has become modified and corrupted in Sind, as in other parts of India, by
  its contact with Hinduism" (p. 158, folio 158). Schimmel's partial defence of him: "Part of Trumpp's
  criticism, however, is born from the fact that his manuscript of the Rzsdl6"[OCR?] "was hopelessly
  confused" (p. 159, folio 159). **A primary document of colonial-era hostility, and worth citing as
  such.**
- **The hostile reading Schimmel rejects outright, and her reply.** Watanmal: "They try to make their
  belief in the prophet Mahommad consistent with their Sufi doctrines, an attempt in which they fail
  most ignominiously …" (p. 252, folio 252); Schimmel: "These patronizing remarks are certainly far off
  the mark. Whosoever has studied Sufi poetry and prose knows that it was just the Sufis who developed
  the love of the Prophet in various forms among the people" (p. 253, folio 253).
- **Two research gaps the author names herself.** "Besides some articles by the present writer (see
  Bibliography) there are barely studies on Mir Dard available" (p. 33, folio not stated); and "It
  would be worth while to study the father-son relation in the history of Sufism, as it would be
  rewarding to find out how many Sufis entered the Path under the influence of their pious mothers"
  (p. 265, folio 265).
- **A hagiographical series pointed to but not used.** Under Quddasi: "Tadhkira-yi sifiya-yi[OCR?]
  Sindh, Karachi 1959 (the same author has collected biographies of the saints from the various
  regions of Pakistan: Panjab, Frontier, Bengal)." (p. 296, folio 296) **A four-region series of which
  only the Sindh volume is cited — a lead for the archive.**
- **An attribution declined in the bibliography.** Under Muhsin Fani: "— (wrongly ascribed to),
  Dabistan-i madhahib, transl. D. Shea and A. Troyer, London 1901." (p. 295, folio 295); in the text,
  the Dabistan is "a 17th century book the authority of which is more than questionable" (p. 252,
  folio 252).
- **What the book's own index will and will not give a reader — the sharpest finding in the whole
  apparatus.** **Present**, with page figures: `ʿurs` (printed "Surs 37,44, 151", the ʿayn read as a
  capital S — **a human must confirm this on the printed page**), Pir, murid, murshid, samaʿ, baraka,
  faqir, sayyid, shaikh, khalifa, malfuzat, Initiation, sohbat, and the silsila names, with
  Naqshbandiyya carrying the longest order-run in the index (pp. 301–310, folios 301–310). **"jizya
  34" (p. 304, folio 304) is the only fiscal or revenue headword in the entire index.** **Absent — no
  headword at all, checked across the full A–Z**: shrine, dargah, mazar, tomb, ziarat, khanqah,
  langar, mela, qawwali, sajjada nashin, offerings, waqf, Auqaf, pilgrimage, custodian, khadim,
  dervish, saint, karamat, endowment, land, revenue, jagir, inam, zakat (pp. 301–310, folios 301–310).
  **A reader who wants shrine practice, custodianship, endowment or festival economy out of this book
  cannot get there through its index.** **The ʿurs entry gives no page anywhere inside the body of the
  Risalo discussion** (pp. 37 and 44 fall in the Dard part; p. 151 is the opening leaf of the Latif
  part).

### A. Sites with a named tomb, burial ground, cult or festival — candidates for future rows

- **Makli Hill near Thatta — the richest shrine-complex material in the whole book, and nothing in the
  169 rows covers it or Thatta.** The burial place of Makhdum Muhammad Hashim and, "as legend has it",
  of "125,000 saints" (p. 26, folio 22); the site where "the Muharram celebrations … were celebrated"
  and were attacked by the man buried there (p. 27, folio 23); and the subject of Qani's
  "Maklindma"[OCR?] [Maklinama], "probably the most charming account we possess about the famous Makli
  Hill near the capital; many of the buildings which are no longer existent, can be reconstructed from
  his remarks, and his notes about the festivities, the sessions of music and mystical dance, as they
  were held there in constant succession gives the modern reader a wonderful picture of popular
  mystical religion in 18th century Sind" (p. 27, folio 23). **A burial count, a named
  eighteenth-century festival calendar with music and mystical dance, buildings since lost, a
  contested Muharram observance and one named burial — this is a candidate row, not a mapping.**
- **The Chaukandi tombs near Karachi — a tomb complex with carved imagery and an approximate date, and

- **Hathungo, District Khairpur, and its Shiva idol** — "he set out with 72 companions to destroy the

- **Ganji Takar, near Hyderabad** — the Yogis' starting village for the Hinglaj walk (p. 159,
  folio 159). **A village named as a pilgrimage origin; no shrine, tomb or custodian stated there** —
  **(not stated)**.
- **Lahut, the cave in Balochistan** — "The fact that Lahut is also the name of a famous cave with
  stalactites in Balochistan, adds to the charme of this imagery" (p. 221, folio 221), and "also a

### B. Sindhi and Panjabi figures with no archive row

- **Makhdum Muhammad Hashim ibn Abdul Ghafur (1692-1761), qadi of Thatta** — buried on Makli Hill;
  "with the support of the Kalhora princes relentlessly fought against the innovations that had spoiled
  Islam in his days"; "more than 300 works"; attacked the Muharram celebrations on Makli Hill; his
  `Tafsir Hashimi` "was usually the first book given to children in the Quran schools, and belongs to
  the first Sindhi books ever printed"; his `Qut al-'adshigin`[OCR?] [Qut al-ʿashiqin] taught devotion
  to the Prophet (pp. 26–27, 253–254; folios 22–23, 253–254). **The strongest non-archive Sindhi
  candidate in the book.**
- **Mir Ali Shir Qani' (1725-1789)** — "the famous writer of Sindhi history", of a Naqshbandi family
  whose "ancestors had reached the Indus Valley in the 16th century, coming from Shiraz"; author of
  `Tuhfat al-kiram`, `Maqalat ash-shu'ara` and "numerous works on the affiliations of Sindhi tariqas",
  and of the `Maklinama` (pp. 27–28, folios 23–24). **He is the book's chief Sindhi source and the
  source of the illiteracy claim about Shah Abdul Latif that Schimmel rejects (p. 29, folio 25).**
- **Abu'l-qasim Naqshbandi**[OCR?] — leader of the "'Naqshbandi reaction'" in the Lower Indus Valley,
  "a follower of Ahmad Sirhindi's grandson Shah Saifuddin ibn Urwat al-Wuthqa Ma'sum"; master of
  Muhammad Muʿin and of Makhdum Muhammad Zaman (pp. 26–27, folios 22–23).
- **Mian Muhammad Thattawi** — co-master of Makhdum Muhammad Zaman (p. 27, folio 23).
- **Mian Abu 'l-Hasan**[OCR?] **(d. 1711)** — author of the `Muqaddamat as-salat`, "Preliminary
  discourse on Prayer", "written in 1700", which "deals with 130 questions concerning the ritual
  practices of Islam, and is the first major didactic poem in Sindhi" (p. 26, folio 22). **A dated
  Sindhi manual of ritual practice with a countable question list.**
- **Muhammad Muʿin** — "a great friend of Shah Abdul Latif", disciple of Abu'l-qasim Naqshbandi, author
  of a `Risala-yi uwaisiyya` on initiation without a living master; "he loved music and mystical dance"
  (p. 26, folio 22).
- **Qazi Qadan of Sehwan** — a sixteenth-century Sindhi mystic with **"seven extant Sindhi verses"**
  (p. 209, folio 209); quoted, "Leave the people with their grammar, I study the beloved" (p. 209,
  folio 209). **A corpus figure worth keeping.**
- **Bedil of Rohri** — "Sachal's spiritual heir" (p. 202, folio 202). **The succession claim is
  archive-relevant; he has no row.**
- **Shah ʿInat / "Shah Inat"** — his collection was "the first to be arranged in 19 Surs, and was
  therefore called a Risalo" (p. 162, folio 162); quoted as prefiguring Shah Abdul Latif's verse

- **Shah Habib** — Shah Abdul Latif's father (p. 29, folio 25). **No death date, burial place or
  shrine** — **(not stated)**.
- **Adam Shah Kalhoro** — "their saintly ancestor, Adam Shah Kalhoro, who had been executed by the
  central government in 1651", whose "former disciples" overthrew his own descendants: "The Talpurs,
  who thus came to power in 1781, ruled the country until it was conquered by the British in 1843"
  (p. 30, folio 26). **A saintly ancestor executed by the state, and the murshid–murid relation
  inverted, with three dates. No tomb is given** — **(not stated)**.
- **Ghulam Shah Kalhoro** — the prince who "erected" the Bhit Shah mausoleum "in 1765" (p. 151, folio
  not stated); and an unnamed "Kalhoro prince" who used a Mathnawi "to gain back the sympathies of

  stated)**.
- **Divan Gidumal** — "the minister of the Kalhoro princes", who offered Ahmad Shah Abdali two bags of

- **Pir Chhata of Sind** — "'Somebody saw (during the dhikr) that the limbs of this love-slain man fell

- **Ali Haydar** — "who died in 1785 near Multan" (p. 17, folio 13), a Panjabi poet; **no tomb is

- **Khushhal Khan Khattak (d. 1689)** — "the tribal chief" and Pashto poet (p. 17, folio 13).

### 9. Known gaps in the archive that this book exposes

- **Makli Hill near Thatta has no row, and it is the richest shrine material in the book** — a
  burial count ("125,000 saints, as legend has it"), a named eighteenth-century festival calendar
  with music and mystical dance, buildings since lost and reconstructible from Qani's `Maklinama`, a
  contested Muharram observance, and one named burial (Makhdum Muhammad Hashim, 1692-1761)
  (pp. 26–27, folios 22–23). **Nothing in the 169 rows covers Makli Hill or Thatta. This is a
  candidate row, not a mapping.**
- **The Chaukandi tombs near Karachi have no row** (p. 216, folio 216) — a dated tomb complex with
  carved imagery, and the only "tomb" headword in the book's index.
- **Hathungo, District Khairpur** — a named Hindu place of worship in a district the archive covers,
  destroyed in a named attack (p. 28, folio 24). **No row matches it.**
- **Ganji Takar near Hyderabad** — a named pilgrimage origin village with no row (p. 159, folio 159).
