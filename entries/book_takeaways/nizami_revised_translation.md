## What this book is

A 12th-century Persian prose work on the four classes of courtier a king needs (secretaries, poets, astrologers, physicians), each discourse built from anecdotes, here in Browne's English translation of 1921 with his Preface and footnotes and an abridgement of Mirza Muhammad's notes. The notes below keep three voices apart: **Nizami** (the author, a poet and courtier of the House of Ghur, whose anecdotes on PDF pp. 19–116 are the primary source), **Browne** (Preface, footnotes, first-person parts of the Notes), and **the Notes (Mirza Muhammad, as abridged by Browne)** on PDF pp. 117–185. It is not a South Asia book: its world is Khurasan, Transoxiana, Ghazna and Ghur, and it touches the archive only twice, through Ayaz (Sultan Mahmud's favourite, Anecdote XIV) and through the poets of Ghaznavid Lahore in Note XIV. Reliability: an archive.org scan with an OCR text layer. Digits, macron vowels and many proper names are damaged (flagged `[text-layer?]`), and about thirty pages carry two-column interleaving, among them p. 55, the description of Ayaz.

### darbar-malik-ahmad-ayaz — Darbar Malik Ahmad Ayaz

**Person-level identification only:** the book's Ayaz is Sultan Mahmud's favourite in Anecdote XIV (Nizami's narrative). The book says nothing of Lahore, a governorship or a tomb. Ayaz's name in the opening sentence is lost in an interleaved line (p. 55), and his description is reconstructed from interleaved fragments. Two chunks (003, 012).

- **Saint**
  - The anecdote is headed "SULTAN MAHMUD AND, AYAZ" (Anecdote XIV; the book's "Ayaz" / "Aydz" [text-layer?] [Ayaz]) (p. 55, folio 37).
  - "The love borne by Sultan Yamtnttd-Dawla [text-layer?] [Yaminu'd-Dawla] Mahmud to … the Turk is well-known and famous". The line is interleaved in the text layer, and Ayaz's name before "the Turk" is not legible (p. 55, folio 37).
  - Ayaz described (interleaved lines; the reading is reconstructed from the fragments [text-layer?]): "not remarkably handsome, but was of sweet expression and olive complexion, symmetrically formed, graceful in his movements, sensible and deliberate in action, and mightily endowed with all the arts of pleasing" (p. 55, folio 37).
  - Mahmud "was a pious and God-fearing maif [text-layer?] [man], and he wrestled much with his love for Ayaz so that he should not diverge … from the Path of the Law" (p. 55, folio 37).
  - At a carousal, with the wine affecting him, Mahmud looked at "the curls of Ayaz" and "lover-like he drew him to himself". The "watchman" of the Qur'anic verse warned him: "O Mahmud, mingte [text-layer?] [mingle] not sin with love". Fearing he could not withstand "the hosts of Ayaz's locks", he gave Ayaz a knife and told him to cut off his curls "in the middle" (p. 55, folio 37).
  - Ayaz "doubled back his locks to get the measurement, executed the king's command, and laid the two tresses before Mahmud". "this ready obedience became a fresh cause of love", and Mahmud gave Ayaz gold and jewels "beyond his usual wont" before falling "into a drunken sleejp [text-layer?] [sleep]" (p. 55, folio 37).
  - Browne's footnote: here and in the next sentence he preferred "the alternative reading of the MSis. [text-layer?] [MSS.]" to the printed text, which places "We believe and we affirm" differently (p. 55, folio 37).
  - Next morning Mahmud "saw the clipped tresses" and was seized by remorse. No courtier dared ask him why, until "Hajib 'All [ibn] Qarib" [text-layer?] ['Ali], "his Chief Chamberlain", sent "'Unsurf" [text-layer?] ['Unsuri] in to restore his temper (p. 56, folio 38).
  - 'Unsuri "extemporized" the quatrain "Why deem it shame a fair one's curls to shear, … When clipped the Cypress doth most trim appear". Mahmud had the poet's mouth filled twice with "precious stones" and drank wine to those verses until nightfall, "whereby his melancholy was dissipated" (p. 56, folio 38).
  - Index entry "Ayaz (xi), 37-8". The only gloss is the bracketed "(xi)", with no title, office, place or tomb (p. 188, folio 170).
  - Ayaz's position, burial, governorship, death date and any link to Lahore: (not stated).
- **Significance**
  - The author's moral: "improvisation is the chief pillar of the Poetic Art" (p. 56, folio 38). The anecdote stands in the Second Discourse, on poets (PDF pp. 45–81).

### Practices, institutions, economy

**Kingship and the four classes of courtier (Nizami)**
- Kingship is ranked next after "the Prophetic Function", "naught else than a Divine gift" (p. 21, folio 3). "The king, therefore, is the lieutenant of the Imam, the Imam of the Prophet, and the Prophet of God" (p. 29, folio 11).
- Kings need "the Secretary, the Poet, the Astrologer and the Physician" (p. 30, folio 12). Plan of the book: four Discourses, each with philosophical considerations then "ten pleasing anecdotes" (p. 30, folio 12).
- Courtly custom of the preface: an author praises his patron; Nizami instead lists the favours God granted the king (p. 20, folio 2).
- The Secretary's qualifications and reading list: the Qur'an, the Traditions, Arabic and Persian works incl. Rudagi, "the Epic of Firdawsi", 'Unsuri (pp. 31–33, folios 13–15).
- Royal councils were "held and adjourned" until unanimous on ambassadors' enigmas; the Ministry of Posts is an obsolete royal custom under the Saljuqs (p. 44, folio 26). Embassy costs: "disbursements from the treasury for the expenses of the ambassadors … and the maintenance of the Imams" (pp. 44–45, folios 26–27).
- Communication: carrier-pigeon followed by courier; Iskafi's despatch "As for Makan, he hath become as his name" (p. 36, folio 18).
- Dedication and royal titulature: the Conclusion praises Husamu'd-Dawla wa'd-Dunya wa'd-Din Abu'l-Hasan 'Ali ibn Mas'ud, "exterminator of heathens and infidels, subduer of heretics and innovators", and Fakhru'd-Dawla wa'd-Din, "King of the kings of the Mountains" (p. 116, folio 98).
- Political submission via the khutba (the Notes): Mahmud ordered Abu'l-'Abbas Ma'mun "to recognize him as his ov^r-lord and insert his name *ri the khutba" [text-layer?] (p. 180, folio 162).
- Official language (the Notes): Maymandi "caused all official documents to be written in Arabic, not, as had previously been the case, in Persian" (p. 122, folio 104).

**Court patronage of poets**
- Nizami's rule: the King should patronize a qualified poet; "no money should be wasted" on a poor one, "especially if he be old"; a talented young poet should be patronized "according to the Code of Nobility" (p. 50, folio 32). Improvisation brings rewards: "thus can money be extracted from the treasury" (p. 56, folio 38).
- Nizami on reciprocity: patron and poet each achieve renown through the other, "these two things being interd£pendent [text-layer?] [interdependent]" (p. 71, folio 53).
- Poetic training (Nizami's excursus): memorize "20,000 couplets of the poetry of the Ancients", keep "10,000 verses" of the Moderns as models, study prosody incl. works of "Abu'l-Hasan Bahrami of Sarakhs" (p. 50, folio 32).
- Salaries and allowances: Browne's footnote after the Editor — "jdmagl [text-layer?] ... means wages in cash, while ijrd ... means allowances, especially in kind"; a draft "on Ispahan" for the salary (p. 64, folio 46; p. 66, folio 48).
- Farrukhi's allowance from a dihqan of Sistan: "two hundred measures of corn, each containing five maunds, and a hundred dirhams"; he asked "three hundred measures" and "one hundred and fifty dirhams" and was refused (p. 57, folio 39). Abu'l-Muzaffar "had 18,000 breeding mares", branding the colts yearly at a branding-ground with tents, wine and a great fire; the Amir "drinks wine and gives away horses" (p. 58, folio 40).
- Transoxianan custom: gold and silver placed in trays in audience-chambers, "which they call sim-tdqd [text-layer?] or juft", dispensed "by the handful" (p. 71, folio 53).
- Royal reward to the author: the Prince filled Nizami's mouth with gold twice, and his sleeve (p. 93, folio 75).
- The Notes: Zinati "receiving a handsome present for his verse" (p. 133, folio 115); Mas'udi incurred Sultan Mas'ud's anger by verses warning against the Saljuqs (p. 134, folio 116); poets imprisoned with their patrons (Mas'ud-i-Sa'd, p. 135, folio 117); Tahir ibn Fadl "a generous patron of poets" (p. 141, folio 123); Qilij Tamghaj Khan Ibrahim celebrated for "his generosity to men of letters", prose works dedicated to him (p. 145, folio 127).

**Patronage of astrologers, physicians and men of learning**
- Rewards for an astrologer: "a horse caparisoned with gold, a royal iobe [text-layer?] [robe], a satin turban, a thousand dinars, a boy slave and a handmaiden" for Abu Rayhan (p. 84, folio 66).
- The author's own grant: the King gave Nizami the revenue of the Warsad lead-mine for a festival period; Browne's footnote: "from the end of Ramadan until the roth [text-layer?] of Dhu'l-Hijja, a period of two months and ten days" (p. 79, folio 61). Browne reports that Mirza Muhammad "suggests that our author, Nizamf [text-layer?] [Nizami], was a Sayyirf [text-layer?] [Sayyid], or descendant of the Prophet, and that the khums, or fifth part of the profits ... was in this case made over wholly to him"; an alternative reading is a net 12,000 maunds after deducting the khums (p. 79, folio 61).
- Ar-Razi's honorarium (Nizami): "a horse fully caparisoned, 3. cloak [text-layer?] [a cloak], turban and arms, and a slave- boy and a handmaiden", and "in Ray from the estates of al-Ma'mun a yearly allowance of two thousand dinars in gold and two hundred ass-loads of corn" (p. 103, folio 85); earlier the Amir "sent a thousand dinars and one of his own private horses fully caparisoned" (p. 101, folio 83). On the Amir's recovery "his servants and retainers and people rejoiced greatly, and gave alms, and offered sacrifices, and held high festival" (p. 103, folio 85).
- Physicians (Nizami): Qabus "maintained Abu 'Ali… in the best manner possible" (p. 108, folio 90); Fadl "enriched the Catholicos and sent him back to Pars" (p. 114, folio 96); Adib Isma'il "derived his income and livelihood from his receipts as a physician" (p. 111, folio 93); at Gurgan "people began to bring him [Avicenna] their water in the morning for him to look at, and he began to earn an income" (p. 106, folio 88).
- Avicenna as minister (Nizami, via Abu Kalanjar's narration): before dawn he wrote "a couple of pages of the Shifa", then taught disciples and led prayers; "A thousand mounted men" met him, "two thousand" by the time he reached the Government Offices; "there was no third person between him and the King" (p. 110, folio 92).
- Earlier patronage (Nizami): Iskafi's salary increased after the pigeon despatch (p. 36, folio 18); the Caliph gave a distracted scribe "means sufficiently ample" (p. 37, folio 19); Fadl scattered wax pearls each naming a village and sent the finder "the title-deeds of that village" (p. 40, folio 22).
- The Notes: from Jurjis I's time "until the middle of the eleventh century some member of the family was always one of the chief physicians of the Court at Baghdad"; Jurjis I stayed at court "in spite of his refusal to forsake the Christian for the Muhammadan faith" (p. 164, folio 146). Razi was "struck on the fyead [text-layer?] [head] by his disappointed patron Manstir [text-layer?], governor of Ray" over failed alchemy, and became blind (p. 167, folio 149). Abu Sahl-i-Masihi was one of "the numerous m^n [text-layer?] of learning who found patronage and protection at the Court of Ma'mun" (p. 173, folio 155); al-Biruni "spent seven years (A.H. 400-407 =A.D. 1010-1017) at the Court of Abu'l-'Abbas, where he held various offices" (p. 180, folio 162).
- Observatory (the Notes, per Ibnu'l-Athir under "A.H. 467 (A.D. 1074-5)"): Nizamu'l-Mulk and Sultan Malikshah assembled astronomers incl. "'Umar ibn Ibrahim al-Khayyami" and fixed the Naw-ruz; "A great amount of wealth was expended upon it"; in use "until the King died in A.H. 485 (A. 1)^1092-3 [text-layer?])", then disused (pp. 154–155, folios 136–137).

**Taxation, revenue and endowments**
- Taxation (Nizami, Anecdote VI): the Lamghanis complain against a tax-gatherer "on account of a maund of chaff or a single egg"; Ahmad-i-Hasan remitted "that year's taxes" and a second year; on the third petition he wrote "The tax is a running sore: its cure is its discharge" (p. 38, folio 20; p. 39, folio 21).
- Land-tax (Nizami): Huyayy son of Qutayba, "revenue-collector of Tus", abated Firdawsi's taxes (p. 73, folio 55).
- Endowment of a public work (Nizami): Firdawsi's refused royal gift was given "to the Imam Abu Bakr ibn Ish^q-i-Krramf [text-layer?]" for "the repair of the rest-house of Chaha ... between Merv and Nfshapur [text-layer?]", which was carried out; the preacher was expelled from Tabaran (p. 77, folio 59). Browne's footnote: he was head of "the Kira"mi [text-layer?] sect at Nishapur", a sect that "inclined to anthropomorphism" (p. 77, folio 59).
- Nizamu'l-Mulk prepared for death (Nizami): he "confirmed all his pious endowments, gave effect to his bequests, wrote his last testament, set free such of his slaves as had earned his approval, discharged the debts" (p. 88, folio 70; the sentence is column-garbled in the text layer).
- Endowment (the Notes, Narshakhi quoted): Amir Isma'il the Samani bought the Ju-yi-Muliyan estates and "made palaces and gardens … most of which he conferred on the Mawalis, and which are still endowments" (p. 139, folio 121).

**Burial, tombs and visits to graves**
- Burial dispute (Nizami): a preacher in Tabaran refused Firdawsi's burial "in the Musulman Cemetery, because he was a Rafidf (Shi'a)"; he was buried in his own garden within the Gate, "and there he lies to this day" (p. 77, folio 59).
- Tomb visit (Nizami): "in the year A.H.5io(A.D. II 16-1 1 17) [text-layer?] I visited his tomb" — at Firdawsi's tomb (p. 77, folio 59).
- Grave visitation (Nizami): he visited 'Umar-i-Khayyam's grave "on the eve of a Friday (seeing that he had the claim of a master on me), taking with me one to point out to me his tomb"; the tomb was "at the foot of a garden-wall, over which pear-trees and peach-trees thrust their heads" (p. 89, folio 71). (The prediction and the flowers: see Legends.)
- Burial (the Notes): Abu Bakr Muhammad ibn Muhtaj "was buried at Chaghaniyan"; his son "buried with his father at Chaghaniyan"; the grandson also "buried at Chaghaniyan" (p. 140, folio 122).
- Funerary dress (the Notes, from al-Mafarrukhi's History of Isfahan): a dying nobleman wants a shroud of Miqradi fabric, "unwilling to appear before God less sumptuously clad" (p. 126, folio 108).
- Avicenna's tomb (the Notes): "He died of colic on the march to Hamadan (where his tomb still exists) in 428/1037" (p. 174, folio 156).

**Medicine: theory, diagnosis, practice, curriculum**
- Physiology (Nizami): the four plant forces (Attractive, Retentive, Assimilative, Expulsive) and three faculties incl. "the Reproductive Faculty" (p. 24, folio 6); five external and five internal senses located in brain ventricles (pp. 25–26, folios 7–8); the "Animal Spirit" in the heart, "Psychic Spirit" in the brain, "Natural Spirit" in the liver (p. 26, folio 8).
- Diagnosis by pulse and urine (Nizami): pulse ("systole,- a diastole, and a pause"; "the pulse is of ten sorts") and urine (p. 94, folio 76); pulse and urine again in the anecdotes (pp. 108, 112, 113; folios 90, 94, 95).
- Treatments (Nizami): letting a nosebleed run, "three hundred dirhams weight of blood" (p. 109, folio 91); Avicenna's mock-butcher cure (p. 111, folio 93); Ansari's urine sent "under the name of another" (p. 112, folio 94); prescriptions in "istar" units — Browne's footnote: "A weight consisting of 4^ [text-layer?] mithqals" (p. 112, folio 94).
- The author's own practice (Nizami): he cured a nobleman's daughter by opening "the basilic vein in both her arms", taking "a thousand dirhams' weight of blood"; "most of the ten indications were present" (p. 115, folio 97); "I called her my daughter, and she called me her father" (p. 116, folio 98).
- Curriculum (Nizami): elementary — Hippocrates' "Aphorisms" (Fusiil [text-layer?]), Hunayn ibn Ishaq's "Questions", ar-Razi's "Guide" (Murshid), the Commentary of "Nih'" [text-layer?]; intermediate — Thabit ibn Qurra's "Thesaurus", ar-Razi's Mansuri, Abu Bakr "Ajwi'n(" [text-layer?]'s "Direction", Ahmad [ibn] Faraj's "Sufficiency", Sayyid Isma'il Jurjani's "Aims" (p. 96, folio 78); advanced — Galen's "Sixteen Treatises", ar-Razi's "Continens", "Kdmilus-Sindfaty" [text-layer?] [Kamilu's-Sina'at], Abu Sahl Masihi's "Hundred Chapters", the Qanun, the "Dnakhira-i-Kkwdrazm-shdht" [text-layer?] [Dhakhira-i-Khwarazmshahi] (p. 97, folio 79). A physician is "worthy of confidence" once he "hath mastered the first volume of the Qdmin [text-layer?] [Qanun], and hath attained to forty years of age" (p. 98, folio 80).
- Religious hostility to medicine (Nizami): Ansari's dislike "arose from religious motives, for the people of Herat believed that he could restore the dead to life, and this belief was injurious to the common people" (p. 112, folio 94).
- Hospitals (the Notes): the Bimaristan at Jundi-Sabur (p. 164, folio 146); Razi directed hospitals at Ray and Baghdad (p. 167, folio 149). Language at Jundi-Sabur: an Arab physician complains (al-Jahiz) that "his language is Arabic" and it "should have been the language of Jund*-Sabur" (p. 165, folio 147).
- Diagnosis (the Notes, Note XXVI): ten genera of pulse, "37 primary varieties of pulse" (pp. 158–159, folios 140–141); urine inspection, the vessel called "Tafsira" (p. 160, folio 142); "sarsdnc [text-layer?] [sarsam]" ("delirium") = Phrenitis, which "would therefore appear to be equivalent to meningitis" (p. 160, folio 142); fever defined and classified from the Dhakhira-i-Khwarazmshahi, Book V, incl. the hot-bath analogy (hectic, humoristic, quotidian) and "Thirteen kinds of fever" (pp. 160–162, folios 142–144).

### Legends and miracles (karamat)

*Attributed as the book attributes them. Most are told by Nizami as anecdotes of foresight, astrology or medicine rather than as saints' miracles.*

- The nasnas (Nizami): "a creature inhabiting the plains of Turkistan", carries off a solitary man, "and it is even said that it will conceive from him" (p. 27, folio 9). Abu Rida's report (Anecdote I): a naked, beautiful woman on a sand-hill fled faster than a horse; the Turkish muleteers called her "a wild man, such as they call nasnds" (p. 27, folio 9).
- Nizami: "the vine flees from the bind-weed" (p. 24, folio 6).
- 'Umar-i-Khayyam's grave (Nizami): 'Umar foretold his grave would be "in a spot where the trees will shed their blossoms on me twice a year"; Nizami found it covered with fallen flower-leaves "so that his dust was hidden beneath the flowers", and wept (p. 89, folio 71; the quotation is column-garbled in the text layer).
- Al-Kindi (Nizami) read the hidden words "The Rod of Moses" by astrology and saw that Abu Ma'shar had "come from the East to slay me" (pp. 82–83, folios 64–65).
- Abu Rayhan (Nizami) foretold the fifth door, and his own fall from a height with a safe landing (pp. 83–84, folios 65–66).
- The woman-servant (Nizami) predicted that a lost son had returned, attributing it to "a fly came and settled on the number of the degree of the Ascendant" (p. 86, folio 68).
- Abu Bakr Daqqaq's report (as heard by Nizami): after all remedies failed he recited the Fatiha "breathing it in that direction", citing "Quran, xvii, 84"; the patient "This very hour ... obtained relief", which Daqqaq attributed to "the blessing of the Fdtiha [text-layer?] [Fatiha]", "a draught ... issued from the Divine Dispensary", and he says it proved beneficial "in many cases" (pp. 95–96, folios 77–78).
- Avicenna at Gurgan (Nizami) diagnosed love by the patient's pulse fluttering at the names of district, street, house and girl (pp. 106–107, folios 88–89).
- Abu Sahl (Nizami) foresaw his own death from his Ascendant: "Henceforth only the intercourse of souls will exist between us" (p. 105, folio 87).
- Khwaja Isma'il raises a butcher reported dead; Nizami frames it as foresight, not miracle: "men were astonished, because that great man had foreseen that the man would be stricken by apoplexy" (p. 112, folio 94). Side by side, the Notes: "The almost miraculous cure of the butcher related in Anecdote XXXIX of this book" is ascribed by Qifti and Ibn Abi Usaybi'a to Thabit ibn Qurra (p. 166, folio 148).
- Note I: the Ghurid ancestor Shansab "is said" to have received from 'Ali "a standard and recognition of his rule" (p. 119, folio 101).
- The Notes: Hunayn was once seen "in the guise of a long-haired wandering bard reciting Homer in the streets" (p. 165, folio 147). Razi refused an eye operation on learning the surgeon "was ignorant of the anatomy of the eye", saying "he had looked on the world until he was tired of it" (p. 167, folio 149).
- Browne: a qasida "ascribed to" Avicenna "foretells with extraordinary prevision the Mongol invasion, the sack of Baghdad" and the battle of 'Ayn Jalut "in A.D. 1260", "based on astrological considerations" (pp. 174–175, folios 156–157).
- Mirza Muhammad, on the authority of adh-Dhahabi: Ansari's opponents produced "a little copper image" claiming he worshipped it; the King, convinced of his innocence, "dismissed him with honour and punished his detractors" (p. 181, folio 163).

### Arguments and interpretations

**Nizami's own judgements**
- Cosmography: water evaporated by the sun bared "one quarter of the earth's surface" — the "Uncovered" or "Inhabited Quarter" (p. 23, folio 5); the kingdoms are linked successively: clay to coral, thorn to date-palm and vine, maggot to nasnas, then man with "the capacity for abstract concepts" (pp. 24–27, folios 6–9).
- Prophets reach knowledge "without master or book", Philosophers by study (p. 28, folio 10). The Qur'an's eloquence proves "this Word did not proceed from the mouth of any created being" (p. 43, folio 25).
- The secretary: "an intelligent and accomplished secretary is the greatest ornament to a king's magnificence" (interleaved lines [text-layer?]) (p. 45, folio 27).
- Poetry is the art whereby the poet "can make a little thing appear great and a great thing small" and moves men's temperaments (p. 45, folio 27); a king's name endures "by reason of the poet's verse" when army and treasure vanish (p. 47, folio 29). Rudagi's couplet contains "seven admirable touches of art"; Browne reports Mirza Muhammad's objection that the first and second are identical and that "style" is not a rhetorical artifice (p. 54, folio 36).
- On Mas'ud-i-Sa'd's imprisonment: "it was not a laudable deed", and "the ill repute of this deed remained on this noble House" (p. 69, folio 51); "that misery of Mas'ud passed, while this ill repute will endure till the Resurrection" (p. 70, folio 52).
- On Firdawsi's verse: "In eloquence I know of^no [text-layer?] poetry in Persian which equals this, and but little even in Arabic" (p. 73, folio 55).
- The astrologer needs "acute mind, approved character, and great natural intelligence, though apparently [some degree of] folly, madness and a gift for soothsaying are amongst the conditions" (p. 81, folio 63); the Da'udi story is told "that the King may know that as regards astrological predictions folly and insanity are amongst the conditions of this craft" (p. 87, folio 69). He attributes the woman-servant's success to "the Part of the Unseen" on "the degree of the Ascendant" (p. 86, folio 68).
- On 'Umar: "I did not observe that he had any great belief in astrological predictions; nor have I seen or heard of any of the great [scientists] who had such belief"; "prognostication by the stars, though a recognized art, is no^£ [text-layer?] to be relied on ... whatever the astrologer predicts he must leave to Fate" (p. 90, folio 72).
- Royal service: Mahmud, as reported by Nizami: "kings are like little children; in order to receive rewards from them, one should speak in accordance with their views" (p. 84, folio 66); Nizami: "one of the conditions of the king's service" is "that one must be with him in right or wrong, and speak according to his wish" (p. 85, folio 67).
- Religion and service: the King should keep near him one who "is a believer in the Holy Law"; "Whoever does not believe in the religion of God ... in him can no man trust" (pp. 90–91, folios 72–73).
- Medicine maintains, restores and embellishes health (p. 93, folio 75). The physician needs tender disposition, wise nature (Logic) and acumen; "all these indications depend on Divine help and Royal patronage" (p. 94, folio 76). Logic illustrated by disease as genus, fevers as species (quotidian, tertian, double tertian, quartan) (pp. 94–95, folios 76–77). If he fails, "let him turn to God" (p. 95, folio 77); "the physician should be of good faith, and should venerate the commands and pro- hibitions ofthe 'Holy Law" (p. 96, folio 78). "Every King who would choose a physician must see that these conditions ... are found in him" (p. 98, folio 80). "One cannot heal by such methods of treatment save by virtue of extreme excellence, perfect science, and unerring acumen" (p. 111, folio 93).
- He praises the Qanun ("could Hippocrates and Galen return to life, it were meet that they should do reverence to this book") and condemns the author of "the Rectification of the Qdniin" [text-layer?] (p. 97, folio 79); Aristotle ordered philosophy after "four thousand years"; only Avicenna in "fifteen centuries" reached his doctrine (pp. 97–98, folios 79–80).
- Statement of purpose: "not to flaunt my merits or recall my services, but rather to guiderthe [text-layer?] [guide the] beginner, and also to glorify my master" (p. 116, folio 98).

**Browne on the book and its author**
- Nizami's poetry "was not ... of the highest order" and "far inferior to his prose", "almost unequalled in Persian" (p. 13, folio xi).
- The book holds "the only contemporary account of 'Umar Khayyam, and the oldest known account of Firdawsf [text-layer?]"; composition placed "with certainty between the years 547/1152 and 552/1 157 [text-layer?], and with great probability in the year 55I/II561 [text-layer?]" (p. 14, folio xii). Side by side, the Notes (Note III): the Maqamat mention fixes the date of composition "between these two limits (A.H. 551-552; A.D. 1156-1157)", since Sultan Sanjar is still living in the text and "died in 552/1157-8" (p. 121, folio 103). (Browne's footnote at p. 85: "about A.H. 551 •or 552?" — see Doubts.)
- Citing Mirza Muhammad: "Fifteen such blunders" of history by the author, some "of the grossest character"; not all ascribable to copyists "since the point of the story is in several cases dependent on the error" (p. 14, folio xii).
- On the Notes on 'Umar: the author "was perspnally [text-layer?] [personally] acquainted with 'Umar and wrote [lines scrambled] some thirty years after his death" — apparently "the oldest account of him yet discovered" (p. 153, folio 135).
- 'Umar is far more celebrated in the West than in Persia, "where no one would think of ranking 'Umar as a poet in the same category as Firdawsi, Sa'di or Hafiz"; reasons: FitzGerald, substance over form, and mood congenial to the age (pp. 157–158, folios 139–140).
- Divergence on the merit of Rudaki's verses between the author and Dawlatshah indicates "a complete change in the canons of taste … and, it must be admitted, a change for the worse" (p. 139, folio 121).
- Razi "was probably the greatest practitioner of the so-called Arabian Medicine who ever lived", and as clinician "far surpassed" Avicenna (p. 166, folio 148); the Hawi "stands on an altogether different plane from the Qdnun [text-layer?]" (p. 167, folio 149). The Kamilu's-Sina'a "is, in my opinion, far superior in style, arrangement and interest to Avicenna's Qanun" (p. 173, folio 155).
