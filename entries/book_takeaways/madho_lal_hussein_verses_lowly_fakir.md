# Verses of a Lowly Fakir — takeaways

*Source: Madho Lal Hussein; Naveed Alam (tr.), *Verses of a Lowly Fakir* (2015). 190 pages,
transcribed `text_layer`, chunked in three parts; provenance in
`out/ocr/madho_lal_hussein_verses_lowly_fakir/`. Page references are PDF page indices; folio =
printed page number where known — in this book the bare number at each page foot equals the PDF index
on every non-blank page of all three chunks, with no offset anywhere, which is itself the single
biggest caveat on every reference below (see Doubts). Reviewed: no.*

## What this book is

A complete English verse translation of the 152 numbered *kafis* attributed to Shah Hussein, with a
long translator's introduction ("The Disgraceful Saint: An Introduction", pp. 6–19), a kafi-keyed
endnote apparatus (pp. 178–187) and an Acknowledgements page (p. 188). It is a primary source in
translation, not a monograph: for the saint's life the translator works from "the scant biographical
sources available" (p. 7, folio 7) and names three of them — *Haqiqat al-Fuqra* (c. 1660, Muhammad
Pir), *Tehqiqat-i-Chisti* (Noor Ahmed Chisti) and the lost *Baharia* of Bahar Khan — while arguing
against all of them (p. 8, folio 8). Naveed Alam's stance is explicitly revisionist and explicitly
queer: he sets the poet and the weaver against the saint the hagiographers made, and states that the
volume "uses Hussein's self-given name in the title as homage to the poet's queer passion" (p. 6,
folio 6).

**Reliability.** The `text_layer` is clean for ordinary English prose — ligatures are intact
("fifteenth", p. 7; "official", p. 8; "flaws", p. 60), as are accented characters ("naïve", p. 12;
"outré", p. 11) and en/em dashes ("1538–99", p. 6). Six word-level `[text-layer?]` flags survive
into this file and are carried on their words below; there are **no `[illegible]` flags anywhere**.
The serious losses are structural rather than lexical: **italics are lost throughout all three
chunks**, which blurs the extent of every quoted kafi line inside the prose, every transliterated
Punjabi term and every book title, and which defeats note 152.3's own instruction ("The italicized
phrases are in Arabic", p. 187, folio 187). **No Punjabi, Perso-Arabic or Gurmukhi script appears
anywhere in the 190 pages** — every name, term and title is in Latin transliteration or English —
so the protocol's "quote the original script" rule cannot be satisfied from this book at all.

### madho-lal-hussain — Shrine of Hazrat Madho Lal Hussain (Shah Hussain Darbar)

The book's subject. The volume spells him **"Shah Hussein" / "Madho Lal Hussein" / "Hussein"**
throughout — never "Hussain", except inside one quotation from N. Hanif (p. 10, folio 10) — against
the archive row's "-ain". Both spellings are preserved exactly where each occurs; the house form is
a decision for Rauf, not for this file (see Doubts).

- **Saint**
  - Dates and the double name, as printed: "The portrait of Shah Hussein (1538–99), popularly known
    as Madho Lal Hussein" (p. 6, folio 6). The fusion is stated as fact and read as doctrine: "The
    fact that Hussein literally fused his name with Madho's and came to be known as Madho Lal
    Hussein is not only testament to the enduring legacy of love, but also illustrates an essential
    tenet of Sufism, the merger of the lover and the beloved" (p. 6, folio 6).
  - Trades and standing, in the book's own order: "Weaver, mystic, fakir, Shah Hussein continues to
    command great reverence in Punjab as a poet–saint more than 400 years after his death" (p. 6,
    folio 6). The translator ranks the weaver above the saint and says the hagiographers reversed
    it: they "are not concerned with Hussein, the poet, or Hussein, the self-proclaimed weaver, but
    remain fixated on Hussein, the saint" (p. 8, folio 8). In the verse the trade is attached to his
    name directly — "O Hussein, the weaver! He earned no profit" (p. 176, folio 176, kafi 151).
  - Family and origins: "Hussein's father, a Rajput from the Kalsrai clan, converted to Islam during
    the Delhi Sultanate (1206–1526) and took on the Muslim name of Sheikh Usman" (p. 6, folio 6);
    matriline via Dara Shikoh's name for him — "He calls him Hussein Dadha, using Hussein's
    matrilineal family name (his mother belonged to the Dadha clan of Rajputs)" (p. 8, folio 8); and
    the family name again in the apparatus — "Sheikh, the scholar, is also the family name of
    Hussein" (p. 181, folio 181). **No birthplace and no ancestral village: (not stated).**
  - Schooling and the two teachers: "As a boy, Hussein joined the local mosque which also functioned
    as a madrasa, where memorizing the Quran by rote was the main method of learning" (p. 6, folio
    6); the itinerant dervish "Sheikh Bahlol Dariai, who took him under his wing" and who "had
    travelled across the wide expanse of the fifteenth-century Islamic world, including Arabia,
    Mesopotamia, Persia and Afghanistan" (pp. 6–7, folios 6–7); then "at the age of thirty-six, while
    studying with another respected scholar of the time, Sheikh Sa'ad Ullah", the turn (p. 7, folio
    7). The two Quranic passages are cited as `Sura Al-Hadid 57: 20` [text-layer?:
    `Sura Al-Hadid 57:20`] (p. 7, folio 7) and "(Sura Ash-Sharh 94:1–4)" (p. 7, folio 7).
  - A third teacher, and the only one named in the verse: "Says Hussein, this the incantation taught
    by Shah Jalal" (p. 177, folio 177, kafi 152). No city, date, order, tomb or gloss for him:
    **(not stated)** — see the Shah Jalal bullet in the next section.
  - ***Malamati* is the book's word for his path, and it glosses it three times.** "the promising
    young student turned malamati—one who seeks disgrace and censure—leading his pack of revellers
    through the town" (p. 7, folio 7); "as the prescribed norm of a malamati mystic" (p. 9, folio 9);
    "For a malamati, the ignominy ensures the grace" (p. 10, folio 10); "Hussein's malamati-style
    self-humiliation reaches its apogee in the line: I'm the bitch at your doorstep (kafi 5)" (p. 11,
    folio 11); and in the apparatus, "Shah Hussein's humility cuts like an axe (kafi 34), for he's a
    malamati, blameworthy, an outcast, a rebel, a transgressor of the bogus conventions of love, the
    one who breaks free" (p. 180, folio 180). **Beyond Bahlol Dariai, Sa'ad Ullah and the bare "Shah
    Jalal" the book names no silsila, no pir–murid chain and no khilafat: (not stated).**
  - His status word, glossed against the Arabic and supplying the volume's title: "In Arabic, fakir
    means poor, but in Punjabi, it's the mendicant, dervish, beggar, pauper. What kind of a fakir is
    Hussein? He is a fakir nimana—a fakir without ma'an or pride, worthless, wretched, of the lowest
    order, a lumpen proletariat" (p. 180, folio 180). The introduction had already named the device:
    "In almost every concluding line, Hussein addresses himself as fakir nimana (the lowly fakir) or
    fakir Sain da (the Lord's fakir)" (p. 16, folio 16).
  - **The signature epithets, as the two verse chunks actually print them** — at least twenty-six
    variants: "the worthless fakir" (pp. 21, 53, 58), "the fakir of the Sain" (p. 22), "the Sain's
    fakir" (pp. 23, 24, 25, 28, 29, 34, 38, 42, 43, 49, 50, 52, 57, 62, 63, 64, 76 and some thirty
    further pages), "the Sain's devotee" (pp. 26, 96), "the humble fakir" (pp. 35, 74), "the lowly
    fakir" (pp. 36, 49, 60, 92 — p. 92 is the phrase the volume is titled from), "the
    beggar-wanderer" (p. 48), "the wanderer" (p. 51), "the worthless devotee" (p. 32), "the devotee
    of the Sain" (pp. 44, 108), "the beggar fakir" (pp. 65, 133), "the Master's fakir" (pp. 69, 70),
    "the miserable mendicant" (p. 73), "the wretched fakir" (p. 82), "Shah Hussein, the poor fakir"
    (p. 84), "the wandering fakir" (p. 104), "the God's fakir" (p. 105), "God's fakir" (p. 165), "the
    host of senses" (p. 102), "the helpless fakir" (p. 174), and the bare "Says Hussein loud and
    clear" (pp. 54, 61) — folios equal the PDF index throughout.
  - **He refuses the title of master in his own voice**: "One called Shah Hussein is a fakir, / don't
    call him a master / I'm not fond of falsehood" (p. 134, folio 134, kafi 110).
  - Appearance and habit: "a rebel fakir dressed in all red, bearing a wine flask in one hand and an
    earthen bowl in another" (p. 7, folio 7); "drunkenly he roams the streets of Lahore in the
    company of fellow carousers" (p. 6, folio 6); "Shah Hussein is known to wander around in a red
    dress" (p. 180, folio 180). The red is read into the poetry as "the colour of passion: lal (red)"
    (p. 13, folio 13). No date or source is named for the red dress: **(not stated)**.
  - He avoids the name Allah: "Hussein rarely uses Allah. If not Sain, then it is Rab, Maula, Ram.
    Actually, he uses Ram quite often (see kafis 68, 80, 81, 89). He even evokes Har, Shiva (kafis
    125, 145). Perhaps perceived as the god of nobles, sultans and emperors, Allah was not in vogue
    among the common folk Hussein chose to associate with" (p. 181, folio 181); restated at "One of
    the rare mentions of Allah. Mostly Sain, Ram, Rab and Maula have been used" (pp. 185–186, folios
    185–186 — the note line is duplicated by the text layer across the page break, see Doubts).
  - He participates in Holi: "whereas Hussein's participation in the Hindu festival of Holi fails to
    draw the authors' attention towards the spiritual syncretism of the lover and the beloved" (p. 8,
    folio 8). No place, year or description of the observance: **(not stated)**.
  - The only property named as his in the verse: "Blackbuck, you grazed / the borders of Shah
    Hussein's fields" (p. 71, folio 71, kafi 49). No location, extent, grant or revenue:
    **(not stated)**.
  - The book's one run of biographical negatives, in kafi 151: "Never got engaged, never got married
    / Never fixed a date, never held a wedding / Neither a householder nor a traveller / Neither a
    man of faith nor an infidel / He is what he is" (p. 176, folio 176).
  - He addresses and abuses himself by name: "Why such self-importance, Hussein? / Who do you think
    you are? You are / a weed among the garden's fancy flowers" (p. 59, folio 59, kafi 37); "Shah
    Hussein, you dotard, / gaps widen between your teeth" (p. 64, folio 64, kafi 42); "Shah Hussein
    stumbling at the very end" (p. 40, folio 40, kafi 18); "Shah Hussein lies at your doorstep, Sain,
    / yearning for your name" (p. 46, folio 46, kafi 24).
  - **The speaking voice of the kafis is a woman's, and specifically a working weaver girl's**: "This
    miserable one is caught in cotton" (p. 68, folio 68); "A weaver woman tied to the loom / I worked
    my spindles despite the detractors" (p. 107, folio 107); "I'm the lowly cleaner at the esteemed
    house" (p. 111, folio 111); "Shah Hussein, I'm the dowry-less" (p. 136, folio 136).
- **Madho Lal, the second figure of this row**
  - Origins and religion, in Muhammad Pir's words quoted by the translator: "Madho was very beautiful
    and eccentrically proud. He was a Brahmin's son… He was a Hindu, an infidel, a playful teaser,
    and cold-hearted" (p. 9, folio 9). His conversion is referred to but never narrated — "Hussein's
    young lover's subsequent conversion to Islam is treated as a necessary step towards the latter's
    salvation" (p. 8, folio 8). His home town: "The tomb of Jehangir in Shahdara, Madho's hometown"
    (p. 18, folio 18). **No dates for Madho at all, and no death year: (not stated).**
  - **The move and the cohabitation, stated flatly in the apparatus**: "The disgrace here for
    proximity to the lover lends itself to the queer interpretation. In pursuit of Madho, Hussein
    moved across the river Ravi to the town of Shahdara. The two later cohabited together" (p. 181,
    folio 181). No date and no source: **(not stated)**.
  - He is addressed directly in one kafi only, and the book says so twice: "Madho is addressed
    directly in only one kafi (kafi 21), and even there it's open to interpretation since the word
    'Lal' can also mean dear or precious one. Ditto for kafi 102 where Hussein mentions Lal in the
    penultimate line (Haven't found Lal yet)" (p. 10, folio 10); "The only kafi where Madho Lal is
    addressed directly, though 'Lal' can also be interpreted as the general term of endearment. Also
    see kafis 101, 114" (p. 181, folio 181). Kafi 21 as printed opens "Dear Lal! How can this life be
    trusted?" (p. 43, folio 43); kafi 102 has "Forest and wilderness she searches / Haven't found Lal
    yet" (p. 126, folio 126); kafi 114 has "Lal, I am inside out, that's my business, / that's my
    occupation" (p. 138, folio 138). The ambiguity is restated three more times: "Here, Lal could
    mean the colour red, beloved, or Madho Lal", "The only kafi with two references to Lal", and
    "Madho Lal or the colour red? I'd prefer the former" (all p. 185, folio 185).
  - An age gap is asserted by a named modern scholar, not by the translator: "Another modern scholar,
    Dr Syed Nazir Ahmed, calculates the thirty-eight-year age gap between the two and concludes:
    '[W]ith such a difference of age even the possibility of a romantic relationship between them is
    repulsive to the mild taste.'9" (p. 10, folio 10). The book gives no birth year for Madho from
    which the gap could be checked: **(not stated)**.
- **Shrine / Darbar**

    still celebrated at his shrine in Baghbanpura, Lahore. In the inner sanctum of the shrine, buried

    folio 6).
  - Restated at the close of the introduction with its neighbour named: "the modest shrine of Shah
    Hussein in Baghbanpura near another dilapidated wonder of the Mughal era, the Shalimar Gardens"
    (pp. 18–19, folios 18–19).
  - **No building date, no founder, no custodian, no sajjada nashin, no Auqaf, no endowment, no
    landholding, no revenue figure, no visitor count, no dimensions and no dispute appear anywhere in

  - **Topography beyond "Baghbanpura" and "near the Shalimar Gardens" is absent**: Baghbanpura,

- **Practices & Events**
  - The ʿurs, named, counted and dated: "The last week of March 2016 will mark the 428th urs of Shah
    Hussein, aka Madho Lal Hussein. The celebration is commonly known as `Mela Chiraga’an`
    [text-layer?: `Mela Chiragha'an`], the Festival of Lamps" (p. 19, folio 19). The book gives the
    count and the window **and nothing else** — no attendance, no lamp ritual described, no
    procession, no programme, no organiser, no cost: **(not stated)**. See Doubts on the arithmetic
    of "428th" against the printed dates.
  - Daily offerings at the two graves: "The side-by-side graves of Hussein and Madho are always heaped
    with fresh rose and marigold garlands offered by the daily throngs of devotees" (p. 19, folio 19).
    This is the book's only statement of shrine practice outside the ʿurs; it names rose and marigold
    garlands and no cash, no langar and no donation box: **(not stated)**.
  - The convention the book reads the ʿurs by: "The urs of a saint is celebrated as a wedding, the
    union of the bride/mortal with the groom/divine" (p. 11, folio 11) — presented as the convention
    behind Hussein's female narrator, not as an observation of this or any particular shrine.
- **Legends & Miracles (karamat)**
  - **Breaking chains and the doubled body before Akbar**, given first as part of a composite portrait
    "based on traditional sources" — he "breaks off the chains when officials approach to arrest him,
    baffles Akbar, the reigning emperor of the time, by appearing in his private chambers when he's
    supposed to be locked up" (p. 6, folio 6) — and then again expressly as legend: "According to
    legend, the emperor was unimpressed by the wine-to-non-alcoholic-drinks miracle performed by
    Hussein when he was brought before Akbar for interrogation, and sent him to prison. While Hussein
    was in the dock, his likeness showed up in the harem attended upon by the royal ladies. Mystified
    by the miraculous feat, Akbar acknowledged Hussein's credentials as a saint, and set him free.1"
    (pp. 7–8, folios 7–8).
  - **Transformation of wine** — he "turns wine into water, sherbet, milk, tea, vinegar and back to
    wine again" (p. 6, folio 6), from traditional sources.
  - **The dance at the Quranic verse** — "The words resonated so deeply with Hussein that he broke
    into a spontaneous dance of self-liberation" (p. 7, folio 7); and **the laughter in the middle of
    the prayer** — "In the middle of the prayer, Hussein broke into laughter and ran away when he
    recited these Quranic verses" (p. 7, folio 7).
  - **Hearing through walls** — "He persists, and demonstrates his mystical prowess by hearing what
    Madho says within the walls of his house and repeating the words verbatim" (p. 9, folio 9).
  - **The two lions** — the spy is "told to go back and verify the truth. He returns and finds two
    terrifying lions sitting face-to-face" (p. 9, folio 9); the scene he had first reported ends "and
    kept kissing each other like milk and sugar . . . and then the `two friends became one.7`
    [text-layer?: block-quote indentation lost across the page break]" (pp. 9–10, folios 9–10).
  - **Eluding the police until the hanging** — "Even hagiographers like Muhammad Pir and Chisti admit
    that Hussein kept eluding Akbar's police till he was finally nabbed at the hanging of 'Dullah
    Bhatti, though they make it sound as if he was attending the event as a mere spectator" (p. 15,
    folio 15).
  - **The wish to Akbar**, attributed to a named hagiography: "According to Muhammad Pir's Haqiqat
    al-Fuqra, when Emperor Akbar asked Hussein for any wish to be granted, Hussein requested he never
    be summoned to the royal court again" (p. 181, folio 181) — the note frames it as evidence of a
    "note of anti-establishment".
  - **The blessing of a military victory**, attributed to unnamed hagiographers and then contradicted
    by the poem: "According to some hagiographers, Hussein gave the blessing of a military victory to
    Khan-e-Khana when he came as a supplicant to the saint; however, this kafi points out something
    different: a caveat of mortality to the powerful" (p. 184, folio 184).
  - **The journey to Amritsar to enter the Granth Sahib**, attributed to "some sources" and marked
    unverifiable by the book itself: "According to some sources, Shah Hussein travelled to Amritsar to
    get his poetry included in the Granth Sahib being compiled by Guru Arjun Dev (1563–1605). He
    offered this kafi for inclusion. Though the account is unverifiable, the thematic content of this
    kafi might have lent itself to the speculation" (p. 186, folio 186). See the Guru Arjun Dev
    bullet in the next section.
  - The verse itself contains **no karamat narrative**: across pp. 68–178 no miracle is attributed to
    any named saint or site, and the only supernatural material is doctrinal or cosmological.
- **Significance — text, transmission and reception**
  - **The rescue of the corpus, with figures.** "Dr Mohan Singh Diwana, an early-twentieth-century
    Punjabi scholar, is acknowledged as the undisputed saviour of Hussein's kafis in written form. He
    discovered 138 kafis in a collection of Hindu, Muslim and Sikh mystical poetry by an unknown
    Sindhi editor. The collection was in Gurmukhi script and published in Lahore around 1900"
    (pp. 16–17, folios 16–17), plus "the twenty-five he found in a handwritten Gurmukhi manuscript in
    the Punjab University library (manuscript #374, dated 1804, the oldest textual record of
    Hussein's work)", which together "made up the first Urdu script edition of Hussein's poetry
    published in 1942" (p. 17, folio 17). The apparatus repeats the manuscript with a rival count:
    "Dr Nazir Ahmed, one of editors of Hussein's work, claims to have found a total of forty-eight
    kafis in the same manuscript; however, seven of them were repeated, which leaves forty-one kafis"
    (p. 186, folio 186). Two kafis lie outside it entirely, traced only to "Dr Mohan Singh Diwana's A
    Brief History of Punjabi Literature (p. 80)" (p. 187, folio 187). Thereafter "As more editions
    followed, new kafis were discovered and contributed, the number of kafis rose, and the differences
    between the various versions of kafis, ranging from minor to major, started to emerge" (p. 17,
    folio 17).
  - **The three early sources, with their dates and their limits.** "Haqiqat al-Fuqra (Truth of the
    Saints), written around 1660 by Sheikh Mahmud, commonly known as Muhammad Pir, established the
    trend for hagiography that continues till the present.4"; "Another oft-cited source is the
    Tehqiqat-i-Chisti: Tarikh-i-Lahore ka Encyclopedia (Researches of Chisti: Encyclopedia of
    Lahore's History) by Noor Ahmed Chisti.5 The latter heavily relies on the former" (p. 8, folio 8);
    and the lost log — "Jehangir, Akbar's successor, is supposed to have appointed a person by the

  - **The archival silence, stated twice as a problem.** "There is no account of Hussein in the annals
    of Akbar, though the Mughals were known for their meticulous record-keeping" (p. 8, folio 8); "No
    mention of Hussein is made even in Tuzk-i-Jehangiri,2 Jehangir's official memoir" (p. 8, folio 8).
    Chisti's claim to have used the *Baharia* is called "unverifiable" (p. 8, folio 8).
  - **The editions of his poetry the book uses**, as its notes cite them: "Shah Hussein, *Kafis: Shah
    Hussein*, ed. Muhammad Asif Khan (Lahore: Pakistan Punjabi Adabi Board, 2014; 6th edn), pp. 19–20"
    (note 3) and "Shah Hussein, *Poetry of Shah Hussein*, ed. Syed Nazir Ahmed (Lahore: Babar Ali
    Foundation, 2014; 3rd edn), p. 3" (note 9) (both p. 178, folio 178); "Madho Lal Hussein, Kafis of
    Madho Lal Hussein, ed. Najm Hosain Syed (Lahore: Suchet Kitab Ghar, 2014)" (note 20) and "Shah
    Hussein, Maiy Ni Mein Kino Akhan (O Mother To Whom Can I Tell): Poetry of Shah Hussein, ed. and
    trans. (from Punjabi to Urdu) Saleem Akhtar (Lahore: Book Home, 2010)" (note 21) (both p. 179,
    folio 179).
  - **The numbering is the APNA website's, and the book says so.** "The numbering of the kafis is not
    based on any authenticated preference of the poet. I am following the order of the APNA website:
    http://www.apnaorg.com/poetry/shah/shfront.html" (p. 179, folio 179), with two declared

  - **Two kafis are not in Punjabi**: "This kafi is not in Punjabi but Persianized Hindi, similar to

  - **The best-known kafi, and a sung one**: "Arguably the most popular kafi. Literal translation of

### garh-maharaja-shorkot — Garh Maharaja (Shorkot)

*Principal figure Sultan Bahu. Mapped under the 18 September ruling for an author-only mention;
the whole of the evidence is one quotation with dates and two bibliographic references. See Doubts
before citing.*

- **Saint** — named and quoted as one of the Punjabi Sufi poets who "looked askance at scholars":
  "You read and read, became a learned scholar, a seeker of gold / One hundred thousand books you
  read, but the cruel self didn't die / Without dispossession no one killed the thief within",
  attributed in the book's own line "(Sultan Bahu, 1629–90)16" (p. 15, folio 15). **No shrine, no

## Cross-cutting material

### Practices, institutions, economy

- **Bhang at Sufi shrines, tied explicitly to the ʿurs season — the single most directly usable
  institutional fact in the book**: "Bhang is a cheap intoxicant made with crushed marijuana leaves
  and milk. It's still readily available at many of the Sufi shrines, especially during the festive
  anniversaries. Bhangi, a bhang drinker, is also a term used for sweepers or janitors." (p. 183,
  folio 183). The book does not name which shrines: **(not stated)**. The corresponding kafi asks for
  the equipment outright: "I ask for an earthen pot, a wooden stick, / a store of marijuana / Give me
  a sieve cloth, red pepper… Give me opium, a bowl, a basin full of sugar", and in its Variation "Give
  me the paraphernalia / for bhang-making: a mortar 'n' pestle, / a sieve cloth, red pepper, plenty of
  sugar… Give me the ingredients of a fakir's contentment" (p. 172, folio 172, kafi 147).
- **Shrines as targets of present-day violence** — "No wonder the Sufi shrines, viewed as the
  hashish-clouded dens of singing and dancing, are high on the list of the God-opiated aspirants of
  martyrdom with a penchant for suicide blasts" (pp. 17–18, folios 17–18), attributed to "the
  puritanical Wahabi ideology fuelled by the petrodollars of Gulf sheikhdoms running amok in
  present-day Pakistan" (p. 17, folio 17). **No incident, place or date: (not stated).**
- **Alms and charity at holy men's doors, described as transactional — the sharpest institutional
  passage in the verse**: "It's a selfishly motivated world / That's the truth, O Fakir / Motives come,
  motives go / Motives draw the crowds / to the doorsteps of holy men / Motives make the generous /
  feed the hungry, clothe the naked / No one offers a glass of water / without an ulterior motive"
  (p. 81, folio 81, kafi 59). Paired with "Says Hussein, the lowly fakir, tear up / the ledger of
  saintly business" (p. 123, folio 123, kafi 99) and the refusal of the title of master (p. 134,
  folio 134).
- **The one shrine named in the verse, and it is a dance at one** — "At the shrine she's the bride who
  stood up / for an unrestrained dance" (p. 132, folio 132, kafi 108). The shrine is not named and no
  location is given: **(not stated)**. The related line is "Even prophets, saints, holy men / lie
  entombed and enshrined" (p. 84, folio 84).
- **The commercial afterlife of shrine music** — "the appropriation of spirituality by the slick
  Mammon of late capitalism is reflected in the popularity of Sufi music produced and marketed by Coke
  Studio, a Coca-Cola-sponsored entertainment venture. The Studio has provided a forum for Pakistani
  artists to fuse classical, folk and modern rhythms into catchy tunes, all the rage on the radio,
  fashion-show runways and in the music collections of the urban youth" (p. 17, folio 17).
- **The transmitting professions, named and defended** — against "ignoramus singers and qawwals who are
  deemed responsible for circulating varying versions that undermine the authenticity of Hussein's
  work" the translator sets "these devotional singers, the sole guarantors of an oral tradition", and
  "the illiterate minstrel caste, derogatorily known as the mirasis, who celebrated these kafis as
  expressions of love, loss and, let's admit it, devotion and reverence" (p. 17, folio 17). Named
  performers: **Pathanay Khan**, "a kafi virtuoso" whose singing produced a printed Variation —
  "Variation (after listening to Pathanay Khan sing this kafi)" (p. 94, folio 94) and "As Pathanay
  Khan… sings, he pauses, stretches and repeats certain lines lending a unique melancholic cadence to
  the poetry" (p. 184, folio 184); **G. Hussein Baksh**, whose recording produced another (p. 181,
  folio 181); **Abida Parveen**, "the famous Pakistani singer of mystical poetry" (p. 183, folio 183).
  In the Acknowledgements: "my eternal gratitude to the singers—well-known artists as well as
  lesser-known minstrels—whose voices led me to Shah Hussein's poetry" (p. 188, folio 188).
- **Sangat as the setting in which the poetry is construed**: "During a Sangat session, Najm Hosain
  Syed offered the following meanings for the Punjabi word an't: end, result, goal, agenda, target"
  (p. 182, folio 182).
- **Two Mughal funerary establishments set against each other as an argument about upkeep** — "Visit
  the mausoleums of the Mughal sovereigns, the marvels of architecture, the marble floors, the floral
  mosaics, the ornate domes, the once gem-studded graves. The tomb of Jehangir in Shahdara, Madho's
  hometown, is a vast complex of sprawling lawns imitating the geometry of a Persian paradise.
  Unfortunately, due to lack of proper conservation, the place is in a sorry state of disrepair;
  patchy repair work here and there, dry fountains, littered grounds and barely a trace of tall
  spruces along the cobbled paths treaded by the tourists around this forlorn grandeur" (p. 18, folio
  18) — set against "the modest shrine of Shah Hussein in Baghbanpura" with its garlanded graves
  (pp. 18–19, folios 18–19). **Tourists are named at Jehangir's tomb, devotees at Hussein's.**
- **The loom described part by part as a devotional machine** — "there's a reference to the specific
  part of the loom called bai'r, a thin rope that joins the spokes of the double wheels. It's covered
  with a sturdier rope and aids the circular motion making a to'n (means you in Punjabi) sound. The
  loom as a whole makes the sound of sain sain. The whole contraption of the loom is referred to as a
  device that constantly pays homage to the Master (You are the Sain, you are the Sain), a prayer
  wheel of sorts." (p. 182, folio 182). With "tana bana (warp and weft)" glossed as both the union of
  lover and beloved and "the weaver's diction commonly used throughout Hussein's oeuvre" (pp. 179–180,
  folios 179–180), and "khuddi, literally a hole, but also the space a weaver places his feet in while
  working on the warp of the cloth" (p. 180, folio 180). The introduction's gloss: "the whole business
  of weaving is allegorical for the thread-by-thread disentanglement of the self" and "In Hussein's
  kafis, it functions almost like a prayer wheel" (p. 13, folio 13).
- **Weaving and spinning as the working economy of the verse, with tools and grades of cloth** — "My
  colourful spinning wheel I painted red" (p. 22, folio 22); "Pretty spins my wheel, attracts /
  weavers far and wide" (pp. 22–23, folios 22–23); "The ox sniffs / the balls of wool I brought to
  spin / The other girls disentangle and spool" (p. 68, folio 68); "The mistakes I make spinning / I
  blame on the faulty loom" (p. 71, folio 71); "What would they know of plain, twill, satin? / Only
  the tool tells a thousand entanglements / I know the warp, know the weft, know the old loom"
  (p. 107, folio 107); "No wool you sheared, carded, or combed…" (p. 136, folio 136); "Good that the
  handloom broke, freeing my life / from drudgery" (p. 146, folio 146); "Smashed up the loom, lost the
  thread / I've kicked away the basket" (p. 61, folio 61); "Go round and round, O Handloom / May she
  live, the one who spins you" (p. 64, folio 64). The oil press and the waterwheel are the other named
  machines (pp. 110, 154; folios 110, 154).
- **A named coin and the household budget it fails to cover** — "For the sake of warming the stove / I
  sweat for the five measly kaseeras / How can I pay for the expenses?" (p. 78, folio 78, kafi 56),
  glossed in the apparatus as "Plural of kaseera, a coin lesser in value than a paisa. Picayune."
  (p. 183, folio 183).
- **Forced, unpaid and voluntary labour, and the officers who record it** — "I'm the lowly cleaner at
  the esteemed house / Attentively I sieve, meditatively I sweep / I dust away the lust, envy, anger /
  The judge knows, the ruler knows / free from the jobs I work voluntarily / The sheriff knows, the
  registrar knows / I serve no one except my Master" (p. 111, folio 111, kafi 88, entire). Also "Help
  me meet my lover, folks, and I'll be your unpaid servant" (p. 129, folio 129).
- **Marriage, dowry and the transfer to the in-laws — the densest ritual material in the book, used
  throughout as the figure for death.** The custom is named in Punjabi in the apparatus: "As a
  patriarchal norm, a bride leaves the parents' house (paykay) to join the husband's family (susral).
  In this line, there's a reference to the particular custom (muklawa) of the bride returning to her
  susral after a short sojourn at her paykay. Literal translation: 'Today or tomorrow is muklawa.'"
  (p. 183, folio 183); "The in-laws, the muklawa givers" (p. 184, folio 184). In the verse: "the
  muklaroos will be here to fetch the newly-wed" (p. 103, folio 103); "Four men lifted my bridal
  palanquin / dropped me at the groom's house" (p. 144, folio 144); "Four men lifted the bridal
  palanquin / Mother-in-law, sisters-in-law mocked: / here comes the dowry-less" (p. 164, folio 164);
  "They forced my marriage, dragged me / in circles around the wedding fire / I cried and screamed,
  they tied / my knot to the cruel Khera" (p. 158, folio 158); "The parents arranged my marriage, /
  didn't give much time to prepare / The in-laws here, I go without a trousseau… Mother sobs
  inconsolably, sister stands weeping" (p. 149, folio 149, kafi 124). The introduction names the
  allegory outright: the narrator "worries about dowry (the deeds of mortal life) and the imminent
  departure from the parental home… to join the husband's family, a foreign household of in-laws
  (afterlife)" (p. 11, folio 11); and "the helplessness as well as defiance of the bride in a culture
  where arranged marriage is the norm and the woman's assent is taken for granted" (p. 12, folio 12).
- **Burial and cremation named side by side, without preference, and the grave measured three ways** —
  "One cremated, the other buried" (p. 127, folio 127); "Golden castles, silver jewels / end up at the
  cremation ground" (p. 97, folio 97); "The perfumed princes, nobles, kings: / fuel for the
  sandalwood-scented pyres" (p. 100, folio 100). The measure: "A few feet of burial ground / is all
  the property you need" (p. 70, folio 70); "A few feet / for the grave is all you need / Why seek a
  greater entitlement?" (p. 97, folio 97); "Three and a half feet is the man's property / The wretched
  grave, the home" (p. 165, folio 165); and "Your bones pressed six feet under" (p. 110, folio 110).
  The funeral itemised: "Sweet friends sew our shroud / drive us down to the grave-end… Limbs tied to
  a pole later we'll be carried" (p. 44, folio 44, kafi 22).
- **Named dances, three or four of them, always Punjabi and collective** — the luddi: "Come, let's
  dance the luddi / Grab that string and let high your kite" (p. 24, folio 24, kafi 3); the jhumar:
  "Come, girl, let's dance the jhumar" (p. 112, folio 112); and "Together we shall dance the
  `jhurmat`" [text-layer?: printed "jhurmat"; cf. "jhumar" at p. 112] (p. 138, folio 138). The
  apparatus glosses three of them without naming which is which: "A Punjabi group dance." (p. 180,
  folio 180); "A form of dance." (p. 184, folio 184); "A Punjabi group dance with orchestrated
  movements danced in a circle." (p. 185, folio 185).
- **Two named games as public spectacle** — polo, "brought to the subcontinent by the Central Asian
  armies" (p. 184, folio 184; the kafi at p. 113, folio 113); and chaupat, "Chaupat has eighty-four
  squares / Only the winner remains unharmed" (p. 91, folio 91), glossed as "A chess-like game. The
  two lines sound like the fragment of a ditty." (p. 184, folio 184).
- **The monsoon swings and the rain festival** — "Monsoon arrives, girlfriends set up / the swings,
  celebrate the rain festival" (p. 73, folio 73, kafi 51); "My girlfriends sway upon the swings /
  hanging down the tall pipal trees" (p. 112, folio 112).
- **Ascetic techniques listed and refused as insufficient** — "One sits ash-smeared, gazing fixedly /
  The other roams the wild barefoot / Their hearts devoid of sincere longing / One stays up all night,
  praying / The other walks on coals, starves to death / Their place is not among the chosen / One
  reads the words of the Quran / The other engages in oral exegesis… Go to the door of the Perfect One
  / Beg the blessing for the beloved" (p. 133, folio 133, kafi 109). Also "the sadhu prides in nudity"
  (p. 153, folio 153).
- **Canonical prayer and its abandonment** — "You fall in love, you discard proprieties, / you forget
  the five times prayers" (p. 116, folio 116); "The water buffalo is milked indoors, keep your
  devotion private" (p. 151, folio 151).
- **Status and caste vocabulary, used to level rather than to rank** — "Judgement based on deeds /
  whether a pious Sufi or a lowly bhangi" (p. 80, folio 80, kafi 58); "Who'd care for the family or
  caste? / We set out in search of the lover, folks" (p. 130, folio 130); "Here a king, there a
  pauper, here an ascetic, there a thief… cattle are better than us" (p. 127, folio 127).
- **Court patronage refused as an economic choice** — "Evidently, he was steering clear of the royal
  patronage and emoluments ensured through the use of court language" (p. 15, folio 15); and the
  vernacular justified by audience economics — kafis "were easy to understand and memorize, and thus
  served as the ideal means to disseminate the mystical teachings among the illiterate masses
  dependent on the oral transmission of knowledge" (p. 15, folio 15).
- **Mughal agrarian and artisanal extraction, quoted from Moreland** — "Weavers, naked themselves,
  toiled to clothe others. Peasants, themselves hungry, toiled to feed the towns and cities. India
  taken as a unit, parted with the useful commodities in exchange for gold and silver or in other

### Arguments and interpretations

- **The organising image, and the method behind it, are the translator's own** — the portrait "makes
  him out to be a Punjabi Dionysus" (p. 6, folio 6), built by "piecing together the details of his
  life based on traditional sources" and on "the scant biographical sources available" (pp. 6–7,
  folios 6–7).
- **The hagiographers are charged with suppressing the poet and the weaver** — "What's conspicuously
  missing from all the above sources is any reference to Shah Hussein's poetry"; "Both men are
  unabashedly partial to Islam" (p. 8, folio 8); "Both writers seem bent upon justifying the blatantly
  heterodox lifestyle of Hussein, especially his state of perpetual inebriation, as the prescribed
  norm of a malamati mystic" (p. 9, folio 9). The apparatus repeats the charge from inside the verse:
  "Perhaps the loudest and clearest message to the hagiographers who succeeded in canonizing Hussein.
  Also see the conclusion of kafi 110." (p. 185, folio 185).
- **The central hypothesis, put as a question** — "Could it be possible that the saint was much more
  palatable for the established elites, religious and political, than the rabble-rouser poet–weaver?
  Reverence, after all, lends itself to uncritical obeisance, but poetry is a powder keg, especially
  if the unschooled populace starts to quote it against the diktats of the sociopolitical order"
  (p. 9, folio 9).
- **Modern sainthood is said to have foreclosed a question** — "For many a modern historian, the
  sainthood of Hussein has precluded the possibilities of exploring the nature of his relationship
  with Madho. The sexuality is either ignored, or downplayed, or dismissed outrightly" (p. 10, folio
  10); the scholars' recoil is then read as proof the poet succeeded — "The scholars' reaction is
  quite indicative of the success of Hussein's avowed intentions: tear up the ledger of saintly
  business (kafi 99)" (p. 10, folio 10).
- **A censorship hypothesis, explicitly flagged as speculation, with its evidence stated** — "There's
  no overt homosexuality in Hussein's work… Considering the speedy posthumous canonization of Hussein
  and the prevalence of the oral tradition, one has reason to speculate that some sort of character
  sanitization was at work. The likelihood of kafis with the 'objectionable' content being censored or
  suppressed cannot be ruled out." The evidence offered is an absence: "no one denies Hussein's
  preference for the forbidden alcohol, yet there is no mention of wine, a common symbol of divine
  ecstasy in Persian mystical poetry, in any of the kafis. Kafi 147 with details of bhang-making
  stands out as the only outré kafi when it comes to intoxicants" (pp. 10–11, folios 10–11).
- **A queer reading is proposed in three places** — "For a queer interpretive version, see the
  translator's poem, Message, published in the online journal The Adirondack Review" (p. 181, folio
  181); "The disgrace here for proximity to the lover lends itself to the queer interpretation"
  (p. 181, folio 181); and the preference for reading "Lal" as Madho Lal, "I'd prefer the former"
  (p. 185, folio 185).
- **The female narrator is argued for on three grounds** — malamati poignancy ("The feminization lends
  poignancy to the suffering, and explains why the narrator of the kafis is invariably female",
  p. 11, folio 11); the ʿurs-as-wedding convention (p. 11, folio 11); and tonal range ("the choice of
  assuming the female persona allows the poet to manipulate the tonal varieties of despair, a common
  device in the devotional poetry of South Asia", p. 11, folio 11). Schimmel's nafs scheme is laid
  over Hussein's refrain, with the spinner aspiring to `nafs mutma ‘inna` [text-layer?:
  `nafs mutmaʾinna`] and the idler at "nafs ammara" (pp. 12–13, folios 12–13).
- **The weaver identity is said to break the standard Sufi reading** — "Hussein's assertive
  self-identification as a weaver complicates the run-of-the-mill Sufi interpretation of his work"
  (p. 13, folio 13), which is what licenses the turn to Moreland and to 'Dullah Bhatti, ending in a
  question: "As a historical figure, could Hussein, as Syed nudges us to think, be playing the true
  role of a fakir—the malcontent, the rebel, the subversive, the saboteur?" (p. 14, folio 14).
- **Anti-intellectualism explains the choice of language** — "Most Punjabi Sufi poets were highly
  learned men, but they looked askance at scholars. Scholarly learning generally qualified one for
  self-enhancement rather than the Sufi goal of self-negation" (p. 15, folio 15) — the claim the
  Sultan Bahu and Bulleh Shah quotations are adduced for; hence "This streak of anti-intellectualism
  explains why Hussein chose Punjabi or the vernacular over Persian despite his erudition." The irony
  is noted against itself: "Ironically, his earliest full-length biography, Haqiqat al-Fuqra, is
  composed in Persian verse" (p. 15, folio 15). The apparatus pairs it with a second term: "Added to
  the anti-intellectualism is the note of anti-establishment." (p. 181, folio 181).
- **The kafi defined as form, performance and relation** — origins "traced to various sources: the
  Sanskrit words kav (poetry) and kama (sensuality), and the Arabic words kamil (perfection) and kafa
  (grouping)", with the hedge "It's quite possible that the word may be rooted in the pre-Islamic
  literary tradition but later Islamized" (p. 16, folio 16); the refrain "called rahau in Punjabi";
  "There is no consistent rhyming scheme… there could be a pattern of `a bb a bb a or aba aca ada`
  [text-layer?: rhyme-scheme notation, italics and spacing lost]" (p. 16, folio 16); and the relation
  — "A kafi is meant to be a conversation between the disciple (murid) and the master (murshid)… the
  narrator of a kafi deliberately blurs the distinction between divine (haqiqi) and temporal (mijazi)
  love to express the anguish of separation (faraq) and yearning for union or oneness (wahdat)… The
  erotic serves as the predicate for the numinous and vice versa" (p. 16, folio 16).
- **The translator refuses "lord" for *Sain*, and gives a colonial reason** — "'Don't translate Sain
  [pronounced Sai'n] into "lord",' Najm Hosain Syed advised me… But I couldn't ignore what he was
  pointing at: the reality of the Indo-Pak subcontinent where the dark native often resorted to the
  most menial and unctuous terms to ingratiate himself with the white colonizer, the lord and master.
  Lord fails to convey what Sain evokes: the guardian, saviour, watcher, protector, the companion who
  comes to the succour of the despairing." (p. 180, folio 180).
- **The "Variations" are declared interpretive, not translations** — "Variations, looser translations
  that accompany some of the kafis, are exercises in interpretive exploration." (p. 180, folio 180).
  Seven are printed in all: after kafi 2 (pp. 22–23) and kafi 10 (pp. 31–32) in chunk 001, and at
  pp. 94–95, 152, 162, 169 and 172 in chunk 002. Only one names its source — "Variation (after
  listening to Pathanay Khan sing this kafi)" (p. 94, folio 94) — and one more is traced in the
  apparatus to G. Hussein Baksh's singing (p. 181, folio 181); for the rest the source is **(not
  stated)**.
- **A translation ethic, quoted from another translator** — "I curbed my interpretive instinct heeding
  Anne Carson, the translator of Sappho: '[T]he more I stand out of the way, the more Sappho shows
  through. This is an amiable fantasy (transparency of self) within which most translators labour.'"
  (p. 183, folio 183).
- **The translator marks one of his own readings as an assumption, not a finding** — "Another five.
  Hussein does not exactly say five 'senses', but the assumption is being made based on the preceding
  kafis and general Sufi philosophy." (p. 183, folio 183).
- **Divine and human beloved are held open, not resolved** — "One of those kafis that can easily go
  both ways: supplication to the human as well as the divine beloved." (p. 181, folio 181).
- **The translator's own method, stated and qualified** — "what I have attempted to (re)produce is not
  a literal translation, but the poetry of Shah Hussein"; "What I found in Hussein's work was not the
  moral piety of a saint confined to a particular religious tradition, but the defiant devotion of a
  sixteenth-century poet upholding the nonconformist values of spirituality"; and the admission of
  risk — "A single wrong weave may not go well with the Master (kafi 136)… Did the poet in me go too
  far, overplaying the music over the message?… forgive me, Murshid" (p. 18, folio 18).
- **The poems' own doctrine, where it bears on shrine religion.** "Die before dying" is stated three
  times (pp. 75, 117, 165; folios 75, 117, 165) and glossed in the apparatus as "the importance of
  killing the selfish ego for the sake of attaining selflessness" (p. 182, folio 182). Self-knowledge
  is put first twice, once with "Sain" and once with "God" in otherwise identical lines (p. 97, folio
  97, kafi 74; p. 170, folio 170, kafi 145). Humility is named as the method in at least seven
  formulations (pp. 105, 115, 122, 123, 149, 168, 175). **Two claims stand unreconciled:** skill earns
  the lover ("Says Hussein, only the skilful girls / get to embrace the lover", p. 68, folio 68; "One
  wrong weave / may not go well with the Master", p. 161, folio 161) against "We, the sinful, are the
  favourites of the Master" (p. 130, folio 130) and "Finally the meek had their day" (p. 132, folio
  132). The book does not reconcile them.

## Doubts and review points

### 1. The folio problem — the largest caveat on every page reference above

A bare number stands alone at the foot of every non-blank page of all 190, and **in every case it
equals the PDF page index**, unbroken across all three chunks (p. 2 = 2 … p. 190 = 190). Either the
transcription route is reproducing an e-book pagination that happens to be the file's own, or the
printed page numbers of this edition happen to align with the PDF from page 2. **Nothing in the book
decides it.** Until a human checks one physical page, every "folio N" above should be read as "the
number printed at the foot of PDF page N", which is all any worker could see. Chunk 002 also warns
that the *kafi* number stands at the page **head** while the folio stands at the **foot**, and the two
sets of values overlap between sections — p. 90 heads kafi 68 and foots folio 90; p. 112 heads kafi 90
and foots folio 112; p. 134 heads kafi 110 and foots folio 134. Every reference above is the foot
number.

    152.3's own instruction, "The italicized phrases are in Arabic (kullu sheyan mahit, kuntu
    kinzan)" (p. 187, folio 187).

**Recorded as clean, so no one re-tests it:** ligature loss (`fi`/`fl`/`ff`) is **absent** throughout;
accented characters survive ("naïve", "ingénue", "outré"); en and em dashes survive. The leading
turned comma in `'Dullah Bhatti` (pp. 14–15) is **printed, not damage** — the book itself gives
"Abdullah Bhatti, commonly known as 'Dullah Bhatti" (p. 14, folio 14).

### 3. Dates and numbers that a human must reckon with

- **The ʿurs count and the death year do not obviously agree, and the book prints both without
  comment.** "Shah Hussein (1538–99)" and "more than 400 years after his death" (p. 6, folio 6)
  against "The last week of March 2016 will mark the 428th urs" (p. 19, folio 19). The book supplies
  no reckoning, no calendar basis (Hijri or Gregorian) and no explanation, and none has been supplied
  here. **Anyone citing "428th" or a founding year for the Mela Chiraga'an must reckon with both
  pages together.**
- **Two publication years contradict each other on the imprint page** — "This collection published
  2016" and "Copyright © Naveed Alam 2016" stand four lines above "This digital edition published in
  2015." (p. 190, folio 190). Reported as printed; not resolvable from the book.
- **Three of the book's printed death dates disagree with the archive's `figure_died`.** All three
  are printed in the book, not OCR noise, and none has been normalised or entered as a correction:
  `(Bulleh Shah, 1680–1758)17` (p. 15, folio 15) against the archive's **1757**; `(Sultan Bahu,
  1629–90)16` (p. 15, folio 15) against **1691**; `Guru Arjun Dev (1563–1605)` (p. 186, folio 186)
  against **1606**. The book also spells him **"Guru Arjun Dev"**, not "Arjan".

### 4. Identifications a human must confirm before an entry cites a page

- **`shrine-of-jalaluddin-surkh-posh-bukhari-jalaluddin-bukhari` / `shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht`
  rest on the bare form "Shah Jalal" (p. 177, folio 177).** Both rows' principal figures are named
  Jalaluddin and both are at Uch Sharif, so the many-row person rule assigns both — but the book
  prints only "Shah Jalal", with no city, date, order, epithet or tomb, does not gloss the name, and
  kafi 152 carries no note marker. **The third possibility a reviewer must rule out is that "Shah
  Jalal" is a figure with no archive row at all.** This is the single most consequential line in the
  book for the archive, and the introduction (which might gloss it) does not.
- **`garh-maharaja-shorkot` and `mazar-of-bulleh-shah` are here on citation evidence only.** The whole
  of the evidence is one quotation with dates apiece (p. 15, folio 15), two bibliographic entries
  (notes 16 and 17, p. 179, folio 179) and one mention of a Bahu translation project (p. 182, folio
  182). Neither man is discussed, dated beyond those parentheses, located or connected to a shrine
  anywhere in the 190 pages. **Weigh these as reading-list facts, not biographical ones.** For Sultan
  Bahu in particular the chunk worker records that the book never names Shorkot, Jhang or the Sarwari
  Qadiri order and never states that this is the Sultan Bahu of that shrine — "the identification is
  mine, from the name and the Punjabi-Sufi-poet context, and it should be confirmed against the
  endnote before an entry cites p. 15."
- **The Kali rows rest on the book's own conjecture.** The name enters only through "Kal—a possible
  reference to Kali" (p. 184, folio 184) — the book's guess about a word in the verse, not a
  statement. The three rows are mapped under the many-row person rule, but a reviewer should decide
  whether a hedged gloss is enough to carry into an entry.
- **`chandragup-baba-chandragup` is the eighth Shiva row and the chunk notes name only seven.** The
  chunk-003 enumeration lists `amb-temples-amb-sharif`, `bhagnari-mandir`, `katas-raj-temples`,
  `shahwala-teja-singh-mandir`, `shree-ratneshwar-mahadev-temple-karachi`, `shiv-mandir-chiti-ghati`
  and `umarkot-amarkot-shiv-mandir`. The eighth is included above because the ruling takes **all**
  rows a figure heads and the archive records that row's principal figure as "Shiva (associated)" —
  flagged so a human can confirm that an "associated" figure counts.
- **"Sial" (p. 130, folio 130) must NOT be mapped to `sial-sharif`.** The line is "If you are the Sain
  of Hazara / Then we the debutantes of Sial", where Sial is Heer's clan, paired with Ranjha's Hazara,
  and the apparatus confirms it — "Reference to Heer who belonged to Sial." (p. 185, folio 185). The
  archive row `sial-sharif` (Sargodha District; principal figure Khwaja Muhammad Shamsuddin Sialvi) is
  a name collision only. Flagged because the string match is exact and would be easy to make
  downstream.
- **"Lal" is never glossed as Madho in the verse itself.** Across pp. 68–178 the word "Madho" is never
  printed and no relationship is stated; only the introduction (p. 10, folio 10) and the apparatus
  (pp. 181, 185; folios 181, 185) make the identification, and both hedge it. **Do not write "Madho
  Lal" from pp. 126 or 138 alone.**
- **Publisher cities are not treated as toponyms and take no rows.** Islamabad (note 1), London, New
  Delhi, New York, North Carolina (p. 178, folio 178) and Fairfax VA (p. 179, folio 179) are
  bibliographic imprints, not the book carrying information about those places, so ruling (a) is not
  applied to them. Lahore is mapped on its many non-bibliographic occurrences, not on its five
  publisher lines. Recorded as a decision, not an open question.

### 5. Textual and editorial points

- **The kafi numbering is declared unauthoritative by the book itself** — "not based on any
  authenticated preference of the poet", and it follows the APNA website's order (p. 179, folio 179);
  two kafis are renumbered against that text (95/95 (A), 114/114 (A), p. 185, folio 185). Seven
  "Variation" blocks carry no number of their own, so "kafi 2" and "kafi 10" may each mean either of
  two texts (pp. 22–23, 31–32; folios 22–23, 31–32). **An entry citing a kafi from this book should
  cite the page, not the kafi number.**
- **One internal discrepancy between the introduction's quotation and the kafi as printed.** The
  introduction quotes kafi 11 as "Outwardly pious, sordid within / What a great man of learning you
  make" (p. 15, folio 15); kafi 11 itself prints "Outwardly pious, sordid within: / What a great man
  of learning you make!" (p. 33, folio 33). Only punctuation differs, but quote p. 33, not p. 15. The
  other four checkable introduction quotations match word for word.
- **A note key is duplicated.** Two consecutive notes carry the key "56.8." (p. 183, folio 183), the
  first on the five senses and the second on the coin *kaseera*; the true key of the second is not
  recoverable.
- **An unexplained whitespace gap on p. 185 (folio 185)**, between the "114 (A)" note and the "115.11"
  note. No word is damaged and no flag was introduced, but if a note stood there it is lost, and the
  duplicated "115.11" line sits immediately after it. **One spot for a human with the printed page.**
- **The book appears to have no bibliography.** Section II runs straight from note 152.3 (p. 187) to
  the Acknowledgements (p. 188) to the publisher pages, with no "Bibliography", "Further Reading" or
  "Works Cited" heading. Either the book has none or it stands outside pp. 179–190; confirm before the
  archive cites this book as having one. What it does carry is roughly a dozen full citations embedded
  in the notes, transcribed above.
- **"Ranjhra" (pp. 94–95, folios 94–95) is printed, not damage** — three occurrences inside one
  Variation, against "Ranjha" everywhere else. Not normalised, and recorded so the two spellings are
  not silently merged. Likewise **"Jahangueir"** (p. 178, folio 178, note 2) belongs to the 1829
  English title and is not a fault.
- **One obscure line, not emended** — "Hey you, listen: the death calls / Recite God's name, the
  customer leaves" (p. 106, folio 106). The second clause does not parse against the rest of the kafi,
  but the words are individually clean; neither flagged nor altered.
- **Kafi 104 (p. 128, folio 128) prints only two lines** with no "Says Hussein" signature and may be
  complete or truncated; kafis 143 (p. 168) and 151 (p. 176) are likewise unsigned in the usual form.
- **Verse lineation above is the text layer's, not certainly the book's** — breaks inside a kafi may be
  reflow. Quotations across a break are marked " / ". Check the printed lineation before setting one.
- **The personal name "Muhammad Pir"**, to whom *Haqiqat al-Fuqra* is attributed (p. 181, folio 181;
  p. 8, folio 8, where he is "Sheikh Mahmud, commonly known as Muhammad Pir"), is unusual enough that
  it is worth a second eye before the Akbar anecdote is cited from this book.
- **Notes 1–13 gloss the introduction and are printed in chunk 002 (p. 178); notes 14–21 and the whole
  kafi-keyed apparatus are in chunk 003 (pp. 179–187).** No note's line reference ("1.3", "42.10",
  "152.3") has been checked against the line it glosses, because the notes and the kafis fall in
  different chunks.

### 6. House-form decision Rauf must make

**The book spells this shrine's saint "Hussein" and the archive row spells him "Hussain".** Row
`madho-lal-hussain` gives "Shah Hussain (Madho Lal Hussain)"; the book gives "Shah Hussein" and "Madho
Lal Hussein" throughout, including in the volume's own title and in the author line of the 2014 Suchet
