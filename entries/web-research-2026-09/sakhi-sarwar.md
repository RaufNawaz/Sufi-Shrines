# Sakhi Sarwar
sakhi-sarwar / researched 5 September 2026 / batch B · Uch Sharif and west-Punjab khanqahs

## Verified findings

1. Official Auqaf & Religious Affairs Department page gives a construction century, a Mughal-era
   expansion, and the local name and season of the urs festival.

   > "Sakhi Sarwar is a town in Dera Ghazi Khan District, Pakistan. It is named after a Muslim
   > Sufi saint Syed Ahmad Sultan (RA), also known as Sakhi Sarwar, whose tomb is situated in the
   > vicinity."

   and

   > "An urs, or festival in the honor of Syed Ahmad Sultan (RA), also locally called 'Sang
   > Mela', has been celebrated for centuries during Vaisakhi (March - April)..."

   The same page states the tomb was built "during the 13th Century" and that the Mughal emperor
   "Zahir-ud-Din Muhammad Babur subsequently expanded the structure."

   Citation: Government of Punjab, Auqaf & Religious Affairs Department, "Hazrat Sakhi Sarwar
   (RA), Dera Ghazi Khan," https://auqaf.punjab.gov.pk/shrine-sakhi-sarwar — accessed 5 September
   2026. Reliability: official body — citable.

2. Established press independently confirms cross-community devotion and gives the town's
   pre-Islamic, Hindu-associated former name.

   > "The town was renamed after its saint while previously it was known as Nigaha or Moqam, a
   > sacred place for Hindus."

   and

   > "Now the Hindus, the Muslims and the Sikhs have great respect and reverence for the saint's
   > shrine and the town."

   and, on the timing of the fair:

   > "The urs-cum-seasonal festival is celebrated at the shrine on every Thursday of March."

   Citation: "Devotees of varying faiths at Sakhi Sarwar," Dawn, 17 March 2006.
   https://www.dawn.com/news/183506 — accessed 5 September 2026. Reliability: established press
   — citable.

## Conflicts

- **Timing of the urs/fair.** The Auqaf Department page says the urs is held "during Vaisakhi
  (March - April)"; the 2006 Dawn article says it is held "on every Thursday of March." These
  are not flatly incompatible (Vaisakhi falls in mid-April, and "every Thursday of March" would
  run up to it), but the two descriptions frame the timing differently — one as a single
  Vaisakhi-linked observance, the other as a recurring weekly one throughout March — and this
  pass does not resolve which framing is more accurate, or whether both describe genuinely
  different observances.
- **Pre-Islamic identity of the site.** The Dawn article's claim that the town was previously "a
  sacred place for Hindus" under the name "Nigaha or Moqam" is not mentioned at all by the
  official Auqaf Department page, which frames the site's origin solely around the Muslim saint.
  The sheet already names the location as "Sakhi Sarwar (Nigaha)," so it is aware of the older
  name, but the current entry does not carry the claim that Nigaha was "a sacred place for
  Hindus" before the saint — a new, sourced claim (established press) the current entry omits,
  and one the official government page neither confirms nor denies (silence, not contradiction).
- **Birth/death dates.** Wikipedia's infobox (not itself citable, fetched only per the README's
  pointer rule) gives "Born 1128 Shahkot, Jhang... Died 1174 Sakhi Sarwar" — but this pass
  confirmed, by reading the article directly, that these specific infobox dates carry no
  footnote citation at all in the article itself (a separate narrative sentence, describing this
  as "a myth prevalent in the 19th century Punjab," does carry a citation, which was not
  independently fetched this pass). Neither the official Auqaf page nor the Dawn article gives a
  specific birth or death year; the current entry's own "year_built: 1200 (century)... 12th-13th
  c." is at least broadly compatible with 1128-1174, but no citable source for either version was
  found this pass. Reported as unresolved, not confirmed.

## Verdict

STRONG — two independent, directly-fetched, citable sources (an official Auqaf & Religious
Affairs Department page and an established-press article) both confirm the shrine's
cross-community, multi-faith character and add detail (a 13th-century founding with a Mughal-era
expansion; a pre-Islamic, Hindu-associated former name) beyond what the current entry states —
alongside a genuine, unresolved conflict between them over how the annual urs is timed.

## Re-verification against the complete text — 5 September 2026

The original pass fetched the *Gazetteer of Dera Ghazi Khan District, revised edition 1893-97*
but could not reach its "Sakhi Sarwar" section (per its own contents listing, starting around
page 33), reporting the fetch as "truncated in every fetch attempt well before that point." The
identifier is `in.ernet.dli.2015.35262`.

Fetched with `pipeline/fetch_archive_text.py`: **628,749 bytes** — the complete book. The
dedicated section named in the contents ("Sakhi Sarwar — Miracles performed by Saidi Ahmad —
Origin of the shrine of Sakhi Sarwar") was located and read in full, resolving the single
specific unverified lead the file flagged — that the gazetteer gives the saint's birth-era as
"1220 AD," son of "Hazrat Zainulabdeen." **Confirmed, essentially verbatim, allowing for OCR
garbling of the proper names:**

> "Sakhi Sarwar, the Lakhdata of the Western Punjab, is said to have been the son of Hazrat
> Zenabuldin, who migrated from Baghdad and settled... 12 miles east of Mooltan, in 660 A.H.
> (1220 A.D.). Hazrat Zenabuldin had two sons; one was Saidi Ahmad, afterwards known as Sakhi
> Sarwar."

**Quotation note (added 5 September 2026 by the calling session).** The blockquote above is a
**normalised** reading of badly-degraded colonial OCR, not the literal bytes of the scan, which
read:

> s said to have been tho son of Ilazral Zennhiildin, who migrated from Baghdad and settled
> ntSialkut, 12 miles east of Mooltau, in 6fi0 A.n. (1220 A.D.). Hazrat Zenabuldin bad two sons ;
> one was Saidi Ahmad, afterwards known as Stikbi Sarwar ; the other was Khan Doda, who died at
> Baghdad, and was not famous.

The reading is correct and the finding stands — including this file's own point that the 1220
date attaches to the **father's migration**, not to the saint. The literal form is recorded
because a reader grepping the scan for the clean string finds nothing. **When quoting OCR this
poor, give the raw string and the reading side by side.**

This is a real, citable confirmation of a claim the earlier pass could only report as an
unfetched search-engine summary — the OCR's "Zenabuldin" is plainly the same name as the
unverified lead's "Zainulabdeen," and "660 A.H. (1220 A.D.)" matches "1220 AD" exactly. **This
sharpens, rather than resolves, the existing Birth/death dates conflict:** the gazetteer dates
here describe when the saint's *father* migrated and settled, not the saint's own birth or death,
and 1220 AD sits roughly a century after the Wikipedia infobox's own uncredited "Born 1128...
Died 1174" — the two chronologies cannot both be read at face value without one of them being
wrong, or without "settled in 1220" referring to a different point in the family's history than
either infobox figure assumes. Reported as a sharper, better-sourced version of the same
unresolved conflict, not adjudicated.

The same section also gives the gazetteer's own origin legend for the shrine's construction — a
folk narrative (a mended camel's leg as a miracle, four camel-loads of money from an unnamed
Emperor) that differs in kind from the Auqaf Department's plainer "built during the 13th century...
subsequently expanded" account and does not mention Babur by name:

> "The Emperor, convinced of the miracle, sent four camel loads of money to Saidi Ahmad... Sakhi
> Sarwar shrine was built with this money."

This is a third account of the shrine's founding, alongside the Auqaf Department page and the
current entry's own `year_built` field — reported as a further, differing version, not resolved.

A separate, targeted check for the pre-Islamic Hindu place-names the Dawn (2006) article gives
("Nigaha," "Moqam") returned **zero hits** in this gazetteer — a genuine silence, not a
truncation artifact, though this gazetteer already independently confirms (via the "hills near
Batil and Sakhi Sarwar" passage already on file) that the surrounding geography is described in
some detail, so the absence of these two specific names is a real, if inconclusive, negative on
that particular claim.
