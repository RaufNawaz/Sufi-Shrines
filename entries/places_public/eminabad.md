## Overview

Eminabad is the town that in the time of Guru Nanak, the founder of the Sikh faith, was called Saidpur, and Babur renamed it Eminabad when he took the city, though accounts of the name differ. Old Saidpur is pictured as an ancient city of great temples and traders, among the most important trading stops between the Chenab and the Ravi. In recent times the spires of old Hindu temples still rose among its houses, the temples abandoned in 1947 and taken over by migrants from across the border, and the town had lost its trade to a newer city nearby.

## History

In Guru Nanak's day Eminabad was the city of Saidpur. When Babur took over Saidpur, he renamed it Eminabad. He destroyed the cities of Eminabad and Lahore. Accounts of how the town came by its name differ.

Tradition tells how the new name came about. Shortly before Babur reached Saidpur, when he had not eaten for days, a woman offered him roasted grain, and the city, it is said, was renamed in her honour.

The town's fortunes followed the road. It was once an important trading centre on the Grand Trunk Road, which connected it with Lahore and then Delhi. The highway has since moved south, and a newer city not far away has replaced Eminabad as an important trading centre.

## Temples and Holy Places

Saidpur in Guru Nanak's day is imagined as a city of magnificent houses and *havelis*, great mansions, and of huge temples with gold tridents. Idols stood in every shop, Laxmi, the goddess of wealth, the most popular, and after her the god Ganesh. The city is pictured as ancient, its temples said to have been built thousands of years ago, long before the birth of Islam. They had grown into complexes with rooms for pilgrims, shops let on lease, stables and an army of servants.

In this picture of the old city the Brahmin pandits, the learned men of the priestly caste, were the bosses. The offerings were kept, given out or sold, and guards kept out the naked and starving beggars.

In recent times the cone like spires of ancient Hindu temples still rose among the houses. To a visitor they were beautiful, in contrast to the monolithic Muslim architecture that now dominates the city of Eminabad. One temple, despite being abandoned in 1947, still looked sturdy, though its paint had worn off and a corrosive layer had formed on it.

The visitor wondered whether the temples had been attacked in 1992, after the destruction of the Babri mosque in India, and looked for signs of an attack, but found none. He marvelled at how much of the town's historical architecture had been preserved.

## Communities

Saidpur in Guru Nanak's day is pictured as a city with a Hindu majority and a few Muslim households, its Hindus mostly traders and its Muslims aristocrats. It is imagined as one of the most important trading centres between the Chenab and the Ravi, the most important stop for traders after Delhi and Lahore, and a city with some of the tallest temples in the Punjab and also the most beautiful courtesans.

In the same picture Malik Bhago, a minister of the Rai, or ruler, of Saidpur, lived in a frescoed mansion of four storeys known as the Shahi Qilla at Saidpur. He held a wedding feast for all Brahmins and members of the high castes. It was at the court of Malik Bhago in Eminabad, it is told, that Guru Nanak was questioned.

The town of recent times had a different people. Its old temples had been taken over by migrants from the other side of the border following the creation of Pakistan, and in recent times their descendants were still there.

## Eminabad in Books

For a writer walking in Guru Nanak's footsteps, Eminabad is a town seen in two times. One is the Saidpur of Guru Nanak's day, ancient and rich in temples and in trade. The other is the town he walked through, its temples abandoned since 1947 and its trade gone to a newer city. After their visit to Eminabad, he and Iqbal Qaiser went on to Sialkot.

A Persian chronicle of Aurangzeb's reign names a *pargana*, or revenue district, and a halting stage spelt as Eminabad is spelt, though nothing but the spelling ties that place to this town. If it is the same place, the chronicle knows it as a *pargana* set up a few years earlier out of Gujrat, as a halting stage on a royal journey, and as a point from which to measure the road.

On a journey the chronicle sets in the forty second year, a man in Kashmir who posed as Umar Shaikh Mirza, son of Sulaiman Mirza, was brought before the emperor at that halting stage and punished there on the spot. The bridge of Shah Daula over the Deg, on the highway, lies five *karoh*, an old measure of distance, from the place towards Lahore. The Deg passes the villages of several *parganas*, Zafarwal, Pasrur and this one among them, before it reaches the bridge.
