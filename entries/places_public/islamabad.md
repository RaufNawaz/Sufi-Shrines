## Overview

Islamabad is the federal capital of Pakistan, the twin city of Rawalpindi, set among the Margalla hills. Because it is the capital, its population consists of officials linked with the federal government and of the foreign embassies and their support staff. On its outskirts stand the village of Nurpur Shahan and Barri Imam's shrine, and Golra, the place of Pir Mehr Ali Shah, *pir* being the title of a Sufi spiritual guide, is named with the city on a devotional print.

The city is also a home of religious organisations and circles. Al-Huda, which aims to educate women about the Quran, has its base here, a Sufi association publishes its English journal from Islamabad, and the city is one of the important centres of Pakistan's Baha'is.

As the seat of the state, Islamabad is a place of patronage. The singer Abida Parvin chose to live here to do business, and the state's institutions in the city sponsor concerts for its officials, hold archives of the music and have sent a performer abroad. Public anger and violence have reached the city too, from a march on the diplomatic enclave to the killings of Salman Taseer and Shahbaz Bhatti.

## The Capital

Islamabad is Pakistan's capital city, and Rawalpindi is its twin. As the capital it is a city of government, whose population consists of officials linked with the federal government, together with the foreign embassies and their support staff.

Protest has also turned towards the diplomatic enclave. On one day of unrest, as horrible news came in from Rawalpindi and Islamabad, a mob of thousands headed towards the enclave to protest outside the United States' embassy. The government put up barriers to stop it, while hundreds of foreigners were inside the enclave.

Two killings took place in the capital within a few months of each other. The Governor of Punjab, Salman Taseer, was shot dead in Islamabad by his own bodyguard, and a couple of months later, on 2 March 2011, Shahbaz Bhatti was murdered in Islamabad outside his residence.

Institutions based in the capital also pass judgement on holy places elsewhere in the country. In 2012 an official of the Jinnah Institute, which is based in Islamabad, branded the Hindu temple of Hinglaj a 'high risk for catastrophe' because of the possibility of extremist attacks.

## Holy Places

Barri Imam's shrine stands with the village of Nurpur Shahan on the outskirts of the capital. Golra, too, is tied to the city: a devotional print names Pir Mehr Ali Shah with his place given as "Golra/Islamabad".

The city may also hold a sacred tree. The travel writer Salman Rashid once said, "I have heard of a bodhi tree in Islamabad and one near Jhelum", the bodhi being the sacred fig tree of the Buddhists.

The capital lies in the Margalla hills, and in the heart of those hills, among the ruins of Taxila, stands Dharmarajika. It is a *stupa*, a domed Buddhist monument, surrounded by the remains of a monastery and by giant sculptures of the Buddha and of a Bodhisattva, one who seeks enlightenment for the sake of others. Dharmarajika is dated to the third century BCE and is believed to have been built by the great Mauryan emperor Ashoka. It is a UNESCO world heritage site and a major tourist attraction, and on one visit Buddhist monks and students from the neighbouring monastery were walking the pathway that circles it.

## Communities and Circles

Pakistan's Baha'is, the followers of the Baha'i faith, are evenly scattered throughout the country, but Lahore and Islamabad are two of their important cities.

Islamabad is the base of Al-Huda, one of the most prominent religious revivalist organisations in Pakistan, which aims to educate women about the Quran and the Sunnah, the example of the Prophet's words and deeds. Al-Huda was established in 1994, and by the mid 2010s it had spread rapidly across the educated middle class of the city.

A Sufi association publishes its journal from Islamabad. The Association of Spiritual Training was founded by Wahid Bakhsh and is run by a number of his senior disciples. Since 1989 it has published an English language journal, *The Sufi Path*, from the city, and fourteen issues had appeared by the 2000s, among them Book 13 in 1995 and Book 14 in 1996.

Not every circle has lasted. A woman *murid*, a Sufi disciple, used to host a weekly *halqa*, a circle gathered for devotion, in Islamabad, but the practice has since been discontinued.

## Music, Patronage and the State

The capital is also a place of patronage. The singer Abida Parvin lives in Islamabad rather than in her hometown of Hyderabad, because Islamabad is the capital and she can negotiate business from there. Her clients are the foreign diplomats in the city and state and private agencies.

Here the state itself is the patron. At the Open University in Islamabad, an institution sponsored by the state, Abida Parvin once sang for three hours in the summer heat of August, at a concert the university sponsored for the benefit of senior government officials and faculty. Invitations to such concerts are among the perks of belonging to the official Islamabad bureaucracy, a practice with a colonial heritage.

That evening she sang in a fiery mood and, through the frames of Sufi poetry, blasted the orthodox government of the time in the presence of its official bureaucracy. Before an august audience of government bureaucrats she has also sung the story of Layla and Majnun, the legendary lovers, both to entertain and to create awareness, through myth, of the minority provinces or the marginalised groups of the country.

The state's cultural institutions in the city hold archives of this music and have sent a performer abroad. The Institute of Folk Heritage and the Allama Iqbal Open University in Islamabad hold key archives of it. Lok Virsa, the Institute of Folk Heritage, contacted one woman performer through Radio Pakistan in Hyderabad, and through Lok Virsa she was able to go to international concerts in the Middle East and Europe; as a state organisation, Lok Virsa could afford to pay for her trips abroad.

## Islamabad in Books

Writers on the religions of Pakistan have known Islamabad as a home, a base for fieldwork and a place of publication.

For one writer on the country's religious minorities and its folk religion, who has also written on Guru Nanak, Islamabad is home. He moved to the city after his marriage and lives there now. He finds its drivers much more courteous than those of Lahore, and he has driven back to Islamabad with Anam after a weekend in Lahore.

For a scholar of Sufism in twenty first century Pakistan the city was a place of fieldwork. He travelled frequently to Islamabad, as to Bahawalpur, Allahabad in southern Punjab and Karachi, to meet the Chishti Sabiri disciples, followers of a branch of the Chishti Sufi order, and he held interviews in the city on 22 and 24 March 2001.

For a scholar of the female voice in Sufi ritual, Islamabad is where much of the material was gathered, in the archives of the Institute of Folk Heritage and the Allama Iqbal Open University. Her first interview took place in the city too, with Ustad Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan, *ustad* being the title of a master musician, at the Marriott Hotel.

The city is also a place of publication and learning. In 1976 the Ministry of Religious Affairs issued *The Message of the Prophet* from Islamabad, under the imprint of the Government of Pakistan: a selection of articles read at the First International Congress on Seerat, the life of the Prophet. The volume also carries an essay on the Prophet by Gerhard Bowering. The city's Persian research centre, the *Markaz-i Tahqiqat-i Farsi-i Iran u Pakistan*, published there in 1978, and a study of Chishti Sufism cites an Islamabad manuscript. A book titled *Shah Hussayn* was published in Islamabad by Lok Wirsa Isha'at Ghar in 1989.
