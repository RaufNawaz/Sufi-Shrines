======== GROUP A ========
VERDICT: FAIL

ISSUES:
1. [distortion] "The story is told that the saint Shah Kaku Chishti went to the enemy as an envoy and was killed, and that Ahmad Shah, drowned in grief and anger, ordered a general massacre." -> In the long text, Shah Kaku assured Ahmad Shah that Lahore was his own city and was sent as Ahmad Shah's envoy, and "the man he went to had him killed". Told from Lahore's side, "the enemy" reads as Ahmad Shah. That makes the story incoherent, because Ahmad Shah would then be grieving a killing done by his own side. It also drops the reason Ahmad Shah grieved. -> "The story is told that when Ahmad Shah came to Lahore, the saint Shah Kaku Chishti assured him that this was his own city and was sent as his envoy, that the man he went to had him killed, and that Ahmad Shah, drowned in grief and anger, ordered a general massacre."

2. [generalisation] "Kashmiri brick sellers became men worth a lakh of rupees, Mughalpura yielded so many bricks that thousands of mansions were built from them, and whole tombs and mosques were dug out until no trace was left." -> The long text says "no trace was left" of only one building: the foundations of Abul Hasan Khan's tomb. For other tombs and mosques it says brick sellers took their fabric, or that a mosque was pulled down and its foundations dug out. The short text turns one case into a plural rule. -> "Kashmiri brick sellers became men worth a lakh of rupees, Mughalpura yielded so many bricks that thousands of mansions were built from them, and brick sellers took the fabric of tombs and mosques, digging out the foundations of Abul Hasan Khan's tomb, which had cost ten lakh, until no trace was left."

3. [distortion] "From Jahangir's tomb, from Shalamar Bagh and from demolished mosques, marble and red stone went to Amritsar for the Sri Darbar Sahib." -> The long text says Shalamar's red stone went into the Ram Bagh, not to the Darbar. It names only one demolished mosque (Khwaja Muhammad Saeed's mosque and tomb), and says only that its stone was "sent to Amritsar", without saying it was for the Darbar. The short text merges three separate transfers into one. -> "Marble from Jahangir's tomb and from Shalamar Bagh went to Amritsar for the Sri Darbar Sahib, and the marble and red stone of Khwaja Muhammad Saeed's demolished mosque and tomb were sent to Amritsar too."

4. [cause not given] "Under the treaty of 1846 a Resident was appointed in the city, and in the end Duleep Singh left Lahore." -> In the long text, what happened "under the treaty of 1846" is that territory passed from the government of Lahore. It says the Resident was appointed "at the request of the people of the state of Lahore". The short text credits the Resident to the treaty, which is a link the long text does not make. -> "Under the treaty of 1846 territory passed from the government of Lahore, a Resident was appointed in the city at the request of the people of the state, and in the end Duleep Singh left Lahore."

NOTES:
- "Daulat Khan Lodi, the governor, later turned against Sultan Ibrahim": the long text does not put Nanak's visit and Daulat Khan's defection in any order. Dropping "later" would be safer: "Daulat Khan Lodi, the governor, turned against Sultan Ibrahim and went over from Lahore to Babur."
- "after Mahmud's conquest Malik Ayaz rebuilt the city": Mahmud appears here without introduction. "Sultan Mahmud" would help the reader.
- "in the struggles that followed his death guns were mounted on the minarets to shell the fort": this is an acceptable inference. The long text dates these events to Rani Chand Kaur's time and the Sandhanwalia siege, both of which come after Ranjit Singh in its own narrative.
- "the samadhs of mahants ... inside their own temples": the long text names only the Shivala Tarpoliya's halls (the samadhs at the Wazir Khan shiwala and Bhauri Sarkar's stand "at" those temples). The plural is a slight generalisation, but it does not fail the text.
- Legend framing holds throughout: "tradition still told today" (Luv and Kush), "Sikh tradition sets" (Nanak), "it is said" (the majzub, Miani), "Tradition holds that the saints guard the city". The accounts that differ on the city's founding are kept as two tellings.
- There is no source talk, no citations, no em or en dashes, and no American spellings. There is no verse in these sections of the long text, and none in the short.
- Completeness at short length is good. Every section carries its key saints, sites, dates, communities and turning points (Hujwiri and the Kashf al-mahjub, Malik Ayaz, the Ghurids, the 1241 Mongol sack, Nanak and Duni Chand, Akbar, Jahangir and Guru Arjan, Dara and Aurangzeb, Abdali, the brick quarrying, Ranjit Singh's powder magazines and stone stripping, the British public buildings, 1931 and 1940, Data Darbar, Mian Mir and Bibi Jamal, Shah Hussain, Bibi Pak Daman, Miani, the Sikh sites, the temples, and the dating, wills and water of the holy places). I found no important omission.

======== GROUP B ========
VERDICT: FAIL

ISSUES:
1. [distortion by compression] "He framed the city's history as a sequence of destructions, thirteen numbered shocks of plunder and demolition, and cast Sikh rule as the time of loss and English rule as the time of restitution." (Lahore in Books) -> The long text says he did this only "in four cases close together". It also says that "elsewhere he reported both regimes in the same register with different verbs" and "drew no general conclusion from the contrast". The short text turns four cases into a general thesis about the whole history. -> "He framed the city's history as a sequence of destructions, thirteen numbered shocks of plunder and demolition. In four cases close together he cast Sikh rule as the time of loss and English rule as the time of restitution, but elsewhere he reported both regimes in the same register and drew no general conclusion from the contrast."
2. [unsupported identification] "At Miani, when a body came, the family of the dead gave the gravediggers their due and gave the *sajjada nashin* something besides, as they were able." (The Religious Economy) -> The long text says only "A burial ground paid its people in the same way. In the nineteenth century, when a body came…". It never says this burial ground was Miani, so the short text makes a link the long text does not. -> "At one burial ground, when a body came, the family of the dead gave the gravediggers their due and gave the *sajjada nashin* something besides, as they were able."

NOTES (minor, not failing):
- "a nawab from Kabul… a hill raja who made the place over to his Brahmins" (Begampura): the long text says "Sansar Chand of Kangra" and gives him no title. Better: "Sansar Chand of Kangra, who made the place over to his Brahmins".
- "Sarfaraz Khan at once made all his property *waqf*": the long text says "all his immovable property". Better: "made all his immovable property *waqf*".
- "Pandit Bishnath, being childless, endowed two *pakka* havelis and four shops so that the temple ran comfortably": the long text gives a result, not a purpose ("from whose rents the temple ran comfortably"). Better: "endowed two *pakka* havelis and four shops, from whose rents the temple ran comfortably."
- "Because it fell in the hot season people suffered, so stalls of sharbat…" (Bhadar Kali): the long text places the two facts side by side and does not say "so". It is harmless, but "Because it fell in the hot season people suffered, and stalls of sharbat… were set up" would be more exact.
- Moran's mosque: the two master teachers are not named, so "three of Ghulam Allah's five sons" comes without any earlier mention of Ghulam Allah. Suggest "its imamat was given to Maulana Ghulam Rasul and Ghulam Allah, the master teachers of the whole Punjab".
- Poetry: "Other mosques carry chronograms" uses a term without explaining it. The long text has a gloss: "a verse whose letters, read as numbers, give a year". Add it.
- Raja Dina Nath and Fazl Shah Majzub: the short text drops "devotees went on holding it after him". It is not wrong, but the move from "great pomp" straight to "only a *fatiha*" reads as a sudden end.
- Nihal Chand "built his mandir": the long text says "began to build".
- Possible additions if words allow, none of them essential: the dates of Mas'ud-i-Sa'd (born about 440 AH, 1048 to 1049; died about 515 AH, 1121 to 1122); the daily and weekly worship paragraph (*arti* at the Kotwali shiwala, the Granth read twice daily at Guru Ram Das); Ranjit Singh's proclamation against bringing out a *taʿzia*; the Jains of nineteenth century Lahore in Communities.
- The text contains no em or en dashes, the spelling is British, and there is no talk of sources outside "Lahore in Books". The two Persian lines match the long text exactly. "It is said" was dropped from the soldier's image story, but only together with the claim it framed, so no legend is now stated as fact. No other legend lost its framing.
