## Overview

Multan is one of the ancient cities of the Punjab, a city with a fort of brick, standing where three rivers merge before the Indus. By the end of the seventeenth century people of every kind lived there and the goods of every land were bought and sold, and it was a commercial hub for national and international trade, including with Persia, Central Asia and the Arab world. Today it is known as a holy city of southern Punjab, the resting place of the great Suhrawardi saint Baha'uddin Zakariya and of many other saints.

Its story begins in legend and runs through conquest. It is said to have been the capital of the Tuganta kingdom in the time of the Mahabharata and to have been ruled for a thousand years by Hindu and Buddhist kings. In 711 the Arabs conquered Sind and the provinces northward up to Multan. Ghaznavids, Ghurids, Delhi sultans, Timur's armies and a dynasty of its own, the Langah sultans, followed, and after the Arghun conquest the city passed to the Mughals, fell to the Afghans and then to Ranjit Singh.

Multan is a city of Sufis. In the twelfth century many Sufi scholars made it their base, and by the time Guru Nanak came there it was famous as a major centre of Sufi learning. Tradition in India holds that Shams-i Tabriz was flayed alive by the *mollas*, the clerics, in Multan, and for the Sindhi and Panjabi poets Multan stands beside Mecca and Medina as a holy city of the heart.

It was a city of more than one faith, known in Guru Nanak's time for the shrines of Muslim saints and for its Jain and Hindu temples. Today its Hindus number little more than a thousand and their temples from before partition are ruins, but Holi, the Hindu spring festival, which was not celebrated in the city as recently as 2010, is now kept every year on a small scale.

Multan has its own speech, verse and song. The Siraiki spoken in the city today is closer to Punjabi. The poets Amir Khusrau and Amir Hasan served Balban's son Prince Muhammad at Multan, and the love poems of Fakhruddin ʿIraqi for his master are still sung at the door of the master's tomb.

## History: Legend, the Arabs and the Ghaznavids

In the time of the Mahabharata, it is said, Multan was the capital of the Tuganta kingdom, ruled by the Katoch clan of the Kshatriya Rajputs, and legend has it that Alexander was hit by an arrow there. For the next thousand years Hindu and Buddhist kings ruled it, and the Chinese traveller Heun Tsang visited Multan in 641. It was the city of gold, and its Suraj temple, the temple of the sun, was claimed to be by far the biggest temple in the subcontinent. Its Hindus today also keep the memory of Hiranyakashipu, the tyrant of the tale of Prahlad Bhagat, who is believed to have ruled Multan thousands of years ago.

Muhammad bin Qasim brought Multan under Muslim rule for the first time, and in 711 the Arabs conquered Sind and the adjacent provinces northward up to Multan. The Carmathians, or Qaramita, an extreme Shia group whose capital was in Bahrain, ruled northern Sind and Multan, and tradition holds that Hallaj had been in touch with them there. Multan later became a dependency of the Fatimids of Egypt, until Sultan Mahmud of Ghazni broke the power of the Ismaʿilis, a branch of Shia Islam, in the city.

Sabuktigin brought Lahore and Multan into his possession, and Mahmud marched on the city more than once. On his second campaign he set out to take the country from Daud bin Nasir, who held a separate rule in Multan, and leaving the direct road so that the ruler would have no warning, he defeated Raja Anandpal, son of Raja Jaipal, on the way. Reaching Multan, he made the ruler submit, fixed a yearly tribute on him and put the commands of the *shariʿa*, Islamic law, into force, but his soldiers could not bear the hot air of Multan and begged to return.

On his fourth campaign Mahmud brought the whole country of Multan under his control. The Qaramita there were seized, and some were thrown beneath the feet of elephants while others lost hands and feet or were beheaded. Daud bin Nasir was taken captive and imprisoned in a fort, where he died a natural death. In the eleventh century Mahmud also damaged the Suraj temple and destroyed its giant idol.

Then came the Ghurids. Shihab al-Din, on his first campaign, took the country of Multan out of the hands of the Qaramita and left his deputy there, and the seizure of Multan by Muʿizzu'd-Din Muhammad bin Sam is dated 1175. Under Ghori Multan joined his Delhi kingdom, and the town became studded with mosques as it had once been studded with Hindu temples.

## History: The Delhi Sultans, Timur and the Langahs

Under the Delhi sultans Multan had its own governors, its *qazi* or judge, its fort and the *madrasa*, a religious college, of Qutbu'd-Din Kashani. Under Balban the city belonged to the heir to the throne, Prince Muhammad Sultan, who was given Sindh in *jagir*, a grant of land revenue, and sent to order the affairs of Multan. When a Mongol army began to harm the country around Lahore and Multan, the prince came out of the city and after a short fight met a martyr's death, in what is remembered as the Battle of Multan. Balban then appointed the prince's son, Kaikhusrau, at Multan with the marks of royalty.

Multan was also a place of refuge. The sons of Jalal al-Din, Rukn al-Din and Ibrahim, fled from Delhi to Multan, and when the sultan's commanders besieged the city the princes submitted through Shaikh Rukn al-Din. The rise of the Tughluqs is tied to Multan, though accounts differ on who governed the city when Ghazi Tughluq marched on Delhi and took the throne. Malik Bahram rose in revolt in Multan and was defeated and killed, and the sultan meant to order a general massacre of the people of Multan, which Shaikh Rukn al-Din averted.

Timur's armies came next. His grandson Mirza Pir Muhammad took the forts of Uch and Multan and was in Multan in 1397. Timur himself then came from Kabul, halted at Multan, put to death those whom Pir Muhammad had taken captive, and left by way of the desert. By most accounts it was Timur who gave Khizr Khan the government of Multan, and Khizr Khan three times gathered an army in Multan and marched on Delhi.

The Lodis had Multani beginnings. Malik Bahram, grandfather of Sultan Bahlul Lodi, came to Multan in Firuz Shah's reign and entered the service of its governor, Malik Marwan, and his five sons all lived in Multan after his death. Later a sultan reached Multan and made the round of the tombs of the shaikhs. In those days the Langah people rose in revolt in Multan and raised the banner of sovereignty.

Then Multan became a kingdom of its own. Because of the Mongol attacks that came every year from the direction of Kabul, the country of Multan was left without a ruler, and its notables and people met in council and with one voice raised Shaikh Yusuf Qureshi to the sultanate. The chief of the Langahs, who held a *qasba*, a small town, of his own, offered him the Langahs' service and gave him his daughter in marriage.

His visits to her were a stratagem. The chief drank a cup of the warm blood of a kid and cried out that a pain had seized his stomach, and near midnight he called the Shaikh's attendants as though to make his last bequests and vomited blood before them. By this trick his kinsmen were let into the fort. He imprisoned the guileless Shaikh and took the title of Sultan Qutb al-Din, having the *khutba*, the Friday sermon that names the ruler, read in his own name.

Sultan Qutb al-Din ruled Multan with full independence for sixteen years and died a natural death. His son Sultan Husain, a man of enterprise and courage, met a commander and Tatar Khan, governor of the Punjab, near Multan, and thirty thousand arrows fell at once on the commander's army, which broke and did not draw rein before Chiniot. Sultan Mahmud, son of Firuz Shah son of Sultan Husain, succeeded his grandfather and reigned twenty seven years, and his son Sultan Husain, seated on the throne at three years old, reigned seven years in name only.

The Langahs fell to the Arghuns. Babur granted Multan in *jagir* to Mirza Shah Husain Arghun, ruler of Thatta, who fought Sultan Mahmud; Mahmud died during the fighting. Under the child sultan, Qawam Khan and Lashkar Khan joined Arghun, fought the young Husain and took the city. The conquerors plundered Multan and took its people captive from seven years old to seventy, Sultan Husain was imprisoned and died, and the city was so ruined that no one thought it would ever be settled again. Lashkar Khan, set to guard Multan together with Arghun's slave Shams al-Din, did away with Shams al-Din and ruled independently.

## History: Mughal, Afghan and Sikh Multan and After

In Ibrahim Lodi's time Daulat Khan, governor of Multan, called in Babur from Kabul. Under the Mughals Lahore and Multan were assigned to Kamran Mirza in *jagir*, and Kamran put his own men in charge of the city, Arghun rule there having lasted four years. In the wars with Sher Khan the emperor's road of flight ran by Multan, and Sher Khan's slave Khawas Khan followed him with a large army as far as Multan and Uch, a town then subordinate to Multan.

The governors of Multan appear at work in the Mughal wars. After a battle near a place subordinate to Multan, Ibrahim Husain Mirza fled towards the city and hid in the house of a Baluch, who handed him to Said Khan, governor of Multan. Rustam Mirza was given, in *jagir*, the country of Multan and Baluchistan, whose revenue was greater than that of Qandahar.

Dara Shikoh's flight brought the war of succession to the city. He left Lahore for Multan with fourteen thousand horse, but he did not stand there: he took money from the treasury of Multan and fled towards Bhakkar, and most of his servants would not go with him. Haji Khan Baluch, one of the chief *zamindars*, the landholders, of Multan, tried to bar the way of the treasure boats but could not stop them.

The emperor followed in pursuit, and his camp stood on the bank where the Ravi and the Chenab flow together, near Multan. The man who held the city on Dara's behalf submitted, the emperor visited the tomb of Bahauddin, and Lashkar Khan was moved from the *suba*, the province, of Kashmir to the governorship of Multan. After five days near the city the emperor marched towards Shahjahanabad, to put down Muhammad Shuja's rising.

At Aurangzeb's death Jahandar Shah was *subadar*, governor, of Multan. The *suba* of Multan was later joined to that of Lahore. Under Alamgir II the Afghans took Multan and Lahore, and Multan is among the cities the Marathas, with Sikh help, are said to have conquered.

Then came the Sikhs. Uprisings led by Muslim leaders began in Kasur and Multan, and the rebellion was given a religious colour against Ranjit Singh as an infidel, who in turn employed the Nihangs, Sikh warriors, to give the battles a religious tone. Hafiz Muhammad Jamal, who died in 1811, is also said to have taken part in resistance to Sikh attacks on Multan. When Ranjit Singh took Multan its Nawab, Muzaffar Khan, was killed, and others of the Nawabs' family were captured and brought to Lahore.

Diwan Mulraj, son of Sawan Mal and *nazim*, governor, of Multan, raised a rising in the city and had two men titled *sahib*, a title of respect, killed together with the newly appointed *nazim*. Multan now lies in Pakistan.

## A City of Saints

Multan is a city of saints. In the twelfth century many Sufi scholars made it their base, and conversion to Islam gained momentum in the early thirteenth century. By the time Guru Nanak came the city was famous as a major centre of Sufi learning and philosophy, its schools fallen into a sense of rivalry. It had its *deras* and *dargahs*, Sufi lodges and shrines, and its *khanqah*, the Sufi lodge, was open to both Muslims and Hindus.

Multan is tied to the Suhrawardi order of Sufis. It is the resting place of Baha'uddin Zakariya, who died in 1267, and Makhdum-i Jahaniyan was the famous fourteenth century Suhrawardi master of Multan. The Persian poet Fakhruddin ʿIraqi attached himself to Baha'uddin in Multan and dedicated *qasidas*, odes, to him, and left for Konya soon after Baha'uddin's death.

Saints of other orders passed through the city. The visit of the Chishti master Qutb ad-Din to Multan became the occasion for Farid ad-Din's pledge of allegiance to him. For the saint Lal, Multan was his first destination; he is likely to have first arrived there in 1263, and in the Suhrawardi tradition he was initiated there by Bahaʾuddin Zakaria.

Multan also has a shrine of Shams Tabriz. Tradition in India holds that Shams-i Tabriz, Maulana Rumi's spiritual beloved, was flayed alive in Multan, where his shrine is still venerated, though there is no settled view whether the Multan shrine is his or that of a local Sufi of the same name. Shrines to the same person in more than one place are not unusual.

Not all the city's holy places are great ones. Multan has one of the shrines of women saints to which men are not admitted, small and decorated with blue and white tiles. The city was also the starting point of the pilgrim road to the tomb of Sultan Sarwar. The city's historical Hindu temples from before partition have all become ruins, and the community has turned other spaces into makeshift temples.

## Communities

Multan has held many peoples. In Guru Nanak's time it "was one of the biggest cities of Punjab and was known for the religious shrines of Muslim saints, and Jain and Hindu temples". Families of Muftis, scholars of religious law, and of Gardezis belonged to Lahore and Multan, and the Baluch appear often in the city's history.

By the end of the seventeenth century the people of Multan spoke a tongue in which the speech of Lahore and of Sindh were mixed. Today the Siraiki spoken in Multan is closer to Punjabi, while at Uch it is more like Sindhi, and Multan lies in the Siraiki belt that runs through Multan, Bahawalpur, Mianwali and northern Sind.

Some holy places have been open to more than one community. A devotee of the Tableeghi Jamaat, a Muslim missionary movement, said that "There are several shrines in the Multan region and in Sindh where people still practise Hinduism and give it the name of Islam", where people light lamps, present offerings and even bow down to the grave, which he called *shirk*, the sin of associating others with God.

The Hindus of modern Multan are few: they number little more than a thousand, and in the Holi locality next to the railway station only about ten to twelve of about a hundred houses belong to Hindu families. Parvati Devi, a classically trained singer and social activist of the city, recalled the mob that came after the demolition of the Babri Mosque, which destroyed the Valmiki temple near her house and wanted to burn her alive. She then took a Muslim name, Shaila Bibi, and stopped appearing on radio and television. Usman, who organised the city's token Holi in 2010, has been alleged to be an Indian agent, and more than once he and his friends have been physically attacked.

## Shrines, Endowments and the State

Rulers were patrons of the holy in Multan. Balban's son Prince Muhammad twice sent to Shiraz for the poet Saʿdi, wishing to build a *khanqah* for him in Multan and to endow villages for it, but Saʿdi could not come because of his age. A sultan made the round of the tombs of the shaikhs at Multan, and the emperor, pursuing Dara Shikoh, visited the tomb of Bahauddin. The saints stood between the state and the city: it was Shaikh Rukn al-Din who turned the sultan from a general massacre of its people after Malik Bahram's revolt.

In the modern city the powerful landowning Syeds of Multan and Bahawalpur, who claim descent from the Prophet's family, support the shrines of their ancestors and arrange yearly rituals there such as the Eid and the Muharram ceremonies marking the tragedy of Kerbala. They also support the training of musicians such as Surraiya Multanikar, who perform in the family shrines of the Syeds.

The keepers of shrines have their critics: the women in charge of the small tiled shrine of a woman saint were found no less greedy than the guardians of many other sacred places. The river carried the city's trade, as merchants from Multan and Bhakkar took their goods by boat down to Thatta.

## Festivals and Practice

A *qawwal*, a singer of *qawwali*, the Sufi devotional music, named Abu Bakr came from Multan to visit Nizam ad-Din's teacher and told of the practice of *samaʿ*, spiritual listening to music, in the hospice of the Multani shaykh. The love poems of Fakhruddin ʿIraqi are still sung by Pakistani musicians at the door of his master's tomb in Multan.

As recently as 2010 festivals including Holi were not celebrated in Multan, and in that year Usman organised a token celebration of Holi in front of the Multan Press Club. Now, though on a small scale, Holi is celebrated every year in Multan. There is no central celebration, and the thirty odd women who burned the Holika at Holi are now the only link the city has with the story of Hiranyakashipu and Prahlad.

Multan also has its place in the *Janamsakhis*, the traditional life stories of Guru Nanak. In the story of the bowl of milk and the jasmine petal, the most senior of the Sufi clerics called a conclave of all the heads of the *silsilas*, the Sufi orders. At the gates they set an earthen bowl filled to the brim with milk, and Nanak laid a jasmine petal on it.

## Poetry, Music and Language

Multan is a holy city in the vernacular poetry of Pakistan. For these poets "Mecca and Medina (and for the Sindhi and Panjabi, also Multan) are inside the heart", and the Indian tradition of the flaying of Shams-i Tabriz at Multan enters a poem of Sachal.

Amir Khusrau of Delhi and Amir Hasan were at Multan in the service of Balban's son, among his boon companions, and Fakhruddin ʿIraqi wrote his poems for his master, Baha'uddin Zakariya Multani. Multani verse has its own genres, among them the tales of Tamim ad-Dari and the Multani *marsiya*, the elegy, and the songs about Puran Bhagat spread as far as Amritsar and Multan.

In the verse of Madho Lal Hussein, in an English translation, a bride's trousseau gathers "The dress / someone carried across the snows / all the way from Kashmir. The dress / someone brought from Gujarat… The dress bought in Multan". Ehsan Wagha has worked in Multan as a music director.

## Multan in Books

At the end of the seventeenth century a Mughal account of the *suba* of Dar al-Aman Multan described the city as one of the ancient cities, with a fort of brick. Merchants brought horses of Arab breed from Iraq by way of Qandahar and sold them there, and its people made fine carpets, flowered *shatranji* rugs and chintz. The winter was mild, the summer excessively hot and the rain little. The same account set Multan on its rivers: the Indus passed the borders of the Baluch and of Multan, and beyond Multan the five rivers of the Punjab joined it.

A book of the nineteenth century often sees Multan from Lahore, where a governor who died at Multan is buried and the defeated Nawabs of Multan live. In the legend of Sakhi Sarwar, Multan has a ruler, called both *hakim*, governor, and king, with a fort, a jail and a gate of Multan.

In the Sikh tradition Multan is the meeting ground of Nanak and the Sufis. It is also a city of routes: Tulamba lies about midway between Lahore and Multan, and Deepalpur stood on an important route connecting Delhi and Multan. An author writing in 1865 spoke of "the desert between Cutch and Multan".

Multan is a place where books are made as well as a place they describe. A study of the Siraiki mystical poetry of Sind was published in Multan about 1976, books on Khwaja Farid were printed there in 1978 and 1983, and Bazm-e Saqafat and Qadimi Kutab Khana are publishers of Multan. The city has a book of its own saints, Bashir H. Nazim's *Auliya-e Multan*.
