## Overview

Peshawar is an ancient city on the western border of Pakistan. Sultan Mahmud fought Raja Jaipal there, three roads lead from it to Kabul, and from the eleventh century it was the centre from which the Nath yogis, an order of Hindu ascetics, moved out to Central Asia and Iran.

In the twentieth century Zauqi Shah, a Chishti Sufi, or Muslim mystic, began his encyclopedia of Sufi terms in the city. Sikh families have come to Peshawar for work and for safety, the city's Hindus travel from it as pilgrims, and it is said to be the place to see the lights of Diwali. In the words of a woman from the city, the frontier has "so many shrines where you can walk into the saint's shrines".

## History

Peshawar is an ancient city, and in older writings its name appears as Farshawar, Parshawar and Bagram.

A battle was fought at Peshawar in the year 391, during Sultan Mahmud's first campaign. Raja Jaipal set out to oppose him and, reaching Peshawar, drew up his army for battle. In that battle, as an old Persian history tells it, five thousand of the unbelievers were put to the sword. A shorter telling has Mahmud come to Peshawar in the month of August with a force of ten thousand horsemen, face Raja Jaipal and make him prisoner.

The city was also a seat of command. Dara sent robes of honour to the armies of Peshawar and Kabul, where Mahabat Khan held the governorship. On another occasion, after the fort of Attock had been laid out, the court turned toward Peshawar, and from that stage a *farman*, a royal order, of counsel was sent to Hakim Mirza.

When Ranjit Singh conquered Peshawar, a Qur'an was sent from the city to a shrine.

In the twentieth century the city has a place in the story of the Chishti Sabiri order, a branch of the Chishti order, a Sufi order prominent in South Asia. Zauqi Shah, a Chishti of that century, worked for newspapers in four cities, Peshawar, Lahore, Hyderabad in Sind and Karachi. His life moved between Agra, Jaipur, Bombay, Peshawar, Hyderabad, Ajmer and, finally, Karachi. Shahidullah Faridi, too, was in Peshawar.

It was in Peshawar, in 1923, that Zauqi Shah began *Sirr-i dilbaran*, a comprehensive encyclopedia of Sufi terminology. He compiled it over a number of years, and it was first published by the *silsila*, the Sufi lineage, in 1951. Its fifth edition appeared in 1985.

## The City and Its Country

In Aurangzeb's time the country around the city, a *tuman* or district known as Peshawar, lay toward Hindustan. Its grapes, peaches and melons were like those of Turan, the lands of Central Asia, while its heat and cold, its seasons and its rain were like those of Hindustan. A rice grown there was better than the rice of Bengal, and crops and grain were plentiful.

Three roads went from the city of Peshawar to Kabul. The first was the Nikshab road, with many hard defiles and a long way to go, which armies passed only with difficulty. The second joined the highway at Jalalabad; its defiles were narrow, its passes hard and its water scarce.

The third was the road of Ali Masjid and the Khyber, and it was the one armies and caravans took. It ran eighteen *kos*, an old measure of distance, from the spring of Jamshid, known as Jamrud, to a place on the bank of the Nilab. The Khyber was hard going for two *kos*, yet this road was easier than the others. Beyond, it ran thirty *kos* without hills, and then forty *kos* more to Kabul, over passes that were not very hard.

## Holy Places

Peshawar was the centre of the Nath yogis. As one historian of Sufism in India put it, "From the eleventh century the Nath yogis began to spread throughout northern India, and from their centre at Peshawar moved to all parts of Central Asia and Iran, at the same time influencing both qalandars and sufis". *Qalandars* are wandering dervishes who set aside the conventions of ordinary religious life.

A place called Gorakhtari lay in Peshawar, and a picture shows Babur visiting it.

The shrines of the frontier, in one woman's experience, are open to women. A woman disciple from Peshawar, comparing her home region with Pakpattan, where women may not walk into the saint's sanctum, said: "In the frontier, we have so many shrines where you can walk into the saint's shrines." She named no shrine in Peshawar itself.

## Communities

In Aurangzeb's time the country around Peshawar was the home of the Mohmand Afghans and of others, who paid revenue.

Peshawar is also a city to which Sikhs move. The elders of Surjeet Singh moved there from a village in Khyber Agency "for better economic opportunities". After the attacks of 1971, Tarvinder Kaur's family "fled to Peshawar, where there was a considerable Sikh population", and stayed for a few months. Sikh refugees from the tribal areas moved to Peshawar, Hassan Abdal and Nankana Sahib and started living at *gurdwaras*, Sikh places of worship, just like the migrants before them.

The city's Hindus set out from it as pilgrims to Katas. At Peshawar University, Diyal teaches comparative religion.

## Festivals and Practice

Diwali, the Hindu festival of lights, is one reason to go to Peshawar, in the words of Prem Gupta: "If you want to see the lights of Diwali, go to Peshawar."

The city's Hindus also travel as pilgrims. Hindus from Peshawar came to Katas in 2010, and in 2011 about a hundred of them arrived there in two large buses. The members of one musical band had also come from Peshawar.

## Peshawar in Books

Many writers name Peshawar only in passing. For a scholar of Sufi poetry, the word Peshawari appears in her paraphrase of the boldest Panjabi and Sindhi mystical verse: "the Sufi is no longer Arab, Hindu, Turk, or Peshawari; eventually Hallaj and the judge who condemned him, the lover and the theologian, are seen as nothing but different manifestations of the one divine reality". In her paraphrase of the monism of folk poetry, "there is no longer Turk, Hindu, or Peshawari".

In both lines, to be Peshawari stands beside Hindu and Turk as a distinction that this mystical verse does away with. The city's own name appears in her study of Islamic mysticism only as the place where a Pashto selection of Khushhal Khan Khattak was published in 1958.

In writing on the Chishti order, Peshawar is one of the cities where Zauqi Shah worked for newspapers, a stage in his travels and the place where his lexicon of Sufi terms was begun. In the words of a woman disciple, it is a frontier town in a region with "so many shrines where you can walk into the saint's shrines".

A modern retelling of the *janamsakhis*, the traditional stories of Guru Nanak's life, names the city as a stage on the return from Baghdad: "He visited Kabul before reaching Peshawar." In a study of the Hindu temple of Hinglaj Devi, Peshawar is named once, and only as the home of robbers in Abadhut's novel.

Among the older histories, the Persian chronicle written in Aurangzeb's reign gives the city the most room: its ancient names, its battle, its armies, its roads to Kabul, and its fruits and rice. A nineteenth century work names it for Sultan Mahmud's battle with Raja Jaipal and for Ranjit Singh's conquest, and a modern history of Sufism in India sees it as the centre from which the Nath yogis spread.

For a modern traveller among Pakistan's religious minorities, Peshawar is "a city on the western border of Pakistan" and the place where the lights of Diwali are to be seen. It is where Sikh families went for work and for safety, and where Hindu pilgrims set out from.
