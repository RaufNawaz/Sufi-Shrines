## Overview

Lahore, an ancient city on the bank of the Ravi, is first of all a great city full of places of prayer. By the end of the seventeenth century craftsmen of every land lived there, and in every street and bazaar there were mosques in great abundance. Its story runs from legendary founders through the Ghaznavid sultans, who made it the first centre of Persian inspired Muslim culture in the Subcontinent, to the Mughal emperors, the Sikh maharajas and the British, who made it the capital of the Punjab. It was ruined by the Mongols, plundered by Nadir Shah and Ahmad Shah Abdali and taken by Ranjit Singh, and under the British the new railway became the chief cause of its progress.

At the heart of its sacred landscape stands Data Ganj Bakhsh, the shrine of ʿAli Hujwiri, who wrote his famous Persian treatise on Sufism in the city and is called the patron saint of Lahore. The city holds the shrines of many other saints: Mian Mir, whose village stands inside his own enclosure; Mauj Darya Bukhari and Sayyid Chiragh Shah Gilani, who founded quarters of the outer city; and the great burial ground of Miani. The holy places are not only Muslim. The Sitla Mandir at Shah Alami, the Jain Mandir at Anarkali and the Bhadra Kali Mandir at Thokar Niaz Baig are among its prominent temples, and for Sikhs the city is where Guru Ram Das was born and where Guru Arjan was tortured outside the Alamgiri gate of the fort.

Lahore's communities lived side by side, sharing some holy places and fighting over others, and today its religious minorities are Hindus, Christians, Sikhs, Baha'is and Zoroastrians. The holy places had an economy and a politics of their own, and every regime remade them: under the Sikhs mosques became powder works, and under the British a tomb could be auctioned and made into a residence while the railway took temple land into its yard. The city's calendar was crowded with fairs, the greatest of them Bhadar Kali's at Niaz Beg, and Lahore is a city of poets too, from the Ghaznavid poets born there to Madho Lal Hussain, who roams its streets in his own verse.

## History: From the Founding of the City to Babur

How Lahore began is told in more than one way. In one telling Raja Ramchandar had two sons, Kushu and Lohu, and it was Lohu who settled the city. A tradition still told today holds that Lahore and Kasur are named after Luv and Kush. The city's name has also been written Lahur and Lahawar.

Before the Mughals, the city's history is a history of conquest. When Sabuktigin first invaded Hind, Raja Jaipal ruled at Lahore. Defeated, Jaipal agreed to pay tribute, then broke the agreement and imprisoned the envoy sent to collect it, and Sabuktigin marched on Lahore; the rajas of Delhi and Ajmer came to Jaipal's aid, and the Hindus were defeated. Over long ages the old city had fallen into ruin, so that Sialkot had become the seat of government of the province, and after Sultan Mahmud's conquest Malik Ayaz rebuilt the city and built a fort of solid brick.

With the Ghaznavids, about the year 1000, came scholars, theologians and poets, and Lahore became the first centre of Persian inspired Muslim culture in the Subcontinent, the capital of the Ghaznavids in India. In this town ʿAli Hujwiri spent his last years and composed the *Kashf al-maḥjūb*, the first major treatise on Sufi teaching in Persian, and he died there in 1071. Not everyone welcomed him: a number of mystics and scholars settled in Lahore were strongly hostile to his views.

Lahore was a refuge and then a capital for the sultans who followed. When the Ghurids drove Khusraw Shah from Ghazni, he took Lahore and made it his capital, and his son Khusraw Malik ruled there for twenty eight years while Shihab al-Din raided the city again and again and wore him down, until Ghurid rule began in Lahore too. Under the first Delhi sultans the city changed hands repeatedly. Aibak became sovereign there and died playing polo in Lahore in 1210, and in Iltutmish's reign Muftis, scholars of religious law, were appointed for the city.

The Mongols came next. In 639 AH the armies of Chinggis Khan's people laid siege to Lahore; the governor slipped out of the city at midnight, and Lahore was ruined and laid waste and a great many of its people taken captive. The Mongols' destruction of Lahore is dated 1241. Through the fifteenth century Lahore was a prize fought over by the Khokhars, the Sayyid sultans and the Lodis, besieged, rebuilt and besieged again, until Tatar Khan, one of the amirs of Sultan Bahlul Lodi, made it his seat of government.

Sikh tradition sets Guru Nanak's second journey in this Lodi Lahore, by then the leading town of the empire north of Delhi. Duni Chand, the *amil*, or revenue collector, of Lahore, kept a great mansion at the centre of the town, over which seven red pennants flew, each standing for a *lakh*, a hundred thousand, of rupees. Nanak gave him a needle to return "when we meet next in the other world", and Duni Chand and his wife became disciples and he gave away his seven lakh rupees. Daulat Khan Lodi, the governor, turned against Sultan Ibrahim and went over from Lahore to Babur. Attacking the Punjab, Babur destroyed the cities of Eminabad and Lahore on his way, and his conquest of the city is dated 1523 to 1524.

## History: Mughal Lahore, the Invasions and the Misls

After the defeat of Rana Sanga, Babur gave his son Prince Kamran Lahore and Multan, and the city flourished while Kamran Mirza lived there. Islam Shah meant to lay the city waste and make Mankot his capital, but the plan never took shape. When Humayun returned, Lahore passed without a battle into the hands of the emperor's servants.

Under the Mughals Lahore became a seat of power. From the start of his reign Akbar turned to its settlement: he built a city and a palace, raised large buildings and for a while moved his capital to Lahore.

The city also grew beyond its ancient walls. Settlement outside the walls began in Humayun's reign and moved south, south east and east, until a city twice the size of the original stood outside it. The names of the city's gates carry their founders' bargains and orders. The Bhats made it a condition with the ruler that a gate be called after them; Shah Alam had it proclaimed that one gate should henceforth be called Shah Alami; Akbar set up a market for every kind of grain inside another and named it Akbari Mandi; and Roshnai Gate was lit every night by royal order.

Jahangir's reign opened with the revolt of his son Khusrau, who plundered his way to Lahore but could not take the fort. From Kamran Mirza's garden to the palace gate Khusrau's companions were impaled in two rows while crowds came out to watch. The same reign is remembered in Sikh history: Guru Arjan was summoned to Lahore by Jahangir and died there. At the end of the reign Jahangir, ill on his way back from Kashmir, came to Lahore and died there. Jahangir was buried north of the city at Shahdara, on the bank of the Ravi, and from the minarets of his tomb the whole of Lahore could be seen.

The city's great buildings belong to this age. Akbar built a fort, the city wall and the palace; Jahangir raised lofty buildings; the princes and amirs built mansions, Asaf Khan's above all; and the city grew further under Shah Jahan, whose governor of Lahore was Nawab Wazir Khan and whose memorial in the city is the Shalamar garden. The city's splendour came from its tiled buildings, above all the Jami mosque of Nawab Wazir Khan. Mughal Lahore was a regional capital and a seasonal one, home to the court in its perennial rotation, and its religious and moral life was governed through its officers: the *kotwal*, the city's police chief, the chief judge, the *Shaykh al-Islam*, the chief religious scholar of the realm, and the *Qadi*, the judge, with the emperor's court above them all.

The war of succession brought Dara Shikoh to Lahore. He entered the imperial fort, took the treasury, the arsenal and the artillery and raised an army, and Guru Har Rai came to Lahore with his forces and met him. When the imperial army approached, Dara left for Multan carrying more than a crore, ten million, of rupees. Aurangzeb, on his own return, rode an elephant towards the city, dismounted at the Wazir Khan mosque and prayed the midday prayer there in congregation, and camped in the Shalamar garden while he settled the province.

The eighteenth century fell hard on the city. Mir Mannu, governing at Lahore, gave a general order to seize and kill the Sikhs, whose numbers had grown. Nadir Shah plundered Lahore thoroughly. In Ahmad Shah Abdali's wars Lahore was the prize possession of the Punjab: in 1751 his forces laid siege to it and for months the citizens were trapped within the walls, and at one of his assaults the city was put to the sword. The story is told that when Ahmad Shah came to Lahore, the saint Shah Kaku Chishti assured him that this was his own city and was sent as his envoy, that the man he went to had him killed, and that Ahmad Shah, drowned in grief and anger, ordered a general massacre.

The city lived by ransom, tribute and self defence. When the Sikhs demanded fifty thousand rupees as a *nazrana*, a gift offered to a superior, the sum was settled at twenty thousand; the *misls*, the Sikh confederacies, were paid to withdraw; and the quarters of the city bought off their plunderers or defended themselves, the Khojas behind the walls of their mosque and Dai Anga's quarter behind gates and barred lanes. After Mir Mannu's death Lahore had three Sikh rulers at once, among them Lehna Singh and Gujjar Singh. The settlements outside the walls did not survive. When the Mughal sultanate fell the outer quarters emptied, and their ruins became a quarry, dug for bricks for forty years under the Sikhs and for years more under the English. Kashmiri brick sellers became men worth a lakh of rupees, Mughalpura yielded so many bricks that thousands of mansions were built from them, and brick sellers took the fabric of tombs and mosques, digging out the foundations of Abul Hasan Khan's tomb, which had cost ten lakh, until no trace was left.

## History: Ranjit Singh, the British and Modern Lahore

When Lehna Singh, one of the rulers of Lahore, began destroying the monuments of former kings, the elders of the city invited Ranjit Singh to come and take possession of it. In 1799 Ranjit Singh was greeted enthusiastically by a population sick of the lawlessness of that time.

Under Sikh rule mosque after mosque and tomb after tomb became a powder magazine, an arsenal or a gun platform. The Masti Darwaza mosque was known through the city as the powder works mosque. Two of these stores exploded. At the Pattharan-wali haveli several thousand maunds of gunpowder blew up in an instant, the Mochi Gate quarter was demolished, and about two hundred lives were lost. The Badshahi Masjid served sometimes as an artillery park and sometimes as a cantonment, Ranjit Singh had the turrets of its minarets pulled down, and in the struggles that followed his death guns were mounted on the minarets to shell the fort.

For his own projects Ranjit Singh had the marble and stone of Lahore's tombs taken up, site by site. When stone was needed for a baradari in the Hazuri Bagh, the stone of many tombs was taken off, Zeb-un-Nisa's first, and even so it ran short. Marble from Jahangir's tomb and from Shalamar Bagh went to Amritsar for the Sri Darbar Sahib, and the marble and red stone of Khwaja Muhammad Saeed's demolished mosque and tomb were sent to Amritsar too. Under Kunwar Nau Nihal Singh a belt half a mile wide was cleared round the walled city, the buildings knocked down and the trees cut. The mosque at Shah Muhammad Ghaus's tomb fell in the clearing, and the people of Lahore, Hindus and Muslims, raised a great outcry, but no one listened.

After Ranjit Singh came the court's wars. Raja Dhian Singh was killed in the fort, and Raja Hira Singh and Pandit Jalla on the bank of the Ravi. Under the treaty of 1846 territory passed from the government of Lahore, a Resident was appointed in the city at the request of the people of the state, and in the end Duleep Singh left Lahore.

Under the British, Lahore was made the capital of the Punjab. By the 1880s the Sadr Bazaar of Anarkali ran for about a mile, as though a second city had been settled outside the first, the population had grown to twice any earlier one, and the chief cause of the city's progress was the new railway. Akbar's wall was thought useless and pulled down and a low wall built in its place, the ditch was filled with earth and laid out as gardens, and gate after gate was demolished or rebuilt, some on the old pattern and some in the English style. The railway also decided which inns for travellers prospered, and Mela Ram's failed because it lay too far from the station.

The Municipal Committee sat under the Deputy Commissioner, a sanitation department was appointed, and the bazaars were swept and sprinkled twice a day. New public buildings each had a date and a cost: Lawrence Hall was finished in 1862, the museum dates from 1864, the New College was begun in 1872 and finished in five years, and the Medical School was begun in 1881. Prisons changed with the regime. Under the Sikhs chained prisoners were sent into the city to beg for their food; under the English the Central Jail had large workshops and a printing press. Race and class were written into the plans of the jails, the asylum and the railway station, with separate rooms for European and native prisoners and for first, second and third class travellers, and the Mughal gardens and buildings passed into new hands and new uses.

The city's story runs on into the twentieth century. In 1931 Bhagat Singh, Rajguru and Sukhdev were hanged at Lahore jail. The Muslim League presented the Lahore Resolution in the city in March 1940, and before the partition riots that spread through the city the Sikh leader Tara Singh drew his *kirpan*, the Sikh's ceremonial dagger, on the stairs of the Punjab Assembly in Lahore.

## A City of Saints: The Sacred Landscape

Lahore is a landscape of saints. In the nineteenth century the city's history could be told, at root, as a history of its saints: of the graves, *mazars* and tombs of its elders, the sacred tombs on every side of Lahore that stand as memorials of the holy men of the past. Beside them stand the mosques, temples, gurdwaras, churches and graveyards of the city's many communities.

At the heart of the city's devotion stands the grave of Ali Hujwiri. "His shrine, called that of Data Ganj Bakhsh, is still a popular place of pilgrimage in Lahore", Data Darbar is Lahore's most prominent shrine complex, and Hujwiri is called Lahore's patron saint. Shah Hussain is counted one of the patron saints of Lahore as well, and Madho Lal Husain is buried together with his Hindu beloved in a modest tomb near the Shalimar gardens. In the Mughal period the Qadiri order was represented in Lahore by Mian Mir, who had come there from Sind together with his mystically inclined sister, Bibi Jamal. In Lahore today the Qalandars, wandering dervishes who abandon legal norms, social conventions and even distinctions of gender, are still associated with the great saints of the city, such as Shah Hussain, Shah Jamal and Mian Mir.

Devotion did not always need an establishment. In the 1880s the two graves of Pir Zaki had no *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of a saint's death, no custodian, no offering and no endowment, yet devotees came to recite the *fatiha*, the opening chapter of the Quran, at both. Bibiyan Pak Daman is a shrine in Lahore dedicated to women saints and particularly revered by the Shiʿa.

Tradition holds that the saints guard the city. Of a *majzub*, a faqir absorbed in divine love, who lies buried at a *khanqah*, a Sufi lodge, it is said that by his kindness Lahore was kept safe from Ahmad Shah Abdali, and a living saint could protect a whole quarter: Ahmad Shah Durrani set a royal guard in Haji Muhammad Saeed's neighbourhood so that no plunderer should touch it.

Saints founded the quarters of the outer city. By the 1880s its quarters were tied to Mauj Darya Bukhari and Sayyid Chiragh Shah Gilani, to Shah Dargahi and Shah Badr al-Din Gilani and to other holy men, and Mughalpura was settled around Khawand Mahmud's tomb. Villages settled inside shrine enclosures: the village of Begampura stands within one, and the village of Mian Mir within Mian Mir's own. The city has also grown over places that once lay outside it. Mozang had become one of the quarters of Lahore by the nineteenth century, and the shrine of Madho Lal Hussain stands in Baghbanpura, once on the outskirts of Lahore and now within the expanding city.

The great burial ground of Miani lies west of Mozang. In Akbar's reign, it is said, the place was a village in which only learned men lived, and because Punjabis call the *ulama*, the learned men of religion, *mian*, it was called Miani; when the village was at last deserted, it became the graveyard it is now.

The fort and its gates stand at the centre of the Sikh memory of the city. Guru Ram Das was born in Lahore, Guru Arjan spent much time in the city, where he was killed and his remains interred, and Guru Hargobind also spent much time there. Lahore has temples as well as shrines. The Neela Gumbad Valmiki Mandir stands in the historical market of Anarkali and serves the city's Balmiki Hindus, and among the city's prominent temples are the Sitla Mandir at Shah Alami, the Jain Mandir at Anarkali and the Bhadra Kali Mandir at Thokar Niaz Baig. In Lahore, Muslims and Christians whose ancestors were once Hindus still visit a Hindu temple that their ancestors visited.

There are smaller sacred places too, among them a shrine of cats next to Bhatti gate, and the churches and the Baha'i centre complete the landscape. The city is also the resting place of modern figures: Muhammad Iqbal, the poet, is buried in Lahore.

## A City of Saints: The Fabric of the Holy Places

Not everything that looks new in Lahore is new. In the Sikh period even temples of old foundation were built anew, so that their fabric looks fresh. One site shows the whole story: its enclosure had belonged to the Wazir Khan mosque in the time of the Muslim kings, and its rents were spent on the mosque; when that rule passed, a man obtained a lease and built a small mud *shiwala*, a temple of Shiva, and Raja Dina Nath then built the present masonry temple at great expense in Ranjit Singh's time.

Many buildings carry their dates on their walls, sometimes in a chronogram, a verse whose letters, read as numbers, give the year. The Wazir Khan mosque records that it was completed in the reign of Shah Jahan, the Sunehri Masjid is dated on its *mihrab*, the prayer niche, and the dome of Data Ganj Bakhsh carries its date. Hindu and Sikh foundations are dated in the Samvat, the Bikrami era of the Hindu calendar: the Devi's mandir followed her appearance in Samvat 1931, which corresponds to 1875, and the *samadhs* of the Sikh nobility, their memorial tombs, carry the same era.

The holy places were carefully measured. Mian Mir's platform is nineteen by nineteen *zira*, an old cubit, square, and Ali Mardan Khan's tomb is three storeys high, with a basement holding three masonry graves, and no tomb in Lahore is as tall. Jahangir's tomb has a marble platform nine feet wide, forty cells and four minarets, and of its twelve tanks eleven survived.

The gilded finial, the *kalas*, shows whether a building of the Sikh era was ever finished. Several grand buildings stood unfinished, among them Raja Teja Singh's samadh, and the work at Khushal Singh's passed to an heir who neglected it.

Wills governed the fabric of graves, and were sometimes built against. Mian Wada's tomb has no dome and his grave is of unbaked earth, because he willed that his grave not be made of masonry, and Shaikh Sadi Balkhari willed that no dome be built over his grave, so none was. Sharaf un-Nisa's tomb was built without stairs and kept so by her will; the Begum climbed up every day by setting a wooden ladder against it. Many tombs were built in their occupants' lifetimes or by their disciples: Mauj Darya's by Akbar's order in the saint's lifetime, and Haji Nur's enclosure on the plan of the Kaaba.

The working fabric of the holy places was close and practical. There are small rooms for the *pujaris*, the temple priests, masonry rooms for faqirs at the *dharamsala*, the pilgrims' rest house, and the samadhs of *mahants*, the heads of temple establishments, inside the Shivala Tarpoliya's own halls. At Mian Mir a grand hall is the seat of the *mujawirs*, the attendants of the shrine, and a separate building the seat of the *sajjada nashin*, the hereditary custodian who sits on the saint's prayer carpet. There are wells at most sites, and a well at Mian Mir took fifty years to make.

Several holy places gave water to the public, among them the well and red stone *sabil*, a place for giving water, at the Sant Shah mandir that serve high and low alike. Canal water ran through the garden belt and the institutions: it runs inside every one of Shalamar's gardens, fills Diwan Ratan Chand's tank, and at the railway works is raised by engine to an iron cistern and distributed to every workshop.

The tilework of the city's tombs and mosques goes by two names. At the Dai Anga mosque it is called *kansi kar* work, in yellow, and at the Nila Gumbad, whose grand dome is green and turquoise, *kashi kari*. On Sharaf un-Nisa's tomb the tilework forms great painted cypresses and gives the building its byname, the Cypress Tomb.

## Communities

In Aurangzeb's time Lahore was a gathering of peoples, where craftsmen of every land lived. By the 1880s Muslim, Hindu, Sikh, Brahmo and Christian foundations stood in the city side by side, and traffic between the communities ran in both directions.

In the 1880s the city's Muslims were divided four ways. The Sunnis came first. The Shia mourned greatly in the ten days of Muharram and made *taziyas*, models of the Imam's tomb. The Wahhabis followed no imam and called themselves *muwahhid*, those who affirm the unity of God. And the Sufis bore enmity to no one, mostly listened to *sama*, Sufi music and spiritual listening, and were called the sect of peace with all and without prejudice. The Sufi orders included the Qadiri, Chishti, Suhrawardi, Naqshbandi and Naushahi.

The titles for the holders of religious buildings follow the community that holds them: a *mahant* held Hindu and Sikh temples, a *pujari* the shivalas and thakurdwaras, and a *mutawalli*, the trustee of an endowment, an *imam* or a *mujawir* the mosques and tombs. Yet the titles crossed from one tradition to another. *Sajjada nashin* was used of the office at a Hindu samadh, and *gaddi nashin*, the one who sits on the seat, of a Sikh *mahant* at Mozang.

Some rites and holy places were shared. On Sundays Hindus and Muslims alike brought children suffering from an illness to Shah Dargahi's well and bathed them there. Hindus attended the Guru Ram Das gurdwara, and *qawwals*, singers of *qawwali*, the Sufi devotional music, sang *kafis* of divine unity there. The shrine of Sitla Mata was the resort of both Hindus and Muslims, and Muslims too, after their children recovered from smallpox, brought them there and made offerings. Hindus and Muslims attended Miran Badshah's tomb on Thursdays, both fairs at Madho Lal Husain drew Hindus and Muslims, and on the twenty eighth of Shaban they held a fair together at Shah Bilawal's *khanqah*.

The communities also acted together. Hindus and Muslims together protested against the demolition of the mosque at Shah Muhammad Ghaus's tomb, and Hindus, Muslims and Christians all gladly joined the subscription to repair the Badshahi Masjid. Buildings changed hands between communities: Ranjit Singh renamed the Moti Masjid the Moti Mandir, and a Hindu temple was founded on an image a Muslim Turkish soldier had sold to the Hindus of a quarter. Two mosques passed to Hindu holy men under the Sikhs and were taken back under the English, each time by a Muslim faqir who evicted the Hindu holder by force.

The communities also met in violence. Under Mir Mannu there was a general order to seize and kill the Sikhs. In 1849 there was a fierce quarrel between Shia and Sunni near Shah Alami Gate that came to blows, until the Deputy Commissioner summoned cavalry from the Anarkali cantonment.

The city knew separation as well as mixing. The settlement of the cow killing butchers was set apart by royal order because the Hindu subjects loathed them. Under the British the honorary magistracy was a communal quota of eight of Lahore's notables, four Hindu and four Muslim, and the hospital kept separate kitchens for Hindu, Muslim, and Christian and European patients. Some communities ran their own observances: the blacksmiths held the annual *ʿurs* of Shaikh Musa, himself a blacksmith, and the gravediggers of Miani were a community of their own. Every Sunday followers of the Brahmo faith gathered at the Brahmo Samaj to worship the one God, and the Christian foundations included the Mian Mir church, which seats about eight hundred.

Women founded and patronised holy places in all three traditions. Musammat Jwala Dai built her mandir and made it *waqf*, a religious endowment, and Rani Jindan built a shivala beside her thakurdwara while she held power. The founder of the Masti Darwaza mosque was Maryam Zamani, wife of the emperor Jahangir; Zeb-un-Nisa Begum, a poet, built her own tomb in her lifetime; and Begampura is named after the mother of Nawab Khan Bahadur, who built all of it. Moran the courtesan built the mosque that bears her name, a renowned mosque of Lahore. Sati is part of the city's fabric too: there are eleven samadhs with Ranjit Singh's, for the eleven ranis who burned themselves on the pyre with him.

Today Lahore is a city of minorities as well as of the Muslim majority. Five religious communities live in and around it: Hindus, Christians, Sikhs, Baha'is and Zoroastrians, the Parsis. Partition runs through their memories: Bishna Ram recalls that friends and relatives told his family "that Lahore was no longer safe for us". The Hindus of Lahore are few and careful, and after the sixth of December 1992 the city's temples were attacked. The Sikhs, once its rulers, are now rare in the city, and only a handful of Parsis remain, thirty five in Lahore. Christians meet an old prejudice, and in one outbreak a mob burned down 170 Christian houses. "On 28th May 2010, two houses of worship of the Ahmadiyya community in Lahore were attacked, resulting in the death of 94 people".

The shrines matter to the women of the city. In the local neighbourhood mosques a woman cannot offer her ritual prayers: "Thus, the important spheres of religious and spiritual participation for women are the Sufi shrines". Sufi devotion in Lahore is also domestic, and two weekly circles of remembrance meet in private homes, one of them led by a female disciple for a gathering made up mostly of women.

## Shrines, Endowments and the State

In the 1880s authority over a Lahore temple or shrine was often stated as ownership, and it rested with the man who served there rather than with any trust. Mosques kept the prayer leader and the trustee apart.

Custodians were paid by shares, by salaries and by stipends. At Data Ganj Bakhsh more than seventy *mujawirs*, the attendants who keep a shrine, divided the offerings in equal shares. A whole royal *samadh* complex was staffed by a single boy, Nikka Singh, seventeen years old, appointed and paid a yearly sum by the government. State and princely stipends came and went: for as long as he lived, Maharaja Kharak Singh kept up a mosque imam's allowance of ten rupees a month, while Mauj Darya's khanqah received forty rupees a month under the Sikhs and nothing at all by the 1880s. Funding often lapsed with its funder. Raja Dina Nath gave thousands of rupees to the khanqah of Fazl Shah Majzub, and while he lived the *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death, was kept with great pomp, and devotees went on holding it after him; by the 1880s only an annual prayer for the dead was left.

Succession passed by discipleship, by heredity and by teaching lineage. At Wazir Khan's mosque the imamat passed through three generations of one family, and teaching lineage could serve as a title deed: when Sikh rule ended and the Qasab Khana mosque fell empty, the *sajjada nashin*, the hereditary head, of Mian Wadda's khanqah seized it on the claim that its old teacher had been Mian Wadda's pupil. The state could appoint the head of a Hindu shrine, as the three rulers of Lahore jointly made Bawa Parsotam Das *sajjada nashin* of a temple, and a gift of land at Shalamar made the office of gardener hereditary, generation after generation.

Some custodians neglected their charge, and custodians also sold what they held, again and again. The fullest case is the Khoja mosque. A man claimed it in the district court on the ground that his grandfather had been its *mutawalli*, the Khoja community bore witness for him, and the Deputy Commissioner gave him the mosque; he ran it as a mosque for a year or two, then made it a residence, let it to English tenants, and finally sold it. *Mujawirs* sold shrine land, and when English rule came the owner of the Gilani Sayyids' graveyard settled many people in it and charged them rent.

Possession was fought over, sometimes for decades. At Begampura the contest ran for fifty years under three regimes, through an artillery officer, Sansar Chand of Kangra, who made the place over to his Brahmins, and a nawab from Kabul whose case stood before Ranjit Singh for years and came to nothing.

Shops built under and over mosques were the usual Muslim endowment: the Sunehri Masjid was raised a storey and shops were built beneath it on every side so that their rent might pay for the mosque. Wazir Khan's endowment shows how much could be lost. The income of everything he built, from the mosque to the Delhi Gate, its shops, an inn and a bathhouse, belonged to the mosque; by the 1880s the inn and the bathhouse were government property and much of the rest had passed into other people's names. *Waqf*, a religious endowment, could also be legal shelter: warned that Aurangzeb had ordered his dismissal, Sarfaraz Khan at once made all his immovable property *waqf*, and the emperor, pleased, restored him to office.

Hindu temples were endowed too, often by purchase, and shop and house rent was the working income of many of them. Pandit Bishnath, being childless, endowed two *pakka* havelis and four shops, from whose rents the temple ran comfortably. How an endowment was made could decide whether it survived. Bakhshi Bhagat Ram set apart a village from his own *jagir*, a grant of land revenue, for a shivala's expenses, but because he had made the grant on his own authority and not the government's, the village was confiscated along with his *jagir*. The *samadh* of Haqiqat Rai lost its grant on its servant's death under the English, until after much effort a yearly cash sum was fixed in the name of the *samadh* itself.

The courts of the Muslim kings had their own order. In their time Lahore had four muftis and one *qazi*, the judge, each mufti reckoned the ruler of a quarter of the city. The Mughal state was also the city's builder and keeper: Jahangir ordered a tall minaret at every *kos*, a road measure of about two miles, along the highway from Agra to Lahore, and under Aurangzeb an embankment ten *kos* long was ordered against the Ravi and repaired every year for more than forty years. The state disciplined the city as well. Jahangir's decrees forbade tobacco, and in the case of Haqiqat Rai the *qazi* of the city wrote a fatwa for his death; offered his life if he became a Muslim, he refused and was beheaded.

The city's monuments were at the mercy of its rulers. When the cantonments of Ranjit Singh's armies were built, the bricks of old tombs and houses were pulled out, so that thousands of tombs, domes, mosques, gardens and old havelis were lost. In recent times the state's hand has reached Lahore's shrines in other ways. Special trains were started to carry pilgrims between Sehwan and Lahore, a terror attack at Lahore's most prominent shrine led to tighter security at major Sufi sites across the country, and the Punjab government banned the use of drugs at a famous Sufi shrine in Lahore.

## The Religious Economy

For the Hindu temples inside the walled city, the offering was the endowment. At one thakurdwara the temple's necessary expenses rested on offerings alone, with no *jagir* and no daily stipend fixed, and at Chor Mor Wala the *mahant*, the head of the establishment, and the pujaris lived on the offerings alone. Elsewhere the custodian took offerings on top of rent and land.

Where a temple stood decided much of what it received. Because one mandir stood on the bazaar, people came in great numbers to worship and much was offered, and at the Bhairo ji seat the *mahant* sat in the window and at once picked up whatever was offered on the road. At Mian Mir the five *melas*, festive fairs, and the large offerings were put down to the population of the cantonment.

Some charges were fixed for each occasion. In one Khatri brotherhood every family holding a wedding offered eight annas at the shivala, an anna being a sixteenth of a rupee. For a mosque's upkeep it was settled that four annas should be taken from the party to every *nikah*, the Muslim marriage contract, in the city. At one burial ground, when a body came, the family of the dead gave the gravediggers their due and gave the *sajjada nashin* something besides, as they were able.

*Nazr*, the offering made to the holy, ran upwards and was sometimes refused. Ranjit Singh gave one holy man lakhs of rupees in cash as *nazr*, and after his illness made an offering at the grave of Miran Badshah; Mastan Shah threw away the *nazrs* of a thousand rupees that Ranjit Singh sent him, refusing to take even one. *Nazr* also ran downwards, from patron to participants. For the ten days of Muharram the Qizilbash household gave lavish meals day and night to Sunni and Shia alike, and in all not less than ten thousand rupees a year was spent.

*Chanda*, subscription, was the alternative to endowment: temples were built by subscription and run on the offerings, and a mosque was finished by subscription after its founder's death. Private money did public work. At the house of the Faqir brothers there were a dispensary and a madrasa where people were taught free, and charitable wells were even built from the proceeds of a theft, the well named after Sadhu the thief among them. Money moved at the scale of the court too: of Moran, the Maharaja struck coin in her name, which circulated through the whole Punjab, and in the 1880s her coins were still kept as keepsakes.

## Religious Property under British Rule

In the 1880s the state's repairs to Lahore's buildings ran through its Executive Engineer, Kanhaiya Lal, the city's historian, who administered the annual repair grant for Shalamar Bagh and urged that the repair of the Badshahi Masjid could be completed if the administration turned to it once more.

Under the British, *nazul*, the property that had passed to the state, and auction were the means by which religious property changed hands. Shaikh Muhtaram's tomb was auctioned at an Englishman's request and made into a *kothi*, a residence, its graves demolished; Abul Hasan Khan's tomb was auctioned and quarried for its stone; and Sultan the contractor bought the Pari Mahal from the government, rooted it out to its foundations and used its bricks to build Landa Bazar. *Nazul* land could also found a temple, as Nihal Chand bought vacant land at auction and began to build his mandir on it.

The state sold and demolished monuments itself. The government pulled down Maharaja Kharak Singh's haveli and sold its bricks and stones, and the royal buildings of the fort were pulled down and quarters built for the British soldiers. At the Badshahi Masjid British troops were quartered, the eastern cells pulled down and windows cut in the cells for the English.

Tombs and mosques were cleared for fortification, roads, pipes and rails, and this began before the British: Ranjit Singh's moat before the Bhati Gate took both a mosque and a tomb. In the early 1880s the government resolved to straighten every large bazaar along one side and to lay water pipes, and it paid settlements to the mosques and temples that lost ground. The railway was the largest taker of all. At Tali Sahib the land came inside the railway's yard, and the *tahli* tree, which had stood since the time of Sri Chand, which gave the place its name and was worshipped, the railway cut down; its root greened again and grew back into a tree. One temple's claim against the state ran its full course: when a government water pipe cracked the walls of the Bhagat Ram shiwala, an inquiry proved the cause and five hundred rupees were paid for the repair.

Under every regime the use of holy buildings changed: tombs and mosques became barracks, *kothis*, offices, kitchens, stores, a treasury, a mess, a police station, a mission school and an asylum. The fullest single case is the Dai Anga mosque, which passed through five occupiers: endowed as *waqf* under the Mughals, it held powder and shot under Ranjit Singh, became the *kothi* of the superintendent of the Lahore press, and then passed to the railway, whose ironworks officer lived in it. The furthest any holy place passed into state hands was Gilani's tomb, whose house became the Lieutenant Governor's residence, the old tomb remaining in its lower storey.

Restitution came through named officers. Major MacGregor, Deputy Commissioner of Lahore, released the Masti Darwaza mosque to the Muslims with great generosity. At Dalip Singh's exile the army was taken out of the Badshahi Masjid and the government handed the mosque to the Muslims, and at the Nila Gumbad Munshi Najm-ud-Din petitioned the government, had the mosque and tomb released and paid for its repair himself. The British also repaired and preserved. They kept Jahangir's tomb in repair from the first and spent thousands of rupees on an embankment to protect it, and the Wazir Khan *baradari*, a pavilion with twelve doors, was repaired so well that it seemed built anew. The repair of the Badshahi Masjid was raised at a Deputy Commissioner's *darbar*, a public assembly, with a government grant and subscriptions from every community; in the 1880s the turrets of its four minarets were still waiting.

Teaching passed from the mosques to the government. Under the Sikhs there was no government madrasa, but boys were taught in every mosque, and the first lesson was the Quran. Under the English, government schools were opened everywhere. At Moran's mosque the change can be seen in one family: by Ranjit Singh's order its imamat was given to Maulana Ghulam Rasul and Ghulam Allah, the master teachers of the whole Punjab, and a madrasa flourished there, but when both had died and government schools opened, its line was broken, and within two generations three of Ghulam Allah's five sons held state teaching posts.

The colonial state licensed, administered and suppressed. The *chiraghan* mela, the festival of lamps, used to last one day, and the government made it two. Outside the Delhi Gate stood the house of the Rasul Shahi order, whom the British licensed to distil for their own drinking only, until the government grew suspicious and closed the still, and from that day no one came to the house. In the 1930s there was a fierce debate in Chishti circles over whether the British could compel a minor who had become *diwan*, the hereditary head, of the shrine at Pakpattan to be educated at a British college in Lahore.

## Festivals and Practice

By the 1880s Lahore kept a crowded calendar of festivals, and Hindus and Muslims shared some of it. Mian Mir had five *melas*, fairs, a year: the *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death, which lasted two days and two nights, and four more on the four Wednesdays of Sawan. At Data Ganj Bakhsh there was a mela every Friday, the *ʿurs* in Safar, the mela of the Last Wednesday and a vigil every Thursday night. Mauj Darya's annual *ʿurs* drew a great crowd, Faqir Sain Qutb Shah Qadri's came twice a year with *qawwali*, the devotional music of the shrines, all day, and many other saints had their days in the Islamic calendar.

Muharram was kept in the great houses. In the Qizilbash household there was mourning for the ten days, with food given out day and night to Sunni and Shia alike, and at Mubarak Haveli the seventh of Muharram drew the *mehndi* and *alam* processions of the whole city, the henna procession and the bearers of the standards.

The Hindu and Sikh calendars were just as full. On every Ekadashi one temple held a great mela, where bhagats sang *bhajans*, devotional hymns, all night, and at the Kali Mata mandir a mela gathered on every one of the nine days of Navratri. On the day of Basant a mela was held at the *samadh* of Haqiqat Rai, and at Mozang the Sikhs gathered on the fifth day of every bright half of the moon, when consecrated food was distributed and lamps were lit.

The greatest mela of the city was Bhadar Kali's at Niaz Beg, held every year in the month of Jeth; no mela in Lahore was greater, and Muslims too, for the most part, attended. Because it fell in the hot season people suffered, and stalls of sharbat, a sweet cold drink, and of water were set up, and sharbat was given to all alike. The crowd stayed a night and a day, and inside the walls, on the same day, the city temple of Bhadr Kali drew an immense gathering of those who did not go out to the village.

By the 1880s many melas, *ʿurs* and *sama* gatherings, the Sufi assemblies of spiritual listening to music, were in decline. At the tomb of Miran Badshah inside the Wazir Khan mosque, Hindus and Muslims still came on Thursdays and the *ʿurs* was still kept, but the *sama* belonged to the past, and the great annual melas of the Sikh period at other shrines had little by little lost their splendour. Two melas grew instead: Mian Mir's, with the population of the garrison, and the *chiraghan* mela, the festival of lamps, whose crowds came from Lahore, Amritsar and distant cities because of the railway. The anniversary of Madho Lal is still celebrated in Lahore near the Shalimar Garden on the last Saturday in March, famous as *Melā Chirāghān*, the Fair of Lamps.

Vows were tied to sickness. At Sitla Mata the special worship came when smallpox was raging, and thousands whose children had recovered came in joy. At Ghore Shah clay horses were offered at the tomb in thousands on the day of the mela.

The *langar*, the free communal kitchen, fed the poor across the traditions. Any poor person who came to the Ram Dwara was fed, a public *langar* ran continuously at Mauj Darya's house, and at Than Bhairo the temple's mahant fed the faqirs and the jogis who gathered for the mela. By the 1880s Mian Wada's khanqah maintained about two hundred blind and lame people, all were taught the Quran, and the blind were made to learn it by heart.

*Sama* and music ran through the city's devotion. In the assemblies Punjabi *kafis*, *barah mah* and *abyat* were sung, mystic lyrics, poems of the twelve months and couplets, and women musicians performed *hazrī*, literally "presence", at the Sufi shrines of Lahore, above all at the time of the *ʿurs*.

The Milad procession, in honour of the Prophet's birthday, began in Lahore in a single year at a single mosque. In 1935 a group of about a hundred people left the Wazir Khan Mosque singing praises of the Prophet and walked around the alleys of the walled city. The event spread beyond the walled city, and in time the procession had to be moved outside Delhi Gate, where the junction where it converged every year took the name Millad Chowk.

The city's religious minorities keep their own calendar: Shri Valmiki's birthday and Krishna Janamashtami, Palm Sunday and Good Friday at the Sacred Heart Cathedral, Easter at the Cathedral Church of Resurrection, Navroz, and the commemoration of Guru Arjan's martyrdom at Gurdwara Dera Sahib. "On religious festivals like Holi, Diwali and Shri Valmiki's birthday, the Neela Gumbad Mandir becomes an island of religious syncretism in Lahore".

## Poetry

Lahore was a city of Persian poets under the Ghaznavid kings. Mas'ud-i-Sa'd-i-Salman's family came from Hamadan in Persia, but he himself was born at Lahore, and in several passages of his poems he speaks of it as his native place. He was the panegyrist, the poet of praise, of five Ghaznavid kings, and he was twice imprisoned before he retired from public life.

Madho Lal Hussain is Lahore's own poet, pictured roaming the streets of Lahore drunkenly in the company of fellow carousers. His verse was first printed in the city: a collection in Gurmukhi, the script of Punjabi used by Sikhs, was published in Lahore around 1900.

The Mughal court spoke verse in the city too, and the mosques of Lahore carry verse in their inscriptions. Over the gate of the mosque of Muhammad Salih Kambo, three niches hold two lines that name its founder:

بانی این مسجد زیبا نگار
بنائے آل محمد صالح است

Other mosques carry chronograms, verses whose letters, read as numbers, give a year, and one chronogram was the work of Farid, a poet of Lahore, whose line is written beneath it.

## Lahore in Books

Two older accounts describe Lahore as a seat of poets and of rulers. In the Ghaznavid age it was the birthplace and native place of Persian poets. To a Mughal chronicler it was a great city and a mighty metropolis, where craftsmen of every land lived and the goods of the seven climes and the wares of sea and land were sold, the seat of the sultanate and the capital of the realms.

In the nineteenth century *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* was written on the orders of the commissioner, who asked for a detailed account of the buildings, tombs and mosques of the environs of Lahore. Its author held that no book had yet covered the antiquities of Lahore in full, and described his own as reporting on the tombs of the Muslim elders, on other buildings and on the old customs of Lahore, while being at root a history of the saints.

*Tarikh-e Lahore*, the history of 1884, was written by a resident of the city who was also an engineer and a historian of property, and his hand shows in it. He dated buildings by counting back from his own day, and where he had no year he dated by reign. He framed the city's history as a sequence of destructions, thirteen numbered shocks of plunder and demolition. In four cases close together he cast Sikh rule as the time of loss and English rule as the time of restitution, but elsewhere he reported both regimes in the same register and drew no general conclusion from the contrast. He vouched in his own words for the state's fairness in paying compensation, and he was also a party to what he reported: state repairs ran through him as Executive Engineer, and he asked the administration publicly, in writing, for a further grant for the Badshahi Masjid. He explained a shrine's prosperity by the man in charge, not by the saint.

Lahore is also a city of manuscripts and print. The Punjab University Library holds a manuscript of Madho Lal Hussain's verse and the unpublished original of a Persian verse life of Lal Husain written by an eyewitness. The city's printing houses issued mystical poetry, among them a Panjabi version of the *Burda* and a commentary on Bullhe Shah, and Lahore publishers also issued the literature on *qawwali*. The texts of a modern Sufi order reach readers through the Urdu bookstores of Karachi and Lahore.

Modern scholars have seen Lahore from very different distances, some studying in the city and making it the base for their fieldwork. For a writer based in the city, Lahore is home and field, and he watches it change. The Anarkali market has become the largest tyre market in the city, and the Pak Tea House a few shops away, once famed for the gatherings of the city's leading intelligentsia, is on its way to becoming yet another tyre shop. The Ravi, which once flowed next to the fort, has moved a few kilometres away, and the Defence Housing Authority sprawls across a vast tract of land, eating into the small historic villages of the area.
