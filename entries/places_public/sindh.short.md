## Overview

Sindh is the country of the lower Indus valley, the southern part of present day Pakistan. By the end of the seventeenth century it was a Mughal *suba*, or province, the *suba* of Thatta, made up of four *sarkars*, or divisions: Thatta, Siwistan, Nasirpur and Umarkot. The name has never had fixed edges, and in Baluch poetry it means not the modern political division but the valley of the Indus. Under the British, Sindh was governed from Bombay rather than Calcutta, and today it is the province of Pakistan with the most significant Hindu population.

The Arabs reached Sindh in 711 and the country was under Arab governors for nearly three hundred years. Local dynasties followed, the Sumra and then the Samma, and from 1520 the Turkish Arghun and then the Tarkhan, under whom Sindh fell to the Mughal Empire; Akbar annexed it in 1592. In the eighteenth century the Kalhora rose from governors to partial independence, and the Talpurs who overthrew them ruled the country until the British conquered it in 1843. At Partition the departure of the Hindu elite and the arrival of Muslim refugees from India changed the make up of the province's people.

After the Arab conquest Sindh never ceased to be a centre of Islamic studies and a seat of Islamic mysticism, and from the thirteenth century, when Lal Shahbaz Qalandar settled in Sehwan, it produced an immense number of mystics, whose shrines are scattered all through the Indus valley. The river had a saint of its own, Khwajo Khizr, whose cult was supported by Muslim and Hindu alike. The province's Hindus are tied to Sufism through tombs visited by Muslims and Hindus alike, such as Bhit Shah, through saints with a double identity, such as Udero Lal, and through Sufi *darbars*, saints' shrines and courts, managed by Hindus, such as that of Pithoro Pir.

In the eighteenth century the people of Sindh were for the most part converted Jats, the majority Sunni but with very influential Shia sections, while the Hindus financed trade and farmed the revenue. The Sufis made the spoken language into a literary one, and the *Risalo*, the collected poems, of Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit is universally acknowledged as the greatest classic of Sindhi literature, loved by Hindus as much as by Muslims. Under the Kalhora the reverence for *pirs*, Sufi masters, Sayyids, the descendants of the Prophet, and holy men became the leading feature of eighteenth century Sindh. Despite the spread of Islamist terrorism in Sindh, the Sufi culture still pervades Sindhi society.

## History: From the Earliest Capitals to the Mughal Conquest

Sindh has been named and bounded in many ways. Arab historians spoke of Hind and Sindh separately: Sindh was bounded by Multan, Jaisalmer and Gujarat, and Hind began where Sindh ended. Its history is hard to write, for reliable records of the years 1690 to 1760 are almost wholly absent, and the land, an alluvial tract over which the Indus has wandered in shifting channels, is poor in informative historical monuments. For the most part Sindh stood apart from the crucial events of neighbouring Asia, and it kept to a late date the theocratic foundation of Islamic society.

Its earliest capitals were Brahmanabad, a great city whose fort had fourteen hundred towers, then Adpur, then Dewal, and in the late seventeenth century Thatta and Debal were the seat of government. The Arabs came first as traders and then as conquerors. The story that the conquest began as a punitive expedition against the ruling Raja Dahir is a myth: it was begun by Hajjaj, Walid's governor in Iraq, as one part of his policy of expansion, and Muhammad bin Qasim al-Saqafi, who began his advance through Makran and Baluchistan in 710, conquered Sindh between 711 and 712. The country was under Arab governors for nearly three hundred years, yet the Arabs left no permanent mark upon the land; they were merely a military garrison living among strangers.

Brahmans in high office converted to keep their positions, and Buddhists, hostile to Brahman domination, converted as well, while the internal administration was left largely in the hands of Hindu landlords paying the land tax, *kharaj*, and the poll tax, *jizya*. The tenth century was the age of the Isma'ilis, and towards its end the Qarmatians ruled northern Sindh and Multan. Little is known of Sufis in Sindh in these centuries, in part because Mansura, the capital of Sindh, accepted the overlordship of the Egyptian Caliphs, so that Sufism there was cut off from other lands; accounts differ on which dynasty held Mansura at the time. When Sindh ceased to receive Arab governors, independent dynasties arose, among which the Sumra, a local dynasty of Rajput origin, soon became prominent. A Sumra line of thirty six princes is said to have reigned for five hundred years, and the Sumra ruled Lower Sindh until the middle of the fourteenth century.

The thirteenth century tied the province to the sultans of Delhi and to the great Suhrawardi centre at Multan. Shihab al-Din went against Dewal, which is known as Thatta, and brought the whole country along the river Sindh under his control. Seven years after November 1221 Iltutmish routed his rival Nasiru'd-Din Qubacha from Sindh, and Shaikh Baha'uddin Zakariya openly sided with Iltutmish; after Iltutmish annexed Multan and Sindh in 1228, the Sultan and the Shaikh grew more intimate.

The fourteenth century was a time of passage from the Sumra to the Samma. The Samma officially took power in 1351, but the Sumra kept control of certain outlying areas, among them the area of Umarkot. In 1367 Firuz Shah Tughluq came to lay siege to Thatta, and in the same century the governors of Sindh sought the help of Makhdum Jahaniyan. The Samma rulers bore the title Jam, and their line ran on, through many rulers, to Jam Nanda and Jam Firuz, the last of them.

Sindh became a Muslim land late. Its people were converted not by the Arabs but by the Central Asian peoples who took up Muslim penetration of India from the twelfth century onwards, and most of the population was converted in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries; under Jam Nizamuddin, about 1461, the mass of the common people became Muslim. By the time of the Lodi Afghans in the sixteenth century the farming population and the landholding class were almost entirely Muslim, and the Hindus were confined to the business of writing and to trade, as they remained down to the British annexation. Persian remained the chief literary language of the Muslim elite down to the Mughal period and beyond.

The Samma line ended in a quarrel within the family. Jam Salahuddin, a kinsman of Jam Firuz, twice raised armies from Gujarat and won; Jam Firuz called in Mirza Shah Beg Arghun from Qandahar, and Salahuddin was killed. Then Mirza Shah Beg Arghun led an army from Qandahar and took Thatta, and Jam Firuz went to Gujarat and died there. Turkish influence prevailed in Sindh from 1520, when the Arghun overcame the last native Sindhi rulers, and the Arghun were followed by the Tarkhan. With these two Central Asian dynasties the Naqshbandi order came to Sindh from the sixteenth century onwards.

Sindh's independent rule ended when Akbar added it to the Mughal Empire in 1592, though accounts of the year differ. Mirza Jani Beg came to court with the Khankhanan, Mirza Abd al-Rahim, and was given the rank of three thousand and Thatta as his *jagir*, a grant of land revenue. Under the empire representatives of the Delhi court came to Sindh, but the native rulers enjoyed much freedom.

## History: Mughal, Kalhora, Talpur and British Rule, to the Present

Under Akbar the country as a whole was made part of the *suba* of Multan, with two *sarkars*, Bhakkar for Upper Sindh and Thatta for Lower Sindh. From the time of Shah Jahan until the first decade of the eighteenth century the emperor's authority was wielded by officials sent from other parts of the empire; with the Kalhora, the emperor's representative was a native of Sindh. The Mughal century ran its course in Sindh through rebellion and disorder. In 1605 the Daudpota rose in Shikarpur and Upper Sindh, from 1648 to 1651 Aurangzeb was governor of Multan and Sindh, and in 1658 the civil war between Aurangzeb and Dara Shikoh ended in Dara Shikoh's defeat and his death after a pursuit through Sindh. In 1659 and the year after, Sindh was ravaged by famine and plague, and by 1699 the countryside was still infested by bands of robbers.

The eighteenth century loosened the empire's hold and brought the Kalhora to power, a Sindhi tribe of obscure origin. The struggle for power in the lower Indus valley first showed itself plainly in 1717, in the conflict around the Sufi Shah Inayat, and the Kalhora clan, particularly active in the struggle against him, soon afterwards became independent in Sindh. In religion, Makhdum Muhammad Hashim of Thatta and his relatives attacked the Sufis who defended the emotional side of religion, and their Naqshbandi group helped to promote a more sober mystical piety.

The century passed through clear stages: Kalhora governors from 1701; partial independence by 1736; Persian overlordship and tribute after the invasion of Nadir Shah in 1739, though accounts of the year differ; about 1747 the passing of overlordship to the Pathan kingdom at Kabul; and from 1778 civil war between the Kalhora and the Talpurs. By 1760 the ruling Kalhora dynasty was independent almost in fact but not at all in theory. When Ahmad Shah Abdali invaded Sindh, Nur Muhammad Kalhoro, the Subedar, or governor, fled and died, and commotion and civil war ended in the ascendancy of Ghulam Shah Kalhoro.

The Kalhora were overthrown by the former disciples of their saintly ancestor, Adam Shah Kalhoro, and the Talpurs, who came to power in 1781, ruled the country until the British conquered it in 1843. The Baluchis, by then the strongest military force in the land, helped the Talpurs destroy the failing power of the Kalhora. The Talpurs gave a distracted land peace for forty years and welded Sindh into a more complete unity than it had ever known, but only by asserting a Baluch supremacy that bore hard on the rest of the population, especially the Hindus. Most Muslims were Sunnis, but the Talpurs themselves were Shia, and under them the Amils, a class of scribes, almost monopolised the post of *kardar*, the local revenue officer.

Between them the Kalhora and the Talpurs made Sindh a petty Muslim state which kept the characteristic features of Mughal administration, adding a peculiar Islamic form of rule close to theocracy and a policy of splendid isolation. The two houses kept Sindh shut to the outside world for a hundred and fifty years. Beneath these changes ran a deep continuity: the Sindh that Sir Charles Napier conquered in 1843 was not very different in essentials from the Sindh of Akbar, Jahangir, Shah Jahan and Aurangzeb.

The British entered on Sindh as if it were almost an unknown land, and governed it from Bombay rather than Calcutta. When Persian was given up as the official language in the 1850s, Sindhi and English became the two languages of administration, and the policy of the British commissioners, Sir Bartle Frere above all, gave birth to the Sindhi intelligentsia. With the growth of new trading centres, Karachi above all, the Lohanas invested in agricultural land, mainly cotton, and by the end of the nineteenth century had become powerful landowners. Figures from the early 1870s count 1,718,688 Muslims and 393,092 Hindus in Sindh, and in 1941 the Hindus made up about 21 per cent of the population.

Sindhi nationalism grew from the struggle for independence from the Bombay Presidency in the 1920s and 1930s. From the 1920s onwards riots between Hindus and Muslims increased, a growing tension that culminated with Partition; it is a common claim that Sindh did not suffer the violence the Punjab knew, but there was a precedent in the Sukkur riots, and there was communal violence in Sindh. Independence in 1947 brought the mass emigration of the Hindu population: by early November 1947, 246,000 Hindus had already left Sindh, and the Hindu Sindhis left above all after the Karachi riots of January 1948. Estimates of their number differ: the general consensus is about a million, though it may have been closer to 0.8 million, if not fewer. The departure of the Hindu elite and the arrival of Muslim refugees from India changed the province's people, and the Sindhi community came to define itself by a sense of being shut out and subjugated by Muhajir power, the power of those refugees. Even so, Sindh is the province of Pakistan with the most significant Hindu population.

## The Land and Its Livelihood

Sindh is the creation of a river, the slow work of the Indus laying down land over the ages. Its rains are scanty and never regular, and its calamities have come instead from the river, in unchecked floods, drifting sand and channels that change their course. Away from the river and its large branches the country is arid, and it has little building stone and no metal at all. Famine ordinarily held no terrors for the Sindhi peasant, but public health in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries was not good.

Irrigation has always been the key to the province. The canals, Persian wheels and wells of Sindh were built by the people of the land without any help from the empire of Delhi, several of the biggest canals were dug by *begari*, forced labour, and irrigation under the Kalhora was probably better than under the Talpurs who came after them. In 1847 millet was commoner in the upper part of the country, wheat and barley in the lower, and rice in the low southern parts, and Sindh has always been noted for its fish.

In the seventeenth century Sindh was a place of trade, with the Indus as its inland waterway, its thriving cotton industry, its vast emporium at Thatta and the busy port of Laribunder. In 1636 the English set up a factory, a trading post, at Thatta; they gave up their factory in Sindh in 1662, opened one at Thatta again in 1758 and gave it up in 1775, and the East India Company was always very much the timid guest of the Sindh government. Inside Sindh all goods went by pack animals, mostly camels. Under the Mughals and the Kalhora the chief industries were cotton goods, saltpetre and indigo, leather and fish, and the popular modern idea of Sindh as a desert is as mistaken as the view that it was a purely agricultural country.

Then the trade declined. By 1770 Sindh suffered with the rest of cotton manufacturing India, and by the time of the British annexation it had for nearly a hundred years ceased to supply cottons to the European market and was importing English chintzes and calicoes. In 1828 and 1832 the population was put at between 850,000 and about a million, and after 1740 the chief concentration of people had shifted from Thatta to Shikarpur, Hyderabad and Karachi. The Sindhi village, with Hindu traders in the bazaar and the Muslim cultivators in their *landhis*, clusters of huts, round it, must have recalled the villages of early medieval Europe, as the small villages of rural Sindh still did in 1940.

Government rested on the land. At the time of the British occupation the system did not differ greatly from that of the Mughals: the state took its share in kind. Tax farming, the farmers being mostly Hindus, was one of the great curses of Mughal, Kalhora and Talpur rule alike, and a poll tax, the *sar shumari*, fell on Muslims who were not Baluchis. Land tenure in Sindh, so far as is known, has always been feudal, and in 1847 land held in *jagir* or *inam*, grants free of revenue, came to a tenth of the government land, the jagirs held mostly by Baluch chiefs.

## Saints and Pilgrimage

The Sufi history of Sindh opens with a traveller. The mystic Hallaj crossed the province shortly after 900 to call people to God and travelled widely there in 905; he is still a very popular figure in Sindh, and many Sufi poets name him in their verses. The Sufi traditions are held to have entered the subcontinent through Sindh, though accounts differ on how far Sufism spread in those early centuries. From the thirteenth century onwards there was a great immigration into Sindh of learned and religious men from the centre of the Muslim world, and the great ascendancy of the Sayyids dates from about that time; most of these Sayyids were Shia.

How Sufism progressed in Sindh cannot be traced in detail, but the landscape of shrines took shape from the thirteenth century, from the day Lal Shahbaz Qalandar settled in Sehwan, and from Baha'uddin Zakariya's Suhrawardi centre at Multan. The Sumra of Lower Sindh, who were Isma'ilis, are believed to have turned to Sunni Islam under the influence of the Sayyid Makhdum Jahaniyan. In the sixteenth century a group of mystics migrated from Sindh to Burhanpur in central India, where they founded a new branch of the Qadiri order. From that time on the native tradition of Sindh holds countless stories of saints, above all of those buried on Makli Hill near Thatta.

Three orders were dominant among the Hindu Sufis of Sindh: the Qadiriyya, the most influential; the Qalandariyya, which reflects the radiance of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar and the pilgrimage centre of Sehwan Sharif; and the Suhrawardiyya, predominant in the Indus valley from the thirteenth century onwards. Perhaps because of the Suhrawardi hold on Sindh, the Chishtiyya never spread there as it did in the rest of South Asia. The Naqshbandiyya has several important centres, above all Lowari Sharif near Badin. The *faqirs*, the wandering mystics, of Sindh belonged to the two great orders of Jelali and Jemali dervishes. Yet Sufi practice in Sindh rested more on personal allegiance to a master than on membership of an order, and a Sufi could belong to several orders at once.

Shah Abdul Latif came from one of the many lineages of hereditary saints in the Sindh countryside, where they have always enjoyed great prestige and power as pirs, though he was not formally linked through a chain of spiritual descent to any of the great orders. The saints of Sindh were so numerous that scarcely a village was without its tombs of holy men, and the belief that the saints sleep in their tombs is the solid foundation of the devotion paid to them. One traveller called Sindh the most priest ridden country on earth, said to possess no fewer than one hundred thousand tombs of saints. Markets and fairs on saints' days at saints' tombs were well established, and still today the trees near Sindhi shrines are hung with pieces of cloth, preferably red, tied there for the sake of a vow.

The cult of ʿAbd al-Qadir Gilani runs through the province: about 1850 there were some hundred large trees in Sindh, all named after Gilani, each with a pole and a flag hung on it to fulfil a vow. The river had its own saint, Khwajo Khizr, whom popular belief has made the spirit of the waters and the deity of the river Indus, and who is also called Zinda Pir, the living saint. His cult was supported by Muslim and Hindu alike, and Udero Lal is the figure most often merged with Khizr in Sindh. At Shah Abdul Latif's own tomb today dervishes perform his music on Thursday nights. Sindh is particularly rich in legends of women saints. The *faqirani*, the woman faqir, could occasionally rise to the high rank of *murshid*, master, and the *lotevali* gypsy women sang only at the shrine of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar, the one place in Sindh where they still came.

Hindu pilgrims and renunciants crossed Sindh too. Wandering yogis traversed the province on pilgrimage to the shrine of the goddess at Hinglaj in Baluchistan, and three of the *surs*, the chapters, of Shah Abdul Latif's *Risalo* are devoted to them; as a young man the poet is believed to have spent time with them. Most visitors to the festival of the Hinglaj goddess are Kohlis, Menghwar and Bhil, and the deified heroes most popular in Sindh were Rama Pir, Pithoro Pir, Pabuji and Guga Pir.

The Hindus of Sindh are tied to Sufism in three ways: most commonly a Sufi tomb visited by both Muslims and Hindus, such as Bhit Shah; a saint with a double identity, Muslim and Hindu, such as Udero Lal; and a Sufi *darbar* managed by Hindus, such as that of Pithoro Pir. The great majority of Hindus used to visit the Sufi shrines; this did not make them Sufis, but it does mean that for them the Sufis are part of their religious legacy. Some darbars of the Nanakpanth, the path of Guru Nanak, had been founded in the late eighteenth century, and in the second half of the nineteenth many new ones were built all over Sindh. A temple at Clifton has been identified as the one Nanak visited on his tour of Sindh, and shrines devoted to Jhulelal, known in colonial Sindh as Amar Lal or Udero Lal, are found across Southern Sindh.

Seen from India, Sindh remains the source of holiness. The darbars that Sindhi migrants founded in India are not centred on tombs, and the *mehndi*, the henna rite, of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar keeps a direct ritual link with the lost territory of Sindh. The *sajjada nashins*, hereditary heads of the shrines, of Daraza, Tando Jahaniya and Jhok Sharif are still in Sindh.

## Communities

Sindh is one of the western wings of India that became regions with a Muslim majority. There, from the fourteenth century through the Mughal period, the growth of Muslim societies went with the adoption of settled agriculture, and the shrines of great Sufis attracted and absorbed pastoral clans who had not yet taken to farming and were not Muslim. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries most of the people of Sindh were converted Jats; the majority were Sunni, very influential sections held Shia beliefs, and the Sayyids were nearly all Shia. The mass conversions blurred the line between Sunni and Shia and left the Islam of the common people much shaped by Hindu ways, with Hindus visiting Muslim shrines and making votive offerings.

From a tolerant Sufism flowed an absence of bitterness between Sunni and Shia and a kind of meeting between the deeper mystical ideas of Muslim and Hindu thought. Hindu writers composed mystical poems in Muslim imagery and even wrote *taʿziya*, laments, for the Muharram mourning of the Shia community of Sindh, and still in the 1920s numerous Hindus, among them some of the best minds of Sindh, were Sufi by religion. Yet eighteenth century Sindh also knew an almost incredible veneration for Sayyids as such and a great growth of superstitious practice among the people.

At the end of the seventeenth century society was seen as three classes: the privileged, that is the Amirs, chiefs, Baluchis, Sayyids and religious mendicants; the pastoral, farming and other Muslim tribes who made up the majority; and the Hindus, who were oppressed in the most open way. By 1843 the Baluchis stood at the top of Muslim society, and the Sayyids were mostly landowners but were also pirs, who mediated between the tribes and between the rulers and the ruled. The bulk of the Muslims were Sindhis, most of them landless farmers, *haris*; the trading Muslims were the Memon, the Khojah and the Bohrah; and excluded groups such as the Shikaris and the Sidis stood at the bottom.

The Baluchis have been in Sindh for many generations, settled long before the Kalhora called them in during the eighteenth century, the moment from which their political importance dates. By 1940 most of the Baluchi population lived in the Upper Sind Frontier, Larkana and Sukkur districts. As a class the Baluchis were not at all intolerant; intolerance in Sindh is traced rather to the Kalhora.

The Hindus kept the money and the trade of the province. Even in the 1640s big business was in the hands of Hindu traders, and under the Kalhora, though their position grew worse, they stayed in Sindh, lending money to the zamindars, financing trade, farming the revenue and owning all the petty shops in the villages. Every year Hindu traders made long voyages by sea to places like Aden, Gujarat and Lanka. The Lohanas, who made up half the whole Hindu population, were divided into Amils, who worked for the local rulers and became the official scribes of the Kalhora and the Talpurs, and Bhaibands, who went on trading. Many of them, the Amils above all, have taken a leading part in making Sufism a main part of Sindhi identity.

Two paths dominated among the Lohanas and related merchant groups. The Daryapanth, in all probability the older, rests on the deification of the Indus and is served by a priestly caste, the Thakurs. In nineteenth century Sindh the Nanakpanth was probably the dominant religious path among the Hindu Sindhis, but after the Singh Sabha reforms the Sindhi Nanakpanthis were not included in the Sikh community, and in the census Lohanas from Shikarpur and Hyderabad were counted as Sikhs in 1881 and as Hindus in 1891. Today many middle and upper class Hindus in Sindh follow the Nanakpanth.

Belonging in Sindh was long a matter of place rather than creed. As early as 1851 the worship of the intercessors was seen as the common religious language of Hindus and Muslims, and in rural Sindh Muslims worshipped Sitala, the smallpox goddess, when epidemics struck the village. Colonial rule changed this: with new factors such as the census, people had more and more to name themselves by one of the great religions, where before they had named themselves by locality, caste and ethnicity. Sufism entered the religion of the Hindu Sindhis in the nineteenth century, carried mainly by the Amils, and for a Hindu, to be a Sufi was to take full part in building a modern Sindhi province.

Some communities stood low. The fishing castes named in the *Risalo*, among them the Mangar, the Me and the Muhano, were traditionally ranked as the lowest of the low in Sindhi society. The Bhils and Kohlis are aboriginal, landless, roving peasants who work on the large landholdings of Sindh and Indian Gujarat, and because Shah Abdul Latif wrote in the Sindhi vernacular, Kohli and Bhil peasants can understand his poetry and relate to it. The Sidis came to the province from Africa, first brought as slaves; they are free now, their native language is Sindhi, and the Sidis of Sindh and Kutch claim descent from Hazrat Bilal.

Most Hindus of present day Sindh belong to the lowest castes. Sindh is home to the majority of Pakistan's Hindus, whose number estimates put at between 6 and 10 million, the figure varying widely, and over 90 per cent of them belong to underprivileged tribes or subcastes such as the Menghwar, the Bhil and the Kohli. Some call them Dalits, and among the Hindus of Sindh there is now a debate on whether the term fits; yet the Sufi legacy is still alive among the Dalits of Sindh. After the demolition of the Babri Mosque on 6 December 1992, Hindu communities in Sindh and Quetta were attacked.

The Nanakpanthi Sikhs are found in very large numbers in the province, and their temples in Sindh and Southern Punjab hold portraits of Guru Nanak beside Hindu deities. It is often said that the caste system is almost absent in Sindh, a picture tied to the assimilating role of the Lohanas and to nationalist portraits of Sindh as a place of religious harmony, while many upper caste Sindhi Hindus deny or ignore acts of segregation, which Dalit organisations fiercely oppose. The peasants of Sindh were in 1940, and always had been, fond of laughter, conversation and songs, and they kept Id, Holi and Diwali with whole hearts.

## Language, Poetry and Music

Poetry is the heart of religion in Sindh: "Sufi poetry is the backbone of Sufism in Sindh, including for the Hindu Sufis". It was the Sufis who made the spoken language of the province into a literary one. Mystical poetry in the mother tongue begins in Sindh, as in the Punjab, in the fifteenth century, and in Sindh too the eighteenth century was the most important period for the making of mystical poetry and prose. Shah Abdul Latif did not begin this tradition: before him there was already a settled culture of Sufi poetry in the spoken language, with poets such as Shah Abdul Karim, who died in 1623, and Shah Inat Rizvi, who died in 1711.

Apart from the Naqshbandis, Sufism in Sindh is dominated by poetry of *wahdat al-wujud*, the unity of being, the teaching that all existence is one in God. The Hindus of Sindh shared their passion for Sufi poetry with Muslim Sindhis, above all through Shah Abdul Latif's *Shah jo Risalo*, and devotional literature in Sindhi formed the basis of a culture that went beyond social, religious and ethnic divisions. It was carried to the villages not by the *ulema*, the Muslim scholars, nor by the *pandits*, the Hindu learned men, but by the caste musicians, the Manganhar or *mirasi*.

The learned languages of the province were not its spoken one. The five native histories of Sindh are all in Persian, the court language of the ruling houses up to the British conquest, and the learned men of Mughal and Kalhora Sindh wrote "in Persian and Arabic and not in the language of the countryside". Arabic's effect on the poetry of Sindh is negligible, and "Urdu has never been a language which has taken kindly to Sind soil". In Mughal days "The great mass of the people remained illiterate".

Northern Sindh had a second language of verse. Siraiki, the Sindhi dialect spoken mostly in Northern Sindh, especially among the Baluch, is also a language of Southern Punjab, and many Sindhi Sufi poets of the north, mainly those of Baluch origin, composed in several languages, Siraiki among them. Sachal Sarmast had many followers among the Hindu trading castes of Northern Sindh, and some of them took their love of singing his poetry to India when they migrated after Partition. For the Sindhis who left, singing Sindhi Sufi poetry keeps "a link, through language and music, with Sindh as a fatherland".

Above this tradition stands one book, the *Risalo* of Shah Abdul Latif. Accounts of the poet's dates differ; he is most often dated 1689 to 1752. By 1940 the *Risalo* was called "the only classic which the language of Sind has yet produced in the realm of deeply imaginative literature", and today it "is universally acknowledged to be the greatest classic of Sindhi literature" in Sindh, in other parts of Pakistan and among Sindhis in India and the wider diaspora. Its poetry "exercises over Hindus in Sind a spell almost as complete as that exercised by it over Muslims".

The stories of the poem have been read as Hindu in origin: apart from the tales of the Punjab and Kutch, they are, on this reading, tales of Lower Sindh told among the Hindu peoples on the edges of the Thar before their general conversion to Islam. Accounts of when the stories are set differ; the heroines' stories are loosely set in the time before the Mughals, when Sindh was ruled by the Rajput dynasties of the Sumra and the Samma. The mystical poets of Sindh and the Punjab used simple folk tales as media for mystical instruction: the first hints appear, veiled, in the verse of Qadi Qadan in the first half of the sixteenth century, grow clearer in the short verses of Shah Abdul Karim of Bulri, and come to full bloom in the eighteenth century in the *Risalo* of his great grandson Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit.

The story of Lila and Chanesar goes back to the time of Jam Chanesar, one of the Samma rulers of Sindh in the fourteenth century, and the tale of Mumal and Rano began in Lower Sindh some time in the fifteenth. Sohni is sung in Sufi poetry in every region of Sindh, and the story of Sassi, who belonged to the old city of Bhambore, and of Punnoo, a Baluch chief, is sung in the languages and dialects of Sindh, Baluchistan and the Siraiki speaking regions. Other *surs* are built on male heroes such as Jakhiro, a Rajput ruler of medieval Sindh famous for his bravery and generosity, who in a famous episode of these legends resisted the invasion of Sultan Alauddin, the Muslim emperor of Delhi.

The *Risalo* makes a world out of Sindh. In its twenty second *sur*, *Sarang*, the monsoon rains are a sign of divine power and grace, and the cloud of mercy rises over the desert of Eastern Sindh, the Thar Parkar. After naming the places the rains reach, the verse asks God to make Sindh flourish forever and closes: "Dear friend and sweet beloved, make the whole world prosperous" (translated by Christopher Shackle). This last blessing on Sindh is one of the most often quoted verses of the *Risalo*. In the poem the crane comes to Sindh as a winter migrant and its plaintive cry is heard as the pain of separation from the flock, and the crow is a bringer of good news, the typical messenger bird of the province.

The poem is also a picture of a time. Shah Abdul Latif "offers in his verse a vivid picture of the conditions of the Sind countryside during the early 18th century", and the spinning of cotton, one of the most important occupations of women, is a frequent theme. The great mystical poets of Sindh devoted wonderful songs to the Prophet, and *virahini* poetry, in which the devotee is a young bride waiting for her bridegroom, who is the Prophet Muhammad, is sung in Sindhi devotional settings such as the *mauluds*, gatherings that celebrate the Prophet's birth.

After 1843 Persian was quite quickly replaced as the language of schooling, government and elite literature by English and by Sindhi, and because Sindh was governed from Bombay rather than Calcutta, Urdu was not installed as Persian's immediate successor. Of Sindhi's many dialects, that of Hyderabad became the standard, it is said, when in 1853 the British asked the scholars of Hyderabad to write the textbooks for the whole of Sindh. The great event for Sufism was the printing of the *Shah jo Risalo* in 1866, and Hindu Sindhis led the publishing of Sufi books in the late nineteenth century. For most Sindhi intellectuals before Partition, Hindu or Muslim, the Theosophical Society, a modern religious movement, gave the frame in which the Sufi legacy of Sindh was read anew.

Shah Abdul Latif stands at the centre of the province's modern image of itself. G. M. Syed is held chiefly responsible for making him the most important poet of Sindh, and Sindhi nationalists see him as the ideal Sindhi, the perfect example of the province's tolerant and mixed history, while more orthodox writers try to explain away his journey as the recklessness of youth. A Sindhi writer, Abdul Qadir Mangi, put it plainly: "From a Kohli Bheel to a Syed, every one considers Bhitai his spiritual guide".

Music came early: "music appeared very early in the Sufism of Sindh", though most Naqshbandis in Sindh are strongly opposed to music and to the singing of devotional songs. "Most of the musicians in Sind have been Hindus and not Mussulmans", one reason, it has been thought, why the poetry holds so strong a spell over those of the land who are not Muslims. The songs of Shah Abdul Latif and the ardent poems of Sachal Sarmast, who died in 1826, were in the 1970s almost as popular as they had been a hundred and fifty years before. *Sufiana kalam*, Sufi poetry sung to music, is a feature of the shrines of Sindh, especially those of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar and of Shah Abdul Latif at Bhit, and musicians in Sindh treat the written *Risalo* with the utmost veneration.

"In Sind there can be no ritual without the Manganhar, be it a birth, a wedding, or a death." The Manganhar are hereditary professional musicians of Sindh and Rajasthan, and with the Sidis, of African descent, they sing the ceremonial devotional narratives. The Sidi, Mohana and Manganhar women attached to the landed households of Sindh sing devotional Sufi poetry at their mentor's shrine only on special occasions such as his *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of a saint's death, and there they perform only before women. The famous *Mast Qalandar dhun*, a tune said to be sung all along the lands from Rajasthan through Sindh and Baluchistan to Iran, ties the province to a wider world of song.

## Shrines and the State

For centuries religion and government were bound together in Sindh. Since the time of the Sumra in the thirteenth century the government had a strong theocratic basis, with much real power in the hands of the Sayyids, and saints and rulers were tied to each other from early on: Baha'uddin Zakariya supported Iltutmish's annexation, and a ruler of Sindh became the disciple of Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus. Under the Kalhora the extraordinary reverence for pirs, Sayyids and holy men may be said to have been the predominant characteristic of eighteenth century Sindh, and by one estimate the expense of religion took a third of the gross revenue.

The saints were both a treasure of the state and an instrument of it. Diwan Gidumal, the minister of the Kalhora princes, offered an invader two bags which, he said, held the holy dust from the tombs of numerous saints and pirs of Sindh; accounts differ on which invader it was, in one telling Nadir Shah, in 1739. In the nineteenth century one writer saw Sufism under the native governments as "as formidable a political engine as most of the secret confraternities recorded in history".

British policy, especially in the Punjab and Sindh, was to treat the hereditary saints in essentially the same way that landlords and tribal leaders were treated. Under indirect rule Sufis were usually content to keep the peace, and the exceptions were the two *hurr* rebellions against the British in Sindh, raised by the followers of the Pir Pagaro, a landlord, political leader and hereditary head of a Sufi brotherhood. The *hurr*, the Free, his absolutely obedient disciples, were first gathered to support Ahmad Brelwi's struggle against the Sikhs between 1829 and 1831, later took an active part in the struggle against the British, and even played a minor part in the war of 1971 between India and Pakistan.

The gap between the wealth of the shrines and their guardians and the poverty of the people is a powerful theme of social and religious criticism, in Sindh as elsewhere in the Muslim world. After Partition the darbars in Sindh were most of the time preserved with different arrangements regarding their management, often with followers in India paying the keepers. The state's Sindhi publications hardly mentioned the saint or the shrine of Sehwan until the late 1960s, while Shah Abdul Latif, Sachal Sarmast and Shah Inayat were regularly featured. Many temples across Sindh have been renovated within a decade, at the same time as changes in the political landscape. The Hindus of Pakistan, Sindh included, are, it seems, more and more under threat from the country's radical Muslims, and Islamist terrorism has spread in Sindh as elsewhere in Pakistan. It has been argued that Sufi dargahs are now its targets, and Sufism does not of itself mean tolerance; yet "the Sufi culture still pervades Sindhi society".

## The Province in Books

Under the Mughals, at the end of the seventeenth century, the province was the *suba* of Thatta, two hundred and fifty *kos*, a measure of distance, in length, from Bhakkar to Kech Makran, and one hundred *kos* in breadth, from the town of Badin to the port of Lahari. Four *sarkars* made up the *suba*, with fifty seven *mahals*, revenue divisions, and five ports, and a third share was taken from the cultivator. The province lived on its river, the Sindh: merchants from Multan and Bhakkar shipped their goods down it to Thatta, travellers and even heavy armies went by water, and there must have been forty thousand boats, large and small.

Much was said of the province's liver eaters, the *jigar khwara*, more famous here than anywhere around. By a spell, it was said, they took the livers of people, especially of children, and the victim died; they rode the hyena and brought news from far away. A liver eater, it was said, would not sink with a millstone tied to the neck, nor burn in fire. Most of them were women, some were men, and they were found in every land, but more in the lands of Thatta.

In 1940 a book on Shah Abdul Latif was dedicated "TO THE PEOPLE OF SIND". To its author, eighteenth century Sindh was a survival of an older world, a relic of the Muslim India of the Middle Ages, and in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries Sindh "was a land of cultivators and herdsmen, keepers of camels, buffaloes, sheep, goats and oxen, of tombs and Sayids, of dust and heat". The same author held that "many of the criticisms of Victorian writers are unjust". Burton despised Sindh for what he thought its lax Islam, while the assistant surgeon Heddle wrote in May 1836 that "The people are orderly and obedient and the laws are respected", and Lieutenant James, reporting in 1847, saw Sindh as one long battlefield.

The word itself is not always the province: in Baluch poetry Sindh means the valley of the Indus. Above all, Sindh has been seen as a mystical country: "Sind has always been praised as the country with a particularly strong inclination towards mysticism", and the country had always been proud of its mystical heritage. Other voices were harsher: Trumpp wrote of how much Islam had "become modified and corrupted in Sind, as in other parts of India", by its contact with Hinduism.

Later writers of the province saw it as a meeting of civilisations. Jethmal Parsram, in *Sind and its Sufis* of 1924, summed up the many religions that had flourished in Sindh, Buddhism, Jainism and Hinduism, to which Sikhism and Sufism had more recently been added. Ram Panjwani was already sure, while still in Sindh, "that Sufism was the core of Sindhi culture, a culture shared by all creeds and social milieus". As both an isolated land and a buffer zone, it has been argued, Sindh "has hosted many unorthodox versions of normative religions, especially Hinduism and Islam".

The same idea has a nationalist form. K. R. Malkani, a Sindhi Hindu who left Hyderabad for New Delhi after Partition, wrote: "Sindh has always been there, Pakistan is a passing show". G. M. Syed, a politician who spent many years in prison for his separatist views, found Sufism in its purest form in Sindh and took as proof the shrines of Sufi saints visited by Hindus and Muslims alike, and Sindhi nationalism ties Hinglaj to a real Sindh that prospered before outsiders began to suppress it. Yet the common belief among Sindhis today that the history of Sindh holds no religious tension between the two communities has been called "merely a nationalist utopian ideal", and a caution has been sounded against seeing Shah Abdul Latif too narrowly as an advocate of Sindhi nationalism.
