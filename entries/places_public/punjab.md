## Overview

The Punjab, the land of the five rivers, has never been one fixed unit. At the end of the seventeenth century it was the Mughal *suba*, or province, of Lahore, a country of five *doabs*, the tracts of land between two rivers, whose rivers join the Indus beyond Multan, with the *suba* of Multan beside it; in accounts of earlier reigns it is the *wilayat*, or territory, of Punjab. In 1875 it meant all the country from Delhi to Peshawar then attached to Punjab, where the tombs of famous saints lie, and in the nineteenth century it was the land of Punjab that the British conquered and governed. Today it is Pakistani Punjab, the most prosperous province of Pakistan.

By the 1880s the Punjab had been, by turns, a territory given in *jagir*, a grant of land revenue, the Mughal *suba* with its *diwan*, its chief revenue officer, the region that Ranjit Singh ruled and the country that the English annexed, and it was then the *suba* whose capital was Lahore. The Siraiki speaking south, with Multan as its capital, was a part of Sindh during the Middle Ages.

The province's history begins before Islam. One tradition holds that Raja Deb Chand of Delhi gave the territory of the Punjab in *jagir* to his brother Lohar Chand, who made the city of Lahore his capital. Mahmud of Ghazni established Ghaznavid rule over the Punjab, and the province became the last refuge of the house of Ghazni, whose government ended with Khusrau Malik's surrender to Sultan Shihab al-Din Ghori. Mongol armies came again and again, bringing plunder, famine and deserted villages, and Mongol pressure drove waves of Turkish settlers into the towns of the Punjab, Lahore, Dipalpur and Multan among them. In the fifteenth century the Punjab enjoyed a peaceful period under Afghan rule, until Babur invaded it.

Under the Mughals the Punjab was a prince's *jagir* and a *suba*, governed for a time by Dara Shikoh, who put up a great many buildings there. The eighteenth century was a time of ruin, the Punjab a battlefield of Afghans, Sikhs and Marathas, its misfortunes beginning in the reign of Muhammad Shah. Ranjit Singh then held the whole region of the Punjab, with Kashmir and the Derajat besides, and ruled it for forty years. After his successors the country passed under British rule, and by the 1880s Lahore was the capital of the *suba* of the Punjab, the seat of its Lieutenant Governor and its courts. The colonial state joined the five rivers with canals and settled farming families on the new land, and in the end the province was divided between India and Pakistan.

The Punjab is first of all a land of saints' tombs. Its Sufi settlement began with the Ghaznavid conquest, when Hujwiri reached Lahore in about 1035. Baba Farid's settling at Ajudhan was the religious side of a very slow change in the Punjab's cities from a Hindu to a Turkish-Islamic orientation, and the great shrines of Punjab and Sindh drew pastoral clans into their ritual, social, economic and political orbits.

The Chishti order is dominant in Punjab. By Guru Nanak's day the Qadiris had made the Punjab the main centre of their activities, the Naushahi *faqirs*, or dervishes, in Punjab numbered in the thousands, and Shaikh Sulayman Taunsawi, who died in 1850, consolidated the Chishti order through religious schools throughout the Punjab. The province is also the home ground of Guru Nanak's journeys, and Sikh tradition holds that by his return to Talwandi there were very few in the Punjab who did not know of him. Not every holy place belongs to a named saint: there are countless shrines to unknown saints on the outskirts of Punjabi villages. Today southern Punjab is believed to be the hub of the country's shrine culture.

The province's faiths met in more than one way. In one view, Islam and Hinduism existed side by side in the medieval Punjab for over three hundred years in a state of constant conflict, yet the conversion of the Punjabi clans to Islam was very slow and probably unconscious, and many of the clans claim to have been converted by Baba Farid. Hindus and Muslims served the same state, and under the Mughals a Hindu amir served as *diwan* of the *suba*. The village of the old Punjab was regarded as one social unit irrespective of caste or creed, and Hindus shared in the Muslim Sufi orders, though more visibly in Sindh than in the Punjab.

Today the Punjab has a Christian community with a formidable presence, a significant Hindu population still living in its south, and Sikhs, many of them Pathans who came from the tribal areas. After Partition several gurdwaras, the Sikh places of worship, and temples were taken over and destroyed by migrants, and among the country's elite the farming festivals of Vaisakhi, Lohri and Basant were given up.

Above all the Punjab is a land of mystical poetry in its own tongue, which may have begun as early as the days of Fariduddin Shakarganj and is traced at least to the fifteenth century. The *kafi*, the Punjabi Sufi lyric, easy to understand and memorise, carried the mystics' teaching to people who heard rather than read it. The golden age of this poetry was the late seventeenth and the eighteenth century, when the province was invaded one time after another, and it reached its height with Bulleh Shah, whose lament that Punjab has gone to the dogs is the best known of the verses in which the province itself is a figure.

The love of Hir and Ranjha is the province's own story. In the 1880s it was on the lips of everyone in the country of the Punjab, and the version of Waris Shah is sung wherever Punjabis meet. The Punjab is one of the two principal regions of *qawwali*, the Sufi devotional music, and in the south its speech shades into Siraiki, spoken in northern Sindh and southern Punjab. With the British conquest of the 1840s Persian was quite rapidly replaced by Urdu and English; Punjabi became a literary medium used almost exclusively by the Sikhs, and today it is looked down upon by the educated elite of Punjab.

Rulers bound themselves to the province's holy places. The Delhi sultans and the Mughal emperors granted huge tracts of rich land for the ceremonies of the great shrines of Punjab and Sindh, and Akbar anchored his imperial legitimacy in the Punjab through Chishti loyalty. Tombs of the shaikhs were destroyed at the hands of the Sikhs, while Ranjit Singh attached vast tracts of land to the gurdwaras, and under him the Moti Masjid in the Lahore fort was renamed the Moti Mandir and held the treasury of the state.

The British treated the hereditary saints of the Punjab and Sindh as they treated landlords and tribal leaders, and many important gurdwaras came to be run by Hindu *mahants*, priestly custodians. After Partition hundreds of temples and gurdwaras all over Pakistani Punjab were taken over by refugees and turned into homes. The colonial state did not try to control shrines directly; the state that followed it has gone further. Reformist movements, the Tableeghi Jamaat and the Deobandi school among them, have turned to the shrine culture of the south, and the Governor of Punjab, Salman Taseer, was killed on 4 January 2011.

## History: From the Earliest Times to Babur

The name Punjab has been given to more than one unit, and each period of its history has its own. At the end of the seventeenth century it was the Mughal *suba* of Lahore, beside the *suba* of Multan; in accounts of earlier reigns it was the *wilayat* of Punjab, or simply Punjab under a governor. In 1875 it meant all the country from Delhi to Peshawar that was then attached to it, and in the nineteenth century the land of Punjab that the British conquered and governed. For Lahore, the Punjab was the larger unit to which the city belonged, the country whose capital it became, lost and became again.

The southern Punjab has not always been reckoned part of the Punjab. The Siraiki speaking south, with Multan as its capital, was a part of Sindh during the Middle Ages, and a shared Sindhian culture, a set of social, religious and cultural traits, existed in the Sindhi area at the time when it took in the southern part of the present Punjab.

The province's history begins long before Islam. Like the Indus Valley, an agricultural society, present day Punjab, Pakistan, is a land where the fertility cult has a particular significance. Alexander's historians name Porus as one of two kings of the Chandar Bansi line, one of whom lived at Hastinapur and the other in Punjab. Tradition holds that after Yudhishthira gave up his throne at the coming of the Kaljug, the dark age of Hindu reckoning, the five Pandava brothers and Draupadi travelled through the lands of Multan and Punjab on their way to the mountain of Badrinath. One tradition holds that when Raja Deb Chand sat on the throne of Delhi he gave the territory of the Punjab in *jagir* to his brother Lohar Chand, and that once his hold over the Punjab was complete, Lohar Chand made the city of Lahore his capital, though accounts of the city's beginnings differ.

In the centuries before Guru Nanak the Punjab was the land through which every invader came, from the Ghaznavids, Ghurids and Tughluqs after the beginning of the eleventh century to Timur in 1398. As one retelling of the Guru's life puts it, "Since Punjab in the north was the gateway to India, it bore the brunt of all these invasions". In that retelling, once the conquerors had settled down in the Punjab, they turned to converting the non believers or destroying them. Sindh, by contrast, was left alone: the invaders who poured over the north western frontier passed it by and moved rapidly on to the more promising fields of the Punjab rivers and the Gangetic plain, until the rise of the kingdom of Afghanistan and of the Sikhs closed that frontier in the middle of the eighteenth century.

In the *wilayat* of Multan, Islam began in 99 AH, the year of the Islamic calendar, with Muhammad Qasim, in the time of Hajjaj bin Yusuf. Sultan Mahmud of Ghazni later took the *wilayat* from the Qarmatians, a sect counted heretics, and made Islam current there. The Muslim conquest of northern India extended the Persianate cultural world to the Punjab, where Persian remained the dominant literary language of the ruling elite through the Mughal period and beyond, and a strong Sufi presence was rapidly established with the arrival of charismatic figures of the main Sufi orders.

The pattern of conquest began in 986, when the Ghaznavid Sultan Sabuktigin, who reigned from 977 to 997, attacked and defeated the Hindu Shahi raja who controlled the region between Kabul and the north west Punjab. When Sultan Mahmud and Sabuktigin of Ghazni attacked the Punjab, Raja Jaipal, a Brahman and ruler of the Punjab, held throne and crown at Lahore. Jaipal is not counted among the rulers of Delhi, yet the lands of Samana and Punjab, and others apart from Delhi, appear to have been in his hands. The raids of that time succeeded because Hindustan had fallen into petty kingdoms and no one ruler held independent power.

Mahmud wished to drive Raja Jaipal out of Lahore and bring the territory of the Punjab under his own rule, and after the sack of the city Malik Ayaz became ruler of the Punjab by the king's order. Mahmud established Ghaznavid rule over Khurasan, Afghanistan and the Punjab, and Al-Beruni came to India in the first half of the eleventh century, after Mahmud attacked Punjab.

Under Mahmud's successors the Punjab passed between princes. When Masud came to the throne after Sultan Mahmud's death, his young son Majdud was given Punjab as his governorship, with Ayaz as his tutor. After Masud died and Maudud took the throne, Majdud rebelled and set up a kingdom of his own in Hindustan; Maudud marched against him, Majdud died suddenly on the day of Eid during the siege of Lahore, and Maudud established his hold over Punjab. From the middle of the eleventh century Mahmud's successors became more and more provincial, their kingdom centred on Ghazni in eastern Afghanistan with extensions into the Punjab, and their raids appear to have been less destructive and more sporadic than those of Sabuktigin and Mahmud.

Another tradition holds that when the Ghaznavid sultans ruled Punjab with Lahore as their capital, their rule held well for a long time, until in Bahram Shah's time their quarrels among themselves left the government of Punjab altogether weak; Raja Anangpal then took Lahore, and there was Hindu rule there for a time until an army came again from Ghazni. The Punjab became the dynasty's refuge. After two defeats by Ala al-Din of Ghur, Bahram Shah was forced to flee to the Punjab, and the destruction of Ghazni by Ala al-Din Husain of Ghur in 1150 to 1151, followed six years later by the invasion of the Ghuzz, drove a large number of people from that region to the Punjab for safety.

The province was the last refuge of the house of Ghazni. When the Ghurids drove the dynasty out of Ghazni, Khusrau Shah set up his government in the Punjab, taking Lahore and governing the *wilayat* of Punjab until his death in 555 AH. His son Khusrau Malik succeeded him on the throne of Lahore and ruled over the Punjab. Accounts of how and when his rule ended differ; the Ghaznavid government came to its end with his surrender to Sultan Shihab al-Din Ghori, when the Ghurid sultanate in Hind began.

The Ghurids followed. In 1175 Mu'izzu'd-Din Muhammad bin Sam seized Multan, establishing firm control and Sunni rule over the southern part of the Punjab; when Shihab al-Din Ghori conquered Hindustan, Multan too came into his possession. After Shihab al-Din's death Taj al-Din Yalduz seized Ghazni and then came to the Punjab to take the throne of Hind from Qutb al-Din. In a second rising, in the reign of Sultan Shams al-Din Iltutmish, he took Multan, came to Lahore, plundered the Punjab without restraint, and crossed the Sutlej to advance on Delhi.

The armies of Chingiz Khan's line came next. Sultan Jalal al-Din Khwarazmi crossed the river of Sindh and entered the Punjab, and when Chingiz Khan's grandson Quli Khan invaded the Punjab, the army of Delhi and the Mongols fought at Lahore. A little known tradition holds that Chingiz Khan's army entered Punjab in pursuit of the prince and plundered the whole land, and that the people of Lahore fought them for two months. After Jalal al-Din's defeat in November 1221 and Chingiz Khan's march back to the west, the Mongol's absence let Iltutmish consolidate his power in the Punjab, and seven years later Iltutmish routed his rival Nasiru'd-Din Qubacha from Sindh.

The Mongols shaped the province for two centuries. From the late tenth century, and most of all in the thirteenth, Mongol pressure forced waves of Turkish settlers into the subcontinent, many of them settling for good in the towns of the Punjab, such as Lahore, Dipalpur and Multan. In the middle of the thirteenth century Balban, to build a defence against Mongol raids, strengthened and peopled certain cities of the Punjab, Lahore, Multan, Sirhind, Bhatinda and Dipalpur, with large garrisons of Turks, which brought to the province's towns not only soldiers but thousands of refugees fleeing before the Mongols. Urged to conquer Gujarat and Malwa, Balban answered that since the Mongols were always raiding the *wilayat* of Punjab it was not right to go from Delhi to distant lands, and that to keep one's own kingdom strong and at peace was better than to raid the kingdoms of others. His nephew Sher Khan, posted to guard the Indus, saved Punjab from the Mongols' attacks and drove them out of Ghazni.

In Jalal al-Din Khalji's reign the Chingizid Mongols set out for Punjab with a great army. The sultan marched against them and they made peace; their leader, a kinsman of Hulagu Khan, came in and became a Muslim with some other amirs, and the sultan called him son, gave him a daughter and settled them at Ghiyaspur, which came to be called Mughalpura.

Of the blows that fell on the province one after another, the fourth came in the time of Sultan Jalal al-Din Firuz Shah Khalji, when a Mongol army sent by a Chaghatai Khan, ruler of Transoxiana, came into the Punjab. There was famine in the Punjab at the time, plunder and pillage ruined the whole region, and villages and towns were left deserted, while the sultan was away on the Gujarat campaign and could do nothing to help. After his victory on the Sutlej the sultan resettled the villages of the Punjab that had been left empty and without a lamp, shared the Tatars' loot among the poor of the Punjab, and posted a large army on the frontier. At the end of the Khaljis, Ghazi Beg Tughluq, governor of Multan, gathered a great army in Punjab against Khusrau Malik.

The fifth blow fell in the time of Sultan Muhammad Tughluq. A Tatar army came from the west into the Punjab and both Dipalpur and Lahore were plundered; the rulers of the Punjab did all they could but fled to Delhi. When the army had gone the king came to the Punjab, worked hard to resettle its people, and beheaded the governor of Multan, at whose signal the Tatar army had come. Before his scheme of 1327, the same sultan raised the cry of *jihad*, holy war, against the Mongols, who in previous years had devastated the Punjab and the country around Delhi. The sixth blow came in the reign of Firuz Shah Tughluq, when the Tatar Mongol army arrived in the Punjab to plunder; the king, then away on a siege, came back and after a bloody battle drove it out.

The seventh came in the reign of Sultan Muhammad Shah, son of Firuz Shah, and with Amir Timur's invasion. A raider came with a great host into the Punjab, drove the royal officers out of the country and laid waste thousands of settlements, and Muhammad Shah sent Prince Humayun with an army to the Punjab. Amir Timur passed through the foothills of the Punjab and entered Delhi, and on his return the people of the Punjab laid before him their complaint of the raider's killing and plunder. Timur's invasion of 1398 to 1399 devastated both the Punjab and Delhi. The eighth blow came in the reign of Sultan Mubarak Shah, son of Khizr Khan, when Jasrath, the raider's brother, raised great disorder in the Punjab, and the king ordered Sultan Shah Lodi, governor of Sirhind, to take his army and drive Jasrath out.

The fifteenth century turns on the governorship of Tatar Khan. Before his time, it is said, a flood surged through Punjab so that all the land from the Sutlej to the Chenab lay under water and towns and villages were destroyed, and the Mughals of Balkh and Kabul raided Punjab every year, so that the country lay waste and its produce was small. Under the Lodis Tatar Khan was governor of Punjab, appointed by Sultan Bahlul Lodi. For fear of him the son of the raja of Jammu left Jammu for Kashmir, and Sultan Muhammad Shah and then Sultan Fath Shah of Kashmir each sought his help. When Tatar Khan became governor of Lahore, Rai Ram Deo Bhatti took the whole of Punjab from him in farm, collecting its revenue for a fixed sum.

Recovery came under the Afghans. In the fifteenth century the Punjab enjoyed a peaceful period: the Mongol invasions of the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries had devastated parts of it, and the Afghans helped to resettle it and build its towns, founding many new ones and promoting trade and commerce. Yet it is said that in the days of Guru Nanak's parents the jungles of Punjab were infested with thugs and dacoits, bandits who attacked innocent travellers.

Meanwhile Multan went its own way. For long the *wilayat* remained in the hands of the sultans of Delhi, until in the reign of Sultan Muhammad Shah, of Khizr Khan's line, Hindustan fell into petty kingdoms and the governor of Multan declared his independence. It is said that in the reign of Sultan Ala al-Din the Second of Delhi the *wilayat* of Multan passed out of his control to the Langah people. Sultan Husain Langah took the fort of Shor from Ghazi Khan, and Chiniot, and in a short time extended his hold further. Tatar Khan, governor of Punjab, was sent against him by Sultan Bahlul Lodi together with Bahlul's son, and after Bahlul, Sultan Sikandar of Delhi made peace with Sultan Husain on the terms that each side would rest content with its own *wilayat* and not raid the other's border.

Babur ended that peace. Guru Nanak came back from eastern India about 1520 to find the Punjab plunged into a number of significant political crises. Babur opened negotiations with Sultan Ibrahim to annex west Punjab to Kabul, without success, and after two minor raids on the region between the Indus and the Jhelum he invaded the Punjab a third time in 926 AH, 1520, reaching as far as Sialkot. Daulat Khan, who had in time become governor of the Punjab, then invited Babur to invade India. Guru Nanak's *Babur Bani* is a long poem written at the time Babur attacked Punjab, destroying the cities of Eminabad and Lahore on the way. Before the battle with Rana Sanga, Babur's ministers advised him to strengthen a few forts and withdraw to the *wilayat* of Punjab; he refused.

Multan, too, came into the new dynasty's hands. Babur, after reaching Punjab, granted Multan to Arghun, charging him to strive for the prosperity of the country and the well being of its subjects, and in Babur's reign the *wilayat* of Multan, long lost to the sultans of Delhi, was conquered by Kamran Mirza's good management and sword.

## History: Under the Mughals

Under the Mughal emperors the Punjab was a prince's *jagir* and a *suba*. Humayun attached Langar Khan to his brother Prince Kamran, in whose *jagir* the territory of the Punjab lay; Kamran's *jagir* was Lahore and Multan. The struggles between the Mughals and the Surs passed through Punjab. Sher Khan by deceit gave Kamran hope that the *wilayat* of Lahore would be confirmed to him, so that Kamran withdrew from aiding Humayun. Later Kamran went to Salim Khan, son of Sher Khan, who had taken the throne of Hindustan after his father's death, and Salim Khan sent his son and Maulana Abdullah Sultanpuri to meet him.

In Islam Shah's reign the Niazis revolted. Azam Humayun defeated Khwaja Wais and pursued him, then laid hands on the towns and countryside of Punjab, plundering the cattle and goods of its people, so that a great disturbance arose in Punjab and a strange state befell those who lived there. Islam Shah marched from Delhi with a heavy army and much artillery, and the Niazis held out in Dhankot until they were defeated.

On Humayun's return to Hindustan, Shah Abul Maali was posted towards Lahore to manage the affairs of the *wilayat* of Punjab. Sultan Sikandar came out of the hills, seized the *parganas* of Punjab, the revenue districts, and began to collect their revenue; Shah Abul Maali, who treated his own soldiers badly, failed to drive him back, and Akbar and Bairam Khan were sent. Akbar was on his way to Punjab against Sikandar when the news of Humayun's death reached him at Kalanaur, and he came to the throne in 963 AH.

The early years of the new reign were spent securing the province. When Sultan Sikandar began to collect revenue in the *wilayat* of Punjab and disorder broke out, the emperor marched from Delhi towards Punjab. In the second year of the reign Mankot fell, Sikandar was driven out, and security returned to the *wilayat* of Punjab. Mir Muhammad Khan Kalan, brother of Atga Khan, and the other amirs posted in Punjab were sent against Sultan Adam the Gakkhar. At Bairam Khan's fall, in the sixth year of the reign, he took the road to Punjab and, after Bikaner, turned towards Punjab and reached it. After Bairam Khan's fall Muzaffar Khan became revenue officer of the *pargana* of Parsarur, a dependency of Punjab.

On his way to Punjab to put down a revolt, the emperor Akbar halted at Thanesar on Saturday 2 Muharram 980 AH. There was a governor of Punjab in these years. When Ibrahim Husain Mirza and Masud Husain Mirza turned towards Punjab, Khan Jahan, governor of Punjab, who was close to finishing his campaign at Nagarkot, made peace with the raja there and marched against them. When the imperial standards moved towards Punjab, the Mirzas came out of Sambhal, plundered and besieged the fort of Delhi, and the emperor went from Punjab towards Delhi.

Yusuf Shah of Kashmir fled by way of Jammu to Mirza Yusuf Khan, governor of Punjab, and with him and Raja Man Singh reached Fatehpur. Muhammad Hakim Mirza crossed the Indus many times to trouble and harm the people of Punjab. Emboldened by the revolt of the amirs of Bengal, he came from Kabul with an army to Punjab and stirred up disorder there, but on the imperial advance he could not stay and returned to Kabul. The emperor set out towards Punjab to put an end to his disturbance.

During the emperor's residence at Lahore, Zain Khan Koka, sent to punish the *zamindars*, the landholding chiefs, of the hills, brought all their rajas and rais to obedience by the force of the sword and the strength of good counsel, and brought several of the rajas to court. Raja Basu, a *zamindar* of the hills of Punjab, later rebelled. In Akbar's reign Raja Todar Mal, the chief *diwan*, confirmed the *wilayat* of Multan to the Baluch, making it a strong vanguard between Khurasan and Hindustan and a firm barrier on the borders of both countries. In Akbar's empire the Punjab was counted as two provinces, the *subas* of Lahore and Multan. In the Mughal age interest in Rumi seems to have been common to all classes, for the emperors, Akbar most of all, were as fond of him as the simple villagers of Sindh and Punjab.

Eight months after Jahangir's accession, Prince Khusrau rebelled. Dilawar Khan, governor of Lahore, held the fort against him, and Raja Basu, one of the chief *zamindars* of the hills of Punjab, who had just joined the emperor, was sent after the prince, who was captured at the end of Muharram. The reign of Jahangir was a time when the Mughals enjoyed effective authority in the Punjab. Some claim that Guru Hargobind took service with Jahangir, serving him in the Punjab.

In Shah Jahan's reign Prince Dara Shikoh, *jagirdar* of the Punjab, laid out the Chauk Dara Shikoh in Lahore, and when the government of the Punjab was entrusted to him he put up a great many buildings. The *suba* had its *diwans*: while Nawab Wazir Khan held the governorship, the Andhi Haveli was built by a Hindu amir who was *diwan* of the *suba* of the Punjab, and his descendants held it until the end of Mughal rule. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries the Punjab, with Sindh, was the home of the leather industry, and against the general rule that Mughal public money went to tombs and palaces rather than to public works, the canal enterprise of the seventeenth century in the Punjab was an exception.

The war of succession was fought partly across the province. Dara Shikoh had agreed with Shuja that while he himself prepared a revolt in Punjab, Shuja would march from Bengal as far as Allahabad, and that after success they would divide the kingdom and its wealth equally. Dara told Sulaiman Shikoh to reach Punjab, and the Barha sayyids advised going to Punjab by way of Saharanpur. At Lahore Dara sent letters of conciliation, full of kindness and promises of favour, through all the surrounding parts of the *suba*, urging the soldiers of every place, people and tribe to enlist with him.

The emperor's march towards the *wilayat* of Punjab came in the rains. Crossing the Sutlej and the Beas without boats or fords was judged all but impossible, and most of the nobles did not advise moving in that season, yet he set out. While the imperial standards were in Punjab in pursuit of Dara, Shuja took his chance and set out for Allahabad. The halt at Lahore was made to put the affairs of the *suba* of Punjab in order and to give the army rest. There Khalilullah Khan was appointed governor of Lahore, Lashkar Khan was moved from the *suba* of Kashmir to be governor of Multan, and the *faujdari*, the military command, of the hills of Punjab changed hands.

The Sikh community too was growing. By the time the Guruship passed to Harkrishan, the Sikh community had spread beyond the confines of the Punjab to places as far as Kabul, which made central control by the Guru increasingly difficult.

## History: Decline, Sikh Rule, the British and Partition

The eighteenth century was a time of ruin, and the Punjab was its battlefield. Bulleh Shah lived in a time of great upheaval, when the Mughal Empire was breaking up and the Punjab was persistently torn by petty chiefs and marauders. After Aurangzeb the calm days the Punjab had enjoyed quickly came to an end, and the badly defended north western region of the empire became more and more subject to foreign invasions and inner quarrels. The beginning of the misfortunes of the Punjab dates to the reign of Muhammad Shah, when the decay of Mughal administration gave rise to small local tyrannies which lasted into the nineteenth century.

Conditions in Punjab worsened from the fall of the Mughal Empire onward. The Punjab has often been the battleground of invading forces from the north, from the famous campaigns of Mahmud of Ghazni to those of Ahmad Shah Abdali, also called Durrani, the Afghan king whose capital was Kandahar and who in 1748 began a series of invasions of India through the Punjab. Waris Shah's lament comes in the epilogue of *Hir and Ranjha*, which he dated 1180 Hijri: "Of all the wretched Punjab I am most concerned for Kasur". The second half of the eighteenth century, to which the poet was referring, marked the decline of the Mughal Empire in India and was a period of great confusion and disturbance.

The *suba* still had its Mughal rulers in these decades. Nawab Zakariya Khan Bahadur, Shahnawaz Khan and other rulers of the Punjab lived in Mughalpura. The last Mughal decades are told through the province's defence against Ahmad Shah Abdali. In Muhammad Shah's time Jani Khan, with his brother Mir Muin al-Mulk, defeated Ahmad Shah Abdali, who went back to Kabul; after he had gone Mir Muin al-Mulk ran the administration of the Punjab in his own way, and Jani Khan remained commander of the army of the Punjab. Mir Muin al-Mulk, popularly known as Meer Manu, was governor of Punjab and was constantly harried by the Sikh fighting bands, the *misls*, which had sprung up all over the province; Abdali had agreed a yearly tax that the governor was to pay him for the right of governing Punjab.

Nadir Shah first conquered Sindh and the Punjab before he reached Delhi in 1739. After his invasion, marauding Sikh bands formed themselves into robber baronies and imposed their own system of extortion on the Punjab farmer, and Ahmad Shah Abdali's treatment of the Punjab weakened the government in Lahore and delivered the various *doabs* into the hands of the Sikh confederacy of the *misls*. As the Mughal empire broke into pieces around 1750 the Sikhs threatened the Punjab, and Ahmad Shah Abdali, Nadir Shah's successor, invaded it several times from his native Afghanistan. After Nadir Shah's assassination Ahmad Shah took Afghanistan and from that base seized the whole of the Punjab, beginning in 1748. His influence was greater in the northern areas of present day Pakistan than in Sindh. Bulleh Shah lived to see invasions of the Punjab by Ahmad Shah Abdali in 1748 and again in 1752, and Abdali died in 1187 AH, 1773.

The same years are remembered through the lives of saints and scholars. In the reign of Alamgir the Second the Afghans attacked Punjab and took Multan and Lahore, while the strength of the Sikhs began to grow, and Ghazi al-Din resolved to conquer Punjab again. When Ahmad Shah Abdali first came to take Lahore, and after plundering Punjab had gone back to Kabul, a man of Lahore told the saint Haji Muhammad Said Lahori that the Afghans had looted his house and carried his daughter off to Kabul. Ghulam Murtaza Qasuri, wearied of the Sikh disorders in Punjab, migrated to Peshawar, and when Ahmad Shah Abdali came into Punjab he put a question of religious law to its scholars, Ghulam Murtaza among those who answered. When the plundering Sikhs spread their pillage in Punjab, Shaikh Karam Shah Qureshi, who had lived in Lahore, went away to Lucknow, and on his way back he was killed by bandits near Shahjahanpur.

Bulleh Shah, who died in 1758, lived at a time of great social upheaval in the Punjab, as the central authority of the declining Mughal empire came under growing challenge from armed rebellion by the Sikhs and from local notables who set up their own power. Those unsettled conditions are sometimes reflected in his poetry. When plunder became routine in the Punjab, Bulleh Shah had no choice of factions; the Sikh marauders under Banda Bairagi were to lay waste the whole province except Lahore and Kasur, and the common peasant was taxed to raise armies on both sides.

His verse carries the age. One line is taken as an allusion to the Sikhs, whose horsemen in the end destroyed Mughal authority in the Punjab, and his verse also touches on the Ranghars, a tribe of Muslim Rajputs who rose to prominence in eighteenth century Punjab and were proverbial for their arrogance. One of his *kafis* seems to speak of a separation from Shah Inayat caused by the troubled conditions of the Punjab, and another speaks of the disturbed state of Punjabi society in the twelfth century of the Hijri era, the eighteenth century of the common era. Of that new century the poet says:

*"The door of doom and torment has opened, and Panjab is in a bad state. It has been struck by fear of the pit of hell." (translated by Christopher Shackle)*

This critique of the upheavals of his time, which so interests modern readers, is only a very small, if vividly expressed, part of his poetry. As Bulleh Shah lay dying in 1758 at the age of seventy eight, the plunder of the Punjab went on apace.

The same years can also be seen from Sindh. When the East India Company entered Sindh again in 1758, Afghans, Sikhs and Marathas were at each other's throats in the plains of the Punjab, and in 1760 the Afghan and Maratha armies were threatening each other there. In these years the Sikhs rose in the Punjab, and in 1758 the Marathas occupied it.

The end of Muslim rule came with famine and plunder. When the rule of Islam passed from the Punjab and the Sikhs gained strength, the first place the Sikhs plundered was the butchers' quarter of Lahore. When the Sikh period came, a severe famine struck the Punjab, after which the descendants of the Andhi Haveli's builder left Lahore. When the turn of the age overturned the Islamic state and the Sikhs took Lahore, the whole region of the Punjab was laid waste by famine, after which Mir Jawad's descendants left Lahore and the officials took over his *haveli*, his mansion.

In 1797, when Shah Zaman Durrani, the grandson of Ahmad Shah Abdali, followed his grandfather's footsteps and attacked Punjab, a Sikh band met him on the banks of the Chenab. As the Maharaja, ruler of the region of the Punjab, Ranjit Singh brought the whole region into his hands, and ruled the Punjab for forty years; besides the Punjab the country of Kashmir, the Derajat and other lands were also his.

Maharaja Ranjit Singh, who lived from 1780 to 1839, was crowned Ruler of Punjab in 1801. In a land ruled for over a thousand years by Afghans, Mughals and other rulers who were not Punjabi, he is called the first Punjabi to rule Punjab, and he brought it a stability that lasted a good forty years. A later generation remembered Shah Ismaʿil Shahid and Sayyid Ahmad of Bareilly for their brave fight against the Sikhs, who had occupied the whole of the Punjab and part of the north western frontier.

The infantry of Maharaja Ranjit Singh drew its pay through Bakhshi Bhagat Ram. Ranjit Singh struck coin in the name of Moran, the courtesan, and her coin ran through the whole of the Punjab; in the 1880s Moran Shahi rupees and paisa were still kept as keepsakes.

Ranjit Singh's Punjab is remembered through its saints too. It was well known throughout Punjab, of the saint Subah Shah, that the outward king was Maharaja Ranjit Singh. The Maharaja had complete faith in the *majzub* Mastan Shah, a holy man absorbed in divine love, and offered him thousands of rupees, which Mastan Shah refused. All the people of Lahore, and indeed of Punjab, knew the quarter of the Faqir family.

On the day Ranjit Singh died, it is said, the *majzub* Taj Shah foretold the ruin of the kingdom of the Sikhs: it would last nine years more, and then the Firangis, the Europeans, would be masters of Punjab. After Ranjit Singh's death his son, Maharaja Kharak Singh, sat on the throne of the Punjab. After the deaths of Maharaja Kharak Singh and Kanwar Nau Nihal Singh, Rani Chand Kaur became mistress of the kingdom of the Punjab, seated on the *gaddi*, the throne, on the proposal of the Sandhanwalia sardars, who became the managers of the state, while Raja Dhian Singh, the wazir, went off displeased to Jammu. Dhian Singh told Sher Singh that, God willing, he would soon be ruler of Punjab, and Maharaja Sher Singh ruled the region of the Punjab for two and a half years.

The Sikh state ended under Maharaja Dalip Singh. A disturbance broke out in Punjab once more and ran through Multan, the country of Hazara and four hard battles, and then the whole country entered the British dominion. The English became masters of the Punjab after the end of Sikh rule. Bakhshi Bhagat Ram's *jagir* had not yet been released when the government annexed the country, after which the Chief Commissioner fixed his pension. When Maharaja Dalip Singh was exiled and English rule over the Punjab was complete, the troops left the Badshahi Mosque. Dalip Singh was the last Maharaja of Punjab, and the province became a British colony, which led in the end to its division at Partition.

The British came late to the Punjab. They arrived in the middle of the nineteenth century, a century also known there as the Sikh period, and the Punjab was colonised almost a century after Bengal, which is why English is less rooted here. With the British conquest of the 1840s the literary culture of the Sufi tradition came to be overlaid by new patterns of modernity, as Persian was quite rapidly replaced by Urdu and English as the language of education, administration and elite literature. Rajab Ali Khan was Mir Munshi, the chief secretary, at the time of the British conquest, with the title Mir Munshi of the dominions of Punjab, and he presented himself to the Lieutenant Governor of Punjab. With British rule a legal settlement was established in Punjab.

By the 1880s Lahore was the capital of the *suba* of the Punjab. The court of the Lieutenant Governor and his secretaries sat there, and its authority ran over the whole *suba*: chiefs, rajas and maharajas, commissioners and deputy commissioners were all subordinate to it. The second court was that of the Financial Commissioner and Collector of the Punjab, and the Chief Court gave the final decision in civil and criminal cases, heard the appeals from the commissioners of the *suba*, and had to sanction every sentence of death.

Egerton was Lieutenant Governor of the territories of the Punjab. Robert Montgomery, long a ruler of the Punjab, rose from commissioner to Lieutenant Governor. Colonel Lawrence was Lieutenant Governor and chief ruler of the Punjab at the time of the Delhi rising, and by his great labour the administration of the Punjab held together while he kept raising a large army and sending it to Delhi, for which he was made Governor General and Viceroy of India. For the two halls named after them the amirs and officials of the Punjab subscribed most willingly. After the uprising of 1857 Imdadullah fled through the Punjab and Sindh, visiting Sufi shrines and in the end sailing from Karachi into exile in Mecca.

The province's institutions gathered in its capital. The Central Jail at Lahore was built as a general jail for the prisoners of the whole region of the Punjab: every district had its own jail for short sentences, but prisoners with long terms were sent here from every district. Women prisoners from all the districts of the Punjab came to the women's prison, built under the supervision of the historian of Lahore, Kanhaiya Lal. The Lahore museum building was put up in 1864, when the government first held an exhibition of the wonders of the region of the Punjab, and the great states of the Punjab and its sardars and *jagirdars* sent jewels, textiles, arms and manufactures to it. An exhibition of the industrial arts of the Punjab was also held in the building of the School of Art.

The colonial state remade the land. In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries the British connected the five rivers of Punjab with each other through canals, turning jungle into farmland. Thousands of new villages, called *chaks*, were founded, and farming families from all over Punjab were invited to settle. The former inhabitants, mostly nomads, could not prove their ownership of the land, and it was taken from them and given to the newcomers. *Jungli*, which literally means people of the jungle, is a popular name for the indigenous Punjabis, as opposed to those who settled after the canal colonies were built. Under the peace of colonial rule the exchange of goods grew between the regions of the north western subcontinent, Punjab, Rajasthan, Gujarat and Sindh among them, and beyond the merchants there were networks of circulating knowledge, especially religious knowledge.

In the twentieth century the shaping of nationalist ideas in the Punjab, among the new middle class of the colonial period, was no simple process. Three closely related languages, Urdu, Hindi and Punjabi, were current at once, and their differences were used to sharpen the rival cultural identities of Muslims, Hindus and Sikhs. Then the Punjab was divided between India and Pakistan, and a second wave of migration followed. In the south the violence of Partition never reached the heights it did elsewhere, so the resentments between communities after 1947 were not that serious.

## Saints and Pilgrimage

The Punjab is first of all a land of saints' tombs. Saints came into it, their orders spread across it, and people came to their shrines. Of the Naqshbandi line, none of the elders before Khwaja Baqi Billah of Delhi has a tomb in the land of Punjab.

The Sufi settlement of the province began with the Ghaznavid conquest. When Mahmud of Ghazni annexed the Punjab and made it part of his empire, many Sufis were prompted to settle there. Khattali ordered his disciple Shaikh Husain Zinjani to move to the Punjab, and later sent Hujwiri after him; Hujwiri reached Lahore in about 1035, while Sultan Masud I reigned in Ghazni. Tradition also holds that in the time of Sultan Masud of Ghazni, Shaikh Muhammad Ismail Muhaddith Lahori, a Bukhari *sayyid*, a descendant of the Prophet, settled in Lahore and taught *hadith*, the sayings of the Prophet, and *tafsir*, the commentary on the Quran. He is said to have been the first of the callers to Islam to come to Lahore, and thousands are said to have become Muslims at his hands, so that through him Islam spread in Punjab.

Women too have their place in the tradition of how Islam came to the province. It is said that the Bibi Pak Daman, whose shrine is in Lahore, involved the men in spreading Islam among the people of the Punjab who were not Muslim, the women speaking to the male scholars and their audiences from behind the veil, and that seven hundred male scholars came with her to Hind, as the land was then known. In the year 615 AH the Mongols invaded the Punjab in pursuit of Sultan Jalal al-Din Khwarazmi, and the people of Lahore were put to death for resisting them. That invasion is the setting of the tradition that the Bibis of Bibi Pak Daman prayed to be taken into the earth.

The family of Baba Farid came from Kabul and migrated to the Punjab in the middle of the twelfth century, and it was the Ghaznavid Sultan of the Punjab who made Farid's father the Qazi, or judge, of Kahtwal. Baba Farid's settling at Ajudhan was the religious side of a very slow change in the Punjab's cities, from a Hindu to a Turkish and Islamic orientation. In the time of Nizamuddin, Sufism became a mass movement in north western India, and a number of Punjabi tribes still claim to have been converted by Fariduddin Shakarganj. In his invocation the poet Waris Shah sang: "When Shakarganj made his abode at Pakpattan, the Punjab was delivered of all its troubles." It is a poet's praise rather than history, for the Punjab had plenty of troubles even after Baba Farid made it his home and his place of burial.

From the fourteenth century through the Mughal period, the shrines of the great Sufis of Punjab and Sindh drew pastoral clans, who had not yet taken up farming and were not Muslim, into their ritual, social, economic and political orbit. The descendants of the Sufis married into the leading families of the clans, as the clans passed from pastoral nomadism to settled wheat farming. The writing of amulets for the people, *ta'widh*, has not changed from the thirteenth century to the present: the successors of Baba Farid and their assistants, like those of other shrines in the Punjab, still write them for the masses.

The shrine stood between the people and God. As an army major wrote in 1911 of the Muslim saints of the western Punjab, in words equally true of the medieval shrines: "The general idea of our riverain folk seems to be that the Deity is a busy person, and that his hall of audience is of limited capacity", and "Thus, all human beings require an intervener between them and God". The shrine helped to draw Heaven and Earth together in the minds of the Muslims of rural Punjab.

Wanderers passed through the province too. In the age of Mongol rule over Central Asia and Iran, wandering dervishes, the *qalandars* of the Qalandariyya and the Jawaliqis, passed through Multan, where they often came into conflict with Shaikh Bahauddin Zakariya. From the Punjab the displeased qalandars moved on to Delhi, making scenes wherever they saw a *khanqah*, a Sufi lodge, and some went as far east as Bengal. In the time of Makhdum-i Jahaniyan the Suhrawardi order spread from Uch to Gujarat, the Punjab and Kashmir, reaching even Delhi.

The Chishti order spread in Hind and Punjab from Khwaja Muin al-Din Hasan Sijzi of Ajmer. It is dominant in Punjab and North India, though it was not widespread in Sindh, and it is usually counted one of the most powerful Sufi orders of Northern India and Punjab. The Punjab is named among five regions, with Gujarat, Bengal, Uttar Pradesh and the Deccan, where the early saints, and the Chishti masters above all, carried a spiritual authority that was at once political, religious and cultural, and where a spectrum of spiritually charged sites became places of devotion for Muslims of several classes, and for Hindus too.

The Qadiri line came to the province from Baghdad, which its ancestors had left in the time of Hulagu Khan. The first of the line to come to Punjab was Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus Uchchi Jili Gilani, who travelled by way of Khurasan to Multan and settled at Uch; he is said to have entered Uch in 887 AH. By Guru Nanak's day the Qadiris had made the Punjab the main centre of their work. The Naushahi order also flourished in Punjab, where its faqirs, the wandering Sufis, number in the thousands; it began with Shaikh Haji Muhammad Qadiri, known as Hazrat Naushah Ganj Bakhsh. The province drew learned men from afar as well. In Shah Jahan's reign Abul Khair came from Baghdad touring the countries, and because the climate of the Punjab pleased him he settled in Lahore, where his *madrasa*, a school of religious learning, stood at Garhi Shahu.

The Chishtis rose again in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. The Nizami Chishtis came to prominence in the eighteenth century, chiefly in Delhi, the Deccan and Punjab, through masters who were related to local political leaders and yet in tension with them. Khwaja Sulayman Taunsawi, born in 1184 AH, who died in 1267 AH, in 1850, made the order strong by stressing learning and founding institutions: the academy at Taunsa on the upper Indus, and other religious schools throughout the Punjab. It was above all through him that the Chishti Nizami Fakhri line spread in Punjab. Muhammad 'Aqil and Gul Muhammad Ahmadpuri did the same, founding religious schools in the southern Punjab that gave free teaching and lodging to hundreds of students. The line of Shah Mirra came to light again in the nineteenth century in Hafiz Sayyid Muhammad 'Ali Khayrabadi, as the Nizami branch spread through the disciples of Shaikh Sulayman Taunsawi in the upper Punjab.

Smaller orders had small followings. In the nineteenth century the Rasul Shahi order had only four faqirs of the line living, and across the whole Punjab it had no house, *takia*, a faqir's resting place, or khanqah beyond a single house and one more near Lahore. The claim heard in Punjab that the Jalali faqirs are Chihil Tani was dismissed in the same century as the talk of the ignorant. Of one boy a *sajjada nashin*, the hereditary head of a shrine, foretold that he would become the *subahdar*, the governor, of *faqr*, spiritual poverty, in Punjab.

The Punjab is also the home ground of Guru Nanak's journeys as Sikh tradition tells them. At Haridwar, in an episode dated 1504, he faces west, in the direction of Punjab, which is very far away; at Prayag he is called "Nanak, the great Guru from Punjab". By the time he returned to Talwandi, it is said, there were very few in the Punjab who did not know of him and his teachings.

On his second journey Guru Nanak crossed Rajputana and the south west Punjab until he came to Multan. He was by then a well known figure in the province, and in the story the Sufis of Multan had shown him a patronising tolerance as long as he preached elsewhere in the Punjab. In some accounts his third journey, of 1514 to 1516, went as far as Tashkent and then back to Punjab by way of the Kashmir Valley. The fourth closed, around the time of Babur's third incursion into India, with a route back to the Punjab from the south east. The fifth, of 1523 to 1524, lasted about a year and covered the towns and regions within Punjab.

The death of a saint could move the whole province. When Hazrat Husain died, there was hardly anyone in Punjab who did not grieve, and in the mourning a clamour like that of Karbala rose across the land. Madho Lal Hussein still commands great reverence in Punjab more than four hundred years after his death.

The beliefs and devotions of the countryside are part of the same world. In popular belief in the Punjab countryside, *pirs*, Sufi masters, *fakirs* and *jogis*, Hindu ascetics, can by the force of their piety make the Moving Finger of Destiny cancel what it has written. Lighting lamps in a mosque, even with ordinary oil, is still a well known way of thanking God for His favours, and Punjabi mothers still promise to light lamps in mosques when they pray for their children. *Bhang*, a preparation of cannabis, is a favourite with the mystically inclined, especially among Muslims and Sikhs, and is still taken in the old Punjab area by wandering mendicants and by many who seek spiritual exaltation or sexual pleasure. A dawn at the mosque, as the poem of Hir and Ranjha describes it, is how a morning begins even now in a Punjab village.

Not every holy place in the Punjab belongs to a named saint. Countless shrines to unknown saints stand on the outskirts of Punjabi villages, and their very anonymity, it has been suggested, is key to their place as mediators between the dangerous forces of nature and cultural continuity. Khidr is often called Zinda pir, the living saint, in Punjab, because he too is thought to have disappeared alive. At the shrines of Punjab and Sindh devotees perform the *dhammal*, a vigorous ritual dance.

A Sufi master's domain could be drawn on the map of the province in the twentieth century. In his last will and testament Shahidullah Faridi wrote that Wahid Bakhsh, whose *khilafat*, his standing as the master's deputy, was publicly confirmed in 1975, had begun *halqa zikr*, the circle of remembrance of God, and spiritual guidance in Bahawalpur, and should spread the *silsila*, the Sufi lineage, in that place only. The will names only Bahawalpur, but it has been read as marking out the southern Punjab as Wahid Bakhsh's personal spiritual domain, his *wilayat*.

Today the heart of shrine devotion is placed in the south of the province. Southern Punjab is believed to be the hub of shrine culture in the country, and *Saeen* is a title used for a feudal lord or a spiritual leader from lower Punjab and Sindh, where shrine culture thrives. The southern region of Punjab, where Uch Sharif lies, holds more traces of religious and cultural syncretism, the blending of traditions, than the northern and central regions, where, in one writer's view, education is more widespread and with it religious intolerance.

## Communities

The meeting of the province's faiths has been seen in very different ways. In one view, for over three hundred years in the medieval Punjab Islam and Hinduism lived side by side in a state of constant conflict. Yet a map of South Asia's religious population shows that Islam took root much more readily in the Punjab and Bengal than in upper India, the region with the deepest traditions of Muslim rule and patronage. In both Bengal and the western wing of Punjab and Sindh, the growth of Muslim societies went together with the spread of settled farming, and both were still frontier societies where Hindu religious values and the hierarchical social ideals of the Brahmin priests had not yet sunk deep. So by the eighteenth century large communities of Muslim peasants had appeared in both the Punjab and Bengal, while in India's heartland conversion was far less significant.

The Jats stand at the centre of this story. Between the thirteenth and nineteenth centuries, as the Jat tribesmen of the western Punjab gave up pastoral nomadism for settled farming, they gradually took on an Islamic identity. The towns of the western Punjab lived by intensive cultivation along the river flood plains. Beyond them lay the *barr*, a vast and sparsely peopled tract between the five rivers, little cultivated because of the very scanty rain, where herders kept goats and camels. They moved down to the rivers in the hot dry months and back to the barr after the rains, never leaving the Punjab plains and covering at most less than a hundred miles, in a relationship of mutual need with the settled peoples of the river country.

Before they entered the Punjab the Jat herders had not yet been brought into Hindu society. By Baba Farid's time, in the 1260s, the Jats are first found living in the Punjab proper; Babur described Jat herding groups in the Sind Sagar doab, the land between two rivers, and round Sialkot; and their movement north and settlement in the grazing tracts of the Punjab is well supported by the evidence of the time. In Akbar's statistics, of the 186 Punjabi *parganas*, or revenue districts, whose dominant *zamindars*, the landholders, are known, 82, nearly half, were held by Jat groups. Only in the far west of the Punjab, in the Sind Sagar doab, where the Janjua Rajputs and the Gakkhars dominated, were the Jats conspicuously absent as zamindars.

The Jats whom al-Biruni had called low Sudras in the eleventh century had become the dominant farming caste in nearly half of the Punjab, and by the 1650s, as today, the word Jat had come to mean almost the same as peasant farmer. The change has been explained by the coming of the Persian wheel, the water wheel turned by animals, which was widely used in the Punjab by Babur's time and made much of the naturally rich soil of the arid western Punjab able to grow food crops.

Conversion went with this migration. As the Jats moved north from Sindh to Punjab, rose from low Sudras to zamindars and changed from herders to farmers, they also became Muslims. Of the Jat and Rajput groups that became Muslim in the medieval period, the great majority claim to have been converted either by Baba Farid or by his contemporary Bahauddin Zakariya, who died in 1263 and whose tomb is in Multan. The Punjabi Muslim clans that claim Baba Farid include the Bhatti, Chhina, Dhudhi, Dogar, Gondal, Hans, Jo'iya, Khokhar, Siyal, Tiwana, Watt, Kharral and Ara'in. In depositions made between 1935 and 1938 before the Lahore High Court, many of the large clans that marry within themselves and are spread across the south western Punjab, mostly as peasant farmers, claimed conversion by Baba Farid, among them the Wattu, the Siyal, the Dogar, the Kathia, the Bhatti and the Tiwana.

The genealogies of the Siyals of Jhang District, which run from the late fourteenth to the early nineteenth century, show that religious conversion among Punjabi clans was very slow indeed, and probably unconscious as well. It happened long before British census officials or zealous reformers began urging Indians to place themselves in sharply separate religious categories. New Muslims from the weaver castes joined the Islamic community in Punjab somewhat later than in Hindustan, where the mystic poet Kabir shows the same blend of Sufi faith and *bhakti*, Hindu devotion, a century earlier.

The province's peoples moved and settled across it. The Mongols who made peace with Jalal al-Din Khalji became Muslims and were settled at the place that came to be called Mughalpura. Under the Langah sultans Malik Sohrab Dodai, father of Ismail Khan and Fath Khan, came with his tribe from the country of Kech and Makran, and Sultan Husain gave him a *jagir*, a grant of land revenue. Many more Baluch followed and the rest of the country along the Indus was assigned to them; little by little a stretch of that land passed to them, and from those days it remained in the hands of Malik Sohrab's descendants. The Baluch who had come from Kech Makran were also given a jagir from Karor Kot to Mankot.

Famine moved people too. A few years before 1884 famine fell on Kashmir, and thousands of starving Kashmiris came into the Punjab, hundreds of them reaching Lahore itself. The famine of the land of Punjab is remembered in the life of Mai Bhagi. It came soon after her marriage at the age of seven, and was known as the two and a half *ser* famine, the *ser* being a measure of weight. The whole country was thrown into confusion, a mother would not look after her son, and Mai Bhagi and her mother left their town because of it.

Other communities moved out of the province. The Bhatia and the Khatris arrived in the eighteenth century from cities of the Punjab, Multan among them, and were in the end absorbed as Lohanas, and migrants from Rajputana and the Punjab who followed Lohana trades also became members of the Lohana caste. The Punjabi tale of Suhini and Mehar is thought to have come into Sindh with the Hindu peoples who migrated there from the Punjab and form an important part of its population.

Hindus and Muslims served the same state. Under the Mughals a Hindu *amir*, a nobleman, served as *diwan*, the chief revenue officer, of the *suba*, the province, of the Punjab, and when Nawab Zakariya Khan Bahadur governed Lahore the brothers Diwan Lakhpat Rai and Jaspat Rai were diwans of the government of the Punjab. At his son's wedding Lakhpat Rai gave a gift to every Hindu and every Muslim in Lahore. When Meer Manu cast the battle between the state and the insurgents as a war between Muslims and Sikhs, there were in fact several Muslims in the Sikh groups and many soldiers who were not Muslim in his own army. The Sikh groups held little land, but because they stood against the established order the lower ranks of society supported and protected them.

A plea to Ranjit Singh shows the Muslims of the Punjab under Sikh rule. Pleading with Ranjit Singh for the mosque by the Baoli, the stepwell, the Fakir sahibs told the Maharaja that nowhere in the mosques of the whole Punjab did the *mullas*, the prayer leaders, give the call to prayer aloud, and offered a bond that the mulla would not give it. As British rule began to take hold of India, Shaikh Sulayman Taunsawi urged his followers to join the resistance against Sikh aggression in the Punjab, and for apparently the first time in Chishti history the written lives of the saints were drawn into taking the side of one political group. Yet Shah Sulayman also worked to keep cordial relations with the Punjabi Hindus even while he opposed their Sikh neighbours. The old ruling class kept its habits into English rule: by the 1880s silk drawstrings had gone out of use and everyone wore cotton, but the amirs of the Punjab were still used to silk ones.

In other ways the boundaries between communities were porous. Hindus shared in the Muslim orders, more visibly in Sindh than in the Punjab, and some saints were claimed by both communities. Shah Abdul Latif of Sindh, like his Punjabi contemporaries and his successors in Sindh, accepted Hindus who were striving on the way to perfection as true pilgrims in the eternal Light. In the village life of the old Punjab the whole community was regarded as one social unit regardless of caste or creed: some of Hir's friends, by their names, are Hindus, and even a sweeper woman would be called daughter, sister or mother. It was by no means unusual in the old Punjab for a Muslim family to depend on Hindu Brahmans to fix a marriage.

The Sikh tradition reached out from the Punjab and came back to it. The *nirguni* tradition of *bhakti*, devotion to God without form, was more influential in Northern India and especially in Punjab, where the Sikh tradition grew from it. The Aroras may have carried the cult of the Nanak Panth, a form of Sikhism that does not recognise the authority of the Khalsa, from Punjab into Sindh during their migrations in the eighteenth century. In the second half of the nineteenth century came the first attempt to make the Sikh tradition rational and uniform; centred on Punjab, it did not touch the Sindhi Nanakpanthis, who were Sikhs outside the orthodox fold.

The land's people are known by caste and clan. Ranjha is still a well known Jat caste of the old Punjab. The Khojas are a well known tribe of Hindu converts to Islam in the old Punjab, mostly traders. The Gujars, a famous tribe that keeps animals and sells their milk, gave their name to towns such as Gujranwala, Gujrat and Gujar Khan. The legend of Hir and Ranjha is set in the world of the Jat pastoral tribes of western Punjab.

Hir's own people, the Siyal, were powerful landowners of the Punjab, and one of the few tribes in the province that allow women to inherit property under customary law. The Jats of the Punjab are zamindars known for allowing widows to marry again, and among some sections of society, such as the Siyal Jats, a widow could remarry and was not forced into *satti*, burning on her husband's pyre. The Kheras, named in the Hir and Ranjha story, were another powerful clan of Punjabi landowners. The Ara'in of the Punjab were gardeners or small cultivators who worked their own land.

Marriage had customs of its own. In the old Punjab brides wore coloured and colourful clothes, while white was by tradition the dress of widows, especially among Hindus, and childless Muslim widows wore black or white; the social pattern has since changed. A traditional Punjab wedding still keeps the symbolism of marriage by capture: the bridegroom is dressed to look like a general, and the bride's side now resists only with abusive words. In the rural Punjab a girl does not see her fiancé between the *mangni*, the betrothal, and the wedding. The *arsi*, a thumb ring set with a mirror, was a favourite ornament of the bride, and a newly married girl was not expected to do housework for a couple of months, as is still the case in the less sophisticated parts of the countryside. A fringe of intertwined strands of hair round the forehead, the *mehndhi*, marked a virgin girl and disappeared at her wedding.

Other marriage customs were known in the nineteenth century. In the story of the marriage of Hazrat Naushah's daughter, the bride's family, following the custom of the Muslims of Punjab, demanded a hundred rupees in customary dues. The killing of daughters was an old custom among the descendants of Guru Nanak through Lakhmi Chand, who counted themselves the noblest people in Punjab and would give a daughter to no one, because by the custom of that country the girl's father becomes subordinate to the boy's family.

Household and village life had its own pattern. Old houses in the Punjab countryside had a big courtyard with living rooms and bedrooms round it, and a joint family of brothers living with their parents was quite common. The *charkha*, the spinning wheel, was the constant companion of every woman, old or young, in the rural Punjab, and an important link in village society: women spun cotton yarn and the village weaver made it into cloth. Spinning parties were a famous institution of the Punjab village, where young girls, mostly unmarried, competed in spinning skill and in scandalous talk, and in a favourite game the girls threw printed red scarves with white flowers at each other.

Food and offerings follow the same pattern. *Lassi*, the drink of churned curd, is a favourite in the Punjab countryside, where washing the hair with curds is also still well known. *Gur*, raw sugar, is a well known ceremonial offering, and an invitation that comes with it cannot be taken lightly. Rice cooked in ghee and sprinkled with sugar is the usual ceremonial dish, a custom still alive in the countryside of the old Punjab, while millet is even now thought an inferior food. Basmati rice, the best in the subcontinent and now a valuable export of Pakistan, is grown in the central districts of the old Punjab, where Sawan and Bhadon are the two rainy months of the year.

Smaller customs complete the picture. Mauj Din is now a common name in the old Punjab, and jugglers with their bears are still a familiar sight. Fingers in the mouth is a typical gesture of the women, and a snub nose is not thought a mark of beauty in men or women. A mother's exaggerated threats are by no means unfamiliar in the villages even now. *Dandasa*, a bark, served as the lipstick of the rural Punjab; shaving with soap came much later, and village barbers still often shave with water alone; and the *pearha* is a piece of furniture common in Punjabi homes. The tufted turban beloved of the rural aristocracy is a recent fashion, said to have been invented in the late nineteenth century by the *mirasis*, the hereditary bards of the Punjab.

Some read the province's history in the character of its people. In this view the period of hardship after the Mughals formed the character of the region's inhabitants, and the crisis of character in the Punjabi of today can be traced to it. A satirical questionnaire by a columnist writing as Janus, "Are you a Punjabi?", makes the point: told that his ancestors came from Central Asia, the Punjabi answers, "What the hell! I am a Bokhari and superior to all the low stock inhabiting the Punjab".

Reform movements have pressed on the shrine culture of the countryside. In the early twentieth century Muhammad Ilyas, founder of the Tableeghi Jamaat, worked in undivided rural Punjab, urging Muslims to "purify" their religious practices, which he held had been corrupted by the dominant Hindu culture, and Muslim reformers like him founded movements of their own that sharpened communal tension in Punjab. Tableeghi preachers focus on southern Punjab and rural Sindh, where a blended religion held sway, and the Tableeghi Jamaat has worked its way into the social fabric of the major cities of Punjab, so that shrines like those at Kamalia and Apal Muri are likely to face a major onslaught in the years to come for their "impure" practices.

The Deobandi school has turned to the same ground. In recent years its focus has been on South Punjab and Sindh, where saints and shrines are everywhere, and the people of the South are drawn to it and feel wronged by a feudal society bound up with the culture of saints and shrines. The Deobandis also gained popularity among the lower and middle ranks of the urban Punjabi Muslims, which they have kept in Pakistan today. Organisations of the Ahl-e-Hadith school have likewise seeped into the social fabric of southern Punjab, a region that historically rejected such strands of Islam and adopted a blended religion of which the Sufi shrines are a symbol, and this is changing as they open seminaries in the countryside and draw in young men without work. They are popular with the young because they offer an explanation for their present oppression. Their first step is to target the shrines and their guardians, who in their eyes have grown immensely rich feeding off the oppressed classes.

Partition changed who lived beside whom. Local people tend to be more accepting of the practices of other faiths than the migrants who came after the division of the Punjab; several gurdwaras and temples have been taken over and destroyed by the migrants, while where the population is largely local such places are better looked after, with exceptions. Southern Punjab has been historically more tolerant than Central Punjab towards other faiths, which is why a significant Hindu population still lives there.

The province's religious minorities today live under pressure. Of Pakistan's citizens who are not Muslim, leaving aside the Ahmadiyya, the majority are Hindus living in the regions of Southern Punjab, inner Sindh and Baluchistan that are counted remote. In the Punjab itself Christians have a formidable presence and are the overwhelming majority among the minorities, while Hindus are a smaller community there, so political parties can ignore them. In urban Punjab the religious lines between Hindus and Christians have become blurred. Many Hindus in Punjab pass as Muslims or Christians by taking names that are not Hindu, a way of surviving in a hostile place. Where minorities in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa are visible in society, Punjab is much more uniform. After the demolition of the Babri Mosque on 6 December 1992, well known Hindus in the cities of Punjab had to hide for several months.

In most villages of the province Muslims are the landowners, and Christians are confined to menial work associated with the untouchable caste, and treated as untouchables. Shanti Nagar, where Christians own the land, stands out as a unique case. Attacks on Christians came at Sangla Hill in 2005 and at Gojra and at Bahmniwala in Kasur in 2009, all in Punjab, and the story of a few burnt pages of the Quran found in local mosques seems to recur. One man who moved to Lahore in the 1980s put it this way: "here in the Punjab, the situation is much different. There are hardly any Zoroastrians here and I have never come across any Hindu. The only Christians that you see are those who are cleaners and sweepers".

The province's Sikhs and Nanak Panthis have their own story. Nanak Panthi Sikhs belong to South Punjab and Sindh, where their temples stand, and the workers of the *langar*, the free communal kitchen, at Dera Sahib are low caste Nanak Panthis from Southern Punjab. Thousands of devotees of Guru Nanak who did not join Guru Gobind Singh's Khalsa live in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Punjab and Sindh, where they are called Nanak Panthi Hindus; in their temples a picture of Guru Nanak hangs beside pictures of Hindu deities. At the festival at Nankana in November 2011 the singers of *kirtan*, Sikh devotional hymns, covered their heads with a loose turban, a style popular in the villages of West Punjab.

Many of the province's Sikhs are Pathans who came from the tribal areas. Moving to the heart of Punjab, most of them could not even speak its language, and they stayed within their own small enclaves. The Sikhs of Nankana remember this migration from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa to Punjab, which shows how different life is in the two provinces. Not all are newcomers: Boota Singh of Nena Kot said that his people were not from the Tribal Areas but Punjabis.

The countryside has its hardships. For a child of a poor rural family in central Punjab who is seen as intersex, the most likely path would lead to a life among the *khwaja-sara*, the community of transgender and intersex people. Among the poor of parts of Sindh and Punjab, unmarried girls in their teens and early twenties are the most vulnerable, facing unequal portions of food within the family and little access to health care.

The province's shared festivals belong to its farming year. The Punjab is still mostly an agricultural society, and the harvest is its greatest source of joy. Lohri, like Vaisakhi and Basant, is an ancient festival of the Punjab that marks the change of season, kept on the 13th of January, generally the last night of the month of Poh, with a village bonfire, dry fruit and folk songs, children gathering wood, and *kheer*, rice pudding, left to cool overnight. Once kept on a large scale throughout the province, it died down after the creation of Pakistan, when the country's elite gave up festivals like Vaisakhi, Lohri and Basant, and the Sikh and Hindu minorities of central Punjab stopped keeping it too. It is ironic that this Punjabi tradition, shunned by Punjab, is now being revived by a group of Pathan men living in the province.

## Language, Poetry and Music

The Punjab is above all a land of mystical poetry in its own tongue. Reliable tradition traces the use of the native language for mystical poetry in the Punjab, as in Sindh, back to the fifteenth century, and popular mystical poetry may have emerged as early as the days of Fariduddin Shakarganj, the Chishti saint of Pakpattan who died in 1265. Numerous folk songs and ballads existed but were never written down. Accounts differ on when folk tales became a means of mystical teaching, but Madho Lal Hussein's allusions to the stories of Hir and of Sohni and Mahiwal seem to begin, or to carry on from unknown beginnings, a tradition typical of the religious poetry of the Punjab and Sindh: the setting of folk tales inside mystical thought. Persian poetry, meanwhile, was common ground: in Guru Nanak's time all literate Punjabis, Hindus and Muslims alike, are held to have known ʿAttar, Rumi and Saʿdi.

The *kafi*, a stanzaic poem built on folk themes and techniques, carried the mystics' teaching. Kafis were easy to understand and to memorise, and so were the ideal way to spread mystical teaching among people who could not read and depended on knowledge passed on by word of mouth. Even today most Punjabis and Sindhis are more likely to have heard a kafi than to have read one. Madho Lal Hussein belongs to the Punjab of Akbar, the Punjab of the rebellion led by Abdullah Bhatti, known as Dullah Bhatti, who was captured and hanged in public on Akbar's orders. Najm Hosain Syed's play *Takht L'hore (Throne of Lahore)* draws out the social and economic conditions of the Punjab in the time of Madho Lal Hussein.

The golden age of Muslim Punjabi poetry came in the late seventeenth and the eighteenth century, when the province was invaded one time after another by Marathas, Sikhs and, in between, Muslim armies. Punjabi mystical folk poetry reached its peak with Bulleh Shah in exactly the years when Shah Abdul Latif was writing in Sindh, and the likeness between the two is striking. Siraiki forms the bridge to Punjabi, so that the imagery of all three language areas is closely related, as one would expect among the people of mainly rural provinces. Shah Abdul Latif stands for a rural mystical tradition in which his contemporaries in the Punjab, and to some extent in the Pashto speaking areas, were also rooted.

The Punjab and the Indus valley share one tradition of mystical folk literature, and the Punjab and Sindh nourished the same kinds of mystical writing. Punjabi and Sindhi were excellent languages for mystical feeling, though not for mystical theory. Their themes were sung again and again in forms inherited from the Indian past and closely tied to the native musical modes, not in the Persian *ghazal*, the classical lyric of love. The mystics of the north west took up the attack on the *mulla*, the cleric of the mosque, in verses that appealed above all to peasants and to those who could not read.

In both provinces poets loved the motif of spinning and weaving, natural in a country that grows cotton. The spinning of cotton was one of the most important occupations of women, and it was sung to the traditional folk melodies that women sang at the wheel. Both provinces also produced hymns in praise of the great masters of the Sufi orders, above all ʿAbd al-Qadir Gilani, in images that everyone in the village could understand.

The same poetry turned to the Prophet and to the soul. While the city poets of India praised the Prophet in grand hymns, poets in the plains of Sindh and the Punjab wrote tender little poems and teaching *Golden Alphabets*, verses arranged by the letters of the alphabet, to plant love for him in the souls of the villagers. The mystical saying *Ahmad bila mim*, "Ahmad without the letter *m*", was repeated in high literature and in popular mystical poetry alike, among the mystical bards of Sindh and the Punjab as among the Turkish Bektashis. The story of Muhammad ibn al-Hanafiyya, a son of ʿAli by a wife other than Fatima, is found in the Punjab as in Bengal, South India and Malaysia.

Sindh and the Punjab also share a form of mystical regard for women rarely met elsewhere in the Muslim world. The longing soul is pictured as a woman, so that the tragic heroines of the Sindhi and Punjabi folk tales, Hir, Sassi, Sohni and many others, stand for the human soul in search of the beloved.

In the south the province's language shades into its neighbour's. Siraiki is spoken in northern Sindh and southern Punjab, mainly in southern Punjab, with Multan as its capital. On the road through Bahawalpur the boundary between Sindh and Punjab becomes a blur: here the cultures of the two provinces merge, some roadside hotels are called Punjab Hotel and others Sindh Hotel, and Siraiki is a blend of the two languages. As the historian Iqbal Qaiser puts it, the Siraiki spoken in Multan is closer to Punjabi, while there it is more like Sindhi.

The love of Hir and Ranjha is the province's own story. By the 1880s the tale was on the lips of good and bad, young and old, rich and *faqir*, the poor holy man, across the country of the Punjab. Many books on it existed in Punjabi, but no one but Punjabis could profit from them, which is why Kanhaiya Lal, the historian of Lahore, made his own version in Persian. Already at the end of the seventeenth century the people of the Punjab composed wonderful verses and designs on the love of Hir and Ranjha and sang them.

Punjabi poets and their eager audiences poured into Hir and Ranjha the same erotic feeling that was given to Radha and Krishna in the lands nearer the Ganges, and the story became a favourite Sufi way of conveying the value of passionate love in spiritual life. Madho Lal Hussein's poems explore divine love in terms Islamic, Hindu and native Punjabi. The romance was worked into more than a hundred versions in Punjabi, Urdu, Sindhi and Persian and reached its final form in the late eighteenth century in the verse of Waris Shah, which is sung, time and again, wherever Punjabis meet. A visitor who stays in a Punjabi village will surely find someone to sing him the old romance in that classical form, and even today a Punjabi is deeply touched when the story is sung.

The romance lives on in performance and in custom. Waris Shah is said to have recited it with great emotion among the people of the Punjab, and many renowned folk artists of the Punjab have sung it, among them Reshma, Zahida Parvin, Nazir Begum, Sabiha Khanum, Tufail Niazi and Iqbal Bahu. Hir's lament as she is carried off in the palanquin is traditionally sung at almost every Punjabi wedding as the bride leaves for her husband's home, and the palanquin itself carries a strong emotional charge in Punjabi ritual. Hir and Ranjha is the most celebrated folk story of the Punjab, sung and dramatised for centuries. Mirza Sahiban is another famous love legend of the province, yet its story is counted such a blemish on the Punjab's spiritual love stories that these two lovers were said to have brought the era of love legends to an end.

Other tales travel across the Punjab's borders. Sohni and Mahiwal is set in the old Punjab, Sohni belonging to Gujrat on the Chenab, and like Sassi and Punnoo it has travelled beyond its first setting to be popular in both the old Punjab and Sindh. In the folk culture of the Punjab the Chenab is the river of love, the river of Sohni and Mahiwal and of Ranjha's Takht Hazara. Sohni is one of the favourite folk tales of both provinces: her story is sung in Sufi poetry in every region of Sindh and in the Siraiki speaking area of the Punjab, and Mahiwal is the hero of the Punjab's Sohni myth. The story of Suhini and Mehar in Shah Abdul Latif's *Risalo*, his collected verse, is a Punjab tale that has found a home in Sindh, one of the stories of the *Risalo* that came from the Punjab.

The poets made the Punjab itself a figure in their verse. Waris Shah's similes draw on the invasions of the province: as if armies from Kandahar had swept over the Punjab; looted as armies have looted the Punjab; today the Punjab has fallen into the hands of Kandaharis, a line taken to refer to Ahmad Shah Abdali. Once the poet turns the image round, writing as if the armies of the Punjab had fallen upon Hindustan; that is an imagined scene, since the armies of the Punjab are known to have defended Hindustan rather than fallen upon it, and even the later Sikh power was held on the Sutlej by the Anglo Sikh treaty of 1809; the poet's flight of fancy may have come from a mistaken sense of local patriotism.

Bulleh Shah's lament for the province is the best known of such lines, and its full bitterness comes through only against that backdrop of upheaval. In the poem *Come See Me Once in a While* the lines run as follows.

*Now hell has opened its bogs. / Punjab has gone to the dogs. / This hell is the nethermost hell.* (translated by Taufiq Rafat)

The province's saints and rulers are named in verse as well. The *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, the lives of the Punjab's saints written in 1875, closes with a chronogram, a verse whose letters give a date, for the year 1292 AH by its author's son, Mufti Ghulam Safdar Lahori, which speaks of the province's saints.

جو خدا کے دوست تھے پنجاب میں
حال ان کا اس سے ہوتا ہے عیان

*A chronogram for the book, on the friends of God in Punjab whose lives it makes known.*

In the nineteenth century Dalip Singh's departure from Punjab after the annexation was marked by a *qita*, a short poem, one of whose lines likens him to a new flower from the garden of Punjab. The *suba*, the Mughal province, of the Punjab is named in verse on its governors' buildings too. On the northern *mihrab*, the prayer niche, of the mosque of Nawab Zakariya Khan, west of the *mazar*, the saint's tomb, of Madho Lal Hussein, a tile inscription recorded in the 1880s carried these lines.

زبدۂ بارگاہ او نواب
زکریا خان صوبہ پنجاب

The verse names the mosque's builder, Nawab Zakariya Khan of the *suba* of the Punjab, and the inscription places the building in the reign of Muhammad Shah.

The province's minstrels carried the songs of every faith. In the time of Guru Nanak and Mardana the Mirasis, a community of minstrels, went from city to city singing of local chieftains, and landlords showered them with gifts. Mardana knew by heart several folk stories of Hindu deities and Muslim saints that he would sing at the festivals of the Punjab, and as a younger man he roamed the cities and villages of the Punjab singing folk songs and legends, the most popular of them Puran Bhagat's. The *nad*, a musical instrument, was popular in the old Punjab.

The poetry of the saints lived on in song. In the time of Shaikh Ibrahim, Baba Farid's twelfth successor, Farid's poetry is pictured as still sung by *qawwals*, the singers of Sufi devotional music, at his shrine and by *faqirs* in the cities of the Punjab. The name Qalandar is still intoned in the popular *qawwali* lyrics of the Punjab.

The Punjab is one of the two principal regions of the *qawwali* tradition, beside Uttar Pradesh, and the tunes of the two can be told apart mainly by their rhythm. Tunes from Uttar Pradesh rest mainly on quantity, the length of the syllables, while Punjabi tunes tend to be organised by stress, the drum marking the metre more strongly in the Punjab, in keeping with each region's drumming. A song by the Punjabi *qawwal* Rahmat Khan is a model of this Punjabi style.

Books reached the province late. In Sikh times no printed book came from Hindustan into the Punjab, and scribes wrote out manuscript books and sold them. The lyrics of Bulleh Shah and the other Sufi poets, who lived at different times and in different parts of Punjab, were never kept systematically by an organised faith community, and most of them were written down and gathered into printed collections only in the late nineteenth century, when publishers in Lahore brought the older Sufi poetry into the new print culture. In the Punjab Urdu took the place of Persian directly, which did not happen in Sindh, governed from Bombay rather than Calcutta.

After 1800 Muslims rarely carried on the literary heritage of Punjabi, though in the twentieth century a number of mystical treatises were written and published in it, among them a partial translation of Rumi's *Mathnawi*. Punjabi became almost entirely the literary language of the Sikhs, while Punjabi Muslims turned to Urdu, though they enjoyed, and still enjoy, listening to the simple mystical verses and ballads of their spoken mother tongue.

The modern picture of the language is gloomy. In the early 1980s Punjabi literature in Pakistan was in a state of neglect: the language had become a purely spoken one, and Punjabis could no longer read it in written form. In written sources the Punjab, and indeed the whole Indus basin, has long seemed a wasteland to scholars. The poetry of Baba Farid and Madho Lal Hussein survived through Sikh collectors, and a lack of intellectual tradition among the Muslims of the Punjab is blamed for their indifference to the classics; Bulleh Shah was treated no differently. The Punjab awaited a proper assessment of its greatest poet, a meeting that might never take place.

As one questionnaire put it, all regional personalities are moulded by a deep love of their idiom, yet while a Sindhi can name the greatest living Sindhi poet, and so can the Pathan and the Baluch, the Punjabi cannot; Punjabi alone, it adds, is not taught in schools and has no dictionary. In Pakistani Punjab the language is looked down upon as the speech of the boorish and the uneducated. At one elite private school any student who spoke Punjabi, even in the playground, was fined, and the educated elite of the Punjab end by disowning, even ridiculing, anything Punjabi, the poetry of Baba Farid, Guru Nanak, Madho Lal Hussein, Waris Shah and Bulleh Shah among it.

## Shrines and the State

Rulers tied themselves to the province's shrines and its land from early on. The Delhi sultans and the Mughal emperors granted huge tracts of rich land for the ritual ceremonies of the great Sufi shrines of Punjab and Sindh, while irrigation allowed wheat production to grow rapidly. Akbar used the Chishti saints, Shaikh Salim and Shaikh Fariduddin Shakarganj in the Punjab, to rule his provinces. Through this doubled Chishti loyalty he could anchor his imperial legitimacy in the Punjab and Rajasthan, two provinces next to Uttar Pradesh that were both crucial to the political and military ambitions of his reign.

Rulers dealt with the province's holy men and its revenue alike. While the imperial armies were quartered in the Punjab grain was dear, and so the assessed revenue of the *parganas*, the revenue districts, was raised. When the armies left Lahore for the Deccan grain became cheap, the peasants could not pay, and a reduction was ordered, told as granted on the petition of Guru Arjan.

The Mughal state built across the province and paid for its builders' tombs. In Shah Jahan's time Ali Mardan Khan raised great and splendid buildings in Hind and Punjab, and in the nineteenth century his gardens remained his memorial in many places. In the same reign the builder of the garden called Sanhla Bagh was given an office over the buildings of Punjab, and at his death money was granted from the treasury for his tomb. Every ruler of the Punjab left a mark on its religious buildings: in Shah Jahan's reign Munshi Muhammad Salih, *diwan*, the chief revenue officer, of the *suba* of the Punjab, built a mosque, and Dara Shikoh built a great deal while he governed the Punjab.

The Sikh years were hard on the shrines as one eyewitness saw them. The author of the *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, the Mufti whose own family was a target of the Sikh disorders, saw the disgrace of the notables of Punjab at Sikh hands and the destruction of the tombs of the shaikhs. Yet Ranjit Singh had faith in the *majzub* Mastan Shah, a holy man absorbed in divine love, who refused the Maharaja's thousands of rupees. Under Ranjit Singh the Moti Masjid in the fort was renamed the Moti Mandir and held the treasury of the state, and by the 1880s the English government too kept the treasury of the *suba* of the Punjab in it. Under the same Maharaja the Fakir sahibs bargained for a mosque by the Baoli, a stepwell, by promising that its *mulla* would not give the call to prayer aloud.

The Sikh state endowed the gurdwaras. When Ranjit Singh became sovereign of Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century he spent large sums renovating and enlarging many gurdwaras all over Punjab, and attached vast tracts of land to them so that the revenue from the land could pay for their running. Under the British their custody changed hands: with sectarian divisions among the Sikh communities and the British takeover of Punjab, a large number of important gurdwaras came to be run by Hindu *mahants*, the heads of temples and religious houses.

The new rulers lived in the old buildings. The troops left the Badshahi Mosque when English rule over the Punjab was complete. When Sikh rule passed and the country of the Punjab came under the English government, an octagonal *kothi*, a residence, built over a tomb housed in turn two Deputy Commissioners and Montgomery, Commissioner, who in the end became Lieutenant-Governor of the Punjab. In the nineteenth century the new government was urged that attention to the *khanqahs*, the Sufi lodges, would win the goodwill of the subjects of Punjab. When British rule and its legal settlement were established in Punjab, Jhuggi Shah returned to Dori Shah's site and set about restoring it.

Under the colonial state and after it, the Punjab's shrines were bound up with land and power. The British saw the hereditary saints chiefly in terms of their economic power, and their policy, especially in the Punjab and Sindh, was to treat them much as landlords and tribal leaders were treated. They tried to keep the traditional social structure intact, securing the loyalty of the *pirs*, the Sufi masters, and of landlords and chiefs by reinforcing their economic positions and educating them in the British tradition.

In the colonial period the battles over shrines in the Punjab, with Taunsa as the example, might seem the test case for the Chishti Nizami Sulaymaniyya line, and the struggles for power within it can be traced to British manipulation and to the rivalry of local Sikhs, Hindus and Muslim reformers; yet political contest is only part of its story. The cult of the holy man and its folk piety are particularly evident in the countryside, where great landlords and large holdings of land are central, especially in Punjab and Sindh, and in many cases the local *pir* represents the local landlord or is at least closely related to him. In central Punjab the colonial state did not try to control shrines directly through its bureaucracy; the state after independence has gone further, driven by a wish to harness the plural traditions of the shrines in favour of a single Islam.

After Partition many non-Muslim holy places lost their purpose. Hundreds of temples and gurdwaras all over Pakistani Punjab were taken over by refugees and turned into households, and the shrine of Ram Thamman escaped that fate only because a family cares for it. At Makhdoom Pur Pahuran the gurdwara stands inside a school, its gate locked, under a banner describing the Punjab government's new policy for improving education in public schools.

The provincial state appears in many guises. The Punjab has 8 reserved minority seats out of 371 in its assembly, against Sindh's 9 of 168. A class V Urdu textbook of the Punjab Textbooks Board, published in Lahore in 2002, teaches that the Hindu has always been an enemy of Islam, and in Pakistani schoolbooks the Hindu is cast not only as a danger to national security but as having always been an enemy of Islam. The road through Changa Manga is much safer since the Punjab government brought in the motorway police. Human rights activists, opposition party leaders and parts of the media have alleged that the Punjab government, dominated by the Pakistan Muslim League Noon, has been supporting radical groups based in Jhang, and the party rejects the claims.

On 4 January 2011 the Governor of Punjab, Salman Taseer, was shot dead by one of his bodyguards, Mumtaz Qadri, for supporting the cause of Aasia Bibi, a Christian woman accused of blasphemy. Qadri became an instant hero, hailed by the lawyers' community, and a retired chief justice of the Lahore High Court, Khwaja Sharif, offered to fight his case free of charge, calling him a hero of Islam. The Aasia Bibi case drew international coverage after the governor came out in support of her and was killed for it.

## The Province in Books

The Punjab has been drawn as many different units, and under the Mughals it was a *suba*, a province of the empire. At the end of the seventeenth century the *suba* of Lahore was a country of five *doabs*, the lands lying between two rivers, divided by the six rivers of the *suba*. The *doabs* lay between the Sutlej and the Beas, between the Beas and the Ravi, between the Ravi and the Chenab, where Sialkot stands, between the Chenab and the Bihat, where Gujrat stands, and between the Bihat and the Indus. The five rivers of Punjab join the Indus beyond Multan in the Baluch country, and from there the whole stream is called the Sindh and becomes a vast sea.

The *suba* of Lahore was a country of good water and air. Its summers were very hot and its winters cold; its melons and grapes were like those of Iran and Turan and its mangoes like Hindustan's; its rice was better than Bengal's and its sugarcane better than the Deccan's. Most of its irrigation depended on wells, worked by a wheel of three hundred and sixty pieces of wood and more than a hundred small pots, turned by a pair of oxen, each turn lifting several hundred *maunds*, a measure of weight, of water, while the autumn and spring harvests and cheap grain depended on the rain.

Gold was washed from the river sand, especially of the Beas and the Bihat, and the northern hills held mines of silver, copper and other metals; the miners profited and paid the duty to the imperial treasury. The *suba* measured one hundred and eighty *kos*, an old measure of distance, from the Sutlej to the Indus, and eighty seven *kos* across from Bhera, and was bounded by Sirhind to the east, Multan to the west and Kashmir to the north. Its five *doabs* held three hundred and sixteen *mahals*, revenue units, yielding eighty nine crore, thirty three lakh and seventy thousand *dams*, copper coins.

Beside it lay the *suba* of Multan, called the abode of safety. It ran four hundred *kos* from Firozpur to Siwistan and one hundred and twenty five *kos* across from Khatpur to Jaisalmer, and six hundred and sixty *kos* in length from Sadgahra to Kech Makran, adjoining the *sarkar*, or district, of Sirhind to the east, Kech Makran to the west, Shorkot to the north and the *suba* of Ajmer to the south. Its three *sarkars*, Multan, Dipalpur and Bhakkar, held ninety six *mahals* and yielded twenty four crore, forty six lakh and fifty five thousand *dams*, and the *sarkar* of Dipalpur was a dependency of the *suba*. The *suba* of Shahjahanabad had the *suba* of Lahore on its west.

The Mughal Punjab was a frontier made safe by union with Kabul. In former times, when Kabul lay outside the rule of the sovereign of Hind, the Turanians raided its borders and the *wilayat*, the territory, of Punjab lay ruined and waste; since Hind and Kabul came under one ruler, this union has been the cause of the prosperity of Punjab and the security of all Hind. The sultans of the *wilayat* of Multan had a history of their own, like those of Malwa, Daulatabad, the Deccan, Jaunpur, Bengal and Orissa, lands that at various times passed out of the control of Delhi and each had a separate ruler.

*Tarikh-e Lahore*, the history of 1884, calls the Punjab by many names: the *ilaqa*, or territory, of the Punjab given in *jagir*, a grant of land revenue; the Mughal *suba* of the Punjab with its *diwan*; the *khitta*, or region, of the Punjab that Ranjit Singh ruled; the kingdom of the Punjab that passed to Rani Chand Kaur; the country of the Punjab that the English annexed; and the *suba* of the Punjab whose capital in its author's day was Lahore. For the province's past it leaned on earlier works it names, the *Tuhfat al-Wasilin* of Shaikh Ahmad Zanjani, set against the *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, and the *Hadiqat al-Awliya*.

In that history the Punjab's edges are drawn by what crossed them. Jalal al-Din entered the Punjab across the river of Sindh; the Tatars came into it from the west; Timur passed through its foothills on his way to Delhi; and the Sutlej was the river Yalduz crossed on his way from the Punjab toward Delhi. Kashmir and the Derajat were Ranjit Singh's possessions besides the Punjab, the Kashmiris came into the Punjab in their famine, and printed books came into it from Hindustan. Its author, Kanhaiya Lal, was also a servant of the province: the women's prison of the Punjab was built under his supervision, and he described its governors, courts and jails as they stood in his own day.

The *Hadiqat al-Awliya* of 1875 defines its own province. Friends asked its author for another short collection in Urdu of the lives of the saints of the land of Punjab, meaning all the territory from Delhi to Peshawar then attached to Punjab, where the tombs of famous saints lie. Its fifth *chaman*, or garden, holds the elders who were learned and renowned scholars in Punjab, its seventh the pious women who lived in Punjab, and a later section the elders of Punjab whose dates of death could not be established. Its author's earlier Persian *Khazinat al-Asfiya* had been so large that those interested in the land of Punjab could not learn from it how many masters of the path had lived there, a difficulty the short Urdu book removed.

To the book's modern editor, Punjab is in this respect the most unfortunate of regions, for no regular and researched history of it has yet been written, and those who wrote its history gave only formal chapters without touching the Sufi *tazkiras*, the memoirs of the saints. The need is to fill the gaps in the political, cultural and spiritual history of Punjab. No comprehensive *tazkira* of the Sufis of Punjab is known to him, and without the Mufti's general *tazkiras*, in his view, no scholar or gnostic of Punjab would be seen today. The book holds the lives of the Sufis of Punjab in seven *chamans*, from Sultan Mahmud of Ghazni to 1292 AH, 1875, and also takes in some well known Sufis who had no connection with Punjab.

Some Punjab shaikhs are found only in this book, and by preserving his contemporaries the Mufti did Punjab a great service, though he wrote up whichever elders of Punjab came to mind at a single sitting. The modern edition calls it an authoritative *tazkira* of the great Sufis of Punjab. Beside it stands the *Tuhfat al-Muslimin* of Maulana Muhammad Aqil Lahori, which holds the sayings of Shaikh Hamid Qari and the lives of the great scholars of Punjab from Shaikh Hamid to Shaikh Bahauddin Zakariya Multani, and whose rare manuscript is in the editor's own library. The author of *Nuzhat al-Khawatir*, in the editor's judgement, took the *Khazinat al-Asfiya* as his largest source for Punjab and generally included its reports without research.

The translators of the province's poets draw it in their own ways. The world of Waris Shah's poem is the old Punjab, with its *bars*, a kind of jungle, such as the Nili Bar in the Montgomery area, now renamed Sahiwal. Madho Lal Hussein's Punjab has been read against W. H. Moreland's account of the Mughal Golden Age, though that account speaks of India taken as a unit rather than of Punjab. The world of Hir and Ranjha is western Punjab. One historian of Islamic mysticism most often speaks of Sindh and the Punjab together, as the two provinces of one tradition of religious poetry and as a cotton growing country, and for her the province is also the land invaded by Marathas, Sikhs and Muslim armies in the late seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Seen from eighteenth century Sindh, in a study of 1940, the Punjab is the theatre of the armies of Afghans, Sikhs and Marathas and a source of migrants and tales.

For historians the Punjab is defined by comparison. The Mughal officials who reached Bengal around 1620 found that the Bengali diet of fish and rice contrasted sharply with the wheat and meat diet of the Punjab and seems to have been a special stumbling block for newcomers. Against W. H. McLeod's picture of the Punjab of Guru Nanak's time it has been argued that he overlooked Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Quddus Gangohi, who lived in Shahabad, his disciple Shaikh Jalal of Thaneswar, and the Qadiris, who had made the Punjab the main centre of their activities.

Modern travellers draw the Punjab to the scale of their journeys. *In Search of Shiva*, a largely self funded project, kept its research to the country around its author's hometown of Lahore, so that only parts of central Punjab are included, and southern Punjab and Sindh, where there are many such shrines, are left out. *A White Trail* was researched in central Punjab, where Hindus live in ghettos and where, in its author's perception, the conditions of Hindu life are the harshest of any region. Most of the Hindu community lives in Sindh and southern Punjab, where both lower and upper castes are proportionately represented, while in central Punjab there are hardly any high caste Hindus, so that nearly all its interviews were with low caste Hindus and do not reflect the communities where Hindus are many. Most of the Christian minority, by contrast, lives in Punjab, so its Christian material can stand for the community across the country.

The traveller's Punjab is also personal. The author of *Walking with Nanak* was born and brought up in Pakistani Punjab and knew Guru Nanak as the founder of Sikhism, a poet and a spiritual leader, yet the Muslim dominant culture of Pakistani Punjab offered him no way to explore his personality. On the road he wonders whether it is the bubble of the urban centres that is out of step with the culture of Punjab, and remarks how rarely one finds so lonely a road in the heartland of Punjab, the most prosperous province of Pakistan. At the banyan of Apal Muri visitors had left their mobile numbers, probably hoping to begin a secret love affair in the closely watched social life of rural Punjab. Yellow was commonly used for buildings in Punjab in older days, and karir, an ancient Punjabi plant, is no longer found anywhere except in abandoned places.

A novelised chapter on the Sikh capture of Hassan Abdal under Hari Singh imagines the province from its western frontier. The Sikh soldiers had arrived from the Punjab, and for almost a thousand years invaders had crossed into the Punjab from the West. Hari Singh wanted the tribesmen to know that now it was the time of Punjab to create havoc in the Khyber.

The modern ethnographers work with parts of the province: central Punjab, the southern Punjab as a Sufi master's domain, and a southern Punjab that was once part of Sindh. It has also been objected that scholars of Sindhi studies treat Sindh's religious encounters as if comparable dynamics were not at work in neighbouring regions such as Punjab, Rajasthan or Gujarat.
