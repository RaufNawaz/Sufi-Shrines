## Language, Poetry and Music

Poetry is the heart of religion in Sindh: "Sufi poetry is the backbone of Sufism in Sindh, including for the Hindu Sufis". It was the Sufis who made the spoken language of the province into a literary one. Sindh had, and still has, "a big treasure of folk poetry, ballads, and working songs", with folk tales at home in every part of the country, from the hill slopes of Balochistan and the desert of Thar Parkar to the fertile plains watered by the Indus and the shifting creeks near its mouth. Mystical poetry in the mother tongue begins in Sindh, as in the Punjab, in the fifteenth century, and in Sindh too the eighteenth century was the most important period for the making of mystical poetry and prose.

Shah Abdul Latif did not begin this tradition. Before him there was already a settled culture of Sufi poetry in the spoken language, with poets such as Shah Abdul Karim, who died in 1623, and Shah Inat Rizvi, who died in 1711, though their names tend to be overshadowed by Shah Abdul Latif's unique reputation.

The works of the Sufis of Sindh, known as *kalams*, devotional poems, "constituted the foundations of Sufi culture". Apart from the Naqshbandis, Sufism in Sindh is dominated by poetry of *wahdat al-wujud*, the unity of being, the teaching that all existence is one in God. The Hindus of Sindh went to Sufi shrines and shared their passion for Sufi poetry with Muslim Sindhis, above all through Shah Abdul Latif's *Shah jo Risalo*. The Sufis to whom the Hindus look belong mostly to Upper Sindh, where Siraiki is used for Sufi poetry besides Sindhi, and all of them belong to this *wujudi* school, leaving out the Naqshbandis. This trend seems to have grown in the nineteenth century, under colonial rule.

The province's religious culture was made in its own language. Manuscripts and early lithographed books show that it had been vernacularised, carried into the speech of the people, and the first step was usually a translation from Arabic and Persian into Sindhi. In the early eighteenth century Naqshbandi scholars from Lowari Sharif wrote treatises showing that the Muslim Sindhis did not know the Quran; they wrote paraphrases and then a Sindhi translation of it, and condemned as un-Islamic some rituals they called Hindu or Shia. Their later treatises concluded that the Sindhis, even when officially Muslim, did not practise orthodox Islam.

"Devotional literature in Sindhi thus formed the basis of a culture that transcended social, religious and ethnic divisions." It was carried to the villages not by the *ulema*, the Muslim scholars, nor by the *pandits*, the Hindu learned men, but by the caste musicians, the Manganhar or *mirasi*. The Shia devotional poetry of the *marsiya*, the lament for the martyrs of Karbala, is cherished by both Muslims and Hindus, and Hindus have been its performers as well as its authors.

The learned languages of the province were not its spoken one. The five native histories of Sindh are all in Persian, "which was the Court language of the ruling houses of the country up to the days of the British conquest". The learned men of Mughal and Kalhora Sindh were not few, but they wrote "in Persian and Arabic and not in the language of the countryside". Most of them were Sayyids, Kazis and Mullas, or men who taught the Quran, and the writers came largely from families that had immigrated into Sindh: "They were not Sindhis by birth".

Learned men had long been drawn to the province. In the days of the Afghans before the Mughals, and earlier, "Sind proved a favourite home for learned men", attracted by its fame for Sufi philosophy. The age of the Arghuns and Tarkhans was especially notable for the arrival of "this kind of intellectual aristocracy", which had "but little effect upon the unlettered cultivator class". In this, Sindh was merely typical of Muslim India generally.

Arabic and Urdu left little mark on the province's verse. Arabic has always been known and spoken in Sindh by a few learned men, and the Quran "is known by heart by large numbers of devout Muslims in the province", but Arabic's effect on the poetry of Sindh is negligible. "Urdu has never been a language which has taken kindly to Sind soil", and the literary currents of Sindh came from Persian and Arabic sources. The Sindhi language itself has been seen as suited to "a peace-loving agricultural and pastoral culture elaborated by an industrious and non-martial people". The province went on producing mystics who wrote either in Persian or in their mother tongue.

Learning reached few. Sindh has always had men called *hafiz*, one who has memorised the whole Quran, but that does not mean they knew Arabic as scholars. In Mughal days Sindh showed simply the working of the Islamic system of education, and "The great mass of the people remained illiterate". The province has never been known for learning, and in medieval times could have had few chances of keeping anything like a Muslim university.

Teaching was not a respected trade. Teachers were seldom paid more than half a rupee a month by each pupil. In the time of the Talpurs Sindh had six *madrasas*, schools of religious learning, among them those at Sehwan, at Trippat, at Matiari, at Mohar or Walhari near Umarkot, and at Chothiyari on the Nara. Sindhi was much neglected as a language of teaching, except by Hindus, who wrote it in a script other than the Arabic, and conditions were unlikely to have been better under the Mughals and the Kalhora.

Northern Sindh had a second language of verse. Siraiki is the Sindhi dialect spoken mostly in Northern Sindh, especially among the Baluch, and it is also a language of Southern Punjab. Many Sindhi Sufi poets, especially from the north and mainly those of Baloch origin, composed in several languages, Siraiki among them. The imagery of the two language areas is closely related, as one would expect among the people of mainly rural provinces, and the mystical literature of Sindh and the Punjab is closely alike, down to the motif of spinning and weaving "in a cotton-growing country". On the road between Bahawalpur and Uch "the cultures of Punjab and Sindh merge": a Punjab Hotel stands beside a Sindh Hotel, Siraiki is a blend of the two languages, and the Siraiki of Uch is closer to Sindhi.

The province's Hindus shared in this poetry. By one account the Bhakti, the devotional movement of Hindu India, reached Sindh only in the sixteenth century, because of the province's isolation, and there is no evidence that its *sants*, the poet saints, shaped the spiritual life of the province. One scholar called Shah Abdul Latif and Sachal "the late participaters in the Bhakti movement which had a major impact in Sindh through the spread of Guru Nanak's teaching". The *slokas*, short verses, of Sami were probably widespread among the Hindus of Northern Sindh.

Sachal Sarmast had many followers among the Hindu trading castes of Northern Sindh, and some of them took their love of singing his poetry to India when they migrated after Partition. For the Sindhis who left, singing Sindhi Sufi poetry keeps "a link, through language and music, with Sindh as a fatherland".

Above this tradition stands one book, the *Risalo* of Shah Abdul Latif. By 1940 it was called "the only classic which the language of Sind has yet produced in the realm of deeply imaginative literature", its poems held in universal and popular esteem and known in some form to all classes. Today it "is universally acknowledged to be the greatest classic of Sindhi literature in both Sindh itself and other parts of Pakistan, as well as among the Sindhi émigré population in India and the wider diaspora".

Hindus love it as much as Muslims do. The poetry "exercises over Hindus in Sind a spell almost as complete as that exercised by it over Muslims", and its appeal lies "deep in the hearts of all classes of Sind's population, Muslim and Hindu, lettered and unlettered". The poet's achievement came when Sindh was still medieval in outlook, and in 1940 it could be said that "the probabilities are all against Sind's producing a second Shah Abdul Latif", though his poems "still bind Sind with a powerful spell of love and admiration". His poetry is "the only gem cast up from the sea of Sind's vernacular literature".

The poem's place has been explained by the province's history. The cultural heritage of Sindh came to fruit in Shah Abdul Latif's poetry just as the Mughal Empire was showing clear signs of breaking up. The province had two peculiar circumstances: most of its people had come late to Islam, and there was no large body of native literature in Sindhi, because its learned class came mostly from the privileged, many of them immigrants rather than people of the land. Under Muslim rule from the fourteenth century on, the thought and religion of the Hindus found little place in the literary forms of the day, while the Sufi strain of thought has always appealed to Hindus in Sindh.

The stories of the poem are Hindu in origin, and most of them belong to the Sindh of the days before Islam. Apart from the tales of the Punjab and Kutch, they are stories of Lower Sindh told among the Hindu peoples on the edges of the Thar before their general conversion to Islam. The Hindus of Sindh in 1940 felt that their culture, no less than that of Islam, went into the making of the poem.

The heroines' stories are loosely set in the time before the Mughals, when Sindh was ruled by the Muslim Rajput dynasties of the Sumiros and the Samos, and other *surs*, the poem's chapters, are built on male heroes "from the heroic Rajput period of Sindhi history". These legends give the province a heroic past. Lakho Phulani was "a legendary bandit from the heroic age of Sindh, who is remembered for his fearless attacks on the ruling Jarejo Samos", and Jakhiro "a Rajput ruler of medieval Sindh famous for his bravery and generosity", the verses on whom are read as a long praise of the Prophet. In a famous episode, Jakhiro resisted the invasion of Sultan Alauddin, the Muslim emperor of Delhi, who died in 1316, and took under his protection the women of his fellow Rajputs, the Sumiros.

The great tales of the province are love stories, and its poets made them teach. The mystical poets of Sindh and the Punjab developed "the art of utilizing simple folk tales as media for mystical instruction", and in Sindh the range of tales used in this way is even wider. The first hints appear, veiled, in the verse of Qadi Qadan in the first half of the sixteenth century, grow clearer in the short verses of Shah Abdul Karim of Bulri, and come to full bloom in the eighteenth century in the *Risalo* of his great grandson Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit. Since everyone knew the tales, the poet usually begins his telling at the most dramatic moment.

The tales have their own history. The story of Lila and Chanesar goes back to the time of Jam Chanesar, one of the Summa rulers of Sindh in the fourteenth century, and the tale of Mumal and Rano began in Lower Sindh some time in the fifteenth. "Sohni is one of the favorite folk tales in both Sind and the Panjab", and the Sohni myth is sung in Sufi poetry in every region of Sindh and in the Siraiki speaking area of the Punjab.

"Sassi-Punnoo is a story of old Sind." Sassi belonged to Bhambore, an old city, and Punnoo was a Baluch chief, and the romances of Sohni and Sassi travelled beyond their first setting to become popular in both the old Punjab and Sindh. The Sassi myth is told in the lands of Sindh and Baluchistan, sung in the oral traditions with all the feeling of her grief, and performers love to sing it in the languages and dialects of Sindh, Baluchistan and the Siraiki speaking regions. Sassi's run through the desert at sunset, in Shah Abdul Latif's verse, is "a scene which can be appreciated by those who know the glowing evenings at the western border of Sind, when all of a sudden, darkness overtakes the wayfarer, and nothing is left but the sounds of a few animals".

The *Risalo* makes a world out of Sindh. It "draws upon a wide-ranging set of interlocking references to the scenery, society, and legends of Sindh to create a whole new imaginative world". In its twenty second *sur*, *Sarang*, the monsoon rains are a sign of divine power and grace, in Sindh, where they transform the normally dry countryside, and across the rest of the world. The cloud of mercy rises over the desert of Eastern Sindh, the Thar Parkar, and is seen floating from Africa to China.

After naming the places the rains reach, ending "Some have passed over Umarkot and have made the ground green", the verse asks God to make Sindh flourish forever and closes: "Dear friend and sweet beloved, make the whole world prosperous" (translated by Christopher Shackle). Coming closer to his home, the poet prays, in another rendering: "My Lord, be kind over dear Sind, / Friend! Sweet darling! You may make the whole world happy!" (translated by Annemarie Schimmel). This last blessing on Sindh is one of the most often quoted verses of the *Risalo*.

The countryside fills the poem. In the eighteenth *sur*, *Ramakali*, the yogi's horn is praised: "There is nothing like it in the north, in Sindh, or in Hind", Hind meaning the Gangetic region, as against Sindh and the Indus valley. The crane, *kūṉja*, comes to Sindh as a winter migrant, and in the *sur* *Dahar* its plaintive cry is heard as the pain of separation from the flock. Few countries are as rich as Sindh in bird life, and there the crow, *kang*, is a bringer of good news, the typical messenger bird of the province as the pigeon was in high Persian poetry.

The poem is also a picture of a time. The spinning of cotton, one of the most important occupations of women, is a frequent theme of the poetry of Sindh and the Punjab. Shah Abdul Latif "offers in his verse a vivid picture of the conditions of the Sind countryside during the early 18th century". The image of the swan must have been popular in Sindh for a long time, and Lila's swinging bed recalls the coloured lacquer work beds that are still a speciality of the province.

Other habits mark the province's verse. Sindhi girls who never learned to hold a pen or read a letter still complain, in song, that no letter has come from their beloved; the complaint is general among the Sindhis, common in wedding songs, and in the folk poetry of Sindh the heroines always make it. In the Golden Alphabets, poems that run through the letters in order, words with the same letters were joined in one verse, since alliteration was a favourite device, particularly in Sindh. The mystical bards of Sindh and the Punjab knew the tradition of *Ahmad bila mim*, Ahmad without the letter *m*.

An Arabic saying was very popular in eighteenth century Sindh: "Affliction is for saintliness the same as are flames for the gold". Even the poetry of Shah Abdul Latif, in the remote province of Sindh, calls the reader to admire Iblis, Satan, as the one true lover.

The Prophet is a theme of the province's poets. Poets in the plains of Sindh and the Punjab wrote tender little poems or instructive Golden Alphabets to fill the villagers with love for him, and "The great mystical poets of Sind have devoted wonderful songs to the Prophet, who appears as the dawn between the night of created human life and the Divine day". *Virahini* poetry, in which the devotee is a young bride waiting for her bridegroom, who is the Prophet Muhammad, is sung in Sindhi devotional settings such as the *mauluds*, gatherings that celebrate the Prophet's birth.

The poet names the province in lament as well as in prayer. In the lament of Marui he says that Marui is no more in Malir, nor Hamir; that "Punhun is no longer in Kech", nor is there an Amir in Sindh; and that there is no Pir in the convents. The singer Alan Faqir recited the line "Punnu is not in the Kec / Sind is not the amir", and read it politically: Sindh does not lead the land, it is not in the position of leadership or amirship.

Under British rule the poem gained a scholarship and a place on the syllabus. In the days of the Kalhora and the Talpurs the poems lived as sung verse, and only after the settled days of British rule was there born "that type of literary curiosity which calls forth books of comment and criticism". Writing on the poet grew largely out of English education in the eighty years before 1940, Trumpp working on it when Bartle Frere was Commissioner in Sindh, and the great growth of general education under British rule spurred the study of the poems.

After 1843 Persian was quite quickly replaced as the language of schooling, government and elite literature by English and by Sindhi, whose growth the colonial authorities in Bombay actively encouraged. The *Risalo*, a classic that had always appealed to every section of the Sindhi people, "was pressed into service for the examinations prescribed by the new education syllabus". Because Sindh was governed from Bombay rather than Calcutta, Urdu was not installed as Persian's immediate successor. Of Sindhi's many dialects, that of Hyderabad became the standard, it is said, when in 1853 the British asked the scholars of Hyderabad to write the textbooks for the whole of Sindh. Colonial Sindh also saw an attempt to impose a script of its own, the Khudawadi.

Print and the colonial state remade this heritage. A printing press was brought to Karachi in the 1850s, but the great event for Sufism was the printing of the *Shah jo Risalo* in 1866, followed in the 1890s by a reordering of the poetry begun by Hindu men of letters. Hindu Sindhis led the publishing of Sufi books in the late nineteenth century, and the Hindus of Sindh played a key part in handing down the Sufi legacy up to Partition.

For most Sindhi intellectuals before Partition, Hindu or Muslim, the Theosophical Society, a modern religious movement, gave the frame in which the Sufi legacy of Sindh was read anew. In the late nineteenth century that legacy was built on the *wujudi* teaching, recast in Theosophical terms through its likeness to *advaita vedanta*, the Hindu teaching that reality is one. The lectures of the Reverend Bambridge in Karachi led to a revival of religious and theological studies in Sindh, and Annie Besant was known in the province as Basanti Devi.

Shah Abdul Latif stands at the centre of the province's modern image of itself. Accounts of his dates differ by a year, placing his birth in 1689 or 1690 and his death in 1751 or 1752. Sindhi nationalist narratives are built around the "Sindhi language, the poetry of [national poet] Shah Abdul Latif, the Indus, the peasant or hari, and above all, Sufism and mysticism", with the link to Mohenjo-Daro besides. G. M. Syed is held chiefly responsible for making Shah Abdul Latif the most important poet of Sindh.

A Sindhi writer, Abdul Qadir Mangi, put it plainly: "Every Sindhi, of whatever faith he may be, believes in the greatness of Bhitai. From a Kohli Bheel to a Syed, every one considers Bhitai his spiritual guide". Sindhi nationalists see Shah Abdul Latif as the ideal Sindhi, the perfect example of the province's tolerant and mixed history, while more orthodox writers try to explain away his journey as the recklessness of youth.

Music was everywhere, and it came early. The *Ain-i-Akbari* describes the music of Sindh as *kami*, amatory. Whatever the reason the Chishti order was absent from the province, "music appeared very early in the Sufism of Sindh", and Shah Abdul Latif's poetry shows how much music mattered to him and to the region, and the role of the musician in its society. The Naqshbandis stand apart: most Naqshbandis in Sindh are strongly opposed to music and to the singing of devotional songs.

"Most of the musicians in Sind have been Hindus and not Mussulmans", one reason, it has been thought, why the poetry holds so strong a spell over those of the land who are not Muslims. There is nothing distinctive in the music of Sindh, whose instruments are chiefly the *ektar*, the sitar, the *sarangi*, the *tambur* and the pipes and drums used elsewhere in India, yet "Sind has long had a reputation for proficiency in musical execution prevailing amongst all classes". As Crowe put it, "The Sindians are excessively fond of singing".

The poetry lives in its singing. In remote corners of Sindh illiterate folk musicians repeat the name of the mystic Hallaj in ecstatic songs and claim "that he was the true lover who paid with his life for his love". The moving mystical songs of Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit and the ardent poems of Sachal Sarmast, who died in 1826, are almost as popular today as they were a hundred and fifty years ago. "Even to this day, most Punjabis and Sindhis are more likely to have heard a kafi than read one", the *kafi* being a short sung lyric.

Shah Abdul Latif and other Sufi poets of the region composed in the spoken languages and dialects and sang them as roving mendicants within an Islamic frame. "For fear of the clergy mystic poetry sung to music remained confined to the minstrel class." *Sufiana kalam*, Sufi poetry sung to music, is usually sung in the spoken language and is a feature of the shrines of Sindh, especially those of Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar and of Shah Abdul Latif at Bhit. Because large parts of the Quran are quoted in the *Risalo*, musicians in Sindh treat the written book with the utmost veneration and never let it lie on the floor.

"In Sind there can be no ritual without the Manganhar, be it a birth, a wedding, or a death. They are the designated community musicians, and each community has its own Manganhars." The Manganhar are hereditary professional musicians of Sindh and Rajasthan, and a woman musician there is called *mangiari* or *mangti*. The Sidis, of African descent, and the Manganhar sing the ceremonial devotional narratives. There were roving minstrels too in the lands of Sindh and Rajasthan, among them the *jogi* snake charmers and flute players.

The musicians have their own forms. Choir singing in Sindh is called *sung*, and speech and song together create ecstasy, a very special setting of some of the province's shrines. In one school of Sindhi Sufi poetry, also called *sung*, the lover sings to win back the estranged beloved, which is called *manana*. The singer Abida Parvin uses the *dohra* or *bait*, a couplet, within her narrative, a style she learned from her male teachers, and it has been claimed that no other woman musician in Sindh has this style, "which is essentially a unique male style".

Women sing too, often for women. In the shrines of Sindh women singers and musicians are sometimes called the *faqiriani*. The Sidi, Mohana and Manganhar women attached to the landed households of Sindh sing devotional Sufi poetry at their mentor's shrine only on special occasions such as his *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of a saint's death; there they perform only before women, and their women patrons pay them well. These musicians sing to women only in the Sayyid households of Sindh, and Sidi women give ritual musical service in the province's households. The singer Taj Mastani deals with television stations and with clients from the wealthy landed households of Sindh, who call on her for ritual ceremonies. In the Siraiki belt around Multan, Bahawalpur, Mianwali and northern Sindh, women's voices are again prominent in the Sufi poetry of the oral traditions.

The province belongs to a wider world of song. "The mystical lore of the region that includes Sind, Baluchistan, and the coastal belt of Iran shares myths, melodies, and linguistic codes", among them the famous *Mast Qalandar dhun*, a tune sung all along the lands from Rajasthan through Sindh and Baluchistan to Iran: "Almost every folksinger in the region can sing it". The Indus and the Baluchistan coast, back through Persia, seem to have been a route used for centuries.

The province has its own ceremonial music. *Gic* are songs usually sung by women at births and weddings; *jamalo* is a folk tune and also a dance; *kapati* are the songs of the cotton spinners; *olang* is a drum rhythm played at the bridegroom's home; and *sadmana* is a melody for ceremonial occasions. *Candar* is both a Sindhi melody for happy occasions and the wooden castanets that keep the rhythm in Sindhi folk music. *Haswari* is a ceremonial tune played on drums and bagpipes, which Sindhi musicians say they play for the *pirs*, spiritual masters, and *murshids*, spiritual guides, when they appear at festive ceremonies such as a wedding, and *laon* is a Sindhi wedding song. *Dhammal* is the vigorous ritual dance performed at Sufi shrines in Punjab and Sindh.

## Shrines and the State

For centuries religion and government were bound together in Sindh. Since the time of the Sumiros in the thirteenth century "the land has been predominantly Muslim in population and the government had a strong theocratic basis with much virtual power in the hands of the priestly class of Sayids". Saints and rulers were tied to each other from early on: Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya supported Iltutmish's annexation, governors sought the help of Makhdum-i Jahaniyan, and a ruler of Sindh became the disciple of Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus.

The Kalhora deferred to the religious class, and this "led to the extraordinary reverence for pirs, Sayids and holy men, which may be said to have been the predominant characteristic of eighteenth century Sind". By one estimate the expense of religion took a third of the gross revenue. Under the Kalhora the true Sindhis were exploited and the position of the Hindus grew worse, the only form, it has been judged, that a state so nearly theocratic could have taken.

The saints were both a treasure of the state and an instrument of it. When Nadir Shah invaded in 1739, Diwan Gidumal, the minister of the Kalhora princes, offered him two bags which, he said, "contain the most valuable of Sind's gifts; they contain the holy dust from the tombs of numerous saints and Pirs of Sind". The story is told as a mark of the country's pride in its mystical heritage. In the nineteenth century Burton saw Sufism in Sindh as a political power: *tasawwuf*, Sufism, "under the native governments was as formidable a political engine as most of the secret confraternities recorded in history". Even among the mild Sindhis a noted Pir might once safely order a disciple to murder an enemy, "Yet the native princes encouraged it partly from superstition and partly because the price of every Pir was well known to them".

The law was the law of a Muslim state. "Sind was a Muslim state and in theory justice was administered according to the tenets of the Koran", though in Mughal and Kalhora days every man was to some degree the defender of his own rights and the police were sadly too few. The collective responsibility of the village, as officers described it in the nineteenth century, may well have held in Mughal and Kalhora days too. Apart from cattle stealing and the wildness of the Baluch tribes, crime was comparatively rare, though the country held many expert thieves and equally expert trackers. Trial by ordeal was practised, and under the Talpurs there were no regular civil courts. As the state religion of Sindh came from the Quran, so did its law; Hindus settled their disputes "by panchayats", councils of elders, and the Baluch seemed generally to take the law into their own hands.

Learning, too, lived on religious property. No school in Sindh appears to have owed its founding to any act of an emperor of Delhi. The chief support of the learned came from revenues granted as *inams*, grants of land or revenue, to mosques and to the *maktabs*, elementary schools, attached to them.

Under British rule the *pirs* became landlords in the eyes of the state. British policy, "especially in the Punjab and Sind, was to treat them in essentially the same way that landlords and tribal leaders were treated", meaning the hereditary saints, the *sajjada nishin*, the keepers of the shrines. The cult of holy men is most evident in the countryside, where great landlords and large holdings of land are crucial, especially in Punjab and Sindh, and there "the local pîr represents the local landlord or is at least closely related to him".

Under indirect rule, Sufis were usually content to keep the peace so long as they could continue their religious routines and keep their estates and authority. The exceptions were the two Hur rebellions against the British in Sindh, raised by the followers of the Pir Pagaro, a landlord, political leader and hereditary head of a Sufi brotherhood who still holds great authority in Pakistan today. The *hurr*, the Free, the loyal and absolutely obedient disciples of the Pir Pagaro, were first gathered to support Ahmad Brelwi's struggle against the Sikhs between 1829 and 1831 and later took an active part in the struggle against the British. "The hurr even played a minor role in the Indian-Pakistani war in 1971", and this was not the first time that mystical leaders in Sindh had taken part in politics or social reform.

The tie between land and shrine reaches the lives of musicians and *faqirs*, holy mendicants. A *vadera* is a landed lord in Sindh, and the singer Alan Faqir said that the *vaderas* quarrel with one another and come to the *faqir* for a prayer. *Saeen* is a title used for a landed lord or a spiritual leader from lower Punjab and Sindh, where the culture of the shrines thrives.

The wealth of the shrines has drawn criticism and reform. Most people continued, and many still continue, to venerate their saints, as hundreds of simple folk songs and popular verses show. The exaggerated veneration of a saint's tomb has been the target of some of the best modern Sindhi short stories, and the gap between the wealth of the shrines and their guardians and the poverty of the people is a powerful theme of social and religious criticism, in Sindh as elsewhere in the Muslim world. In Sindh as in Bengal, the Naqshbandiyya and some more militant groups of mystical inspiration tried to curb the overflowing emotion of the folk songs.

In the present the reformers come from outside the shrines. Tableeghi preachers "focus on southern Punjab and rural Sindh where syncretistic religion held sway", and in recent years the Deoband school has turned its attention to South Punjab and Sindh, "where there is an ubiquitous saint and shrine culture".

Partition separated the keepers from many of their followers. "The darbars in Sindh were most of the time preserved with different arrangements regarding their management", often with *gaddi nashins*, the seated heads of a line, or followers in India paying the keepers, and despite the changing relations between Pakistan and India it was always possible to cross the border. Yet one Hindu Sindhi Sufi, Molchand, worried after Partition that it had become hard for Hindu Sindhi Sufis to visit the Sufi places of Sindh, now in Pakistan. In Sindh the groups of Sufis and Nanakpanthis, followers of Guru Nanak's path, had no central body ruling over all their followers.

The Pakistani state has shaped which saints the province celebrates. The state's Sindhi publications hardly mentioned the saint or the shrine of Sehwan until the late 1960s, while "other saints of Sindh (especially Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai, Sachal Sarmast, and Shah Inayat) were regularly featured during this time, and their annual fairs were marked by special editions". Only from the 1960s onward did Urdu and Sindhi newspapers report the annual fair of Sehwan in detail. The publications were two prominent state journals, *Nayin Zindagi*, published by the Information Department of Sindh, and *Mehran*, published by the Sindhi Adabi Board. Today state bodies such as the Department of Culture and the Sindhi Adabi Board routinely publish books and brochures on the life of the saint of Sehwan and on Sehwan's history and heritage.

The Sufi Shaykh Wahid Bakhsh, who died in 1995, warned against nationalist slogans, among them "Sindhu Desh", the country of the ethnic Sindhis: "They do not realize that four smaller countries, which they wish to create, will be swallowed by India overnight". In his view the divisions of ethnic rivalry at home were as destructive as any enemy from outside.

The modern state also shows in representation and money. The Sindh province "has 9 minority seats out of 168". Government money for the places of minority communities shows in a wave of temple restorations across Sindh, many temples having been renovated within a decade, at the same time as changes in the political landscape. Violence against a temple could be settled outside the state: in one of the rumours about the attack on a temple at Larkana in March 2014, "According to tradition in Sindh, the culprits took the Quran and asked the Hindu family for remission". The instability of the country and "the feudal environment in Sindh" are thought to have kept scholars away from the province.

The threat to the province's shared culture is growing. The Hindus of Pakistan, Sindh included, are, it seems, more and more under threat from the country's radical Muslims, and Islamist terrorism has spread in Sindh as elsewhere in Pakistan; yet "the Sufi culture still pervades Sindhi society". Sufism does not of itself mean tolerance. It has been argued that "the most active people working for the conversion of young Hindu girls to Islam are well-known Sufi pirs", and "the Sufi dargahs are now targets of Islamist terrorism", a *dargah* being a saint's shrine.

## The Province in Books

Under the Mughals, at the end of the seventeenth century, the province was the *suba*, or imperial province, of Thatta. It measured two hundred and fifty *kos*, a measure of distance, in length, from Bhakkar to Kech Makran, and one hundred *kos* in breadth, from the town of Badin to the port of Lahari. It was bounded on the east by Gujarat Ahmadabad, on the west by Kech Makran, on the north by Bhakkar and on the south by the salt sea. Four *sarkars*, districts, made up the *suba*, Thatta, Siwistan, Nasirpur and Umarkot, with fifty seven *mahals*, revenue divisions, and five ports. In this country a third share of the crop was taken from the cultivator.

The province lay at the edge of Hindustan. The realm of Hindustan then adjoined Bengal to the east and the Deccan to the south, and on the west Thatta reached the salt sea. Kutch was a separate country, two hundred and fifty *kos* in length and breadth, with Sindh to its west. The *wilayat*, or country, of Sind known as Thatta had, like Gujarat Ahmadabad, passed at various times out of the hands of Delhi's rulers and come under an independent ruler of its own, and a history of those rulers, the *Tarikh-i Bahadurshahi*, covered them both.

The province lived on its river. The greatest river was the Sind. Merchants from Multan and Bhakkar shipped their goods down it to Thatta, travellers and even heavy armies went by water, and large armies seldom went by land because of the jungle and the want of water. Most things turned on the boat, and there must have been forty thousand boats, large and small.

The land was rich. Salt and iron mines were plentiful, and there was a quarry of yellow stone that could be cut to any size for building. There were wild ass, hare, hog deer, pig and fish, and the people lived on rice, curds and fish. Dried fish was shipped to the ports, and one fish came up from the salt sea into the Sind, was caught in nets, and was found in no other country. Curds kept for four months, there were flowers and many fruits, the mango above all, and small melons grew in the desert.

Much was said of the province's liver eaters, the *jigar khwara*, more famous here than anywhere around. By a spell, it was said, they took the livers of people, especially of children and especially at mealtimes; some said a trance came over them; they shared the liver with their fellows, and the victim died. They made others like themselves by feeding them a piece and teaching them the spell, and they rode the hyena and brought news from far away. When one was caught, those who knew the art cut open the calf of the leg and took out a seed like a pomegranate seed, which was fed to the victim, who recovered.

A liver eater, it was said, would not sink with a millstone tied to the neck, nor burn in fire. To cure one, the experts branded both temples, filled the eyes with salt, hung the person up in a house for forty days, gave food without salt and recited spells. Most of them were women, some were men, and they were found in every land, but more in the lands of Thatta.

In 1940 a book on Shah Abdul Latif was dedicated "TO THE PEOPLE OF SIND" with a verse on a peace that followed an age of war: "The drums of peacefulness are struck", runs one line, and the next, "Your land a full abundance yields". To its author, eighteenth century Sindh was a survival of an older world. When the poet's verses were composed, "Islam in Sind preserved the true native form of its characteristic power", and the ideas of popular thought went much deeper into the Muslim mind than in the Sindh of 1940, when that rule over the land had been lost. The Muslim India of the Middle Ages, "of which eighteenth century Sind was a relic", recalled medieval Christian Europe in some of its features.

The Sindh from which the poetry sprang was, in this view, more like a "Merrie England" of villages that sufficed for themselves: "It was a society of landholders, petty cultivators, herdsmen, craftsmen and traders, knowing little of the outside world except what could be seen of it at markets and fairs, in the gatherings of strangers at tombs on saints' days or in the company of fellow pilgrims". In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries Sindh "was a land of cultivators and herdsmen, keepers of camels, buffaloes, sheep, goats and oxen, of tombs and Sayids, of dust and heat, of the annual rise and fall of the waters of the Indus but inefficiently controlled by a crude system of irrigation". Of the poem's rustic scenes, "No one familiar with Sind will dispute how true a portrayal this is of a simple rustic civilization of husbandmen and cattle herds", and the poems are loved by all classes in Sindh.

The same author argued with the nineteenth century writers, holding that "many of the criticisms of Victorian writers are unjust". Burton despised Sindh for what he thought its lax Islam, and his view that Sindh came into British hands almost exhausted cannot be accepted "without considerable qualification". Against such critics stands the assistant surgeon Heddle, in May 1836: "The large masses of the indigenous population are particularly industrious", the merchants of Sindh are active and intelligent, and "The people are orderly and obedient and the laws are respected". "The peoples of Sind, whether indigenous or immigrant, have never shown any military genius", ran the verdict of 1940, and "The people as a whole have always been peaceful and industrious". Lieutenant James, on the other hand, reporting in 1847 on the Chandookah *pargana*, a subdivision, saw Sindh as one long battlefield, the scene of usurpation, tyranny and bloodshed, "till finally the British bayonet gave the worn-out country peace".

The word itself is not always the province. Baluch poetry speaks often of Sindh, but the word there means not the modern political division of that name but the valley of the Indus, though some places it names are indeed Sindhi places in the country south of Multan that runs far down the river towards the sea. One Baluch bard sings that the drunkards "are the young men of the Sindh country", since "there is much water and bhang is cheap". Bayezid's master is said to have been Abu ʿAli as-Sindi, "from Sind", but the name may point to the lower Indus valley or to a village called Sind close to Bistam; and even in the first case, "not every man from Sind could be expected to know all the intricacies of Hindu monistic philosophy".

Above all, Sindh has been seen as a mystical country. The Arabs conquered it in 711, "and this province never ceased to be a center of Islamic studies and a seat of Islamic mysticism". "Sind has always been praised as the country with a particularly strong inclination towards mysticism", and a Sindhi author, J. Parsram, wrote: "Sind has been flattened and has been in a state of negation. This is the cause of the many disadvantages from which it suffers; but it has been also the cause of a great blessing", the blessing being the mystical tradition, something of which India is in need. The country had always been proud of its mystical heritage.

One portrait of the province ends in its light: "the villages in Sind are covered with a veil of golden dust; the walls, built of unbaked clay, look like bronze, and the blue and white tiles which cover a lonely saint's tomb match the purity of the blue sky", and the ways of Dard and Shah Abdul Latif are "as different as are the sunsets in their native provinces". Other voices were harsher. Trumpp, introducing the *sur* *Sorathi*, wrote of "how much even the strong monotheistic deism of Islam has become modified and corrupted in Sind, as in other parts of India, by its contact with Hinduism", and the province's veneration of Sayyids and its growth of superstitious practice have also drawn censure.

Later writers of the province saw it as a meeting of civilisations. Jethmal Parsram, in *Sind and its Sufis* of 1924, summed up the many religions that had flourished in Sindh, Buddhism, Jainism and Hinduism, to which Sikhism and Sufism had more recently been added, and wrote that "there is hardly a country in the whole of Asia, including India, in which the mystic thought of two great civilizations, the Indian and the Arabic-Iranian, is seen as in so beautiful a union as in Sind". Ram Panjwani was already sure, while still in Sindh, "that Sufism was the core of Sindhi culture, a culture shared by all creeds and social milieus". He set out to acquaint the Sindhis "with their past and the glorious cultural heritage of Sind which was inhabited by their forefathers", his Sindh was "a land of the mystic poets, who are humanists as well", and by the religious culture of Sindh he meant the spirit of Sufism.

Sindh, it has been argued, "hosted a specific shared religious culture resulting from a process of vernacularization", though some of its features are found in neighbouring provinces too. Its Sufi culture was the most shared product of a society that was still highly fragmented. One explanation finds the cause in Sindh's place as a frontier, with a constant flow of migrants, far from the main imperial centres, and as both an isolated land and a buffer zone Sindh "has hosted many unorthodox versions of normative religions, especially Hinduism and Islam". British rule had a double effect: it made Sufism into an object, "enthroning it as the fundamental of Sindhi culture at large", as in most regions of India, and it renewed religion through a mixed middle class. Yet for centuries the province had drawn a kind of religion of its own based on devotion, which put faith beyond formal belonging to any one institutional religion.

The same idea has a nationalist form. In nationalist writing "on both sides of the border", Sindh is presented "as a distinct entity lacking any cultural links to other provinces or the state of Pakistan generally". K. R. Malkani, a Sindhi Hindu who left Hyderabad for New Delhi after Partition, wrote: "Sindh has always been there, Pakistan is a passing show", and "A Sufi Sindh and an Islamic Pakistan cannot coexist". Nationalist writers also blame British rule for wronging Sindh. Hussain Soomro writes that the province was once so prosperous that it was said to flow with milk and honey, and in such narratives Pakistan and the British become "the culprits in the case of lost harmony and affluence in Sindh". Sindhi nationalism ties Hinglaj itself to a real Sindh that prospered before outsiders began to suppress it, and makes the shrine a hallmark of the province's golden past.

The nationalist case joins the province to Sufism. G. M. Syed, a politician who spent many years in prison for his separatist views, "directly linked the province of Sindh with a tradition of Sufism", found Sufism in its purest form there, and took as proof the shrines of Sufi saints that are "to this day, visited by Hindus and Muslims alike". In this reading, "both Sindhi Hindus and Muslims were influenced by mysticism and religious tolerance. This was the Sindhi essence that both Hindus and Muslims shared". Ishtiaq Ansari ties Mohenjo-Daro directly to today's Sindhis, claims that the Veda was composed on the shores of the Indus, and holds that Shiva and the Mother Goddess "were already known and honored in the ancient civilization of Sindh". Badar Abro defines the real Sindh, *asal sindh*, as the valleys between Rohri and eastern Baluchistan where the Sindhis once lived in prosperity, and argues that because Sindh stayed under the sway of Sufi mysticism the area is, in his English word, secular, so that its religious places belong to the shared heritage of that real Sindh. Khadim Soomro writes: "A Sindhi has a special place in human civilization because he stands for good and liberal attitude and behavior".

This picture has its limits. The common belief among Sindhis today "that in the history of Sindh, there is no religious tension between the two communities" has been called "merely a nationalist utopian ideal". The fifteenth *sur* of the *Risalo*, *Marui*, allows a nativist reading that exalts the authenticity of the values and landscape of Sindh, but a caution has been sounded against seeing Shah Abdul Latif "too narrowly" as "an authentic spokesman of the Sindhi folk tradition or as an advocate of Sindhi nationalism".

The province also belongs to a larger cultural region: "the Sind-Rajasthan continuum", and the lands that include Sindh, Baluchistan and the coastal belt of Iran, to which the Sassi myth belongs. The singer Alan Faqir spoke of Sindh as a land invaded again and again, when the Mongols, the Tartars and the Sassanids came, looted, plundered and assaulted, which yet goes on and will not die: "Sindhri is beaten", he said, and yet Sindhri goes on, Sindhri being a name for Sindh in the poetic traditions. From Manora one looks out at "the Makran mountain range, the boundary line between Sindh and Baluchistan".
