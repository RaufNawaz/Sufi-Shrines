## Saints and Pilgrimage

The Punjab is first of all a land of saints' tombs. Saints came into it, their orders spread across it, and people came to their shrines. A saint of the province is one whose tomb lies in its soil; of the Naqshbandi line, none of the elders before Khwaja Baqi Billah of Delhi has a tomb in the land of Punjab.

The Sufi settlement of the province began with the Ghaznavid conquest. When Mahmud of Ghazna annexed the Punjab and made it part of his empire, many Sufis were prompted to settle there. Khattali ordered his disciple Shaikh Husain Zinjani to move to the Punjab, and later sent Hujwiri after him; Hujwiri reached Lahore in about 1035, while Sultan Masud I reigned in Ghazna. Tradition also holds that in the time of Sultan Masud of Ghazni, Shaikh Muhammad Ismail Muhaddith Lahori, a Bukhari *sayyid*, a descendant of the Prophet, settled in Lahore and taught *hadith*, the sayings of the Prophet, and *tafsir*, the commentary on the Quran. He is said to have been the first of the callers to Islam to come to Lahore; thousands became Muslims at his hands, and through him Islam spread in Punjab.

Women too have their place in the tradition of how Islam came to the province. It is said that the Bibi Pak Daman, whose shrine is in Lahore, involved the men in spreading Islam among the people of the Punjab who were not Muslim, the women speaking to the male scholars and their audiences from behind the veil, and that seven hundred male scholars came with her to Hind, as the land was then known. In the year 615 AH the Mongols invaded the Punjab in pursuit of Sultan Jalal al-Din Khwarazmi, and the people of Lahore were put to death for resisting them. That invasion is the setting of the tradition that the Bibis of Bibi Pak Daman prayed to be taken into the earth.

The family of Baba Farid came from Kabul and migrated to the Punjab in the middle of the twelfth century, and it was the Ghaznavid Sultan of the Punjab who made Farid's father the Qazi, or judge, of Kahtwal. Baba Farid's settling at Ajudhan was the religious side of a very slow change in the Punjab's cities, from a Hindu to a Turkish and Islamic orientation. In the time of Nizamuddin, Sufism became a mass movement in north western India, and a number of Punjabi tribes still claim to have been converted by Fariduddin Ganj-i Shakar. In his invocation the poet Waris Shah sang: "When Shakarganj made his abode at Pakpattan, the Punjab was delivered of all its troubles." It is a poet's praise rather than history, for the Punjab had plenty of troubles even after Baba Farid made it his home and his place of burial.

From the fourteenth century through the Mughal period, the shrines of the great Sufis of Punjab and Sind drew pastoral clans, who had not yet taken up farming and were not Muslim, into their ritual, social, economic and political orbit. The descendants of the Sufis married into the leading families of the clans, as the clans passed from pastoral nomadism to settled wheat farming. The writing of amulets for the people, *ta'widh*, has not changed from the thirteenth century to the present: the successors of Baba Farid and their assistants, like those of other shrines in the Punjab, still write them for the masses.

The shrine stood between the people and God. As a colonial officer wrote in 1911 of the Muslim saints of the western Punjab, in words equally true of the medieval shrines: "The general idea of our riverain folk seems to be that the Deity is a busy person, and that his hall of audience is of limited capacity", and "Thus, all human beings require an intervener between them and God". In this way the shrine helped to draw Heaven and Earth together in the minds of the Muslims of rural Punjab.

Wanderers passed through the province too. In the age of Mongol rule over Central Asia and Iran, wandering dervishes, the *qalandars* of the Qalandariyya and the Jawaliqis, passed through Multan, where they often came into conflict with Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya. From the Punjab the displeased qalandars moved on to Delhi, making scenes wherever they saw a *khanqah*, a Sufi lodge, and some went as far east as Bengal. In the time of Makhdum-i Jahaniyan the Suhrawardi order spread from Uch to Gujarat, the Punjab and Kashmir, reaching even Delhi.

The Chishti order spread in Hind and Punjab from Khwaja Muin al-Din Hasan Sijzi of Ajmer. It is dominant in Punjab and North India, though it was never widespread in Sindh, and it is usually counted one of the most powerful Sufi orders of Northern India and Punjab. The Punjab is one of five regions, with Gujarat, Bengal, Uttar Pradesh and the Deccan, where the early saints, and the Chishti masters above all, carried a spiritual authority that was at once political, religious and cultural, and where a spectrum of spiritually charged sites became places of devotion for Muslims of several classes, and for Hindus too.

The Qadiri line came to the province from Baghdad, which its ancestors had left in the time of Hulagu Khan. The first of the line to come to Punjab was Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus Uchchi Jili Gilani, who travelled by way of Khurasan to Multan and settled at Uch; he is said to have entered Uch in 887 AH. By Guru Nanak's day the Qadiris had made the Punjab the main centre of their work. The Naushahi order also flourished in Punjab, where its faqirs, the wandering Sufis, number in the thousands; it began with Shaikh Haji Muhammad Qadiri, known as Hazrat Naushah Ganj Bakhsh. The province drew learned men from afar as well. In Shah Jahan's reign Abul Khair came from Baghdad touring the countries, and because the climate of the Punjab pleased him he settled in Lahore, where his *madrasa*, a school of religious learning, stood at Garhi Shahu.

The Chishtis rose again in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. The Nizami Chishtis came to prominence in the eighteenth century, chiefly in Delhi, the Deccan and Punjab, through masters who were related to local political leaders and yet in tension with them. Khwaja Sulayman Taunsawi, born in 1184 AH, who died in 1267 AH, in 1850, made the order strong by stressing learning and founding institutions: the academy at Taunsa on the upper Indus, and other religious schools throughout the Punjab. It was above all through him that the Chishti Nizami Fakhri line spread in Punjab. Muhammad 'Aqil and Gul Muhammad Ahmadpuri did the same, founding religious schools in the southern Punjab that gave free teaching and lodging to hundreds of students. The line of Shah Mirra came to light again in the nineteenth century in Hafiz Sayyid Muhammad 'Ali Khayrabadi, as the Nizami branch spread through the disciples of Shaikh Sulayman Taunsawi in the upper Punjab.

Smaller orders had small followings. In the nineteenth century the Rasul Shahi order had only four faqirs of the line living, and across the whole Punjab it had hardly any house, *takia*, a faqir's resting place, or khanqah beyond a single house and one more near Lahore. The popular Punjabi claim that the Jalali faqirs are Chihil Tani was dismissed in the same century as the talk of the ignorant. Of one boy a *sajjada nashin*, the hereditary head of a shrine, foretold that he would become the *subahdar*, the governor, of *faqr*, spiritual poverty, in Punjab.

The Punjab is also the home ground of Guru Nanak's journeys as Sikh tradition tells them. At Haridwar, in an episode dated 1504, he faces west, in the direction of Punjab, which is very far away; at Prayag he is called "Nanak, the great Guru from Punjab". By the time he returned to Talwandi, it is said, there were very few in the Punjab who did not know of him and his teachings.

On his second journey Guru Nanak crossed Rajputana and the south west Punjab until he came to Multan. He was by then a well known figure in the province, and in the story the Sufis of Multan had shown him a patronising tolerance as long as he preached elsewhere in the Punjab. His third journey, of 1514 to 1516, went as far as Tashkent and then back to Punjab by way of the Kashmir Valley. The fourth closed, around the time of Babur's third incursion into India, with a route back to the Punjab from the south east. The fifth, of 1523 to 1524, lasted about a year and covered the towns and regions within Punjab.

The death of a saint could move the whole province. When Hazrat Husain died, there was hardly anyone in Punjab who did not grieve, and in the mourning a clamour like that of Karbala rose across the land. Shah Hussein still commands great reverence in Punjab more than four hundred years after his death.

The beliefs and devotions of the countryside are part of the same world. In popular belief in the Punjab countryside, *pirs*, Sufi masters, *fakirs* and *jogis*, Hindu ascetics, can by the force of their piety make the Moving Finger of Destiny cancel what it has written. Lighting lamps in a mosque, even with ordinary oil, is still a well known way of thanking God for His favours, and Punjabi mothers still promise to light lamps in mosques when they pray for their children. *Bhang*, a preparation of cannabis, is a favourite with the mystically inclined, especially among Muslims and Sikhs, and is still taken in the old Punjab area by wandering mendicants and by many who seek spiritual exaltation or sexual pleasure. A dawn at the mosque, as the poem of Hir and Ranjha describes it, is how a morning begins even now in a Punjab village.

Not every holy place in the Punjab belongs to a named saint. Countless shrines to unknown saints stand on the outskirts of Punjabi villages, and their very anonymity, it has been suggested, is key to their place as mediators between the dangerous forces of nature and cultural continuity. Khidr is often called Zinda pir, the living saint, in Punjab, because he too is thought to have disappeared alive. At the shrines of Punjab and Sind devotees perform the *dhammal*, a vigorous ritual dance.

A Sufi master's domain could be drawn on the map of the province in the twentieth century. In his last will and testament Shahidullah Faridi wrote that Wahid Bakhsh, whose *khilafat*, his standing as the master's deputy, was publicly confirmed in 1975, had begun *halqa zikr*, the circle of remembrance of God, and spiritual guidance in Bahawalpur, and should spread the *silsila*, the Sufi lineage, in that place only. The will names only Bahawalpur, but it has been read as marking out the southern Punjab as Wahid Bakhsh's personal spiritual domain, his *wilayat*.

Today the heart of shrine devotion lies in the south of the province. Southern Punjab is believed to be the hub of shrine culture in the country, and *Saeen* is a title used for a feudal lord or a spiritual leader from lower Punjab and Sindh, where shrine culture thrives. The southern region of Punjab, where Uch Sharif lies, holds more traces of religious and cultural syncretism, the blending of traditions, than the northern and central regions, where education is more widespread and with it religious intolerance.

## Communities

The meeting of the province's faiths has been seen in very different ways. In one view, for over three hundred years Islam and Hinduism lived side by side in the Punjab in a state of constant conflict. Yet a map of South Asia's religious population shows that Islam took root much more readily in the Punjab and Bengal than in upper India, the region with the deepest traditions of Muslim rule and patronage. In both Bengal and the western wing of Punjab and Sindh, the growth of Muslim societies went together with the spread of settled farming, and both were still frontier societies where Hindu religious values and the hierarchical social ideals of the Brahmin priests had not yet sunk deep. So by the eighteenth century large communities of Muslim peasants had appeared in both the Punjab and Bengal, while in India's heartland conversion was far less significant.

The Jats stand at the centre of this story. Between the thirteenth and nineteenth centuries, as the Jat tribesmen of the western Punjab gave up pastoral nomadism for settled farming, they gradually took on an Islamic identity. The towns of the western Punjab lived by intensive cultivation along the river flood plains. Beyond them lay the *barr*, a vast and sparsely peopled tract between the five rivers, little cultivated because of the very scanty rain, where herders kept goats and camels. They moved down to the rivers in the hot dry months and back to the barr after the rains, never leaving the Punjab plains and covering at most less than a hundred miles, in a relationship of mutual need with the settled peoples of the river country.

Before they entered the Punjab the Jat herders had not yet been brought into Hindu society. By Baba Farid's time, in the 1260s, the Jats are first found living in the Punjab proper; Babur described Jat herding groups in the Sind Sagar doab, the land between two rivers, and round Sialkot; and their movement north and settlement in the grazing tracts of the Punjab is well supported by the evidence of the time. In Akbar's statistics, of the 186 Punjabi *parganas*, or revenue districts, whose dominant *zamindars*, the landholders, are known, 82, nearly half, were held by Jat groups. Only in the far west of the Punjab, in the Sind Sagar doab, where the Janjua Rajputs and the Ghakkars dominated, were the Jats conspicuously absent as zamindars.

The Jats whom al-Biruni had called low Sudras in the eleventh century had become the dominant farming caste in nearly half of the Punjab, and by the 1650s, as today, the word Jat had come to mean almost the same as peasant farmer. The change is explained by the coming of the Persian wheel, the water wheel turned by animals, which was widely used in the Punjab by Babur's time and made much of the naturally rich soil of the arid western Punjab able to grow food crops.

Conversion went with this migration. As the Jats moved north from Sindh to Punjab, rose from low Sudras to zamindars and changed from herders to farmers, they also became Muslims. Of the Jat and Rajput groups that became Muslim in the medieval period, the great majority claim to have been converted either by Baba Farid or by his contemporary Baha' al-Haqq Zakariya, who died in 1263 and whose tomb is in Multan. The Punjabi Muslim clans that claim Baba Farid include the Bhatti, Chhina, Dhudhi, Dogar, Gondal, Hans, Jo'iya, Khokhar, Siyal, Tiwana, Watt, Kharral and Ara'in. In depositions made between 1935 and 1938 before the Lahore High Court, many of the large clans that marry within themselves and are spread across the south western Punjab, mostly as peasant farmers, claimed conversion by Baba Farid, among them the Wattu, the Siyal, the Dogar, the Kathia, the Bhatti and the Tiwana.

The genealogies of the Siyals of Jhang District, which run from the late fourteenth to the early nineteenth century, show that religious conversion among Punjabi clans was very slow indeed, and probably unconscious as well. It happened long before British census officials or zealous reformers began urging Indians to place themselves in sharply separate religious categories. New Muslims from the weaver castes joined the Islamic community in Punjab somewhat later than in Hindustan, where the mystic poet Kabir shows the same blend of Sufi faith and *bhakti*, Hindu devotion, a century earlier.

The province's peoples moved and settled across it. The Mongols who made peace with Jalal al-Din Khalji became Muslims and were settled at the place that came to be called Mughalpura. Under the Langah sultans Malik Sohrab Dodai, father of Ismail Khan and Fath Khan, came with his tribe from the country of Kech and Makran, and Sultan Husain gave him a *jagir*, a grant of land revenue. Many more Baluch followed and the rest of the country along the Indus was assigned to them; little by little the land passed to them, and from those days it remained in the hands of Malik Sohrab's descendants. The Baluch who had come from Kech Makran were also given a jagir from Karor Kot to Mankot.

Famine moved people too. In the years before 1884 famine fell on Kashmir, and thousands of starving Kashmiris came into the Punjab, hundreds of them reaching Lahore itself. The famine of the land of Punjab is remembered in the life of Mai Bhagi. It came soon after her marriage at the age of seven, and was known as the two and a half *ser* famine, the *ser* being a measure of weight. The whole country was thrown into confusion, a mother would not look after her son, and Mai Bhagi and her mother left their town because of it.

Other communities moved out of the province. The Bhatia and the Khatris arrived in the eighteenth century from cities of the Punjab, Multan among them, and were in the end absorbed as Lohanas, and migrants from Rajputana and the Punjab who followed Lohana trades also became members of the Lohana caste. The Punjabi tale of Suhini and Mehar is thought to have come into Sind with the Hindu peoples who migrated there from the Punjab and form an important part of its population, as the census of 1931 for the Bombay Presidency makes clear.

Hindus and Muslims served the same state. Under the Mughals a Hindu *amir*, a nobleman, served as *diwan*, the chief revenue officer, of the *suba*, the province, of the Punjab, and when Nawab Zakariya Khan Bahadur governed Lahore the brothers Diwan Lakhpat Rai and Jaspat Rai were diwans of the government of the Punjab. At his son's wedding Lakhpat Rai gave a gift to every Hindu and every Muslim in Lahore. When Meer Manu cast the battle between the state and the insurgents as a war between Muslims and Sikhs, there were in fact several Muslims in the Sikh groups and many soldiers who were not Muslim in his own army. The Sikh groups held little land, but because they stood against the established order the lower ranks of society supported and protected them.

A plea to Ranjit Singh shows the Muslims of the Punjab under Sikh rule. Pleading with Ranjit Singh for the mosque by the Baoli, the stepwell, the Fakir sahibs told the Maharaja that nowhere in the mosques of the whole Punjab did the *mullas*, the prayer leaders, give the call to prayer aloud, and offered a bond that the mulla would not give it. As British rule began to take hold of India, Shaikh Sulayman Taunsawi urged his followers to join the resistance against Sikh aggression in the Punjab, and for apparently the first time in Chishti history the lives of the saints were drawn into taking the side of one political group. Yet Shah Sulayman also worked to keep cordial relations with the Punjabi Hindus even while he opposed their Sikh neighbours. The old ruling class kept its habits into English rule: by the 1880s silk drawstrings had gone out of use and everyone wore cotton, but the amirs of the Punjab were still used to silk ones.

In other ways the boundaries between communities were porous. Hindus shared in the Muslim orders, more visibly in Sind than in the Punjab, and some saints were claimed by both communities. Shah Abdul Latif of Sindh, like his Punjabi contemporaries and his successors in Sind, accepted Hindus who were striving on the way to perfection as true pilgrims in the eternal Light. In the village life of the old Punjab the whole community was one social unit regardless of caste or creed: some of Hir's friends, by their names, are Hindus, and even a sweeper woman would be called daughter, sister or mother. It was by no means unusual in the old Punjab for a Muslim family to depend on Hindu Brahmans to fix a marriage.

The Sikh tradition reached out from the Punjab and came back to it. The *nirguni* tradition of *bhakti*, devotion to God without form, was most influential in Northern India and especially in Punjab, where the Sikh tradition grew from it. The Aroras may have carried the cult of the Nanak Panth, a form of Sikhism that does not recognise the authority of the Khalsa, from Punjab into Sindh during their migrations in the eighteenth century. In the second half of the nineteenth century came the first attempt to make the Sikh tradition rational and uniform; centred on Punjab, it did not touch the Sindhi Nanakpanthis, who were Sikhs outside the orthodox fold.

The land's people are known by caste and clan. Ranjha is still a well known Jat caste of the old Punjab. The Khojas are a well known tribe of Hindu converts to Islam in the old Punjab, mostly traders. The Gujars, a famous tribe that keeps animals and sells their milk, gave their name to towns such as Gujranwala, Gujrat and Gujar Khan. The legend of Hir and Ranjha is set in the world of the Jat pastoral tribes of western Punjab.

Hir's own people, the Siyal, were powerful landowners of the Punjab, and one of the few tribes in the province that allow women to inherit property under customary law. The Jats of the Punjab are zamindars known for allowing widows to marry again, and among some sections of society, such as the Siyal Jats, a widow could remarry and was not forced into *satti*, burning on her husband's pyre. The Kheras, named in the Hir and Ranjha story, were another powerful clan of Punjabi landowners. The Ara'in of the Punjab were gardeners or small cultivators who worked their own land.

Marriage had customs of its own. In the old Punjab brides wore coloured and colourful clothes, while white was by tradition the dress of widows, especially among Hindus, and childless Muslim widows wore black or white; the social pattern has since changed. A traditional Punjab wedding still keeps the symbolism of marriage by capture: the bridegroom is dressed to look like a general, and the bride's side now resists only with abusive words. In the rural Punjab a girl does not see her fiancé between the *mangni*, the betrothal, and the wedding. The *arsi*, a thumb ring set with a mirror, was a favourite ornament of the bride, and a newly married girl was not expected to do housework for a couple of months, as is still the case in the less sophisticated parts of the countryside. A fringe of intertwined strands of hair round the forehead, the *mehndhi*, marked a virgin girl and disappeared at her wedding.

Other customs belong to the nineteenth century. Following the custom of the Muslims of Punjab, the bride's family at the marriage of Hazrat Naushah's daughter demanded a hundred rupees in customary dues. The killing of daughters was an old custom among the descendants of Guru Nanak through Lakhmi Chand, who counted themselves the noblest people in Punjab and would give a daughter to no one, because by the custom of that country the girl's father becomes subordinate to the boy's family.

Household and village life had its own pattern. Old houses in the Punjab countryside had a big courtyard with living rooms and bedrooms round it, and a joint family of brothers living with their parents was quite common. The *charkha*, the spinning wheel, was the constant companion of every woman, old or young, in the rural Punjab, and an important link in village society: women spun cotton yarn and the village weaver made it into cloth. Spinning parties were a famous institution of the Punjab village, where young girls, mostly unmarried, competed in spinning skill and in scandalous talk, and in a favourite game the girls threw printed red scarves with white flowers at each other.

Food and offerings follow the same pattern. *Lassi*, the drink of churned curd, is a favourite in the Punjab countryside, where washing the hair with curds is also still well known. *Gur*, raw sugar, is a well known ceremonial offering, and an invitation that comes with it cannot be taken lightly. Rice cooked in ghee and sprinkled with sugar is the usual ceremonial dish, a custom still alive in the countryside of the old Punjab, while millet is even now thought an inferior food. Basmati rice, the best in the subcontinent and now a valuable export of Pakistan, is grown in the central districts of the old Punjab, where Sawan and Bhadon are the two rainy months of the year.

Smaller customs complete the picture. Mauj Din is now a common name in the old Punjab, and jugglers with their bears are still a familiar sight. Fingers in the mouth is a typical gesture of the women, and a snub nose is not thought a mark of beauty in men or women. A mother's exaggerated threats are by no means unfamiliar in the villages even now. *Dandasa*, a bark, served as the lipstick of the rural Punjab; shaving with soap came much later, and village barbers still often shave with water alone; and the *pearha* is a piece of furniture common in Punjabi homes. The tufted turban beloved of the rural aristocracy is a recent fashion, said to have been invented in the late nineteenth century by the *mirasis*, the hereditary bards of the Punjab.

Some read the province's history in the character of its people. In this view the period of hardship after the Mughals formed the character of the region's inhabitants, and the crisis of character in the Punjabi of today can be traced to it. A satirical questionnaire by a columnist writing as Janus, "Are you a Punjabi?", makes the point: told that his ancestors came from Central Asia, the Punjabi answers, "What the hell! I am a Bokhari and superior to all the low stock inhabiting the Punjab".

Reform movements have pressed on the shrine culture of the countryside. In the early twentieth century Muhammad Ilyas, founder of the Tableeghi Jamaat, worked in undivided rural Punjab, urging Muslims to "purify" their religious practices, which he held had been corrupted by the dominant Hindu culture, and Muslim reformers like him founded movements of their own that sharpened communal tension in Punjab. Tableeghi preachers focus on southern Punjab and rural Sindh, where a blended religion held sway, and the Tableeghi Jamaat has worked its way into the social fabric of the major cities of Punjab, so that shrines like those at Kamalia and Apal Muri are likely to face a major onslaught in the years to come for their "impure" practices.

The Deobandi school has turned to the same ground. In recent years its focus has been on South Punjab and Sindh, where saints and shrines are everywhere, and the people of the South are drawn to it and feel wronged by a feudal society bound up with the culture of saints and shrines. The Deobandis also gained popularity among the lower and middle ranks of the urban Punjabi Muslims, which they have kept in Pakistan today. Organisations of the Ahl-e-Hadith school have likewise seeped into the social fabric of southern Punjab, a region that historically rejected such strands of Islam and adopted a blended religion of which the Sufi shrines are a symbol, and this is changing as they open seminaries in the countryside and draw in young men without work. They are popular with the young because they offer an explanation for their present oppression. Their first step is to target the shrines and their guardians, who in their eyes have grown immensely rich feeding off the oppressed classes.

Partition changed who lived beside whom. Local people tend to be more accepting of the practices of other faiths than the migrants who came after the division of the Punjab; several gurdwaras and temples have been taken over and destroyed by the migrants, while where the population is largely local such places are better looked after, with exceptions. Southern Punjab has been historically more tolerant than Central Punjab towards other faiths, which is why a significant Hindu population still lives there.

The province's religious minorities today live under pressure. Of Pakistan's citizens who are not Muslim, leaving aside the Ahmadiyya, the majority are Hindus living in the remote regions of Southern Punjab, inner Sindh and Baluchistan. In the Punjab itself Christians have a formidable presence and are the overwhelming majority among the minorities, while Hindus are a smaller community there, so political parties can ignore them; most of the country's Hindus are scattered in other provinces, with only a few in Punjab. In urban Punjab the religious lines between Hindus and Christians have become blurred. Many Hindus in Punjab pass as Muslims or Christians by taking names that are not Hindu, a way of surviving in a hostile place. Where minorities in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa are visible in society, Punjab is much more uniform. After the demolition of the Babri Mosque on 6 December 1992, well known Hindus in the cities of Punjab had to hide for several months.

In most villages of the province Muslims are the landowners, and Christians are confined to menial work associated with the untouchable caste, and treated as untouchables. Shanti Nagar, where Christians own the land, stands out as a unique case. Attacks on Christians came at Sangla Hill in 2005 and at Gojra and at Bahmniwala in Kasur in 2009, all in Punjab, and the story of a few burnt pages of the Quran found in local mosques seems to recur. One man who moved to Lahore in the 1980s put it this way: "There are hardly any Zoroastrians here and I have never come across any Hindu. The only Christians that you see are those who are cleaners and sweepers".

The province's Sikhs and Nanak Panthis have their own story. Nanak Panthi Sikhs belong to South Punjab and Sindh, where their temples stand, and the workers of the *langar*, the free communal kitchen, at Dera Sahib are low caste Nanak Panthis from Southern Punjab. Thousands of devotees of Guru Nanak who did not join Guru Gobind Singh's Khalsa live in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Punjab and Sindh, where they are called Nanak Panthi Hindus; in their temples a picture of Guru Nanak hangs beside pictures of Hindu deities. At the festival at Nankana in November 2011 the singers of *kirtan*, Sikh devotional hymns, covered their heads with a loose turban, a style popular in the villages of West Punjab.

Many of the province's Sikhs are Pathans who came from the tribal areas. Moving to the heart of Punjab, most of them could not even speak its language, and they stayed within their own small enclaves. The Sikhs of Nankana remember this migration from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa to Punjab, which shows how different life is in the two provinces. Not all are newcomers: Boota Singh of Nena Kot said that his people were not from the Tribal Areas but Punjabis.

The countryside has its hardships. For a child of a poor rural family in central Punjab who is seen as intersex, the most likely path leads to a life among the *khwaja-sara*, the community of transgender and intersex people. Among the poor of parts of Sindh and Punjab, unmarried girls in their teens and early twenties are the most vulnerable, facing unequal portions of food within the family and little access to health care.

The province's shared festivals belong to its farming year. The Punjab is still mostly an agricultural society, and the harvest is its greatest source of joy. Lohri, like Vaisakhi and Basant, is an ancient festival of the Punjab that marks the change of season, kept on the 13th of January, generally the last night of the month of Poh, with a village bonfire, dry fruit and folk songs, children gathering wood, and *kheer*, rice pudding, left to cool overnight. Once kept on a large scale throughout the province, it died down after the creation of Pakistan, when the country's elite gave up festivals like Vaisakhi, Lohri and Basant, and the Sikh and Hindu minorities of central Punjab stopped keeping it too. It is ironic that this Punjabi tradition, shunned by Punjab, is now being revived by a group of Pathan men living in the province.
