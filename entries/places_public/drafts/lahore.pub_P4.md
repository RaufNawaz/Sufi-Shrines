## Festivals and Practice

By the 1880s Lahore kept a crowded calendar of festivals, and Hindus and Muslims often shared it. On the twenty eighth of Shaban Hindus and Muslims together held a *mela*, a fair or festival gathering, at Shah Bilawal's *khanqah*, the Sufi lodge where disciples gather. Mian Mir had five melas a year: the *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death, which lasted two days and two nights, and four more on the four Wednesdays of Sawan, with offerings in abundance. Ghore Shah had an annual mela, and at Shaikh Musa's grave the blacksmiths, many of them pledged disciples, or *murids*, of his family, kept the yearly *ʿurs*.

Many other saints had their days. At Hamid Qari's khanqah the *ʿurs* fell in Jumada al-Thani, on the date of his death, and Shaikh Mahmud Shah Naqshbandi's tomb had its annual *ʿurs* too. Hassu Teli's fell on the third of Shawwal, Fateh Shah Mast's on the seventh of Shawwal and Shah Chiragh's on the seventeenth of Rabi us-Sani. Faqir Sain Qutb Shah Qadri's *ʿurs* came twice a year, with *qawwali*, the devotional music of the shrines, all day, and Mauj Darya's annual *ʿurs* drew a great crowd. At Shah Abu Ishaq Qadri's shrine, on the fifth of Muharram, the Quran was read all night.

Abul Muali's shrine had three melas a year besides both Eids, with illumination and dancing courtesans on the first night and *qawwali* on the second. At the *rauza*, the tomb, of Shah Abu al-Maali there were melas on both Eids, offerings and an annual *ʿurs*. Taj Shah Majzub's *ʿurs* fell on the date of his death, and it was paid for privately: its managers, Nur Muhammad and his son Bota, held the annual mela on his date at their own expense, in fulfilment of a prayer that had been granted.

At Data Ganj Bakhsh there was a mela every Friday, the *ʿurs* in Safar, the mela of the Last Wednesday and a vigil every Thursday night. Muhammad Fazil's *ʿurs* fell in Rajab and Shah Sharf's in Safar, while Shah Sarwani's was kept twice, on the ninth of Safar and the ninth of Rabi al-Awwal. Shah Yaqub Zanjani's shrine had once held a Thursday mela and had a great mela in Rajab. The Huzuri khanqah held a two day annual mela, with lamps on the first day and *qawwali* on the second, and at a Qadiri and Shattari *mazar*, a saint's tomb, Sufis gathered every year for a mela to hear *sama*, spiritual listening to music, and fell into ecstasy.

Muharram was kept in the great houses. In the Qizilbash household there was *taʿziyadari*, the mourning observance of Muharram, for the ten days, with food given out day and night to Sunni and Shia alike. On the seventh, the day of the *mehndi*, the henna procession, the *alam* bearers, who carry the standards, came to the house and each received from one to ten rupees. At Mubarak Haveli the seventh of Muharram drew the *mehndi* and *alam* processions of the whole city. Under Ranjit Singh it had been proclaimed in the city that whoever brought out a *taʿzia*, the model of a tomb carried in the Muharram procession, would lose his head.

Congregations could be large. Six or seven thousand worshippers came together for the Eid prayers at the Badshahi Masjid, and the church at Mian Mir had about eight hundred seats.

The Hindu and Sikh calendars were just as full. On every Ekadashi, the eleventh day of the lunar fortnight, one temple held a great mela: devotees gathered in numbers, bhagats sang *bhajans*, devotional hymns, all night, and offerings were made in abundance. At an Ekadashi assembly inside a *thakurdwara*, a temple of Vishnu or Krishna, *qawwals* came and delighted the listeners with songs of divine unity. At the Kali Mata *mandir*, or temple, a mela gathered on every one of the nine days of Navratri; hundreds of Hindus came to bow their foreheads, Hindus from other lands came too and were blessed, and offerings were many. On the day of Basant, the spring festival, a mela was held at the *samadh*, the memorial shrine, of Haqiqat Rai, and its offerings brought a good income.

On the night of the fourteenth of Phagan a great mela was held at a *shivala*, a temple of Shiva, beside a well, and countless people came from the city to worship. The Devi Dwara of Rajo and Dharman held two annual melas, each on an Ashtami, the eighth day of the lunar fortnight, when great crowds gathered and offerings were abundant, and through the year it kept vigils at which the bhagats sang the hymns of the Devi, the goddess, all night. The Mata temple held eight vigils a month, among them one every Tuesday and one on the *panchami*, the fifth day of the fortnight. At Mozang the Sikhs gathered on the fifth day of every bright half of the moon, when *prashad*, consecrated food, was distributed and lamps were lit.

The greatest mela of the city was Bhadar Kali's at Niaz Beg, held every year in the month of Jeth; no mela in Lahore was greater, and Muslims too, for the most part, attended. Because it fell in the hot season people suffered. Stalls of sharbat, a sweet cold drink, of water and of pounded bhang, a drink made from hemp, were set up, and sharbat was given to all alike; there was little shade, and tents were pitched. The crowd stayed a night and a day, and shopkeepers moved their shops there in advance and prepared sweets. Inside the walls, on the same day, the city temple of Bhadr Kali drew an immense gathering of women and men, those who did not go out to the village coming there to worship.

Than Bhairo's great mela fell on a Sunday at the new moon of Bhadon, with offerings and an all night vigil of *bhajans*, and besides it hundreds of people came from the city every Sunday to bow their heads there.

By the 1880s many melas, *ʿurs* and *sama* gatherings were in decline. At the *mazar* of Miran Badshah inside the Wazir Khan mosque, in the time of the Sikhs, a mela had been held every Thursday where thousands gathered, and the annual *ʿurs* and *sama* were held with great pomp; Hindus and Muslims still came on Thursdays and the *ʿurs* was still kept, but the *sama* belonged to the past. At Imam Ghulam Muhammad's tomb the annual mela and the *sama* gathering had stopped entirely. At Sayyid Sarbuland's *mazar* crowds had gathered on Thursdays in the Sikh period and the annual *ʿurs* had been kept with pomp, but that brisk trade was no more, and the same was true of the tomb at the Masjid Suf and of the bookbinders' shops. Fateh Shah Mast's great mela of Sikh times no longer drew such a crowd, and at Shah Husain Zanjani's *mazar* the great annual mela of the Sikh period had little by little lost its splendour.

Shah Yaqub Zanjani's melas were killed by the building around them: on two sides Lala Ratan Chand's *sarai*, a travellers' inn, and the buildings of his tank hid the khanqah, it was hemmed in on all four sides, and its graveyard too had been closed. At Fazl Shah Majzub's khanqah the *ʿurs*, kept with great pomp in Raja Dina Nath's lifetime, had dwindled to a yearly *fatiha*, the short prayer for the dead, shrinking to that with the death of its patron. Two melas grew instead: Mian Mir's, with the population of the garrison, and the *chiraghan* mela, the festival of lamps, whose crowds came from Lahore, Amritsar and distant cities because of the railway. The anniversary of Madho Lal Hussain is still celebrated in Lahore near the Shalimar Garden on the last Saturday in March. It is famous as *Melā Chirāghān*, the Fair of Lamps, and is held every year at the Shalimar Gardens.

Vows were tied to sickness, and some of these rites were shared across the traditions. On Sundays Hindus and Muslims alike took children suffering from a sickness to Shah Dargahi's well and bathed them there. Each person brought four sweet loaves and four salted ones, and after the bath the zamindar of the well, its landholder, took the loaves together with one paisa for the lamp. At Sitla Mata worship went on all the time, but the special worship came when smallpox was raging: while the sickness was at its height people gave offerings, each as much as he could, and thousands whose children had recovered came in joy. At the Sitla Devi mandir, after the bath of recovery from smallpox, cash and a goat were offered.

Offerings were also made in kind. At Ghore Shah clay horses were offered at the *mazar* in thousands on the day of the mela, so that heaps of them always lay on the parapets of the tomb. At one grave a vow of bread was current, told as a *karamat*, a miracle of the saint. Whoever came to the grave in need vowed a number of loaves, and when at last by God's grace his work was brought to completion, he cooked the loaves, brought them to the grave and shared them out.

Daily and weekly worship kept its hours. At the Kotwali shiwala the *arti*, the worship with lamps, was performed at four in the morning and seven in the evening, and again whenever a notable came, so that no hour was empty of worship, with special worship on Mondays. Its instruments were the great kettledrum, the pipe and the gong, and the pipe, the gong and the conch sounded so loudly that no one could hear anyone else speak. *Arti* was performed twice daily at the Bhagat Ram shiwala, and worship twice daily at the Radha Kishan thakurdwara and in Chhajju Bhagat's house, whose great hall was counted a shivala; the Granth, the Sikh scripture, was read twice daily at the Guru Ram Das gurdwara. At Chor Mor Wala the *mahant*, the head of the establishment, being a learned man, himself gave the *katha*, the recitation and exposition of scripture, every evening.

At the Ram Dwara the *katha* of the Bhagavata, the Ramayana and other holy texts was recited twice a day. There every attender was handed a rosary of *tulsi*, holy basil, by the mahant and told to sing the praise of Ram; a heap of rosaries lay in the middle of the circle, and one man was appointed solely to look after them. A lamp burned night and day at the Bhairo ji *asthan*, or seat, and at Than Bhairo's fire place it never went out. A *naqara*, a kettledrum, was played at two times of day at the Baoli and at *arti* time at Banke Bihari, and mystic songs were sung in the evening at the Kuka *dharamsal*, a Sikh place of worship and shelter. At a Hindu bhagat's samadh, every Monday and Tuesday, Hindu devotees, women and men, attended while *qawwali* was sung in the mystic raga.

Some rites gathered around ordinary places. A saint's shop became a second site of his cult: a flag still flew over it, a lamp was lit there every day, and on Thursdays many lamps were lit. Boys played all day at Pir Bhola's grave and climbed up on it, and every Friday the boys of the city made a *mazar* of Pir Bhola in lane after lane, offering flowers on it and lighting lamps.

The *langar*, the free communal kitchen, fed the poor across the traditions. By old custom any poor person who came to the Ram Dwara was fed, and any traveller too, and its offerings went to two heads only, the necessary expenses of Pandit Kanshi Ram and the cost of the daily *langar*. Shah Bilawal Qadri spent thousands of rupees a day on a public *langar*, and a public *langar* ran continuously at Mauj Darya's house. At Than Bhairo the temple's mahant fed the *faqirs*, holy mendicants, and the jogis, wandering ascetics, who gathered for the mela, while lay visitors cooked their own food. At both of Shah Sharf's melas faqirs gathered and food was given out; at Shah Sarwani's tomb faqirs gathered on both *ʿurs* dates to eat and to hear *qawwali*; and at Mubarak Haveli the food given to the poor and the faqirs during the ten days of Muharram was cooked in one part of the building.

Land and mills stood behind the kitchens. One *dharamsal* had forty *bighas*, a bigha being a measure of land, and a running well, whose income Nihang Singh took and with which he fed travellers, and a mill was built there to grind flour for them. A mill ran continuously inside Mian Wada's enclosure, and another was built for grinding flour on the Bhauri Sarkar estate. Mian Wada's *madrasa*, a religious school, was a charity for the disabled with a headcount and a curriculum. Under the Sikhs the blind, the lame and the crippled, a hundred dervishes and faqirs, and students in their hundreds received bread and clothing from it; by the 1880s the khanqah maintained about two hundred blind and lame people and five dervishes, all were taught the Quran, and the blind were made to learn it by heart.

At one establishment hospitality was the mahant's rule. One dharamsala lived wholly on offerings: its faqirs were supported only by the service and gifts of devotees, *bhajans* were sung daily in the third watch, offerings were made on the Granth, and any travelling faqir was given food. Before moving the image of Thakur ji from Rangilpur to Lahore with great honour, Hari Narayan first spent a great deal of money there feeding sadhus, Hindu holy men, and Hindus.

Bhang had its place in shrine practice. At the samadh of Guru Arjan a building held a stone mortar sunk in the ground and a pestle for grinding bhang. At Shahid Ganj the Nihang Sikhs, the order of Sikh warriors, ground bhang all day in a roadside hall where its cauldrons stood, and the stalls of the Bhadar Kali mela served it pounded. One mahant's own offering, his *nazr*, was a little bhang that he called *prashad*.

*Sama* and music ran through the city's devotion, held at some shrines and fallen silent at others. In the assemblies Punjabi *kafis*, *barah mah* and *abyat* were sung: the *kafi* is a mystic lyric, the *barah mah* a poem of the twelve months, and *abyat* are couplets. In the sixteenth century, it is told, a man of Lahore came and said that in his time there was no one worthy of listening to music, *samaʿ*: "In this time there is no one worthy". Women musicians performed *hazrī*, literally "presence", at the Sufi shrines of Lahore, above all at the time of the *ʿurs*, and the same word was used of the qawwals who sang at the city's shrines both before Partition and after it. In recent fieldwork a grassroots musician, Zarina Khatoon, was interviewed in Lahore, and a discography of Sufi music lists four recordings by qawwal parties from the city, those of Kausar Jabin, of Mubarak Ali and Fateh Ali, of Muhammad Siddiq and of Alaf Din Pathan.

A modern Sufi order keeps a quieter practice in the city. Its *zikr* circles, gatherings for the remembrance of God by repeated recitation, meet in Lahore, and their full repertoire of remembrance was recorded at a gathering on a Thursday evening in October 2000. Hospitality is refused at these gatherings: "That is why serving any food or even a cup of tea is strictly forbidden, because some people then might come just to socialize".

Lahore drew pilgrims in and sent them out. Pilgrims passing through visited its shrines: Fakhr-ud-Din, on his first journey towards Ajmer, visited all the *mazars* of Lahore. At the time of a saint's festival, a fakir arriving at Data Darbar found that "countless other fakirs had gathered in the city". In the autumn of 2011 a visiting fakir from Lahore was at Sehwan "to perform his duty at the shrine of Lal", keeping his retreat by the river there. A Lahore woman of Shahdara is a regular at the Maryabad festival, where on the second day the bishop comes from Lahore.

The Milad procession, in honour of the Prophet's birthday, began in Lahore in a single year at a single mosque. In 1935 a group of about a hundred people left the Wazir Khan Mosque singing praises of the Prophet and walked around the alleys of the walled city. The mosque's prayer leader, Maulana Syed Dedar Shah, had argued that if Hindus could hold festivals and processions for what he called their fake gods, Muslims could do the same for their Prophet, though most of his followers rejected the idea. Soon afterwards Karam Elahi, also of Lahore, organised his own little procession, and Inayatullah Qadri, another religious scholar, began holding an annual Mehafal-e-Millad, a gathering in the Prophet's honour. The event crossed the boundaries of the walled city and spread to neighbouring villages and towns; in time the procession had to be moved outside Delhi Gate, where a permanent platform was built, and the junction where it converged every year took the name Millad Chowk.

The city's religious minorities keep their own calendar. Lahore's year includes Shri Valmiki's birthday and Krishna Janamashtami, Palm Sunday and Good Friday at the Sacred Heart Cathedral, Easter at the Cathedral Church of Resurrection, Navroz and Hazrat Bab's birthday, and the commemorations of Guru Arjan's martyrdom at Gurdwara Dera Sahib and of Maharaja Ranjit Singh's death anniversary. "On religious festivals like Holi, Diwali and Shri Valmiki's birthday, the Neela Gumbad Mandir becomes an island of religious syncretism in Lahore". Before 9/11, a Catholic remembers, a huge procession used to leave the Don Bosco School on Railway Road for the Sacred Heart Cathedral, and all the Catholic churches of the city merged into it. The city's yearly religious conferences of about three thousand people were stopped at the government's request, following threats of rising terrorism.

Easter breakfast at the Cathedral Church of Resurrection is "halwa puri and channa, typical Lahori nashta", the city's own breakfast. At Navroz, Daria Boatwala says, "Nothing really happens here as you can see", and tambola is the only game played in Lahore now. On a Sunday on Mall Road, cricket in all the grounds is a usual Sunday feature.

Older and humbler customs linger at the edges. A man in Lahore claimed to have spent forty days inside a grave. In the nineteenth century the Shakir Shahi *konda*, a clay bowl, was famous in Lahore, made by Shakir Shah Kahar at two annas a *konda*. Urdu almanacs printed in Bombay and Lahore still prominently feature the death anniversaries of the Sufi saints of the subcontinent, and copies of al-Busiri's *Burda*, the devotional ode, are still sold in the shops of Lahore. And once the army of Khalilullah Khan and Raja Jai Singh camped outside the city on the day of Eid al-Adha.

## Poetry

Lahore was a city of Persian poets under the Ghaznavid kings. Mas'ud-i-Sa'd-i-Salman's family came from Hamadan in Persia, but he himself was born at Lahore, and in several passages of his poems he speaks of it as his native place. He was probably born about 440 AH (1048 to 1049) and died about 515 AH (1121 to 1122). He was the panegyrist, the poet of praise, of five Ghaznavid kings, Ibrahim, Mas'ud III, Shirzad, Arslan and Bahramshah, and his special patron was Sultan Ibrahim's son Mahmud, who was made governor of India in 469 AH (1076 to 1077).

He shared his master's disgrace and was imprisoned; once freed, he returned to his father's estate in India. When Mas'ud III gave the government of India to his son Shirzad, the commander in chief made the poet governor of Jalandar, a dependency of Lahore. Imprisoned again, he was released about 500 AH and, being then sixty, retired from public life and spent the rest of his days in seclusion. The poet Sana'i, who first collected and edited his poetry, was among those who bore witness to his pre-eminence, and the poet Rashidi was his contemporary and rival.

Another poet of the age, Abu'l-Faraj-i-Runi, is tied to the city by his name. Runa, from which he took his *nisba*, the part of a name that tells where a person comes from, was a place near Lahore, though accounts differ and some put it in Khurasan.

Madho Lal Hussain is Lahore's own poet, pictured roaming the streets of Lahore drunkenly in the company of fellow carousers. His verse was first printed in the city: a collection in Gurmukhi, the script of Punjabi used by Sikhs, made by an unknown Sindhi editor, was published in Lahore around 1900, and the Punjab University library holds a manuscript of it, number 374, of 1804. Najm Hosain Syed's play *Takht L'hore*, the Throne of Lahore, carries the city in its title.

The Mughal court spoke verse in the city too. In the thirty seventh year of his reign, on his court's second journey from Lahore to Kashmir, the emperor Akbar set out in the rains, and as he crossed the Ravi below the fort of Lahore he asked about whom this Persian couplet had been written:

کلاه خسروی و تاج شاہی
بہر گل کی رسد حاشا و کلا

The blinded Shahryar, a prisoner in the fort of Lahore, spoke a quatrain whose last line says that the eye of the sun has gone blind, and Jahangir's palace at Lahore was praised in a *rubaʿi*, a Persian quatrain.

The mosques of the city carry verse in their inscriptions. Over the gate of the mosque of Muhammad Salih Kambo, three niches hold two lines that name its founder:

بانی این مسجد زیبا نگار
بنائے آل محمد صالح است

The outer gateway of Masjid Chinian Wali carries a chronogram couplet, a verse whose letters, read as numbers, give a year. One chronogram, a quatrain, was the work of Farid, a poet of Lahore, and his line is written beneath it. Of four chronograms on one mosque, three call it a second Kaaba, and all four put the date in the mouth of the *hatif*, the unseen voice.

At Roshnai Gate a parapet once fell suddenly on two men and dashed out their brains. The accident was read as recompense, in the words of a Persian couplet that warns against heedlessness of the requital of deeds.

## Lahore in Books

Writers of many centuries have seen Lahore as a seat of poets and of rulers. In the Ghaznavid age it was the birthplace and native place of Persian poets, and the seat on which Jalandar depended. To a Mughal chronicler it was a great city and a mighty metropolis, where craftsmen of every land lived and the goods of the seven climes and the wares of sea and land were sold. He called it the seat of the sultanate and the capital of the realms, and Aram Shah's Lahore a land that delights the heart. His city was a capital of rulers, a store of treasure and arms, and a place that Islam Shah feared for its very greatness.

In the nineteenth century *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* was written on the orders of the commissioner, who asked for a detailed account of the buildings, *mazars*, tombs and mosques of the environs of Lahore. Its author called the task very hard, because the history of hundreds of years had to be gathered. He held that no book had yet been written or printed that covered the antiquities of Lahore in full, and described his own as reporting on the tombs of the Muslim elders, on other buildings and on the old customs of Lahore, while being at root a history of the saints. A modern translator gives its title as "Tehqiqat-i-Chisti: Tarikh-i-Lahore ka Encyclopedia (Researches of Chisti: Encyclopedia of Lahore's History)".

Its author wrote in conversation, and in argument, with earlier writers on the city. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar had long sought an account of the elders of Lahore's environs and could not obtain it. Lala Amin Chand's *Safarnama*, printed by Koh Nur, gave an account of the city of Lahore that the author disputed, and Amin Chand's work on the buildings and people of Lahore was cut short when the same commissioner left for Shahjahanabad. The author was also a witness: he saw with his own eyes what was done in the city with the heads of Raja Hira Singh and Pandit Jalla.

*Tarikh-e Lahore*, the history of 1884, was written by a resident of the city who was also an engineer and a historian of property, and his hand shows in it. He reconstructed vanished features: when a mosque was built its founder must have kept the plinth very high, and there must have been shops beneath its northern wall. Where a building had no vocabulary of its own he supplied one, again and again saying that a thing should be called so: the door of a veranda should be called the outer door of the temple, one part the fifth degree, another a three arched seat. A house may rightly be called a collection of temples, and a great hall should be thought of as a *shivala*.

His judgements of beauty were either backed by European opinion or delivered flat. The Wazir Khan mosque, in his eyes, was famous among Europeans and might rightly be called the pride and glory of Lahore; great painters learned their art from it, and travellers from far away came to Lahore and carried off copies of its niches. The Badshahi Masjid was larger than the Jami Masjid of Delhi, and it was a pity that the dome of the Sufi Wali mosque was never built, for its beauty would have been doubled. One parapet was shaped so that from a distance it looked like domes, and the unrepaired Shiv Gar shivala in truth needed no repair, so solidly was it built. At Sher Singh's modest *samadh* he gave a moral reading: the visitor's tears fall unbidden, and he becomes certain that the world is transient.

He reported a private donor's work with an engineer's interest. Nura Iman Wala repaired the Wazir Khan mosque at a cost of thousands of rupees, and of the eight openings above the dome of each of its four minarets he had four closed and four left open for greater strength. At Nawab Mian Khan's tomb, when a mosque was built on the west a matching one was built on the east, purely for beauty.

He dated many buildings by the years that had passed since their founding, counting back from his own day. The Teja Singh thakurdwara had been built under English rule twenty five years before, on the site of a demolished old *haveli*, a great mansion; Baikunth Das's thakurdwara had been raised by subscription ten years before; the Shivala Ragnath Mishr was forty years old and the Kuka dharamsal fifteen. Bishno Devi's *devi dwara*, a temple of the goddess, was forty years old, Chor Mor Wala had been founded fifty years before in Ranjit Singh's time, Jwala Dai's mandir nine years before, Mehr Das's own mandir not less than two hundred years before, and the Chhajju Bhagat thakurdwara ten or twelve years before. The Wazir Khan mosque was two hundred and fifty one years old, two hundred years had passed since Shah Bilawal's exhumation, and the Pir Sabz tree was nearly two centuries old. Khuda Singh had died eighteen years before, Shiv Gar forty and Daryagar ten.

Where he had no year he dated by reign: the time of the Tughluq sultanate and of Sultan Muhammad Tughluq, the Lodi sultanate, Akbar's time, when the foundation was laid of a mandir that still stood and drew devotees in numbers for its antiquity, the reign of Shah Jahan, the Alamgiri period, the reign of Ahmad Shah, and the Sikh period. In his closing pages the Chughtai kings, the kings of old, the Sikh government and the English government stand side by side without comment, and both 1857 and the fall of the Sikhs are called only a disturbance, *mufsida*. He framed the city's history as a sequence of destructions: thirteen numbered shocks, *sadme*, of plunder and demolition, followed by the present form of the city of Lahore.

He cast Sikh rule as the time of loss and English rule as the time of restitution, in four cases close together: the confiscated shops, the powder magazine, the stripped gateway and the encroached chowk. Elsewhere he reported both regimes in the same register with different verbs: the English demolishing cells for some reason of expediency and cutting windows for their own troops, while also convening *darbars*, public assemblies, subscribing and restoring mosques by name and date; the Sikh regime confiscating, quartering artillery, prising up flooring and transferring endowments. He drew no general conclusion from the contrast. On the city's defences he gave the administrations' own reasons, that so high a wall was thought superfluous, that the work was done to remove a nuisance, and that the time of the English peace had come, without endorsing or contesting them.

Where he vouched for the state's fairness, the words were his own. In his telling the government, as a matter of justice, paid full compensation for every loss; land taken from houses was paid for to the owners' satisfaction, so that no one was displeased; flood damage was paid to the last penny; and for the rebuilt tank the government paid the full price of the houses, cleared the ground and began building. This was a compulsory acquisition inside the walled city, and he recorded no dissent. He was also a party to what he reported. State repairs ran through him as Executive Engineer, the annual repair grant for the Shalamar Bagh among them, and for the Badshahi Masjid, a building in his own division, he asked the administration openly for a further grant, saying that if it turned its attention to the mosque once more, the mosque would be repaired in full.

His economic argument was a counterfactual: had the income of its property not been attached to the Wazir Khan mosque, the very form of its habitation would have been lost. He explained a shrine's prosperity by the man in charge, not by the saint. Dola Wari kept its standing because some holy man or other always lived there, and Bhai Prem Singh, a blessed and courteous man, kept his place well peopled. The Teja Singh thakurdwara's splendour was doubled on its sadhu's account until he left, and a Brahman kept one shivala in great splendour. One mosque was in full splendour while its founder lived and had little splendour now, and at a lecture platform, after the death of the pandit in whose time it had flourished, Pandit Gopi Nath, editor of the *Akhbar-e-Aam*, lectured there, but that splendour did not return.

One passage is his judgement rather than description, a hostile portrait of the community that supported a mosque. Of the Sadhus of Takiya Sadhuwan, Muslim Kashmiris who came under Sikh rule, he wrote that they had all once been tenants and had become owners of property and wealthy; that they plied no visible trade or calling, their business being to bring in money by every stratagem; and that wherever they went they changed their names, one calling himself Sufi Shah, another Shatranji Shah, another Miskin Shah, one becoming a faqir on the path, another a *majzub*, one absorbed in divine love, another a prince. It is an accusation that a Lahore community impersonated faqirs for a living, not a description of it.

He named the texts he relied on, and where they failed him. *Tuhfat al-Wasilin* was his authority twice, and it was silent where he needed it: of one grave, no one knew the real name of the person buried there, and *Tuhfat al-Wasilin* had not recorded it either. Munshi Inayatullah, author of *Bahar-e Danish*, was one man's maternal uncle and father in law. For the marble of Bahadur Khan's tomb he had only the testimony of old people.

Lahore is also a city of manuscripts and collectors. *Haqiqat al-Fuqara*, by Pir Muhammad, is a Persian verse life of Lal Husain written by an eyewitness; the Punjab University Library holds its unpublished original manuscript, under the shelfmark 3253/248 Farsi, and a printing said to date from 1966 could not be found in the libraries of Lahore. Mr Khalil al-Rahman Dawoodi of Lahore supplied modern scholars of Sufism with manuscripts, among them one copied at Taunsa.

Modern scholars have seen Lahore from very different distances. For a student of a modern Sufi order it was home in the field: he spent the academic year of 1995 and 1996 studying Urdu in the city, and in Pakistan was "based in the cultural capital of Lahore", and a photograph of the dead shaykh circulated among friends there. The translator of Madho Lal Hussain signed his introduction "Lahore / August 2015". A scholar of women's voices in Sufi ritual worked from Lahore too: she started from the city on a spring afternoon, measured Kasur as seventy miles from it and placed Jhang Siyal to its southwest, and noted that landlords' sons now live in large urban centres like Karachi, Lahore or Islamabad. Khalida and Jaleel Naqvi, custodians of the shrine of Hazrat Pir Sabir at Paran Kalyar, took part in her fieldwork in Lahore and Kasur and were interviewed in Lahore.

The city's saints have their own local literature, among it M. Latif Malik's *Aulīyā-e Lahore*, Samina Qureshi's *Lahore: The City Within*, a Lahore reprint of *Tarikh-e Lahore*, and Tahir Lahori's essay *Aulīyā ke Urs*, published in *Sohna Shehr Lahore*.

Other studies name the city only in passing. Lahore marks the far edge of the area from which volunteers come to serve at the *ʿurs* at Ghamkol Sharif; it is the city to which one woman, Rabi‘a, returns, and its neighbourhood of Samnabad is named. A study of the goddess Hinglaj names Lahore as a Punjabi city in its survey of Pakistani Hinduism, and as the home of an informant of the Nath sect. The twentieth century Chishti Zauqi Shah worked as a journalist in Peshawar, Lahore, Hyderabad in Sind and Karachi.

Lahore is a city of print. A Sufi Foundation founded by a senior *murid* published translations and original writings by Wahid Bakhsh during the 1980s; Bazm-i Ittehad al-Muslimin was founded in 1984 by the shaykh's nephew; and from 1996 Talifat-i Shaheedi, set up by another *murid* of Shahidullah Faridi, published four more texts, three of them in English. Commercial houses carry the rest, among them Ziya al-Qur'an, Qausin Publishers and al-Faisal Publishers, and some fifty different texts of the order are produced and distributed from a number of publishing houses, reaching readers through the Urdu bookstores of Karachi and Lahore. The city has its press too, among it the English language weekly *The Friday Times*, and from Lahore Pakpattan is measured as 120 miles to the southwest.

Older scholarship knows Lahore as a place of publication and nothing more. Lahore houses printed mystical poetry, among them Ashraf, the University of the Punjab, Urdu Dunya and Allah wale ki qaumi dukan, whose numbered chapbook series carried Panjabi mystical verse: a Panjabi version of the *Burda*, *Qasida-i Burda Panjabi*, a commentary on Bullhe Shah, and a collection of Panjabi mystical verse with no author named. Two Panjabi verse translations were published at Lahore, by Pir Imam Shah and by Maulana Muhammad Shahuddin, the second in 1939. Lahore publishers also issued the literature on *qawwali*, among them Sheikh Muhammad Ashraf and the Institute of Islamic Culture.

For a writer based in the city, Lahore is home and field: "Based as I am in Lahore, much of my fieldwork took place in the city itself and its vicinity. This is bound to have had its limitations." On a Sunday, Mall Road, "one of the busiest boulevards in the city", is deserted, and a drive along it is "a journey through the colonial era", past the Lahore High Court, combining European and Eastern architectural techniques. The National College of Arts was established in Lahore in 1857 and is considered the most prestigious art college in the country.

He watches the city change. The Anarkali market, named after the legendary lover of the Mughal emperor Jahangir, has over the years become the largest tyre market in the city, and the Pak Tea House a few shops away, once famed for the gatherings of the city's leading intelligentsia, is on its way to becoming yet another tyre shop. For one resident, Rettigan Road was a prominent locality of colonial Lahore, now taken over by small businesses and shops and no longer the clean, spacious area it once was. Behind the Roshnai Gate the musicians remain, and it is still one of the largest markets for traditional instruments in the city. The Ravi, which once flowed next to the fort, has changed its course and moved a few kilometres away, and today the mighty Ravi of history flows like a stream in Lahore.

The metropolis swallows its villages. The Defence Housing Authority, the neatest and most expensive residential area of Lahore, sprawls across a vast tract of land, eating into the small historic villages of the area, whose people, having sold their abundant land for very little to the housing authority, are left congested and poor behind a wall. Hanjarwal is a village subsumed by the ever growing metropolis of Lahore. Iqbal Qaiser was working on an encyclopedia of Lahore, the first of its kind, in five parts of about a thousand pages each, drawn from land revenue records and government archives, until administrative changes at the Punjab Cultural Institute put an untimely end to the project.

Lahore is also a crossroads and a city of crafts. The Grand Trunk Road connected with Lahore and then Delhi, the caravanserai route ran through Lahore, Satgaraha, Deepalpur, Harrapa, Multan and Uch Sharif, and the Bedian road is the old route to Amritsar. The Kashmiri binders who used to live in Lahore were known for their meticulous binding, and Bhai Ram Singh was Principal of the Mayo School of Arts in Lahore. Rohtas was not the kind of fort found in Lahore, where only royalty stayed.

In a lecture on Sikh architecture in Lahore, Iqbal Qaiser argued that "like the Sikh religion, which picks up from both Hinduism and Islam, Sikh architecture draws inspiration from both Hindu and Muslim architecture", and that domes once part of Muslim buildings, made smaller by the Sikhs, are now used in Muslim buildings in Pakistan too. New apartments have been built to meet the growing demands of urban Lahore, and indigenous trees are being replaced by imported ones in its developed areas.
