## Overview

Uch Sharif, also known as Uch Gilanian, is an ancient town near Bahawalpur, in the southern region of Punjab, and is called the city of saints in Bahawalpur. Built on a hill overlooking the confluence of the Sutlej and Chenab rivers, it was a centre of learning, trade and pilgrimage, and it lies seventy miles from Multan, though accounts differ on its direction from that city. For a while Uch was the centre of the Suhrawardiyya, a Sufi order, and it was here that the first missionaries of the Qadiriyya, the Sufi order founded by Abd al-Qadir Gilani, settled before that order spread across the Subcontinent.

The town is known through its holy families. Its Sayyids, descendants of the Prophet, are famous, and once they accept a man as of sound descent no one dares dispute it. On the mound of the older city stand the massive havelis, or great mansions, of the shrine's present caretakers, the Gilanis and the Makhdooms. A family of *qawwals*, singers of *qawwali*, the Sufi devotional music, at Kasur says that it came from Uch Sharif with the family of the poet Bulle Shah.

Uch Sharif lies where the cultures of Punjab and Sindh merge, and its language is Siraiki. Guru Nanak passed through on his way to Sindh. In the early twenty first century the abandoned well of Nanak could still be seen, beside a grassy mound where the Gurdwara of Nanak, a Sikh place of worship, had stood.

## History

Legend tells how the town began. In a story told of Shaikh Safiuʾd-Din Kaziruni, an order was given to mount a camel, to travel in whatever direction the animal led, and to remain where it finally halted. The camel stopped in the middle of a desert, and there, it is said, the Shaikh founded a town later called Uch.

Uch was also a prize of war. Shihab al-Din's first campaign took the town and left a deputy there, and his second came again to Multan and Uch before going on by way of the desert to Gujarat. Mirza Pir Muhammad crossed the Sind and took the fortress of Uch and Multan into his possession, and Khawas Khan's pursuit went as far as Multan and Uch.

Uch was also a city of saints. Jalaluddin Surkhposh Bukhari, who died in 1291, was of Uch. For a while Uch was the centre of the Suhrawardiyya, thanks chiefly to the active and pious Jalaluddin Makhdum-i Jahaniyan, "whom all the inhabitants of the world serve", a prolific writer in almost every religious field, who died in 1383. One of the masters of the order's second period was based at Uch, and Baha ad-Din Zakaria, the Suhrawardi saint, is also named as a saint of Uch Sharif. Local legends suggest that a man who went to Multan and Uch may have been formally initiated into the Suhrawardiyya there.

The town was also a gate for the Qadiriyya. Its first missionaries settled at Uch in the late fifteenth century, though accounts differ on when the order first reached the Subcontinent, and from here it spread, soon gained a firm footing and was carried on to Indonesia and Malaysia. The first Qadiri *khanaqah*, or Sufi lodge, at Uch was founded by Shaykh Muhammad al-Husseini al-Jilani, a native of Turkey who travelled to Khurasan and then Multan before settling in Uch with his family. His eldest son, Shaykh 'Abd al-Qadir Jilani Thani, who died in 1533, is the famous Qadiri saint of Uch Sharif, yet no written record of his life and legacy seems to survive.

Families came and went through Uch. The genealogy of Hazrat Pir Muhammad Shah Sahib has the Gilani ancestor coming from Uch Sharif in the reign of Akbar Shah. The family of a man whose original name was Abdullah Shah were Syeds who had settled in Uch Gilanian, the town where Bulle Shah was born.

Guru Nanak came to Uch on his way to Sindh, after he had settled down. In one retelling of the visit, Uch Sharif was then one of the most famous Muslim shrines in southern Punjab, and Nanak had arrived only a day before, travelling from Kartarpur. Accounts differ over the rivers today. The Sutlej is said to have flowed from here until it was lost after the Indus Water Treaty was signed between India and Pakistan in 1960, and in Nanak's day, it is thought, the town's old shrines stood intact with a mighty river flowing beside them.

## A City of Saints

In the early twenty first century a visitor could spend a day wandering through the magnificent blue and white tiled mosques and desolate tombs of this ancient town. The older city stands on a mound, and the way to its group of shrines of the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries leads through the market. "This place has changed since the last time I came here", Iqbal Qaiser remarked on one visit. On the mound several graves are covered, without any order, with blue and white Multani tiles recovered from these historic shrines.

The town keeps the graves of saintly families. The four sons of Hazrat Muhammad Ghaus rest at Uch Sharif, beside the tomb of Shah Muhammad Ghaus. Among its mosques is the Hajj mosque, in whose well Baba Farid kept his inverted fast, and another story tells of a Baba who found a lonely mosque in Uch.

There is a Sikh trace too, now almost gone. "Do you want to see the well of Nanak?", Iqbal Qaiser asked, adding that there was nothing left there anyway. The well lies hidden within a field, shielded by a grove of trees; it was found abandoned, with juice tetrapaks and wrappers floating in its black water. Beside it a grassy mound marks where the Gurdwara of Nanak stood, and nothing of it remains.

## Communities

At the centre of Uch stand the Sayyids, known as the Sadat of Uch. Where a dispute over someone's descent arises, once they accept a person as of sound descent, no one dares say a word against it. The first elders of one family, the Faqirs, lived at Uch Sharif Sayyidan and served the Sadat of Uch.

Families and their musicians carried Uch elsewhere. Muhammad Bakhsh, of the *qawwals* of Kasur, put it plainly: "We came with Bulle Shah's family from Uch Sharif, and we have learned his poetry from our ancestors, who have always sung here". Bulle Shah's mentor, Inayat Shah, also belonged to Uch.

The southern region of Punjab, where Uch Sharif lies, has more traces of religious and cultural syncretism, the blending of traditions, than the north and centre of the province. Here the cultures of Punjab and Sindh merge, the language is Siraiki, and Bahawalpur too is counted within the Siraiki belt.

The older stories tell of rivalry with the yogis, Hindu ascetics. The stories of three generations of Sufis at Uch all recount conflicts with Hindu yogis, and a story is also told of a flying contest with a yogi at Uch.

Uch also stood on the edge of other peoples' lands. In Aurangzeb's time the province of Thatta was described as running from the bounds of Multan and Uch to Thatta and Kach Makran. To its north lay high mountains of hard rock and the dwellings of Baloch tribes and some Afghans, and to its south, from Uch to Gujarat, hills of sand and the homelands of the Bhatti.

## Custodians, Patrons and the State

The shrine's present caretakers are the Gilanis and the Makhdooms, whose massive havelis stand on the mound of the older city. In the nineteenth century some of the descendants of Abd al-Qadir Sani held the *sajjada*, the seat of a saint's spiritual successor, in Uch.

Rulers appear beside the saints. There was a Malik, or lord, of Uch and Multan, and a Nawab came to the town's Sayyids for their prayers. In the wider region, Bahawalpur and Multan are the seat of landowning Syeds who support the shrines of their ancestors.

## Devotion and Practice

Uch was a place of austerities. Farid ad-Din, Baba Farid, performed the inverted fast of forty days, the *chilla-i ma'kus*, in the well of the Hajj mosque at Uch, a *chilla* being a retreat of forty days. "For 40 days they would suspend him from a branch of the tree that overhung the well every night. At daybreak they would pull him out."

It was also a place of initiation, as the local legends of one man's entry into the Suhrawardiyya at Uch suggest, and its Sayyids were sought for their prayers. A Nawab who suffered at Uch Sharif from an ailment of the cheek that would not heal went to the Sadat of Uch Sharif for prayer, and they named a skilled physician. The Nawab brought the physician to Lahore and was cured. The story comes down from Hazrat Shah Sardar Sahib, son of Hazrat Pir Muhammad Shah Sahib Gilani.

Its poetry travelled with its families. The *qawwals* of Kasur learned Bulle Shah's poetry from ancestors who came with his family from Uch Sharif. In the words "Thou art an inhabitant of Uch city", Uch literally means high or great.

## Uch Sharif in Books

The town goes by many names. It is Uch Sharif, Uch Gilanian, Uch city and Uch Sharif Sayyidan, and it is also written Ucch, Utch and Uchh; in Aurangzeb's time it was the *qasba* of Uch, a small town. Writers place it near Multan or near Bahawalpur.

In histories of Sufism, Uch is a station in the story of two orders: for a while the centre of the Suhrawardiyya, and the door by which the Qadiriyya entered the Subcontinent. Other studies touch it once each, as the seat of a Suhrawardi master and as the home of Jalaluddin Surkhposh Bukhari. A history of Sufism in India gives Uch a section of its own, in which the stories of three generations of Sufis there all recount conflicts with Hindu yogis, and treats its founding story with caution, as mythical in appearance but typical of medieval tales of Sufis.

A Persian chronicle of Aurangzeb's reign knows Uch as a town of campaigns and boundaries, taken and passed through by armies and marking the edge of the province of Thatta. In a nineteenth century account it is the home of Sayyid families, the place a Gilani line came from, the home of the elders of a family who served the Sayyids, and of Sayyids to whom a Nawab turned for prayer.

For modern visitors it is a town of tiled mosques and desolate tombs, of old shrines reached through a market, of a river said to be lost and of a vanished gurdwara. No written record of its famous Qadiri saint's life seems to survive. A book devoted to the town itself is Mas'ud Hasan Shahab's *Khitta-i pak Uch*, published in Bahawalpur by the Urdu Academy. In a study of Chishti Sufism it is the town of the Hajj mosque, where Baba Farid hung in the well, and in a study of Sufi ritual it is the city of saints from which a poet's family and its singers came.
