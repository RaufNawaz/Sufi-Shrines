## Overview

Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, also called the North-West Frontier Province or NWFP, is a province of Pakistan. Since partition it has been home to a large proportion of religious minorities, chiefly Hindus, who form its largest minority, and it has a considerable Sikh population. Hindu festivals are celebrated there with much pomp, and a woman from Peshawar remembered her home, "the frontier", as a land of many shrines that women could walk into.

In the Sikh period Maharaja Ranjit Singh captured the Pathan regions of what is now Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, and as a result several people there became Sikhs. At partition the riots were less intense here than in Punjab, and many Hindus and Sikhs stayed on in their ancestral lands. Since then Sikh families from the province have moved to Nankana Sahib, in Punjab.

In the national elections of October 2002 a coalition of religious parties, the Muttahida Majlis-i Amal, won a controlling majority of the province's Provincial Assembly. In one scholar's view the NWFP "has more in common with the Middle East than with South Asia", and the ritual meetings of its women, like those of Afghanistan and Iran, dwell on grief and lament.

## The Province in History

During his reign Maharaja Ranjit Singh captured the Pathan regions of what are now Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and the tribal areas, the Pathans being the Pashtun people. As a result several people there converted to Sikhism.

At partition the riots in the province were less intense than in Punjab. Accounts differ on why, crediting the calm either to the influence of the Indian National Congress there or to that of Abdul Ghaffar Khan, known as the Sarhadi, or Frontier, Gandhi. Because the political situation was comparatively peaceful, many Hindus and Sikhs stayed rooted in their ancestral lands even after the creation of Pakistan.

The province's Sikhs later began to move to Punjab. In the early days of Sikh migration to Nankana Sahib, after the wars of 1965 and 1971, many Sikh families from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and the Tribal Areas found refuge in a *gurdwara*, a Sikh place of worship, Gurdwara Patti Sahib. In the early 2010s the Sikhs of Nankana were recent migrants from the province, where there is a considerable Sikh population.

The NWFP has also known numerous religiously led uprisings. In the national elections of October 2002 the Muttahida Majlis-i Amal (MMA), a coalition of religious political parties, made unprecedented gains. It won 19 percent of the seats in the National Assembly, the upper hand as the largest party in Baluchistan, and a controlling majority of the Provincial Assembly of the North-West Frontier Province. The coalition was pro-Taliban, anti-American and predominantly ethnic Pashtun.

## Shrines, Festivals and Pilgrims

For one disciple, a woman originally from Peshawar, the shrines of her home were open to women in a way that those of Pakpattan were not. "I am quite used to the culture of segregation when it comes to the saints and the shrines," she said. "In the frontier, we have so many shrines where you can walk into the saint's shrines. Women can go inside, we can touch the graves. At many places there is a separate side for the women. It is not like this at Pakpattan Sharif."

At the ritual meetings of women in the province, as in Afghanistan and Iran, the emphasis falls on *gham pasandi*, loving grief, or *dard-e del*, telling one's heart's sorrow, in a lamenting way. That tone is notably different from the informal and positive conversation at Mama Ji's *darbar*, literally a court, in Rawalpindi.

Hindu festivals are celebrated with much pomp in the province, and its people were present at two festivals of 2011. At Baisakhi, the spring harvest festival, at Hassan Abdal that year, most of the Sikhs were fair skinned and blue eyed Pathans from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and the tribal areas. At Shivratri, the Hindu night of Shiva, at Katas in the same year, the four marriages held there were of girls from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and FATA, the Federally Administered Tribal Areas, who had suffered during the floods of 2010.

## Communities

Since partition the province has been home to a large proportion of religious minorities who have lived there rather peacefully. They are primarily Hindus, who form the largest minority. Thousands of them are part of mainstream society, visible often in its economic and political life, and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa is among the provinces across which most of the country's Hindus are scattered.

Several people in the Pathan regions converted to Sikhism after Ranjit Singh captured them, and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa has a considerable Sikh population. Thousands of Nanak Panthi Hindus, followers of Guru Nanak who are also counted as Sikhs, live in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Punjab and Sindh. Independent followers of Guru Nanak, who refused the Khalsa, the order of initiated Sikhs, still exist in Sindh and in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa.

The *shalwar kameez*, loose trousers worn under a long shirt, is the traditional wear of the province's people, and one man, Kartar, wore it grey and baggy.

## Khyber Pakhtunkhwa in Books

To a writer travelling among Pakistan's religious minorities in the early 2010s, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa was a province where Hindus and Sikhs stayed on after partition, where thousands of them are part of the mainstream of society, and where Hindu festivals are celebrated "with much pomp". He read the movement of its Sikhs into Punjab as a sign of the different socio-economic conditions of the two provinces.

In essays on Sufi cults published in 1998, the province is the NWFP. One scholar suggested that its numerous religiously led uprisings might be set beside other exceptions of the colonial era, but held that from his perspective it "has more in common with the Middle East than with South Asia". Another grouped the ritual meetings of its women with those of Afghanistan and Iran, and contrasted them with the tone at a *darbar* in Rawalpindi.

In a study of politics and piety in Pakistan published in 2007, the province is where a coalition of religious parties won control of the Provincial Assembly in 2002. To the disciple from Peshawar, speaking in Lahore in October 2000, it was a frontier of open shrines, where women walk in and touch the graves.
