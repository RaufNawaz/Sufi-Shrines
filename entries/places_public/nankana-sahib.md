## Overview

Nankana Sahib is the town where Guru Nanak, the founder of the Sikh religion, was born. It lies about forty miles to the south west of Lahore, in what is now Pakistan, and it was once a village called Talwandi, in full Rai Bhoi di Talwandi, renamed Nankana Sahib in honour of the first Sikh Guru. For Sikhs it is one of the holiest cities in the world, if not the holiest, and several *gurdwaras*, Sikh places of worship, mark events from Nanak's childhood.

Sikh tradition remembers Talwandi as a village between the Ravi and the Chenab, in the path of the armies bound for Delhi, which rose again each time it was destroyed. By the time Nanak came back from Pak Pattan, it is said, it was no longer an obscure little village but a place of pilgrimage.

The town of the early twenty first century has a Muslim majority and a small Sikh community of about two hundred households, most of them families that came recently from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. Its gurdwaras stand locked for most of the year and open for the festivals. At Guru Nanak's birthday pilgrims come from all over the world, and for a few days the whole town looks like a little India.

## History

As Sikh tradition tells it in the *janamsakhis*, the traditional stories of Guru Nanak's life, Talwandi lay between the rivers Ravi and Chenab, directly in the path of the armies that had set their eyes on Delhi. Thirteen times it was destroyed, and thirteen times, like the fabled phoenix, it rose again from its ashes. Its last rebirth took place under the supervision of Rai Bhoe, a Bhatti who had converted to Islam. The village also lay in the path of numerous mendicants, holy men and *sadhus*, Hindu ascetics, who camped in a grove of trees on its outskirts.

The same story is told on the ground. Behind Gurdwara Janamsthan rises a mound that Iqbal Qaiser describes as an archaeological mound: the settlement there was destroyed, and Rai Bhoi di Talwandi was then built within a boundary wall. Nanak, in his account, was born in the new Nankana.

Tradition names the families of the village. Rai Bhoe and his son Rai Bular were of the Bhatti. Shiv Ram, head of a Bedi family, was accountant to Rai Bhoe, and was succeeded by his son Kalyan Chand Das Bedi. In the story of Nanak's youth, his father Mehta Kalu, a Hindu, keeps the accounts of the ruler of Talwandi, Rai Bular Bhatti, who is his friend.

Guru Nanak was born in Talwandi in 1469. Accounts differ on the day, which is given as 14 April or as 20 October. One account places his birth elsewhere, but the other writers of the *janamsakhis* and Sikh scholars are unanimous that he was born in Rai Bhoi di Talwandi. He lived in his grandfather's house in the place where he was born.

The land around the village enters the story of his birth. Mata Tripta, the mother of Guru Nanak, had no child for a long time after her marriage, it is said, and would regularly visit an ancient Hindu temple on top of a mound not far from the village to pray for one. In another telling she presents herself to the *sadhus* and fakirs, wandering mystics, in the forest around Talwandi.

Tradition shows the boy at school in Talwandi with a brilliance never seen before in any child of the village. He met ascetics and Sufis on its outskirts and spent time at the abandoned mound there, in conversation with the yogis, *sadhus* and dervishes who passed through. It is believed that he began to frequent the mound in the six or seven years between the incident of Sacha Sauda, the true bargain, and his move to Sultanpur.

His circle in the village appears in the stories too. Bebe Nanaki and Jai Ram came to visit Talwandi from Sultanpur, and Mardana was Nanak's best friend from Talwandi. When Nanak declared that there is no Hindu and no Muslim, his friends and family at Rai Bhoi di Talwandi questioned it. As a youngster in Talwandi he planned a visit to Katas Raj.

The story of Sacha Sauda begins and ends in the village. Nanak was given goods to be sold in Talwandi, and the story ends in the forest and in Talwandi. In the version a storyteller at Sachkand tells, Nanak's father grew worried, the mules were loaded for Nankana Sahib, and there the father asked what trade his son had done. The way home from Chuhrkhana to the town, then still called Rai Bhoi di Talwandi, crossed a vast jungle that lay at that time between Nankana and Chuhrkhana.

Talwandi is the place Nanak's travels leave from and return to. Sultanpur lay around 60 km away, and Nanak travelled there from Nankana. His first *udasi*, one of his long journeys, passed from Sultanpur to Talwandi. On one return his father Mehta Kalu, disappointed, said nothing when Nanak left again, and Sulakhni and her two children took leave of him with Mehta Kalu. On another departure Nanak and Mardana left the familiar streets of Talwandi while friends and families gathered at the edge of the town.

When Nanak and Mardana at last came back to Talwandi, Mata Tripta felt great joy, and Nanak spent time each day with Rai Bhoe, who looked upon him as his Guru. Mehta Kalu was a little in awe of his son. After a journey to Pak Pattan Nanak travelled back to Talwandi, which by then had become a place of pilgrimage, and sat by Rai Bular, who was sick and failing fast. Nanak sent for his family from Talwandi to Kartarpur, which lies 180 km from Nankana Sahib by way of Lahore, and Kartarpur in turn became a town of pilgrims, like Talwandi and Sultanpur.

Partition emptied the town of its Sikhs. "There was no Sikh left here after partition. Our Gurdwaras became barren and were in a bad state", a Sikh of the town named Kartar recalls. Most of the Muslims of Nankana Sahib today, Iqbal Qaiser explains, came from across the border after Partition, and their attitudes were shaped by those experiences. From a rooftop the remains of a few Hindu temples could be seen, cone like structures rising from houses abandoned at Partition, and the traditional war games of Guru Gobind Singh's birthday ended here at the same time.

The years of the Khalistan Movement, as a Sikh of the town named Rajbir puts it, were "one of the most tumultuous times in the city's history". In the Musharraf era, from 2001 to 2008, new roads and other government facilities reached the city, and in 2005 Nankana Sahib was separated from Sheikhupura and made an independent district, with the city as its headquarters. By the early 2010s its population was over a million.

## Holy Places

Because Guru Nanak spent his early years here, Nankana Sahib has several gurdwaras, six or seven by different counts, most of them associated with incidents from his life, and pilgrims visit them all. For most of the year they stay locked, opened only at the time of a festival, and security guards stand at the entrance to every one.

From a rooftop the town shows the minarets of its mosques, the small white domes of its gurdwaras, the remains of a few Hindu temples and the railway station. One main road runs through the whole city. The buildings are old, most of them from the late British or the early Pakistan era, and the road has potholes.

Behind Gurdwara Janamsthan, away from the hub of the new settlement, rests the mound of the older village. The climb is steep, with no proper path, to a height of at least twenty metres, over ground covered with small shrubs of *karir* and scattered with shards of broken pottery and other mud utensils. It was there, it is believed, that the young Nanak talked with the yogis, *sadhus* and dervishes who passed through. Another mound stands near Shahkot, only a few kilometres from the holy city, and tradition places, not far from the village, the ancient temple on a mound where Mata Tripta prayed for a child.

A Muslim saint keeps the Guru company here. At the shrine of Guru Nanak in Nankana Sahib there is a picture of Baba Farid beside the picture of Guru Nanak.

## Communities

The Sikhs of Nankana Sahib today are not natives of the city. Most are recent migrants from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, where there is a considerable Sikh population. Within ten years, by the early 2010s, the community grew almost sevenfold, from only about thirty to forty Sikh houses to about two hundred, as families came after the alienation that followed the wars of 1965 and 1971 and the demolition of the Babri Mosque in 1992. Refugee families moved to Peshawar, Hassan Abdal and Nankana Sahib and began living at the gurdwaras. For several years Jatha Singh has played the role of the community's leader.

Most of the newcomers were housed in the gurdwaras, in rooms usually kept for pilgrims, and the local community helped them with small businesses or with rent. The influx gave a boost of confidence to Sikhs who had until then been dominated by the Muslim majority. Families arrived at different times. Kartar's was one of the first to move to Nankana Sahib. Rajbir and his cousin moved from Parachinar at a time when there were hardly any Sikhs in the town. Boota Singh's family came from the Tribal Areas, and Inderjit's moved to Nankana in 1965. Madan Singh's family has lived there since the late seventies, and he is one of the richest Sikhs of the city, while Baljeet's is one of the latest families to have moved there.

The newcomers brought their languages. Surjeet and Udam speak Urdu with a Pashtu accent, their families having moved to Nankana a few years before; not all the Sikhs here can speak Urdu or Punjabi, and the commentator at a cricket match shifts between Urdu and Pashtu. Kartar remembers a professor at Nankana Sahib remarking in class, during his undergraduate studies, that Punjabi is a language of the unrefined.

A number of Sikhs of the town practise *hikmat*, traditional medicine, as *hakeems*, following a practice that goes back several generations. Dr. Manoj Singh, of one of the Pathan families that settled here after the unrest in the tribal areas, now lives in Lahore but travels to Nankana Sahib regularly and treats old people free of charge. More and more Sikh boys and men leave for bigger cities such as Lahore, Faisalabad and Gujranawala, to work in grocery shops, the cloth trade and *hikmat*. Surjeet's cousin Manjeet has an MBA and works in Lahore, and Udham Singh, originally from Nankana, has his business in Gujranawala.

The town carries Nanak's name in its trade. There is a Guru Nanak High School, a Guru Nanak grocery store and a Guru Nanak cloth shop, all run by Sikhs; the Muslims of the town generally refrain from honouring Guru Nanak in this way. Guru Nanak Government College has only a few Sikh students, and most are Muslim. Madan Singh, a successful businessman and a former head of the PSGPC, runs Guru Nanak High School, "the only school in the country where the Granth Sahib is taught to Sikh children", the Granth Sahib being the Sikh scripture.

Living side by side has its lines. At a juice shop there are separate glasses, and a few shopkeepers in the town keep separate utensils for Muslims and for others. Aslam Chaudary, who has kept a restaurant on the main artillery road for ten years, turns Sikh customers away with "We do not have your utensils", and complains that "We become prisoners within our own city"; yet not all traders are resentful, since the pilgrims boost the local economy. During one Sikh procession through the town, Muslims in the shops and on bikes watched with burning curiosity. One visitor found that the respect for Nanak shown by the Muslims around Sachkand was not to be found among the Muslims of Nankana Sahib; most of them, Iqbal Qaiser explains, came from across the border at Partition.

Sikhs of Nankana Sahib also keep other places. The Pathan Sikh caretakers of Rori Sahib moved there from Nankana Sahib, and Baba Bair is said to be run by a few Sikhs living in the town.

## Gurdwaras, Custodians and the State

The first care of the gurdwaras after Partition came from India. Giani Pratap came with an Indian pilgrimage party sometime in the sixties and, seeing the dilapidated state of the shrines, stayed to look after them. It was the first time since Partition that anyone had taken it upon himself to care for the gurdwaras of Nankana Sahib. He housed Kartar's family inside a gurdwara for a short time and made Kartar's father a *sewaadar*, a worker at the gurdwara. Giani Pratap later died in Lahore. A speech by Chauhan invited Pakistani Sikhs to move to Nankana Sahib to take care of the gurdwaras.

In 1985 the PSGPC had not yet been formed, and the SGPC, based in Amritsar, managed the Pakistani gurdwaras through the Indian High Commission in Islamabad, whose staff received the pilgrims at the border and arranged their stay inside the gurdwaras. That November, at Guru Nanak's birthday and a year after Operation Blue Star, thousands of Sikh pilgrims came to Nankana from all over the world. After the Akhand Path, the unbroken reading of the Sikh scripture, and the *bhog*, the ceremony that concludes it, Sardar Ganga Singh Dhillion, an American Sikh, spoke for a separate Sikh state.

During the speech the gate of the gurdwara was closed, and Counsellor B. Jain and First Secretary K. K. Khanna were attacked and barely escaped alive. Madan Singh, only nineteen at the time, was among the attackers; he was arrested with twenty five others and held at Sheikhupura Jail for ten days. Afterwards banners reading "Khalistan Zindabad" and pictures of Sant Jarnail Singh Bhindrawale hung all over the gurdwara. Today the religious festivals at Nankana and in other cities of Pakistan have gone back to being religious festivals.

The PSGPC's standing in the town is disputed. Several people in Nankana Sahib feel that it does not represent the Sikh community of Pakistan, and Madan Singh says of its current head, "He never visits the city".

Guards, police and officials surround the gurdwaras and the festival. Security guards stand at the entrance to every gurdwara, and at Guru Nanak's birthday the police are posted at all the entrances to the city and no car is allowed inside. Representatives of the ETPB and the PSGPC receive the Indian pilgrims at Wagah, where a *langar*, a free communal kitchen, is laid on for them. The name of Kar Seva appears on yellow dustbins and other boards in the gurdwaras of the city. Sikhs living abroad have asked for an international hotel and for visa free status as a holy city for Nankana Sahib, so far without progress.

## Festivals and Practice

On 10 January 2011 Nankana Sahib kept the birthday of Guru Gobind Singh. The ground of Guru Nanak Government College was reserved for the Sikh community that day, and a twenty over cricket tournament was played among Khalsa XI, Nankana XI and Singh XI. Two Sikh boys gave a live commentary, a *dhol*, a large drum, was played, a dismissed batsman danced the *bhangra*, a Punjabi folk dance, women watched from a tent and a few Muslim students looked on. It was the first time such a tournament had been arranged. The festival closed at the gurdwara at eight on the morning of 11 January, with people who worked away from the town back in Nankana, trophies brought from the main shrine, the tallest of them for Khalsa XI, and a communal prayer.

These celebrations are new. Lohri, the Punjabi winter festival, and Guru Gobind Singh's birthday had begun to be kept only a couple of years before, made possible by the growth of the Sikh population through migration from the tribal areas after the war; before that there was only a prayer at the gurdwara. That year's Lohri was an attempt to revive this cultural tradition of Punjab. Guru Nanak's birthday, by contrast, was among the events that foreign pilgrims had kept alive.

The Sikh New Year, on 14 March 2011, was quieter. Unlike the other festivals it was not publicised, being not only a religious event but also a political one, with no precedent in Pakistan, and the visit of Sindhi pilgrims coincided with it. Pakistani Sikhs like Kartar follow Purewal's original calendar, which is why the New Year was kept here on 14 March while Indian Sikhs keep it on 13 April. The reading of the Granth in a pavilion ended in prayer, and *kirtan*, the singing of hymns, was sung with tablas and harmonium. A speaker named Kartar Singh declared from a pulpit, "We Sikhs are a separate nation and we will not let the Indian sarkar, which is a Hindu lobby, take our identity away", and the crowd listened indifferently.

Then six *qawwals*, singers of *qawwali*, the Sufi devotional music, in brown shalwar kameez and Muslim caps, sang first to the Muslim saint Baba Fareed. "This is the first time since partition that Muslim Qawwalis have been invited to perform at Nankana Sahib", Kartar said; they had been called from Faisalabad.

The great festival is Guru Nanak's birthday, Guru Nanak Jayanti or *Gurpurab*, the celebration of a Guru's anniversary. Thousands come to Nankana Sahib for it from all over the world. Official estimates put the number at ten to fifteen thousand every year for the festival, which falls in the last days of November; in 2011, when it ran from 8 to 10 November, only three thousand were reported to be attending. Cars are kept out of the city, police stand at its entrances, and devotees walk barefoot. Mahtab Singh, seventy two years old, became a celebrity when his interview was broadcast on national television at the festival of 2010, having vowed to go shoeless throughout his pilgrimage.

Trains bring pilgrims from India, from Delhi, Gujarat and Amritsar, across the border at Wagah, and they stay three days at Nankana. There are three days of Akhand Path, and on the final day the Nagar Kirtan, the procession of hymn singing through the town, follows the *bhog*. The pilgrims then go on to other cities, while richer pilgrims from Britain, America and Canada come by chartered plane and hire buses, and the posters of travel companies promise all the gurdwaras of Pakistan.

The pilgrims buy souvenirs; women wear the *mangalsutra*, the necklace of a married Hindu woman, and *sindoor*, the vermilion worn in the parting of the hair; banners reading "STD" are put up for the Indian visitors, and for these few days the whole city looks like a little India. Tourists on pilgrimage come to Nankana Sahib and Hassan Abdal. The festival is felt far away: Jeeta Singh of Manora holds a small festival of his own every November, when Guru Nanak's birthday is celebrated at Nankana Sahib.

## Nankana Sahib in Books

In the traditional stories of Nanak's life, Talwandi is the village of his birth and youth, and the village changes: by the time he comes back from Pak Pattan, it has grown from an obscure little village into a place of pilgrimage, and Kartarpur in turn becomes a pilgrim town like Talwandi and Sultanpur. An older history of Sufism gives the place a line, as "the village of Talwandi later known as Nankana Sahib", about forty miles south west of Lahore.

Modern travel writing on Pakistan's religious minorities gives the town the most space. There it is one of the holiest cities in the world for the Sikhs, and four of the festivals it follows, Guru Gobind Singh's birthday, Lohri, the Sikh New Year and Guru Nanak's birthday, are celebrated there. It is placed for the reader as "the historical city of Nankana Sahib, associated with the birth of the founder of the Sikh religion", near Itan wali, the village of Aasia Bibi. Small things catch the eye as well: Nankana still has Pepsi bottles with the older logos. And when the birthday festival ends, the town empties and calms down, at least until the next year.

For a writer walking in Nanak's footsteps, Nankana Sahib is "the holy city", a few kilometres from the mound near Shahkot, and Talwandi the small town of his youth, where he met ascetics and Sufis on the outskirts and where his father watched him leave again in disappointed silence. For Jeeta Singh of Manora, Nanak was simply "from Punjab, Nankana Sahib". In a study of the folk religion of Pakistan, the picture of Baba Farid beside Guru Nanak's at the town's shrine of Guru Nanak appears in a discussion of syncretism in the mainstream.
