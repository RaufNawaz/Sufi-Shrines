## Overview

Multan is one of the ancient cities of the Punjab, a city with a fort of brick, standing where three rivers merge before the Indus. By the end of the seventeenth century people of every kind lived there and the goods of every land were bought and sold, and it was a commercial hub for national and international trade, including with Persia, Central Asia and the Arab world. Today it is known as a holy city of southern Punjab, the resting place of the great Suhrawardi saint Baha'uddin Zakariya and of many other saints.

Its story begins in legend and runs through conquest. It is said to have been the capital of the Tuganta kingdom in the time of the Mahabharata and to have been ruled for a thousand years by Hindu and Buddhist kings, and its Hindus still keep the memory of Hiranyakashipu, the tyrant believed to have ruled the city thousands of years ago. In 711 the Arabs conquered Sind and the provinces northward up to Multan. The city later became a dependency of the Fatimids of Egypt, until Sultan Mahmud of Ghazni broke the power of the Ismaʿilis, a branch of Shia Islam, there, and in 1175 Muʿizzu'd-Din Muhammad bin Sam seized it. Delhi sultans, Timur's armies and a dynasty of its own, the Langah sultans, followed. After the Arghun conquest Multan was so ruined that no one thought it would ever be settled again, and the city then passed to the Mughals, fell to the Afghans and then to Ranjit Singh, whose conquest cost the life of its Nawab, Muzaffar Khan.

Multan is a city of Sufis. In the twelfth century many Sufi scholars made it their base, and by the time Guru Nanak came there it was famous as a major centre of Sufi learning and philosophy. Besides Baha'uddin Zakariya, who died in 1267, many saints lie at rest in the city. Tradition in India holds that Shams-i Tabriz was flayed alive by the *mollas*, the clerics, in Multan, where his shrine is still venerated, and for the Sindhi and Panjabi poets Multan stands beside Mecca and Medina as a holy city of the heart. One of its saints protected the city: when a sultan meant to put the people of Multan to the sword after Malik Bahram's revolt, Shaikh Rukn al-Din turned him from it.

It was a city of more than one faith. In Guru Nanak's time Multan was known for the shrines of Muslim saints and for its Jain and Hindu temples, a sacred space for people of all religious denominations, and its *khanqah*, the Sufi lodge, was a meeting place open to both Muslims and Hindus. Today its Hindus number little more than a thousand and their temples from before partition are ruins, but Holi, the Hindu spring festival, which was not celebrated in the city as recently as 2010, is now kept every year on a small scale.

Multan has its own speech, verse and song. By the end of the seventeenth century its people spoke a tongue that mixed the speech of Lahore and of Sindh, and the Siraiki spoken in the city today is closer to Punjabi. The poets Amir Khusrau and Amir Hasan served Balban's son Prince Muhammad at Multan as his boon companions, and the love poems of Fakhruddin ʿIraqi for his master are still sung by Pakistani musicians at the door of the master's tomb. In the modern city musicians such as Surraiya Multanikar are supported by the landowning Syeds of Multan and Bahawalpur and perform at their family shrines.

## History: Legend, the Arabs and the Ghaznavids

Multan's history before Islam is a long one. In the time of the Mahabharata, it is said, the city was the capital of the Tuganta kingdom, ruled by the Katoch clan of the Kshatriya Rajputs, and legend has it that Alexander was hit by an arrow there. For the next thousand years Hindu and Buddhist kings ruled it, and the Chinese traveller Heun Tsang visited Multan in 641. It was the city of gold, and its Suraj temple, the temple of the sun, was claimed to be by far the biggest temple in the subcontinent.

The Hindus of the present city keep the memory of another story. Hiranyakashipu, the tyrant of the tale of Prahlad Bhagat, is believed to have ruled Multan thousands of years ago. Yet the city, as one writer puts it, "continues to move forward unaware of its ancient past and the mythical association that it has with the story of Hiranyakashipu and Prahlad Bhagat".

Multan had its first experience of Muslim armies in the second half of the seventh century, and Muhammad bin Qasim brought it under Muslim rule for the first time. In 711 the Arabs conquered Sind and the adjacent provinces northward up to Multan, and the western provinces of the Subcontinent became part of the Muslim Empire. When Muhammad bin Qasim took Multan, its subsidiary Chunian also fell under his control.

The Carmathians, or Qaramita, an extreme Shia group whose capital was in Bahrain and which conquered central Arabia in 930, ruled northern Sind and Multan, and their branches reached the Indus valley. Tradition holds that Hallaj had been in touch with them at Multan, and this supposed connection was one cause of the suspicion against him. Multan later became a dependency of the Fatimids of Egypt, until Sultan Mahmud dealt a blow to Ismaʿili power there. By the middle of the eleventh century Sufism had reached the country around the city.

The Ghaznavids made Multan theirs. Sabuktigin brought Lahore and Multan into his possession, and Sultan Mahmud marched on the city more than once. Because Daud bin Nasir held a separate rule in Multan, zeal for the faith moved the Sultan, on his second campaign, to take that country from him. He left the direct road and went by a roundabout one, so that the ruler would have no warning and he might fall on him suddenly. On the way Raja Anandpal, son of Raja Jaipal, opposed him, was defeated and withdrew into the hills of Kashmir. Reaching Multan, the Sultan made its ruler submit, fixed a yearly tribute on him and put the commands of the *shariʿa*, Islamic law, into force; but his soldiers could not bear the hot air of Multan and begged to return, and he went back to Ghazni.

On his fourth campaign Mahmud brought the whole country of Multan under his control. The Qaramita there were seized: some were bound and thrown beneath the feet of elephants as large as mountains, those who rejected the commands of Islamic law lost hands and feet, those who had turned from it were beheaded, and those who out of conceit ignored the Sultan's orders lost nose and ear. Daud bin Nasir was taken captive and imprisoned in a fort, where he died a natural death. In the eleventh century Mahmud also damaged the Suraj temple and destroyed its giant idol. He sent a great army with four thousand boats towards Multan and Sindh, and on his return from Somnath he went towards Multan by way of the desert, reaching Ghazni only with great hardship, many of his men dying of thirst and hunger.

Masʿud, returning from Hansi, appointed his own son governor of Multan. Then came the Ghurids. Shihab al-Din, on his first campaign, took the country of Multan out of the hands of the Qaramita and left his deputy there, and the seizure of Multan by Muʿizzu'd-Din Muhammad bin Sam is dated 1175. On a later campaign he came again to Multan and Uch on his way to Gujarat. Under Ghori Multan joined his Delhi kingdom, and the town became studded with mosques as it had once been studded with Hindu temples.

## History: The Delhi Sultans, Timur and the Langahs

Under the Delhi sultans Multan had its own governors. In the reign of Rukn al-Din Firuz Shah the nobles called in the governor of Multan, who gathered a heavy army there and set out for Delhi. The city of these centuries had its governors, its *qazi* or judge, its fort and its townsfolk, and the *madrasa*, a religious college, of Qutbu'd-Din Kashani; its *ʿulamaʾ*, the religious scholars, are remembered for refusing a *fatwa*, a legal ruling, and Maulana Husamu'd-Din was of Multan.

Under Balban the city belonged to the heir to the throne. Prince Muhammad Sultan, heir to the throne, was given the country of Sindh in *jagir*, a grant of land revenue, and was sent, fully equipped, to order the affairs of Multan. When a Mongol army began to harm the people in the country around Lahore and Multan, the prince came out of Multan of necessity and after a short fight met a martyr's death, in what is remembered as the Battle of Multan. Balban then appointed the dead prince's son, Kaikhusrau, at Multan and granted him the parasol and the *durbash*, marks of royalty. By Balban's will Kaikhusrau, who was living at Multan and had come to court, was to take the throne, but Fakhr al-Din by a ruse sent him back to Multan.

Multan was also a place of exile and of refuge. After one revolt the sultan had the rebel seated in a litter and sent to Multan, ordering that he be kept with honour in a house and given every means of pleasure and ease. The sons of Jalal al-Din, Rukn al-Din and Ibrahim, fled from Delhi to Multan, and the sultan sent forty thousand horse against them under Alf Khan and Zafar Khan. The commanders besieged the city, and the princes, unable to hold out, submitted through Shaikh Rukn al-Din. Malik Ala al-Mulk counselled that Samana, Dipalpur and Multan must be made strong.

The rise of the Tughluqs is tied to Multan, though accounts differ on who governed the city when Ghazi Tughluq marched on Delhi; he killed Khusrau Malik and took the throne as Ghiyas-ud-Din. Malik Bahram rose in revolt in Multan and was defeated and killed, and the sultan meant to order a general massacre of the people of Multan, which Shaikh Rukn al-Din averted. In the contest after Firuz Shah, Prince Muhammad Shah wrote orders to the people of Multan, Lahore and other cities that wherever they found a slave of Firuz Shah they should kill him, and in most places there was general slaughter and plunder.

Timur's armies came next. His grandson Mirza Pir Muhammad crossed the Indus, took the forts of Uch and Multan, fought a hard battle near the city and stayed some days in Multan, where he was in 1397. Timur himself then came from Kabul, halted at Multan, put to death those whom Pir Muhammad had taken captive, and left by way of the desert for Bhatnir.

By most accounts it was Timur who gave Khizr Khan the government of Multan. Afterwards Khizr Khan held Lahore, Multan and Dipalpur as far as the borders of Sindh, while Iqbal Khan marched towards Dipalpur and Multan. Khizr Khan came from Multan with a great army as far as Samana and Sirhind, and three times gathered an army in Multan and marched on Delhi; his seat of government was Fatehpur, a dependency of Multan. Later Shaikh Ali of Kabul set out for Multan and was beaten in a battle near the city, then returned to raid the country around Lahore and Multan, and Sultan Mubarak Shah set out towards Lahore and Multan.

The Lodis had Multani beginnings. Malik Bahram, grandfather of Sultan Bahlul Lodi, was a trustworthy man who in Firuz Shah's reign, after a quarrel with his brother, came to Multan and entered the service of its governor, Malik Marwan. His five sons, among them Malik Kala, Malik Firuz, Malik Muhammad and Malik Khwaja, all lived in Multan after their father's death. Later a sultan reached Multan, made the round of the tombs of the shaikhs, sent a force against a Khokhar and returned to Delhi. In those days the Langah people rose in revolt in Multan and raised the banner of sovereignty. When Sultan Mahmud of Jaunpur besieged Delhi, the sultan, who had set out towards Multan, turned back from Dipalpur on hearing the news.

Then Multan became a kingdom of its own. Because of the Mongol attacks that came every year from the direction of Kabul to raid and plunder, the country of Multan was left without a ruler. Its notables and the people of the city met in council, agreed that a ruler was needed to protect the people and keep the affairs of the realm in order, and with one voice raised Shaikh Yusuf Qureshi to the sultanate of Multan. The chief of the Langahs, who held a *qasba*, a small town, of his own, warned the Shaikh that Sultan Bahlul Lodi, having taken Delhi, might march on Multan, and offered him the Langahs' service. He gave the Shaikh his daughter in marriage, always sent her fitting gifts and sometimes visited her.

The visits were a stratagem. The Langah chief brought a body of his own men into Multan to be reviewed, and the guileless Shaikh, unaware of the deceit, showed him favour. The chief drank a cup of the warm blood of a kid and cried out that a pain had seized his stomach. Near midnight he called for the Shaikh's attendants as though to make his last bequests and vomited blood before them, and by this trick his kinsmen were let in from outside the city into the fort. He set men to guard all four gates, let no servant of the Shaikh pass from the fort into the city, posted men through the Shaikh's private apartments, seized and imprisoned him, and took the title of Sultan Qutb al-Din, striking coin and having the *khutba*, the Friday sermon that names the ruler, read in his own name.

Sultan Qutb al-Din ruled the country of Multan with full independence, died a natural death and reigned sixteen years. His son Sultan Husain, a man of enterprise and courage, succeeded him. Near Multan a commander and Tatar Khan, governor of the Punjab, drew up against Sultan Husain, who met them with ten thousand horse and foot; his men loosed their arrows at once, so that thirty thousand arrows fell on the commander's army, which broke and did not draw rein before Chiniot. Sultan Mahmud, son of Firuz Shah son of Sultan Husain, succeeded his grandfather and reigned twenty seven years. His son Sultan Husain was three years old when he was seated on the throne, and reigned for seven years in name only.

The Langahs fell to the Arghuns. On reaching the Punjab, Babur issued a decree granting Multan in *jagir* to Mirza Shah Husain Arghun, ruler of Thatta, and Arghun fought Sultan Mahmud, who died during the fighting. Under the child sultan, Qawam Khan and Lashkar Khan, chiefs of their people with a following of their own, seized most of the districts of Multan, joined Arghun, fought the young Husain and took the city. The conquerors plundered Multan, and its people from seven years old to seventy were taken captive; Sultan Husain was imprisoned and died; and the city was so ruined that no one thought it would ever be settled again. Arghun set a slave of his named Shams al-Din to guard Multan together with Lashkar Khan, but Lashkar Khan took over the revenue and the government, did away with Shams al-Din and ruled independently.

## History: Mughal, Afghan and Sikh Multan and After

In Ibrahim Lodi's time Daulat Khan, governor of Multan, called in Babur, king of the Mughals, from Kabul. Under the Mughals the emperor assigned Lahore and Multan to Kamran Mirza in *jagir*; Kamran summoned Lashkar Khan from Multan, gave him another *jagir* and put his own men in charge of the city, Arghun rule there having lasted four years.

In the wars with Sher Khan the emperor's road of flight ran by Multan. From Lahore the emperor went towards Multan and Thatta, and Khawas Khan pursued him as far as Multan before turning back. With Hindal and Yadgar Nasir Mirza the emperor went by way of Multan and reached Bhakkar, while Khawas Khan, Sher Khan's slave, followed him with a large army as far as Multan and Uch, a town then subordinate to Multan.

The governors of Multan appear at work in the Mughal wars. When Khan Jahan, governor of the Punjab, went after Ibrahim Husain Mirza and Masud Husain Mirza and fought them near a place subordinate to Multan, Masud Husain Mirza was taken and Ibrahim Husain Mirza fled towards Multan and hid in the house of a Baluch. The Baluch seized him and handed him to Said Khan, governor of Multan, and he died of the wound he had received in the battle. The Khankhanan, sent against Qandahar, was ordered on reaching the neighbourhood of Multan to take Thatta first, and halted between Multan and Bhakkar, which was his *jagir*; during his siege of Jani Beg, boats of grain from Lahore and Multan reached his army. Rustam Mirza was given a rank of five thousand and, in *jagir*, the country of Multan and Baluchistan, whose revenue was greater than that of Qandahar.

Dara Shikoh's flight brought the war of succession to the city. From Lahore Dara sent robes of honour to the *zamindars*, the landholders, and the *faujdars*, the military commanders, of Multan, Bhakkar and Thatta, all of which belonged to his assignment. Meaning to go on towards Multan and Qandahar, he left Lahore for Multan on the twenty second of Dhu'l-Qaʿda with fourteen thousand horse, and the imperial court heard that he had reached the city with much artillery and the gear of war. But Dara did not stand at Multan. On the twenty fifth of Dhu'l-Hijja he took money from the treasury of Multan and fled towards Bhakkar, and most of his servants would not go with him. Haji Khan Baluch, one of the chief *zamindars* of Multan, came upon the treasure boats with a band of men and tried to bar their way, but after a small skirmish could not stop them; he later waited on the emperor near Multan and received a robe of honour, an elephant and a horse.

The emperor followed in pursuit. Khalilullah Khan was ordered to wait at Multan until the imperial train arrived, and on the seventh of Muharram the imperial camp stood on the bank where the Ravi and the Chenab flow together, near Multan. The man who held the government of Multan on Dara's behalf submitted, and with him Sayyid Izzat Khan, who received a robe of honour and a rank of three thousand *zat*, personal rank, and five hundred horse. The emperor visited the tomb of Bahauddin, and Lashkar Khan was moved from the *suba*, the province, of Kashmir to the governorship of Multan. After five days near the city, having sent forces towards Bhakkar, the emperor marched from outside Multan on the twelfth of Muharram towards Shahjahanabad, to put down Muhammad Shuja's rising. News later reached the court that Dara had come from Multan towards Gujarat.

At Aurangzeb's death Muhammad Muʿizz al-Din Jahandar Shah was *subadar*, governor, of Multan, and as its governor he joined his father at Lahore. Abdus Samad Khan was appointed *nazim*, governor, of Multan; he died there, and his body was brought to Lahore and buried beside the grave of Nawab Khan Bahadur. The *suba* of Multan was later joined to that of Lahore, and in a time of disorder in Multan Nawab Shahnawaz Khan was killed by a horseman. Under Alamgir II the Afghans took Multan and Lahore, and Multan is among the cities the Marathas, with Sikh help, are said to have conquered.

Then came the Sikhs. Uprisings led by Muslim leaders began in Kasur and Multan, and the rebellion was given a religious colour against Ranjit Singh as an infidel, who in turn employed the Nihangs, Sikh warriors, to give the battles a religious tone. Hafiz Muhammad Jamal, who died in 1811, a successor of Nur Muhammad and a contemporary of Sulayman Taunsawi, is also said to have taken part in resistance to Sikh attacks on Multan. An army under Kharak Singh was sent to conquer Multan, and the city was conquered. When Ranjit Singh took Multan its Nawab, Muzaffar Khan, was killed, and others of the Nawabs' family were captured with their women and children and brought to Lahore, where they were living in the nineteenth century.

Diwan Mulraj, son of Sawan Mal and *nazim* of Multan, raised a rising in the city and had two men titled *sahib*, a title of respect, killed together with the newly appointed *nazim*. Some of the Hindus of the city today lived there before Multan became part of Pakistan, in which it now lies.

## A City of Saints

Multan is a city of saints. In the twelfth century many Sufi scholars made it their base, and conversion to Islam gained momentum in the early thirteenth century. By the time Guru Nanak came to Multan the city was famous as a major centre of Sufi learning and philosophy, its schools fallen into a sense of rivalry. It had its *deras* and *dargahs*, Sufi lodges and shrines, and its *khanqah* was the meeting place and residence of the Sufis, open to both Muslims and Hindus. Besides its named saints, many other saints lie at rest there. In a present day Sufi order Multan stands beside Lahore and Karachi in a network that one member likened to the Internet.

Multan is tied to the Suhrawardi order of Sufis. It is the resting place of the great Suhrawardi saint Baha'uddin Zakariya, who died in 1267. Makhdum-i Jahaniyan was the famous fourteenth century Suhrawardi master of Multan. The Persian poet Fakhruddin ʿIraqi attached himself to Baha'uddin in Multan and dedicated impressive *qasidas*, odes, to him, and soon after Baha'uddin's death ʿIraqi left Multan for Konya. Qutbuddin Bakhtiyar Kaki, coming from Baghdad, met Shaikh Baha al-Din Zakariya at Multan.

Saints of other orders passed through the city. The visit of the Chishti master Qutb ad-Din to Multan, a strategic commercial centre in the Punjab, became the occasion for Farid ad-Din's pledge of allegiance to him. For the saint Lal, Multan was his first destination: a few sacred sites in Balochistan and Sindh are the only traces of the route his journey might have taken there, and he is likely to have first arrived in Multan in 1263. Lal is found in Multan in a rare scene of ecstatic performance in the company of other Sufi figures, and in the Suhrawardi tradition he was initiated there by Bahaʾuddin Zakaria. Shaykh Muhammad al-Husseini al-Jilani, a native of Turkey, travelled to Khurasan and then Multan before settling with his family in Uch, a town near Multan. Zauqi Shah told of a miracle in which he and Muʿin ad-Din Chishti travelled instantaneously from Ajmer to Multan to help a sick friend, and the body of Moosa Pak Shaheed was later moved to Multan.

Multan also has a shrine of Shams Tabriz. Tradition in India holds that Shams-i Tabriz, Maulana Rumi's spiritual beloved, was flayed alive by the *mollas* in Multan, where his shrine is still venerated, though there is no settled view whether the Multan shrine is his or that of a local Sufi of the same name. Shrines to the same person in more than one place are not unusual, sometimes holding an actual relic and sometimes bound to the saint only through a *silsila*, a Sufi chain of transmission.

Not all the city's holy places are great ones. In the provinces of Muslim India and Pakistan there are shrines of women saints to which men are not admitted, and Multan has one. A visitor recalled it as "one such small shrine in Multan, decorated with lovely blue and white tiles", whose cheerful women keepers were no less greedy than the guardians of many other sacred places. Multan was also the starting point of the pilgrim road to the tomb of Sultan Sarwar.

The city's Hindu holy places are remembered too. The Suraj temple was once claimed to be the biggest in the subcontinent, before Mahmud destroyed its giant idol. "There were several historical temples in Multan from the pre-partition days. All of them have become ruins now", one Hindu woman of the city said, and the community has turned other spaces into makeshift temples. The thirty odd women who burned the Holika at Holi are now the only link the city has with the story of Prahlad.

## Communities

Multan has held many peoples. By the end of the seventeenth century people of every kind lived there and the goods of every land were bought and sold. In Guru Nanak's time Multan "was one of the biggest cities of Punjab and was known for the religious shrines of Muslim saints, and Jain and Hindu temples". At his later visit "The Hindus were assured of their superiority, while the Muslims were assured of theirs." Families of Muftis, scholars of religious law, and of Gardezis belonged to Lahore and Multan, and Bedil was the scion of a Qurayshi family that had migrated from Multan two generations before him.

The Baluch appear often in Multan's history. The agents of the Baluch chiefs attended on the governor at Multan, the seat of government; the Baluch handed the fugitive Ibrahim Husain Mirza to the governor of Multan, and Haji Khan Baluch was one of the chief *zamindars* of Multan. The Afghan tribes lived from the borders of Qandahar and Multan to Swat, over a stretch of more than three hundred *kos*, an old measure of distance. The Langahs, who became the city's sultans, were a people whose chief held a small town of his own.

Multan has its own speech. By the end of the seventeenth century its people spoke a tongue in which the speech of Lahore and of Sindh were mixed. Today the Siraiki spoken in Multan is closer to Punjabi, while at Uch it is more like Sindhi, and Multan lies in the Siraiki belt that runs through Multan, Bahawalpur, Mianwali and northern Sind.

Some holy places have been open to more than one community. The Sufi *khanqah* of Multan was a meeting place and residence open to both Muslims and Hindus. A devotee of the Tableeghi Jamaat, a Muslim missionary movement, said that "There are several shrines in the Multan region and in Sindh where people still practise Hinduism and give it the name of Islam", where people light lamps, present offerings and even bow down to the grave, which he called *shirk*, the sin of associating others with God.

The Hindus of modern Multan are few. The Holi locality, next to the railway station, is one of several areas where they live; they number little more than a thousand, and of about a hundred houses in that settlement only about ten to twelve belong to Hindu families. Parvati Devi, a classically trained professional singer and social activist of the city, recalled the mob that came after the demolition of the Babri Mosque, which destroyed the Valmiki temple near her house and wanted to burn her alive. She then took a Muslim name, Shaila Bibi, and stopped appearing on radio and television. Usman, who organised the city's token Holi in 2010, has been alleged to be an Indian agent, and more than once he and his friends have been physically attacked. Multan remains among the places where Pakistan's Hindu communities live.

The city knew harsh times between its communities in the past as well: Mahmud's punishment of the Qaramita, a prince's order that every slave of Firuz Shah be killed in Multan, Lahore and other cities, and the uprisings against Ranjit Singh, which were given a religious colour.

## Shrines, Endowments and the State

Rulers were patrons of the holy in Multan. Balban's son Prince Muhammad twice sent his people from Multan to Shiraz to fetch the poet Saʿdi, wishing to build a *khanqah* for him in Multan and to endow villages for it, but Saʿdi could not come because of his age. Rulers came to the tombs: a sultan made the round of the tombs of the shaikhs at Multan, and the emperor, pursuing Dara Shikoh, visited the tomb of Bahauddin.

The saints also stood between the state and the city. When the princes Rukn al-Din and Ibrahim were besieged in Multan they submitted through Shaikh Rukn al-Din, and after Malik Bahram's revolt it was Shaikh Rukn al-Din who turned the sultan from a general massacre of its people. When Multan became a kingdom of its own, the man raised to the throne was a Shaikh, Yusuf Qureshi, chosen in council by the notables and the people of the city.

In the modern city great families keep up the shrines. The powerful landowning Syeds of Multan and Bahawalpur, who claim descent from the Prophet's family, support musicians such as Surraiya Multanikar: "Their training is supported by the Syed households, and these musicians perform in the family shrines of the Syeds". The affluent Syeds, with their large landholdings, support the shrines of their ancestors, and the families arrange yearly rituals there such as the Eid and the Muharram ceremonies marking the tragedy of Kerbala. The landowning Syeds of Burewala, Multan, are a family of politicians in the Pakistan Muslim League.

Offerings and their keepers have their critics. The women in charge of the small tiled shrine of a woman saint were found no less greedy than the guardians of many other sacred places, and a Tableeghi devotee condemned the offerings presented at the shrines of the Multan region.

Multan's wealth also passed through the hands of the state. Dara Shikoh, fleeing, took money from the treasury of Multan, and Rustam Mirza's *jagir* of Multan and Baluchistan yielded more than the revenue of Qandahar. The river carried the city's trade: merchants from Multan and Bhakkar carried their goods and wares by boat down the river to Thatta.

## Festivals and Practice

Music has long sounded at the tombs and hospices of Multan. A *qawwal*, a singer of *qawwali*, the Sufi devotional music, named Abu Bakr came from Multan to visit Nizam ad-Din's teacher and told of the practice of *samaʿ*, spiritual listening to music, in the hospice of the Multani shaykh. The tender and intoxicating love poems of Fakhruddin ʿIraqi are still sung by Pakistani musicians at the door of his master's tomb in Multan, and performers also sing around the shrines of Multan and Sahiwal to earn a living.

Devotion at the shrines takes many forms. The Syed families of Multan and Bahawalpur arrange the Eid and the Muharram ceremonies marking the tragedy of Kerbala at the shrines of their ancestors. At the shrines of the Multan region people light lamps, present offerings and bow down to the grave, as a Tableeghi devotee described it with disapproval. Pilgrimage ran outward too, along the road from Multan to the tomb of Sultan Sarwar.

Holi has returned to the city. As recently as 2010 festivals including Holi were not celebrated in Multan, and in that year Usman organised a token celebration of Holi in front of the Multan Press Club. Now, though on a small scale, Holi is celebrated every year in Multan and is regularly featured in the local media. There is no central celebration, and the thirty odd women who burned the Holika at Holi are now the only link the city has with the story of Hiranyakashipu and Prahlad.

Multan also has its place in the *Janamsakhis*, the traditional life stories of Guru Nanak. In the story of the bowl of milk and the jasmine petal, the oldest and most senior of the Sufi clerics called a conclave of all the heads of the *silsilas*, the Sufi orders, which lasted four days. At the gates they set an earthen bowl filled to the brim with milk, and Nanak laid a jasmine petal on it. Nanak often came to them to learn, and when he finally left Multan they were sorry to see him go. On his fourth journey, the story goes, Nanak's party first travelled north to Multan.

## Poetry, Music and Language

Multan is a holy city in the vernacular poetry of Pakistan. For these poets the Great Feast is to meet the Beloved, and "Mecca and Medina (and for the Sindhi and Panjabi, also Multan) are inside the heart". The Indian tradition of the flaying of Shams-i Tabriz at Multan enters a poem of Sachal.

Poets gathered at the prince's court in Multan. Amir Khusrau of Delhi and Amir Hasan were at Multan in the service of Balban's son, among his boon companions, receiving salaries and gifts, and one poet spent considerable time in Multan with Prince Muhammad. The prince's wish to bring Saʿdi from Shiraz to Multan failed only because of the poet's age. Fakhruddin ʿIraqi wrote his poems for his master, Baha'uddin Zakariya Multani.

Multani verse has its own genres. The tales of Tamim ad-Dari in Multani verse are among the epic elaborations of early Islamic subjects, and the Multani *marsiya*, the elegy, has been studied as popular religious poetry. The songs about Puran Bhagat spread as far as Amritsar and Multan, where local dialects gave the story unique flavours.

In the verse of Madho Lal Hussein, in an English translation, Multan is a place of fine things: a bride's trousseau gathers "The dress / someone carried across the snows / all the way from Kashmir. The dress / someone brought from Gujarat… The dress bought in Multan". The red wedding dress and the references to Kashmir, Gujarat and Multan come before the image of Krishna with his flute, and all add to the poem's mystery.

The city has its musicians. Ehsan Wagha has worked in Multan as a music director, Surraiya Multanikar is among the musicians the Syeds support, and Parvati Devi, the Hindu singer who took the name Shaila Bibi, stopped appearing on radio and television after the attack that followed the demolition of the Babri Mosque.

## Multan in Books

At the end of the seventeenth century a Mughal account of the *suba* of Dar al-Aman Multan described the city as one of the ancient cities, with a fort of brick. Merchants brought horses of Arab breed from Iraq by way of Qandahar and sold them there, and its people made fine carpets, flowered *shatranji* rugs and chintz in imitation of those of the port. The winter was mild, the summer excessively hot and the rain little, and the heat of Multan had once sent Mahmud's soldiers home. Earlier, Sultan Husain Langah had ruled the city.

The same account set Multan on its rivers and borders. The Indus passed the borders of the Baluch and of Multan, and beyond Multan the five rivers of the Punjab joined it. The *suba* of Lahore had Multan on its west, the Ravi joined the combined Chenab and Bihat near a *sarai*, a travellers' inn, twenty *kos* from Multan, and one stretch of country ran from the borders of Multan and Uch to Thatta and Kech Makran.

A book of the nineteenth century often sees Multan from Lahore: in it a governor of Multan joins his father at Lahore, a governor who died at Multan is buried in Lahore, and the defeated Nawabs of Multan live there. In the legend of Sakhi Sarwar, Multan has a ruler, called both *hakim*, governor, and king, with a fort, a jail and a gate of Multan.

In the Sikh tradition Multan is the meeting ground of Nanak and the Sufis, and the Puratan account of Nanak's first journey, of 1499 to 1506, places Tulamba, the modern Makhdumpur, in Multan. It is also a city of routes. Tulamba lies about midway between Lahore and Multan, by the old Multan road, and Deepalpur stood on an important route connecting Delhi and Multan. An author writing in 1865 spoke of "the desert between Cutch and Multan", and for the scholars of Sufism Multan is a fixed point on the map, by which Uch, near Multan, is placed.

Multan is a place where books are made as well as a place they describe. A study of the Siraiki mystical poetry of Sind was published in Multan about 1976, books on Khwaja Farid were printed there in 1978 and 1983, and Bazm-e Saqafat and Qadimi Kutab Khana are publishers of Multan. The city has a book of its own saints, Bashir H. Nazim's *Auliya-e Multan*.
