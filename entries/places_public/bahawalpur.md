## Overview

Bahawalpur is a city on the edge of two provinces, where the boundary between Sindh and Punjab becomes a blur. It is counted within the Siraiki belt, the lands where the Siraiki language is spoken. Uch Gilanian, the city of saints, lies in Bahawalpur, and Bahawalpur, with Multan, is the seat of landowning Syeds, descendants of the Prophet Muhammad, who support the shrines of their ancestors. Bahawalpur was also an area with a considerable population of Hindus, whose musicians, boys and pilgrims travelled out to festivals and holy places.

## Between Punjab and Sindh

On the road through Bahawalpur, the boundary between Sindh and Punjab becomes a blur. The city is counted as a point in the Siraiki belt. The rural village of Allahabad, in southern Punjab, lies sixty miles southwest of the city of Bahawalpur.

## Saints, Syeds and Patrons

Uch Gilanian, the city of saints, lies in Bahawalpur. A family of Syeds had settled there, in the town where Bulle Shah was born. The Syeds also sustain shrines: Bahawalpur, with Multan, is the seat of the landowning Syeds who support the shrines of their ancestors. A Nawab of Bahawalpur was a patron too. The local Nawab, Nawab being a title of Muslim rulers and nobles, was a patron of Wahid Bakhsh, behind his commission, his schooling and his post in the Civil Secretariat.

## Communities and Festivals

In the early twenty first century Bahawalpur was home to a considerable population of Hindus, and they travelled out to festivals and holy places. Organisers at Bahawalnagar, about one hundred and sixty five kilometres away, called a band of musicians from Bahawalpur.

Boys from Bahawalpur came to the festivals as well. Twelve boys rode on bicycles from Bahawalpur to Navratri, a Hindu festival of nine nights in honour of the goddess, chanting to the Mata, the Mother Goddess, as they came. The Hindus of Bahawalpur went on pilgrimage too: on the way to Katas, buses of Hindu pilgrims from Rahim Yaar Khan and Bahawalpur were also on their way.

## Bahawalpur in Books

Writers have reached Bahawalpur by different roads. For a writer travelling among Pakistan's religious minorities, it is a home of Hindus who travel out: a band of musicians called to Bahawalnagar, boys cycling to Navratri, buses bound for Katas. In a later book the same writer found it a stretch of road where the provinces blur, and the home of Ayesha Siddiqa, who belongs to Bahawalpur.

For a scholar of Sufism in twenty first century Pakistan, the city was a base for fieldwork, and he held interviews there on 28, 29 and 30 April and 2 May 2001. He measured the village of Allahabad from it, named its Nawab as a patron of Wahid Bakhsh, and, for further reading on Uch, pointed to Mas'ud Hasan Shahab's *Khitta-i pak Uch*, published in Bahawalpur by the Urdu Academy. For a study of the female voice in Sufi ritual, Bahawalpur belongs to the Siraiki belt, to the landowning Syeds who support their ancestors' shrines, and to the city of saints at Uch Gilanian.
