## Overview

Karachi is a port and a great modern city, the largest city of Pakistan, which was only a minor port at the time of Partition until migrants from India made it what it is. It is a city of many faiths: one of two important urban centres of Sufism in the subcontinent, the other being Hyderabad in India, and the home of a living Chishti order, of Hindu temples and of most of Pakistan's Zoroastrians. It is also the city from which pilgrims set out for the shrine at Sehwan and for the Hindu temple of Hinglaj.

Its older name survives in legend. The area, it is said, is known as the area of Mai Kolachi, the old woman who tore apart the fish that had killed her son, and the city came to be known as Kolachi after her.

Modern Karachi was shaped in colonial Sindh. Sir Charles Napier, the conqueror and first governor of Sindh, built a Masonic lodge in the city in 1843, a printing press came in the 1850s, and in 1896 the Theosophical Society was founded there. In 1907 the first meeting of the Muslim League was held in Karachi, and the Chishti master Muhammad Zauqi Shah was present at it.

Karachi is a city of *qawwali*, the Sufi devotional music, kept alive there by singers of the Qawwal Bachche lineage, and of the weekly circles of remembrance of a Chishti order whose teaching shaykh lives there. Alongside them stand Hindu temples at Clifton, at Ram Bagh and inside the Port Grand complex, the Parsis' Fire Temple, and the gathering of the Sidis at Manghopir.

## History

Karachi's story begins with the legend of its name. The area is known, it is said, as the area of Mai Kolachi: an old woman of that name, whose son was killed by a fish, tore the fish apart and retrieved him, and the city came to be known as Kolachi. Fisherfolk lived on this shore before Kolachi became Karachi, and "these Hindu fisherfolk are now the only connection" the city keeps with the village of Kolachi, "the village that Nanak once visited". Guru Nanak, the founder of the Sikh faith, may have known some of the city's temples: those at Clifton and at Ram Bagh are both believed to have stood in his time, and he may have visited them, though accounts differ on whether the Clifton temple is the temple of the sea god that Nanak is said to have visited.

In the colonial period Karachi grew as a new emporium of Sindh. Sir Charles Napier, the conqueror and first governor of Sindh, built a Masonic lodge in the city in 1843. A printing press was brought to Karachi in the 1850s, and the Reverend Bambridge held his debates there. The port is a modest structure, a minor tower with a clock placed within it, a symbol of colonial rule.

In 1896 the Theosophical Society was founded in Karachi by Dhunjishaw, Jamshed Nusserwanji and Dayaram Jethmal. It first met at the new Dayaram Jethmal Science College, and Jamshed Nusserwanji then decided to build it a new building with an auditorium, a library and a lecture room. The city had its colleges. Dadi Dhan took an M.A. in Philosophy from Karachi, which was then under the University of Bombay, and Ram Panjwani began his career in education as a teacher at the D. J. Sindh Government Science College in Karachi.

Karachi drew others too. Pandit Brahmashankar Mishra, who lived from 1861 to 1907, spent years in Karachi and Hyderabad. Rochaldas worked as a medical doctor in the sanatorium he had created in Karachi, while Hiranand Advani left his work in Karachi in haste and went to Calcutta. Among the city's charitable institutions was a leper asylum at Manghopir.

The Chishti master Muhammad Zauqi Shah was present at the first meeting of the Muslim League in Karachi in 1907. Accounts differ on when he made the city his home. Karachi was one of the four cities of his newspaper work, and in 1945 and 1946 he published numerous essays in *Dawn*.

The port was also a place of departure. Hajji Imdadullah went from Karachi into exile in Mecca. A *murid*, a Sufi disciple, named Liaqat ʿAli Bhatti was about to sail from Karachi port to Iraq when, it is told, he saw the face of his *murshid*, his spiritual guide, in the water.

Partition remade the city. At the time of Partition Karachi was a minor port city, and migrants from India made it the largest city of Pakistan. Shahidullah Faridi moved from Bombay to Karachi in February 1947 on Zauqi Shah's advice, and following Partition a disciple later transferred a company to Karachi.

The city of the present is a large and troubled megapolis. On a Friday, in the parking lot of the Park Towers mall, the *khutba*, the sermon of the Friday prayer, sounds from the loudspeakers of the mosques. Karachi is a city torn by conflict, where security guards and law enforcement authorities are quick to react, and it has seen violence against Hindus. Its growth has also erased much of the old pilgrim road to Hinglaj: "The ongoing growth of Karachi and the corresponding urbanization of the areas where the early stops occurred caused all but a few of the resting places to completely disappear."

## Holy Places

Karachi has its Sufi shrines. Zaheda, a devotee who came to know the shrines of Sindh after moving to the city, petitioned at the shrine of Abdullah Shah two or three times, without result. The shrine of Sheikh Yusuf Shah is among the shrines with their own local networks. The shrine of Baba Ahmad Shah holds an *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of a saint's death. Near the tomb complex of Shahidullah Faridi the teaching shaykh of his order leads a large circle of *zikr*, the Sufi remembrance of God, in a private home, and a shaykh's personal library is kept in a house nearby.

Manghopir is the scene of a miracle with alligators, as the story goes. The Sidis, a community of African descent, hold an April congregation there, which has drawn the attention of the press, and the place was also known for its leper asylum.

Karachi also has a memorial shrine, told of in the story of Bava Gor's journey: everywhere he stopped, it is said, a shrine was created, and each time one of the Sidis of his army remained in the place. Jamil Dehlavi's film *Immaculate Conception* is about a shrine of the *hijṛā*, the transgender community of South Asia, in Karachi. After the three day annual fair at Sehwan, fakirs, wandering mystics, follow other fakirs on to the shrines of Karachi.

Karachi has its Hindu temples. A large part of Clifton, which has one of them, is built on reclaimed land. Another stands at Ram Bagh, an area now called Aram Bagh, the added letter taking away its religious connotation. Under the roof of the Ram Bagh temple Krishna is painted "with his maidens"; its main door was locked, with a peacock guarding the entrance. The temple stood before Partition, though accounts of its exact age differ.

Behind the Ram Bagh temple are living quarters, most of them Hindu households, with a Muslim family among them, and cows that are probably used in religious ceremonies. The temple is much more elaborately decorated, and its community much larger, than in Lahore.

A third temple stands within the Port Grand complex, and a newly built mosque for tourists stands right on top of it. Women and men enter by separate doors, and food is prepared there, probably as an offering from a rich Hindu. Stairs lead down into water blackened by industrial waste; there are the boats of fishermen and pigeons fed with rice, and rooms are kept for different deities. A mural shows a male deity seated on a giant fish: Jhule Lal.

Karachi is also the seat of two *akhāṛā*s, orders of Hindu ascetics: the Nagnath *akhāṛā* and the Jagnath *akhāṛā*. The city's Parsis, the Zoroastrians of South Asia, say the morning prayer of Navroz, their New Year, at the Fire Temple, and Karachi has a Tower of Silence, where Zoroastrians lay out their dead, which Lahore does not.

## Communities

Karachi keeps a living Hindu community, and Hindus elsewhere look to it for idols. A vendor's idols are brought from Karachi, "where there are still a reasonable number of Hindus living", or from India through illegal channels, and boys from Bahawalnagar bought an idol of Durga there. The Hindu fisherfolk of the shore remain, and women on a boat spoke "a combination of Sindhi and Makrani". Karachi is the home of the Devipujak-Vagri community and the engine of change on the pilgrimage route to Hinglaj. The donors of Melari Mata came from Karachi, and an organisation known as the HSM has its seat in the city; one of its elderly volunteers came from Karachi.

The Parsis are the other community at home here: "The majority of the Zoroastrian community of Pakistan is based in Karachi". Parsis compare it with Lahore. "Karachi is a much more happening city with a huge population of Zoroastrians and other non-Muslims", said Daria Boatwala, where "One interacts with Hindus, Christians, etc. in several social forums"; there she would wear dresses everywhere, and "there are thousands of Parsis in Karachi which makes occasions like Navroz a lot of fun there", though she also called the city "really a desert". For a traditional wedding, Farah said, the Parsis of Lahore "either invite the priest up here or just travel down South to Karachi".

The Chishti order of Zauqi Shah and Shahidullah Faridi drew disciples from abroad. A shaykh of the order met regularly with a group of foreign students at a large Islamic Center in Karachi, lecturing in English and Urdu, and some of the lectures were recorded on tape. "Most of the foreign disciples from the time of Hazrat Shahidullah Faridi" went to the Islamic Center to learn the Qur'an and Arabic at the *madrasa* there, a religious school, and a Malaysian disciple who reached Pakistan in 1978 enrolled at once in a *madrasa* in Karachi. "Just here in Karachi there may be three or four hundred branches" of the Chishti *silsila*, the Sufi chain of transmission, one member said.

People move to and from the city. Zaheda came to Karachi in the early 2000s, when her husband found work in the army. Shah-Bibi was forced to flee Karachi to escape a criminal case after a fire destroyed her blanket factory, and lived in hiding for a few years in a village in Sindh. Among those at the *qadimi ʿalam*, the ancient standard, was a woman of middle age from Karachi, and the sons of landlords live in large urban centres like Karachi, Lahore and Islamabad.

## Custodians, Institutions and the State

The shrine of Sheikh Yusuf Shah had as its *sajjadanashin*, the hereditary custodian, Baba Zahin Shah Taji, remembered as "the late Baba Zahin Shah, leading Sheikh of Karachi".

The Chishti order has its own institutions in the city. "First and foremost among them is Mahfil-i Zauqiyya. Located in Karachi, it publishes the bulk of the original Urdu works of Muhammad Zauqi Shah and Shahidullah Faridi". Shahidullah founded it himself, and it is run by a senior male disciple whom the shaykh expressly ordered to lead the order's publishing. The order keeps separate living compounds for men and women beside the saint's tomb in Allahabad, Karachi and Pakpattan, and a shaykh's library is kept in a house near Shahidullah Faridi's shrine, not inside it.

A holy area has been renamed and a temple built over: Ram Bagh has become Aram Bagh, and a newly built mosque for tourists stands on top of the Port Grand temple. Many works on Lal Shahbaz Qalandar have been published in Karachi, several of them by the Department of Culture, Government of Sindh.

## Festivals and Practice

The Chishti order's week in Karachi has a fixed shape. "In Karachi, the order's current teaching shaykh, Siraj 'Ali Muhammad, leads weekly halqa (communal zikr sessions) on Thursday evenings", and on Sunday mornings he holds a *dars*, an informal public session of questions and answers, at his home. At the order's tomb shrines, though, "Disciples generally avoid zikr in Karachi and Pakpattan altogether".

On 20 February 1983 Sufi Abdul Karim Sumar died during the *ʿurs* of Baba Ahmad Shah in Karachi, in the middle of the recital of a verse by Jami.

The Sidis hold their April congregation at Manghopir. For the Parsis the great occasion is Navroz: its morning prayer is said at the Fire Temple, and its evening is spent at the Beach Luxury Hotel, with lawn games such as the spoon and lemon race and the three legged race, stalls of Pakistani and traditional Parsi food, and dancing.

Karachi is a city pilgrims set out from, and at Navratri, the Hindu festival of nine nights, pilgrims now come from as far as Karachi. Sehwan lies hardly 300 kilometres north of the city, a drive of four hours. Hinglaj is about two hundred and fifty kilometres from the coastal city, and the road route to it starts in Karachi. Pilgrims from India fly from Bombay to Karachi and set out from the city, the 4x4 Off-Road Club leaves from it, and Karachi sends pilgrims at *ʿīd*, a Muslim festival, and in *muḥarram*, the first month of the Islamic year.

## Qawwali and Song

Karachi is a city of *qawwali*. The verses and settings of Amir Khusrau "are favourite songs in Qawwali assemblies all over the subcontinent, but especially in two important urban centres of Sufism", Karachi and Hyderabad in India, "where members of the same Qawwal Bachche lineage actively maintain their special Qawwali heritage".

The city's Qawwals, the singers of *qawwali*, included three of the Qawwal Bachche, Bahauddin, Raziuddin Khan and Manzur Niyazi, together with Pyare Khan, Faruq Ahmad Nizami, Iftekhar Ahmad Nizami, Ghulam Farid Sabri and Madan. Parties heard in assemblies of *samaʿ*, spiritual listening to music, in Karachi also included those of Anwar, Bakhshi Salamat, Bashir Ahmad, Ghulam Farid Maqbool Ahmad Sabri, Kalian, Muhammad Ali Afridi and Tuti, and Ahmad Azad, Ismail Azad, Haji Fayyaz and Muhammad Husain Warsi were Karachi singers as well. Its devotees and experts included Iftekhar Ahmad Khan Adni, Mohammad Vazir Ahmed Kureshy, Ataul Haq Khan, Anwar Enayatullah, Prof. Abul Khair Kashfi, Jaliluddin Faridi, Mrs Zahidi, Mrs Mariam Khan and Mrs Husan Bano Jamil, and Anwar Enayatullah wrote for the *Karachi Morning News*.

The city recorded its music as well as hearing it, at companies such as Rita Recording Co. and Jugun Recording Co.

## Karachi in Books

Writers of different kinds see a different Karachi. For one historian of music it is one of "two important urban centres of Sufism". For a modern Sufi order it is one end of a wide reach: "From Karachi to Kuala Lumpur, these twenty-first-century Sufi adepts perpetuate a tradition that remains grounded in Indo-Muslim history", and one member likened the order's network to the Internet, where "if you come to Lahore it's the same data available on a computer in Karachi or Multan".

For a writer following the steps of Guru Nanak it is a megapolis torn by conflict, whose temples Nanak may have seen. For a traveller among Pakistan's religious minorities it is the city where Hindus still live in reasonable numbers and from which idols are brought, and the city that Parsis set beside Lahore.

It is also a city of colonial and modern institutions, Masonic and Theosophical, medical and educational. The activities of a society in Karachi stopped after the assassination in 2007 of its president, Dara Feroze Mirza, who lived from 1937 to 2007.

Karachi is a city of scholarship and publishing. The Seerat Conference held there in 1975, whose proceedings appeared as *Message of the Prophet*, represented Muhammad as "the model of tranquillity in a turbulent age" and as the "seal of prophecy", while topics such as "The convergence of business management thought to the Holy Prophet's teaching" were also discussed. Many books on the Sufis and poets of Sindh and beyond were published in Karachi: by the Anjuman-i taraqqi-yi Urdu in 1953, by the Iqbal Academy in 1967 and 1968, and by the Sindhi Adabi Board, whose editions include works of Bedil Rohriwaro and Sachal Sarmast, Rashid Burhanpuri's book on Sindhi saints and H. J. Sadarangani's *Persian Poets of Sind*.

Oxford University Press publishes from Karachi, and a doctoral dissertation has been written at Karachi University.
