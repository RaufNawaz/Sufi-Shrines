## Overview

Hyderabad was the late capital of independent Sindh before the British conquest. It is a city of Amil families, a community of Sindhi Hindus, and of Sufis, among them Qutub Ali Shah, whose *dargah*, or Sufi shrine, stands in Jahaniyya, a neighbourhood of the city. It is also a city of Sufi music, where the radio and local recording companies have let singers of Sufi lyrics, women among them, earn a living. Pilgrims travel from Hyderabad to the Hindu temple of Hinglaj, and in 2014 a temple in the city was attacked.

## History

Before the British conquest, Hyderabad was the late capital of independent Sindh. The Talpur mirs, *mir* being a title of chiefs, are said to have looked after the *astano* that Dalpat Sufi founded in the city, an *astano* being a place of residence for a renunciant, one who has given up worldly life. A railway line once connected Hyderabad to Barmer and Jodhpur. On 28 March 2014, a temple in Hyderabad was attacked.

Hyderabad was also a city of lectures, printing and schooling. Annie Besant came to the city, "where she delivered a number of lectures", and her speech in Hyderabad was about Hindu religion. A printing house founded in the city was named Blavatsky Printing. "In 1901, aged 22, Rochaldas married and settled in Hyderabad to enrol as a student at the medical college of the city", and Mulchand studied up to class 6 at the Hiranand Academy in Hyderabad.

## Holy Places

Tando Jahaniyya in Hyderabad is counted as one of three Sufi traditions. Qutub Ali Shah was a famous Sufi from Hyderabad, and his *dargah* is in Jahaniyya, a neighbourhood of the city. The *dargah*'s *sajjada nashin*, its hereditary custodian, travelled to perform its rites. He performed the *dastarbandi*, the ceremony of tying the turban, for two people, and for it came from Pakistan twice, in 1957 and in 1980.

The city also holds the shrine of Qutb Din Darya, a *pir*, or Sufi spiritual guide. His shrine is called Tando Jahana, and it stands in the Civil Lines at Hyderabad.

Dalpat Sufi's *astano* stood on the Sipahimalani Lane. After his death it became a pilgrimage centre under the guidance of his successor, Keshavram Sipahimalani, and it was known as Guru Sehwani's place. There is also a huge sanctuary close to Hyderabad, and the city has the graveyard where its great musicians are buried.

## Communities

The Sipahimalanis were a well known Amil family of Hyderabad, and it was on the Sipahimalani Lane that Dalpat Sufi's *astano* stood, with Keshavram Sipahimalani as his successor there. Hiranand Advani was born in Hyderabad into a family of Khudabadi Amils, and Molchand Kripalani was born in the city in 1887 into a Krishnaite family, devoted to the god Krishna. Dalpat Sufi drew followers of both faiths: he is said to have attracted many followers, Muslims and Hindus alike.

People from Hyderabad also travel to Hinglaj. Visitors come from the city to Hinglaj at *mīlād-al-nabī*, the festival of the Prophet's birthday, and at *ʿāśūrah*, the tenth day of *muḥarram*, the first month of the Islamic year. Pilgrims also come from Hyderabad to Hinglaj at *ʿīd*, a Muslim festival, and in *muḥarram*.

## Music, Radio and Women's Voices

Hyderabad is a city where Sufi singers can live by their art. Taj Mastani left Sehwan for Hyderabad because it is a large urban centre. There, she affirmed, she could earn a living from the radio, and negotiate business contracts with television stations and with her clients from the affluent feudal households of Sindh.

By the early 2000s the local recording companies in places such as Hyderabad and Faisalabad had helped women musicians earn a living by recording their Sufi lyrics with music. Their songwriters were male relatives or men in the community, and the trade had become "a source of income for many women and men".

At Radio Pakistan Hyderabad the director was Sikander Baloch and the music director Naseer Mirza. The radio in Pakistan has played a significant role in identifying musicians and giving them a livelihood. Baloch and Mirza affirmed that they no longer had to go to the *melās*, the fairs, at the Sufi shrines to hunt for talent. Many educated performers, women and men, came to the radio and television themselves for auditions and were contracted to sing.

The city keeps musical lineages and a resting place for its musicians. There are two *gharānās* in Hyderabad, a *gharānā* being a hereditary school of musicians: the Indore *gharānā* and the Gawalior *gharānā*. Zeena Bai is buried in the Hyderabad graveyard where all the great musicians of the city lie.

Hyderabad is also a place where this music is recorded and studied. The Institute of Sindhology in Hyderabad is a source for its documentation, and it publishes a cassette of the musicians of Bhit Shah along with several written works.
