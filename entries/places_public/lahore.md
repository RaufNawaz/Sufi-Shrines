## Overview

Lahore, an ancient city on the bank of the Ravi, is first of all a great city full of places of prayer. By the end of the seventeenth century craftsmen of every land lived there, and in every street and bazaar there were mosques in great abundance. Its story runs from legendary founders through the Ghaznavid sultans, who made it the first centre of Persian inspired Muslim culture in the Subcontinent, to the Mughal emperors, the Sikh maharajas and the British, who made it the capital of the Punjab. It was ruined by the Mongols, plundered by Nadir Shah and Ahmad Shah Abdali and taken by Ranjit Singh, and under the British the new railway became the chief cause of its progress.

At the heart of its sacred landscape stands Data Ganj Bakhsh, the shrine of ʿAli Hujwiri, who wrote his famous Persian treatise on Sufism in the city and is called the patron saint of Lahore. It remains a popular place of pilgrimage and the city's most prominent shrine complex. The city holds the shrines of many other saints: Mian Mir, whose village stands inside his own enclosure, and who with his sister Bibi Jamal represented the Qadiri order in the Mughal city; Mauj Darya Bukhari and Sayyid Chiragh Shah Gilani, who founded quarters of the outer city; and the great burial ground of Miani. The holy places are not only Muslim. The Sitla Mandir at Shah Alami, the Jain Mandir at Anarkali and the Bhadra Kali Mandir at Thokar Niaz Baig are among its prominent temples, and for Sikhs the city is where Guru Ram Das was born and where Guru Arjan was tortured outside the Alamgiri gate of the fort.

Lahore's communities lived side by side, sharing some holy places and fighting over others. By the 1880s its Muslims were counted as Sunnis, Shia, Wahhabis and Sufis, the last bearing enmity to no one. On Sundays Hindus and Muslims alike brought their sick children to bathe at Shah Dargahi's well; at the Guru Ram Das gurdwara *qawwals*, singers of the Sufi devotional music called *qawwali*, sang *kafis*, Punjabi Sufi lyrics, of divine unity; and Hindus, Muslims and Christians all gave to repair the Badshahi Masjid. There was conflict too: a Mughal governor ordered the Sikhs seized and killed, Shia and Sunni came to blows near Shah Alami Gate in 1849, and Ranjit Singh renamed the Moti Masjid the Moti Mandir. Today the city's religious minorities are Hindus, Christians, Sikhs, Baha'is and Zoroastrians; it is now rare to meet a Sikh in a city the Sikhs once ruled, and only a handful of Parsis remain.

The holy places had an economy and a politics of their own, and every regime remade them. By the 1880s more than seventy *mujawirs*, the shrine attendants, divided the offerings at Data Ganj Bakhsh in equal shares, while the temples inside the walls lived on offerings alone, with no *jagir*, a grant of land revenue, and no fixed stipend. Under the Sikhs mosques became powder works and the stone of Muslim tombs went into new buildings; under the British a tomb could be auctioned on an Englishman's application and made into a *kothi*, a residence, and the railway took temple land into its yard. The state's repairs to Lahore's buildings in the 1880s ran through its Executive Engineer, Kanhaiya Lal, himself a historian of the city. The city's calendar was crowded with fairs, or *melas*: the greatest was Bhadar Kali's at Niaz Beg in the month of Jeth, which Muslims too mostly attended, while Mian Mir had five a year, Data Ganj Bakhsh a Friday fair and its *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death, in Safar, and Madho Lal Hussain his Fair of Lamps near the Shalimar Garden. Lahore is a city of poets too: the birthplace of Ghaznavid poets, the city whose streets Madho Lal Hussain roams with fellow carousers in his verse, which was first published there around 1900, and a home of printing houses that issued mystical poetry.

## History: From the Founding of the City to Babur

How Lahore began is told in more than one way. In one telling Raja Ramchandar had two sons, Kushu and Lohu, and it was Lohu who settled the city. A tradition still told today holds that Lahore and Kasur are named after Luv and Kush. The city's name has also been written Lahur and Lahawar, and the old name of the Ravi, the river that flows beside it, is remembered as Parsuni.

Before the Mughals, the city's history is a history of conquest. When Sabuktigin first invaded Hind, Raja Jaipal ruled at Lahore, and Sabuktigin, having won the Afghans over, brought Lahore and Multan into his possession. Defeated, Jaipal asked that a trusted man of Sabuktigin's come with him to Lahore to collect the tribute. At Jaipal's court the Chhatris stood on the left and the Brahmins and Khatris on the right; the Chhatris urged the raja to keep his word and the Brahmins urged him not to pay. Jaipal broke the agreement and imprisoned the envoy, and Sabuktigin marched on Lahore. The rajas of Delhi and Ajmer came to Jaipal's aid, and the Hindus were defeated.

Sultan Mahmud, on his ninth campaign, failed before a fort in Kashmir and then set out towards Lahore and Bangar, took plentiful booty and returned in triumph. Over long ages the old city had fallen into ruin and little trace of it was left, so that Sialkot had become the seat of government of the province. After Mahmud's conquest, Malik Ayaz rebuilt the city and built a *pakka* fort, one of solid brick.

With the Ghaznavids, about the year 1000, came the second wave of Muslim conquest in India, bringing scholars, theologians and poets into the Subcontinent. Lahore became the first centre of Persian inspired Muslim culture there, the capital of the Ghaznavids in India. In this town ʿAli Hujwiri spent his last years and composed the *Kashf al-maḥjūb*, the first major treatise on Sufi teaching in Persian, and he died there in 1071. Not everyone welcomed him: a number of mystics and scholars settled in Lahore were strongly hostile to his views, and some Lahore Sufis believed that gnosis, the direct knowledge of God, came from inspiration.

Lahore was a refuge and then a capital for the sultans who followed. Mas'ud, hard pressed by the Turkmans of Tughril Beg, was forced to set out towards Lahore, and on the way his army looted the treasury at the Indus and put the blinded Muhammad back on the throne. In Maudud's time the Hindu rajas besieged Lahore, then the capital of the people of Islam, until the besieged drove them off. When Ala al-Din Husain Ghuri drove Khusraw Shah, son of Bahram Shah, from Ghazni, Khusraw Shah turned to Hindustan, took Lahore and, out of necessity, made it his capital, governing the Punjab until his death in the territory of Lahore in the year 555 AH. His son Khusraw Malik succeeded him and ruled at Lahore for twenty eight years, while Shihab al-Din raided the city again and again and wore him down. In the end the Ghurids took the country by a thousand tricks and deceptions, and Ghurid rule began in Lahore too.

The Ghurid campaigns against the city came one after another. On the third, Khusraw Malik sent his son with an elephant as *peshkash*, a tribute offering, and made peace, and the Sultan fixed a yearly tribute. On the fifth, Shihab al-Din besieged the city, Khusraw Malik shut himself in the fort, and the Sultan, unable to master Lahore, plundered the country round about. On the sixth, Khusraw Malik submitted, was taken to Ghazni and died there, and the Sultan installed his own deputy in Lahore, though accounts differ on whether he took the city. Later, when the Khokhars rose in the country round Lahore, the Sultan came from Ghazni to the city, Qutbuddin Aibak joined him there from Delhi, and after punishing the rebels the Sultan left Lahore for Ghazni.

Under the first Delhi sultans the city changed hands repeatedly. Aibak came from Delhi to Lahore, became sovereign there and later fought Yildiz over the city; he died playing polo in Lahore in 1210. Aram Shah was enthroned in the delightful territory of Lahore, but when Iltutmish came he moved out towards Delhi and fled after a brief fight. In Iltutmish's reign Muftis, scholars of religious law, were appointed for the city of Lahore, and one family still counts its ancestors among them. Sultan Jalal al-Din, routed by Chinggis Khan, came and besieged Lahore and afterwards fled towards Sind and Siwistan. Through these centuries Lahore also stood on the roads of the Sufis, on one of the main routes from Multan to Lahore and Delhi; Lahore had become the capital, and Sufis and their families came from Lahore to Delhi.

The Mongols came next. In Bahram Shah's reign, on Monday the sixteenth of Jumada al-Akhir 639 AH, the armies of Chinggis Khan's people laid siege to Lahore. The governor, Malik Qaraqash, finding the people unwilling to stand together, slipped out of the city at midnight and went to Delhi, and Lahore was ruined and laid waste, and a great many of its people were taken captive. The Mongols' destruction of Lahore is dated 1241. The wazir Nizam al-Mulk, sent towards Lahore to drive the Mongols off, instead turned the amirs against the Sultan on the bank of the Beas near Sultanpur, and they marched on Delhi.

The raids went on. In Balban's reign the Mongols raided the country round Lahore and Multan. In Kaiqubad's reign they reached the country round Lahore again, laid waste its towns and villages, killed its people, carried off women and children and burnt its settlements, until the Sultan's army met them there, defeated them and brought the captives before him at Delhi.

Through the fifteenth century Lahore was a prize fought over by the Khokhars, the Sayyid sultans and the Lodis. Timur's grant to Khizr Khan was headed by the government of Lahore, Multan and Dipalpur. Shaikh Ali reached Lahore, took its fort and left twelve thousand picked horsemen to hold it; later he plundered round Jalandhar and brought many captives into the city, and Jasrath came from Jalandhar and besieged Lahore, lifting the siege when the wazir Malik Sarwar reached Samana. After Mubarak Shah drove Shaikha Khokhar beyond the Chenab, he camped at Lahore, resettled those whom Shaikha's oppression had ruined and ordered the fort repaired and rebuilt.

Shaikha besieged the city a second time without success, turned on Kalanaur, and killed Raja Bhim Jammuwal, who had come out to help the governor of Lahore. In the year of Nasir al-Din Mahmud Shah's accession an army was sent against Shaikha, who had seized Lahore; he was beaten twelve *kos*, an old measure of distance, from the city and fled to the hills, and the country round Lahore was rid of the trouble. Letters were then sent to the people of Multan and Lahore ordering the slaves of Firuz Shah's house to be killed, and massacres followed in most places.

The Lodis then made the city theirs. In Ala al-Din's reign Malik Bahlul acted independently from Lahore and Dipalpur as far as Panipat; Sultan Muhammad Shah gave him the title of Khan Khanan and the province of Lahore and Dipalpur, and under Hamid Khan's vizierate Sirhind, Sunam, Lahore, Dipalpur and Hisar Firuza were taken by force one by one. For a time no sultan of Hind lived in Lahore and the city declined, until Tatar Khan, one of the amirs of Sultan Bahlul Lodi, made it his seat of government.

Sikh tradition sets Guru Nanak's second journey in this Lodi Lahore, by then the leading town of the empire north of Delhi. Its governor, Daulat Khan Lodhi, had risen to rule Lahore and the lands around it, and was away in Delhi at the time. An evening gathering for the Guru drew a large crowd. Duni Chand, the *amil*, or revenue collector, of Lahore, kept a *haveli*, a great mansion, at the centre of the town, set in a large compound of its own, and seven red pennants flew over it, each standing for a *lakh*, a hundred thousand, of rupees. Nanak gave him a needle to return "when we meet next in the other world", and Duni Chand and his wife became disciples and he gave away his seven lakh rupees. From Lahore, the story goes, Nanak went on to Sialkot.

Daulat Khan Lodi turned against Sultan Ibrahim and went over from Lahore to Babur. Babur's fourth expedition from Kabul reached as far as Lahore and Dipalpur. Before his last campaign Daulat Khan and Ghazi Khan wrote from Lahore asking him to come, Daulat Khan himself set out from Lahore for Kabul, and once in revolt the two meant to fight the imperial amirs who had already reached the city. Attacking the Punjab, Babur destroyed the cities of Eminabad and Lahore on his way, and his conquest of the city is dated 1523 to 1524.

## History: Mughal Lahore, the Invasions and the Misls

After the defeat of Rana Sanga, Babur gave his son Prince Kamran Lahore and Multan in *jagir*, and Kamran came from Kabul to Lahore and summoned Lashkar Khan there from Multan. The city flourished while Kamran Mirza lived there. Muhammad Zaman Mirza came from Gujarat across the desert towards Lahore and stirred up trouble. After his second defeat Humayun came to Lahore and held a council with his brothers, each of whom spoke against his wishes, and Kamran left with Askari for Kabul. After the defeat at Kanauj the emperor could not stay at Agra or Delhi; he rested a few days at Lahore and went on towards Multan and Thatta, while Sher Khan's men rode as far as Lahore.

Islam Shah came to Lahore with guns dragged by men, a thousand or two thousand to each gun. Because Lahore was a great city, he reasoned, the trappings of royalty, the equipment of armies, plentiful troops and every means of war could be gathered there in a short time, and it was the gate by which the Mughals came in from Kabul; so he meant to lay the city waste and make Mankot his capital, but the plan never took shape. Azam Humayun, called Haibat Khan, raised the standard of revolt at Lahore. When Humayun returned with Prince Muhammad Akbar and three thousand horse, the Afghans scattered and Lahore passed without a battle into the hands of the emperor's servants. At Akbar's accession at Kalanaur, Shah Abu al-Maali was arrested and sent to Lahore, and escaped through the negligence of the *kotwal*, the city's police chief.

Under the Mughals Lahore became a seat of power. After Hemu took Delhi, Akbar gathered a force at Lahore and marched on Delhi. From the start of his reign Akbar turned to the settlement of Lahore: he built a city and a palace, his *daulat khana*, raised large buildings and stayed there for a time, and for a while he moved his capital to Lahore. Malik Ali was kotwal of Lahore. When Muhammad Hakim Mirza rose in revolt at Kabul, he crossed the Indus again and again and once besieged the fort of Lahore for twenty two days, while Raja Bhagwant Das, the *subadar*, or governor of the province, held it firmly; the Mirza withdrew without gaining his end after Kunwar Man Singh defeated him.

While the court was at Lahore, Zain Khan Koka was sent against the hill zamindars, the landholders of the hills, and Rustam Mirza was received a stage from the city, and grain boats went from Lahore and Multan to the army before Thatta. Todar Mal stayed behind in Lahore when the court went to Kashmir, and died there. After the emperor's fall from an elephant, rumour spread, the revenue went unpaid and the roads grew unsafe, until the disturbance subsided when the imperial standards came to Lahore. Lahore also stands in the story of Nur Jahan's family: her father, Khwaja Ayaz, travelled with his wife and the infant in a caravan as far as Lahore, and there he took service and rose to be *mir munshi*, chief secretary, under Akbar.

The city also grew beyond its ancient walls. Settlement outside the walls began in Humayun's reign and moved south, south east and east, until a city twice the size of the original stood outside it. In one suburb no deed of sale or mortgage was counted valid in Mughal times without the seal and signature of its Mughals; under Ranjit Singh the Baloch were dominant there; and the Arains arrived in the famine called the *Chalisi*, the Forty famine. In the nineteenth century it was still remembered that Lahore had once been settled as far as Mozang, and as far as the site of Hashim Pura.

The names of the city's gates carry their founders' bargains and orders. The Bhats made it a condition with the ruler that a gate be called after them, and he agreed. Shah Alam had it proclaimed that one gate should henceforth be called Shah Alami. Akbar set up a market for every kind of grain inside another and named it Akbari Mandi. Inside Taksali Gate stood the royal mint, of which only the remains of the mint's mosque survive, and Roshnai Gate, in origin a gate of the fort though counted among the city's gates, was lit every night by royal order. In the nineteenth century the city had twelve gates, among them the Lahori, Shah Alami, Mochi, Akbari, Delhi, Kashmiri, Masti, Roshnai, Taksali, Bhati and Mori Gates, and the Delhi Gate was famous.

Jahangir's reign opened with the revolt of his son Khusrau. Khusrau and Hasan Beg Badakhshi plundered their way to Lahore, where the *diwan*, the chief revenue officer, Abd al-Rahim, joined them; but the subadar, Dilawar Khan, would not meet them and made the fort fast, and however hard the prince tried, it did not fall to him. News of Khusrau's capture reached the emperor in Kamran Mirza's garden at Lahore. Hasan Beg was sewn into an ox hide and Abd al-Rahim into a donkey hide and paraded through the city, and from Kamran Mirza's garden to the palace gate Khusrau's companions were impaled in two rows while crowds came out to watch. Khusrau was led between the stakes on an elephant and then imprisoned in the fort.

The same reign is remembered in Sikh history. Guru Arjan was summoned to Lahore by Jahangir and died there, and Chandu Lal was appointed Diwan of Lahore by the Mughal authorities. Sikh tradition traces the quarrel between Prithi Chand and Guru Arjan to the Guru's trip to Lahore.

The end of the reign also played out in Lahore. After Mahabat Khan's victory, Nur Jahan fled towards the city. Jahangir, ill on his way back from Kashmir, came to Lahore and died there. Prince Shahryar, who was in the city, laid hands on its treasuries and workshops, but when Dawar Bakhsh, set up by Asaf Khan, reached the country round Lahore, Shahryar's ranks broke at the first shock. He took refuge in the fort, was brought out with his hands tied by his own waistband, imprisoned and, two days later, blinded. By Shah Jahan's orders Dawar Bakhsh, his brother Garshasp, Shahryar, Tahmuras and Hoshang were put to death in Lahore.

The dead emperor's body was brought to Lahore and buried north of the city, on the bank of the Ravi, at Shahdara, four miles from the city. From the minarets of his tomb the whole of Lahore could be seen, with the Badshahi mosque, Wazir Khan's mosque and its other buildings. Shah Jahan's coinage and *khutba*, the Friday sermon read in the ruler's name, were then fixed in the hall of audience of the palace in the capital, Lahore. Shah Jahan, too, stopped in the city, and he is said to have had many buildings raised there; Nawab Wazir Khan was his *nazim*, or governor, of Lahore. The Shalamar garden in Lahore was Shah Jahan's memorial, a garden such as the eye of the age can seldom have seen.

The city's great buildings belong to this age. Akbar built a fort, the city wall and the palace; Jahangir raised lofty buildings that were still standing in Aurangzeb's reign; the princes and amirs built mansions, Asaf Khan's above all; and the city grew further under Shah Jahan. Great amirs raised large buildings of stone, and those of Abu al-Hasan, son of Asaf Khan, were among the chief ornaments of Lahore; the great buildings stood until the reign of Alamgir. The city's splendour came from its tiled buildings: besides the Jami mosque of Nawab Wazir Khan, the city proper had three mosques with tile work, the first of them the mosque of Muhammad Salih Kambo, whose inscription speaks of the Valley of Ayman, of the sanctity of Lahore and of the grace of the Haram.

Mughal Lahore was a regional capital and a seasonal one, home to the court in its perennial rotation. A large public square stretched before the high walls of the fort, called the Red Fort, and crowds surged into it to watch the public execution of criminals: rebels against the Mughal emperor, highway robbers and tax evaders. The city's religious and moral life was governed through its officers: the kotwal, the chief judge, the *Shaykh al-Islam*, the chief religious scholar and jurist of the realm, and the *Qadi*, the judge, who set *taʿzir* penalties at his discretion, with the emperor's court above them all. *Hadd* was exemplary punishment of a fixed form dictated by scripture; *taʿzir* was discretionary punishment of a flexible form determined by the will of the authorities.

The war of succession brought Dara Shikoh to Lahore. He left Delhi for the city meaning to seize the imperial treasury, the arsenal and the artillery and raise an army; he entered the imperial fort, took the treasury, arsenal, artillery and workshops, gave freely, and horsemen gathered to him. Men joined him there, among them Khanjar Khan, *faujdar*, or military commander, of Bhera and Khushab, and Guru Har Rai came to Lahore with his forces and met him. Raja Rajrup left a son and agent with Dara, who slipped away by night, and Guru Har Rai left the same way. When the imperial army approached, Dara could not stay, and left for Multan carrying all the stores and treasures of Lahore, more than a crore, ten million, of rupees in *ashrafis*, gold coins, in rupees and in uncoined gold and silver, with the workshops' goods and most of the artillery. The man who betrayed Dara, having handed him over in Delhi, set off towards Lahore and was killed on the road.

Khalilullah Khan and Raja Jai Singh sent officers to secure what was left in the city of Lahore, which was without a governor, and to reassure its people. The two commanders camped outside the city on the day of Eid al-Adha and, ordered not to enter Lahore, moved to Ichhra. On his own return Aurangzeb camped at Ichhra, received there the amirs who were in Lahore, and the next day rode an elephant towards the city. He paused at the gate of the fort to inspect it, dismounted at the Wazir Khan mosque and prayed the midday prayer there in congregation, and camped in the Shalamar garden, staying some days to settle the province. During this stay Khalilullah Khan was made governor of Lahore, with a *mansab*, an imperial rank, of six thousand.

In the succession war after Aurangzeb, Muhammad Mu'izz al-Din, governor of Multan, came to Lahore with a large army and stores to his father, Sultan Mu'azzam Bahadur Shah, and both went on to Akbarabad. Shah Alam died in the city of Lahore. Zakariya Khan was appointed nazim of Lahore through the support of the Sayyid brothers, his father moving to be nazim of Multan, and he continued in Lahore under Muhammad Shah; he had a great passion for building. Mir Momin Khan became nazim of Lahore in Muhammad Shah's time, though he did not rule on his own: the real nazim of Lahore was another Khan. Most of the buildings between the Mochi Gate and the Kashmiri Bazaar are Mir Momin Khan's memorials.

In time the province of Multan was joined to Lahore. Mir Mannu, also known as Moin-ul-Mulk, governed at Lahore in Muhammad Shah's reign; the Sikhs had grown in number, and he gave a general order to seize and kill them. Under Nawab Abdullah Khan, nazim of Lahore, a famine fell on the city and he squeezed its people. Khwaja Abid, subadar of Lahore, attacked Charat Singh's fort, the *garhi*, and victory fell to the Sikhs.

The invasions that followed fell hard on the city. After Shahnawaz Khan's defeat Nadir Shah plundered Lahore thoroughly, taking cash and goods from Begumpura, while Shahnawaz Khan's petitions to Delhi went unanswered. In Ahmad Shah Abdali's wars Lahore was the prize possession of the Punjab. In the attack of 1751 his forces reached Lahore and laid siege to it, and for months the citizens were trapped within the walls; his men raided the villages around the city for supplies, and an informant from within told him of the Sikh village of Maraka, a few kilometres from Lahore.

At one of Ahmad Shah's assaults the city was put to the sword. The story is told that when he came to Lahore, Shah Kaku Chishti assured him that this was his own city and was sent as an envoy, and the man he went to had him killed, although killing an envoy is forbidden. Drowned in grief and anger, Ahmad Shah crossed the river and ordered a general massacre, and he watched it sitting at a gate. Shah Nawaz Khan answered him that if Ahmad Shah was a merchant he should sell him, if a tyrant imprison him, and if a king forgive him; he was spared, and Ahmad Shah conferred the crown and set off for Hindustan.

Hafiz Lutfullah lived until the time of Nawab Zakariya Khan, after which Ahmad Shah Abdali carried out a slaughter in Lahore and Begumpura was plundered; whenever Ahmad Shah came, Hafiz Lutfullah's family moved into the walled city, going back to Baghbanpura when he left. Under Alamgir II the Afghans attacked the Punjab and took Multan and Lahore. Ahmad Shah Abdali had given the government of Lahore to a woman of Pathan descent; Ghazi al-Din arrested her, and Abdali invaded a third time. The Marathas, with help from the Sikhs, are said to have conquered Delhi, Agra, Multan and Lahore and driven the Afghans back across the river at Attock.

In the eighteenth century the city lived by ransom, tribute and self defence. When the Sikhs demanded fifty thousand rupees in cash as a *nazrana*, a gift offered to a superior, the sum was settled at twenty thousand; they took it and withdrew, and Ahmad Shah then imprisoned Karim Dad Khan for paying it and sent him to Kabul. On another occasion the noses and ears of several butchers were cut off, and the city paid a large nazrana to have the Sikhs leave; the *misls*, the Sikh confederacies, had demanded that the butchers who slaughtered cows be killed or handed over to them, and the matter was settled by the same mutilation. The city paid tribute to an invader, a man was beheaded for collusion, and plunder was redistributed among the poor subjects of the Punjab.

The quarters of the city bought off their plunderers or defended themselves. The Khojas put the raiders off each time by paying them money, and the walls of their mosque served as their defence. Ten thousand rupees were paid as nazrana to Jai Singh Kanhiya, whose promise in return was in name only. The Mughals of Mazang survived sometimes by flattery and entreaty, sometimes by bribery and sometimes by force of courage. Dai Anga's *mohalla*, or quarter, had pakka gates and a rampart, and inside the walls the lanes were barred against the Sikh army so that it could not lay its hands on the city as it wished.

The Sikh period began in fragmentation. After Mir Mannu's death, wherever any Sikh found the chance he set himself up as ruler. Lahore then had three rulers, among them Lehna Singh and Gujjar Singh, who built a fort of his own, and the time when the three rulers were appointed in Lahore became a date by which other events, such as the resettlement of a village, were remembered. The ruin of the city recurs in its memories. In the story of one *gaddi*, the seat of a spiritual line, it is said that after Jiju Bhagat merged into the spot where he sat, the city was ruined; and the mohalla of the Tamarind *takia*, a faqir's resting place, was deserted when the city lay desolate.

The settlements outside the walls did not survive. When the Mughal sultanate fell the outer mohallas emptied, and when the Sikhs laid waste the settlement outside the city, one mosque escaped only because it was too solidly built to pull down. The ruins became a quarry. The foundations of the outer settlements were dug for forty years under the Sikhs and for years more under the English, and still the bricks did not run out, until the English gave an order that no one should take bricks out of the ground. The trade made fortunes: Kashmiris struck by calamity, busy digging and selling bricks, became *lakhpatis*, men worth a lakh of rupees, and often came upon buried treasure as they dug.

The brick trade moved from site to site: the walls of Langar Khan's and Daya Lado's quarters, the Lakhi mohalla, Zakariya Khan's graveyard, the *chowk*, or square, of Dara Shikoh, and Mughalpura, which yielded so many bricks that thousands of mansions were built from them. At Ali Mardan Khan's complex the Kashmiri brick sellers of Lahore did the work, and Sardar Gulab Singh pulled down the remaining buildings for bricks for his cantonment. Brick sellers took the fabric of Khawand Mahmud's *rauza*, or tomb, and a Kashmiri brick seller that of Sayyid Abdul Wahab Qadri's tomb. The foundations of Abul Hasan Khan's tomb, which had cost ten lakh, were dug out so that no trace was left.

Sardar Gujjar Singh carried away the bricks of Begampura and founded the village of Qila Gujjar Singh with them, and a thousand rupees' worth of the bricks went into the building of the cantonment. Colonel Gulab Singh, of Court Sahib's platoons, took the bricks of the tank of the Qasab Khana mosque for his own house. Brick sellers took the walls of Bagh Nau Lakha and the bricks of the tank at Ichhra, and the old settlement on which Bagh Lala Ratan Chand was later laid out had been pitted by brick sellers before it.

A mosque could be mined in the same way. The *lambardar*, or village headman, of Mazang claimed Shah Badr's mosque, so that it was taken out of the *nazul*, the government's land, and passed to him. He then began to pull it down, treating it as a mine of bricks, sold thousands of rupees' worth, some of them to the government, and dug out its foundations; the government refused to let him dig out the bricks of its well because of the gunpowder buried in it. Muhammad Sultan the contractor pulled down many old mosques and buildings that were memorials of former kings, only out of greed for bricks, and of one mosque he bought and pulled down it was said that he martyred it. His contract turned a Mughal monument into a bazaar and a sarai, and he took over Dara Shikoh's mosque, pulling down the cells of its plinth and two of its domes before his death, so that one dome of that mosque now stands as before and the rest has fallen.

## History: Ranjit Singh, the British and Modern Lahore

Lehna Singh, one of the sardars of Lahore and its ruler, began destroying the monuments of former kings, and the elders of the city invited Ranjit Singh to come to Lahore and take possession of it. Shah Zaman gave Ranjit Singh leave to take Lahore if he could, and he took it. In 1799 Ranjit Singh was greeted enthusiastically by a population sick of the lawlessness of that time. The Maharaja then brought all the *chaudhris* and *numbardars*, the village notables and headmen, of the country round the city under his rule.

Ranjit Singh's Lahore was full of works and courtiers. Nura, having gained access to the Maharaja's government, was appointed to build the moat of Lahore. A weapon made by Ahmad Shah was placed at the *baradari*, a pavilion open on every side, of Nawab Wazir Khan. The first of the misls was of the Jat people of the district of Lahore, with ten thousand horse, and the sardar of the third misl became ruler of the eastern districts of Lahore. Khushal Singh received *havelis* and gardens in Lahore and Amritsar, and Dina Nath built a large *shivala*, a temple of Shiva, in the city. Among the nobles and chiefs of Lahore who visited Mai Bhagi were Raja Dina Nath, the Sandhanwalia sardars and Raja Dhian Singh.

Under Sikh rule mosque after mosque and tomb after tomb became a powder magazine, an arsenal or a gun platform. Abd al-Razzaq's *maqbara*, or tomb, and his mosque were full of gunpowder, with iron shot made in the courtyard. Shah Badr's mosque held powder and shot and a company of soldiers, gunpowder was made in its *hujras*, its cells, and all the powder was later sunk in the mosque's well and earthed over. The Khojas' mosque, the sole surviving building of their quarter, was kept filled with gunpowder. The Masti Darwaza mosque was under government control and powder was made in it for years, until it was known through the city as the powder works mosque.

In Ranjit Singh's time Daya Anga's mosque was kept full of powder and shot for years on end. General Gulab Singh filled Khawand Mahmud's rauza with powder and kept it locked for years, and under the British the rauza was emptied. Muhammad Salih Sindhi's mosque was used the same way by Ranjit Singh's order. At the Nila Gumbad the tomb was filled with gunpowder, and a separate building was put up for the smiths, in which they made guns. The Kambo tomb was used likewise, and the Pari Mahal held troops, shot and powder.

Two of these stores exploded. The powder sunk in the well of Shah Badr's mosque went up when a neighbour lit a fire on the filled well: the ground shook as in an earthquake, bricks flew far, the ground was pitted for fifty yards, and the zamindar or zamindars there were killed. At the Pattharan-wali haveli, run as a gunpowder factory, several thousand maunds of gunpowder blew up in an instant, the Mochi Gate quarter was demolished, and about two hundred lives were lost.

The Badshahi Masjid passed from royal splendour to military use. In the royal era its floor coverings, lanterns and other furnishings were worth lakhs of rupees; it then served sometimes as an artillery park and sometimes as a cantonment for infantry and cavalry, and its floor was plundered. Ranjit Singh had the turrets of its minarets pulled down, so that all four minarets were left without their domes. In Rani Chand Kaur's time Sher Singh mounted *zamburaks*, small camel guns, on the minarets and shelled the fort, while the shot from the fort fell inside the mosque, and so it went on for three days; Raja Hira Singh did the same during the Sandhanwalia siege.

For his own projects Ranjit Singh had the marble and stone of Lahore's tombs taken up, site by site. At Sayyid Abdul Wahab Qadri's tomb the floor inside and the floor of the grave were taken up by his order and the grave was knocked down. At Shahzada Parvez's tomb the marble floor was taken up. At the graveyard of the Gilani Sayyids the stone was taken off the graves, so that the graves fell in, and the grand marble dome over Sayyid Qasim's graves was pulled down, its stone taken and an enclosure wall built. Shah Rustam Ghazi's marble grave was knocked down too.

The list runs on. Sayyid Ahmad's marble at Bibi Pak Daman was taken. At Pir Hadi's tomb the marble lattices and red stone columns were taken down by Raja Dhian Singh and carried off. Makhduma Begum's grave was once of marble, and the Sikhs knocked it down. At Nawab Mian Khan's tomb Raja Suchet Singh broke the marble *taʿwiz*, the raised stone over the grave, and destroyed the grave: only the platform remained, and the place where the taʿwiz was broken off could still be seen.

The stone had two purposes. When Ranjit Singh needed stone to send to Amritsar, and it was proposed to build a stone baradari in the Hazuri Bagh, stone was taken off many of the tombs of Lahore. Countless stone *rauzas* and tombs of Muslim nobles then stood outside the city, and the order was given that their stone be taken off and set into the Hazuri Bagh baradari: first the stone of Zeb-un-Nisa's tomb, then Shah Sharaf's, and in the same way the stone of many tombs, to finish the building. Even so the stone ran short, and the floors of the lower, second and third storeys were made not of stone but of lime.

Stone also passed between traditions by written order. From Jahangir's tomb the columns for lighting candles and the marble lattices were prised up and taken to Amritsar, to be set on the bridge and elsewhere at the Sri Darbar Sahib; countless stones were sent there, and lime work was put in their place. The stone of Khwaja Muhammad Saeed's demolished mosque and tomb, marble and red stone, was sent to Amritsar. From Shalamar Bagh countless marble slabs were taken down for the building of the Darbar at Amritsar, and its red stone slabs were set into the Ram Bagh. One exception to the destruction is remembered: Khushhal Singh's garden was levelled, but the *samadh*, the memorial shrine, of Ram Singh survived, because Shiva Ji had also been installed in it.

Under Kunwar Nau Nihal Singh a belt half a mile wide was cleared round the walled city. The order was that for half a mile outside the wall, on all four sides of the city, the ground be made an open plain, the buildings knocked down and the trees cut. The work began outside the Delhi Gate, and in it the mosque at Shah Muhammad Ghaus's tomb fell too, with its inner and outer enclosure walls. The people of Lahore, Hindus and Muslims, raised a great outcry and wished the holy place saved from demolition, but no one listened. The same shrine later lost its western wall to a government garden laid out by filling the city ditch.

After Ranjit Singh came the court's wars and the annexation. After the deaths of Maharaja Kharak Singh and Nau Nihal Singh, Raja Dhian Singh summoned Sher Singh to Lahore to take the throne, and he came and pitched his camp near the cantonment of the Khalsa army and was enthroned. The Sandhanwalia party came into the fort and killed Raja Dhian Singh, and Sher Singh's killers were dispatched after the siege of the fort of Lahore and a great battle. Suchet Singh was killed in Lahore. In Raja Hira Singh's wazirship Raja Suchet Singh lodged in Mian Wada's *madrasa*, a religious school, and on Hira Singh's order the Sikh army, foot and horse, surrounded the *khanqah*, the Sufi lodge, and attacked it. Suchet Singh was killed, and many of the khanqah's dervishes and about a hundred and fifty of Suchet Singh's companions died.

Raja Hira Singh and Pandit Jalla were killed on the bank of the Ravi on the Id of sacrifice, and their heads and bodies were seen hanging, the heads staying there for some days. After their defeat in battle, the troops fled, looting, into Lahore. Under the treaty of 1846 territory passed from the government of Lahore, and at the request of the people of the state of Lahore a Resident was appointed in the city, while disorder continued. In the end Duleep Singh left Lahore.

Under the British, Lahore was made the capital of the Punjab, and Delhi itself became subordinate to this province. By the 1880s the Sadr Bazaar of Anarkali ran for about a mile, with trade in lakhs of rupees, as though a second city had been settled outside the first, and the population had grown to twice any earlier one. The chief cause of the city's progress was the new railway. Rajab Ali Khan, who had known the city before, found Lahore adorned, with clean roads and lofty buildings, thousands of times more splendid than he had seen it.

Under the English the city's own defences were remade. Akbar's wall stood until the end of Sikh rule; then so high a wall was thought useless, and first about half of it and then the rest was pulled down, and a low masonry wall built in its place. The bricks of the ditch were lifted, the ditch filled with earth, gardens laid out on that ground and a canal set running. Gate by gate the city changed: the Delhi Gate was demolished and rebuilt in two storeys; the Akbari rebuilt anew on the old pattern; the Mochi demolished and its bricks sold; the Shah Alami and Lahori rebuilt on the old pattern; the Mori widened; the Bhati rebuilt in the English style; the Masti pulled down by order of the English officials; the Kashmiri rebuilt in the new English style, on the pattern of the Bhati Gate; and the Zaki, or Makki, demolished, to be rebuilt soon. Of Akbar's original fabric, only the Delhi Gate kept any.

The railway decided which *sarais*, the inns for travellers, prospered. At Muhammad Sultan's sarai outside the Delhi Gate thousands of travellers rested every day, with a bazaar beside it. Outside Shah Alami Gate stood Diwan Ratan Chand's sarai and tank; the tank was always full, and thousands of men and women came from the city to bathe there.

Diwan Ratan Chand built a shivala there, and his son built a *thakurdwara*, a temple of Vishnu, on the southern bank of the tank. The complex had rooms on all four sides for travellers, two masonry shops flanking the gate and shops along the bank where traders lived, and salt, ghee, oil, grain and goods of every kind from outside were sold there, so that something like a great market gathered. It was built in the period of English rule by a serving Honorary Magistrate of Lahore. Mela Ram's sarai outside Bhati Gate, by contrast, failed because it lay too far from the railway station.

The Municipal Committee sat under the Deputy Commissioner, and its native members were appointed only with the consent of the city's people. One gate held a court, a municipal chamber and a police station at once: police sepoys lived below and behind, and above were rooms for the sittings of the honorary magistrates and the members of the Municipal Committee. A sanitation department was appointed and the bazaars were swept and sprinkled twice a day. The tank of a scheme for piped water shattered within an hour for want of concrete, and the scheme was suspended from that day. Municipal drainage once worked in a shrine's favour, filling the stinking tank at Haveli Mian Khan at government expense. Wages rose: in the Sikh period a mason earned four *annas*, sixteenths of a rupee, a day and a labourer two; by the 1880s a mason earned eight annas and a labourer four, and master masons were paid from one to two rupees a day.

The new public buildings of the British city each had a date and a cost. The *kotwali*, the police headquarters, was rebuilt at twice its former size. Lawrence Hall was finished in 1862, and the museum dates from 1864. The government hospital was completed after three years of building, and the New College was begun in 1872 and finished in five years. The Medical School was begun in 1881.

The Sadr Kachahri, the district court, cost one lakh of rupees. The Senate Hall was paid for with thirty four thousand rupees from the Nawab of Bahawalpur, a gift recorded in English on a carved stone. The New College cost three lakh twenty thousand rupees of government money. For the Art School forty two thousand were sanctioned, thirty eight thousand spent on the school and four thousand on staff quarters, and the telegraph office cost thirty thousand and a further fifteen thousand, forty five thousand in all. The government spent thousands of rupees on the first Punjab exhibition and thousands every year on Government House.

Montgomery and Lawrence Halls were raised by subscription, the nobles and officials of the Punjab giving most willingly, and the subscribers' names are engraved twice inside the hall, once in English script and once in Persian. The slate of the New College came from the Dalhousie hills and its upper columns are of black Chiniot stone, the stone that runs through all the buildings of Nawab Mian Khan, who also built a mosque of it at Chiniot. Four iron girders carry the ceiling of the Art School hall, iron girders stand in the Medical School, and iron sheet covers the truss of Montgomery Hall. The whiteness inside the Mian Mir church was made with marble ground from an old building outside the city.

Prisons changed with the regime. Under the Sikhs the kotwali was given nothing to feed its prisoners: every day in the third watch the chained prisoners were sent into the city to beg, one sepoy to every seven, and the sepoy took half of what they were given. Punishment was mutilation at the post called Lal Lakkar, hands, ears, noses and feet cut off after five to ten days in the lockup, or else a fine. Under the English the same labour was brought inside the walls. The Central Jail had large workshops for weaving shawls and carpets and a printing press doing work worth lakhs of rupees, so that the prisoners learnt a trade, the government profited, and the prisoners never left the jail.

The women's jail took women prisoners from every district of the Punjab, held new arrivals apart for forty days, and had workrooms in which the women worked. In the solitary blocks one prisoner lived to a cell under hard labour, with both doors locked at all times, twenty four prisoners to a block. Race and class were written into the plans: there were buildings for European prisoners and for European women, separate lockups at the court for native and for European accused, separate buildings for European insane men and women, and railway station rooms graded first, second and third class, with separate seating for memsahibs and for Punjabi and Hindustani third class travellers. At the asylum one garden was kept so that the insane could be brought there for recreation. The hospital had a smallpox house, a building for examining the dead, a servants' latrine and a house for destroying bedbugs with an iron tank and a furnace, and it kept two wells, the ancient great well with a wooden Persian wheel and a new small one dug for drinking water.

Buildings changed their uses as the regimes changed. When the army moved to Mian Mir, the empty barracks of the Anarkali cantonment became the district's courts, so that all the *kachahris* gathered in one place; a hospital became the *Munsifi* court, the court of the civil judge; and a temporary exhibition became the permanent museum, which it remained. One Mughal baradari in Wazir Khan's garden passed through European soldiers, the office of the Settlement department, the Telegraph department, the museum's stores and a library, and remained in government hands.

The Mughal gardens passed into named hands. Angoori Bagh went into the possession of Pandits after Ranjit Singh; Bagh Aluwalia went to Sardar Fateh Singh Aluwalia, its gardeners cultivating its land for their own living; Bagh Lala Ratan Chand went to Lala Bhagwan Das, with European gentlemen paying rent as tenants in its four towers; and the gatehouse of Bagh Nau Lakha went to Sardar Lehna Singh Majithia, who made it his own residence, and which by the 1880s housed high officials. The Golian Wali sarai at Ichhra became family property when Jamadar Khushhal Singh petitioned Ranjit Singh for it, and by the 1880s it was owned by Raja Harbans Singh.

The city's story runs on into the twentieth century. In 1931 Bhagat Singh, Rajguru and Sukhdev were hanged at Lahore jail. Sir Zaffarullah Khan drafted the Lahore Resolution, which the Muslim League presented in the city in March 1940. Before the partition riots that spread through the city, the Sikh leader Tara Singh took out his *kirpan*, the Sikh's ceremonial dagger, on the stairs of the Punjab Assembly in Lahore and said that the time to take revenge for the Gurus had come. Until 1972, Chunian was part of Lahore district.

## A City of Saints: The Sacred Landscape

Lahore is a landscape of saints. In the nineteenth century the city's history could be told, at root, as a history of its saints: of the graves, *mazars* and tombs of its elders, the sacred tombs on every side of Lahore that stand as memorials of the holy men of the past. Beside them stand the mosques, temples, gurdwaras, churches and graveyards of the city's many communities.

In Aurangzeb's time Lahore was thick with places of prayer, with mosques in great abundance in every street and bazaar. Beside Ali Hujwiri, other intimates of the Eternal Lord lay at rest in the city, and on its outskirts lay many gardens that delighted the heart and thousands of flower gardens that added to its joy. The Ravi added to the splendour of Lahore, the seat of the sultanate. The royal crossing, the *shahguzar*, lay at the foot of the royal buildings, and the royal canal drawn from the river ran to the Shalamar garden.

At the heart of the city's devotion stands the grave of Ali Hujwiri. "His shrine, called that of Data Ganj Bakhsh, is still a popular place of pilgrimage in Lahore", and his tomb still draws the Punjabis on pilgrimage. Data Darbar is Lahore's most prominent shrine complex, and Hujwiri is called Lahore's patron saint. Shah Hussain is counted one of the patron saints of Lahore as well. Madho Lal Husain is buried together with his Hindu beloved in a modest tomb near the Shalimar gardens.

In the Mughal period the Qadiri order, the *Qadiriyya*, was represented in Lahore by Mian Mir, who had come there from Sind together with his mystically inclined sister, Bibi Jamal. A fine biography of Mian Mir and the Qadiriyya in Lahore, the *Sakinat al-auliya*, is associated with the Mughal heir apparent. In Lahore today the Qalandars, wandering dervishes who completely abandon legal norms, social conventions and even distinctions of gender, are still associated with the great saints of the city, such as Shah Hussain, Shah Jamal and Mian Mir.

Not every Sufi welcomes that company. One living Sufi order teaches caution about the city's lesser tombs as a class: "There are many mazars of unknown shaykhs in Lahore. Some of them are qalandars", who "can only give their visitors what they have", so that "they make them qalandars as well". As that order puts it, "In our silsila we avoid this", a *silsila* being a Sufi spiritual lineage.

Devotion did not always need an establishment. In the 1880s the two graves of Pir Zaki had no *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of a saint's death, no custodian, no offering and no endowment, yet devotees came to recite the *fatiha*, the opening chapter of the Quran, at both, and both were visited by high and low alike. The people of Lahore revere the mazar of Miran Badshah. Fakhr-ud-Din, on his first journey toward Ajmer, visited all the mazars of Lahore. Bibiyan Pak Daman is a shrine in Lahore dedicated to women saints and particularly revered by the Shiʿa.

Tradition holds that the saints guard the city and claim it. Of a *majzub*, a faqir spiritually intoxicated and absorbed in divine love, who lies buried at a *khanqah*, a Sufi lodge, it is said that by his kindness Lahore was kept safe from Ahmad Shah Abdali. When Ahmad Shah came to Lahore, Shah Kaku Chishti, before going as envoy to his death, told him that this was his own city. A living saint could protect a whole quarter: Ahmad Shah Durrani came to visit Haji Muhammad Saeed and set a royal guard in his *mohalla*, his neighbourhood, so that no plunderer should touch it. On the outskirts of Lahore stands a small shrine raised by the villagers there.

Saints founded the quarters of the outer city. By the 1880s its mohallas were tied to Mauj Darya Bukhari and Sayyid Chiragh Shah Gilani, to Sayyid Abd al-Razzaq, Shah Sharaf, Shah Dargahi and Shah Badr al-Din Gilani, to Shaikh Muhammad Tahir and Shah Abu al-Maali, to Pir Aziz Mazang and to the faqir Taja. Mughalpura was settled around Khawand Mahmud's mazar. It was the wealthy quarter whose plunder so enriched Ahmad Shah Durrani's army that it had no need to loot the rest of the city.

Villages settled inside shrine enclosures. The village of Begampura stands within one, and the village of Mian Mir within Mian Mir's own. A dervish compound became Garhi Shahu, where possession passed from a plunderer to the displaced residents of a mohalla and then to four Shaikhs. Their descendants still held it in the 1880s, and the Shaikhs also took into their own possession whatever ownerless land lay waste around it. The dervishes' cells and towers round Abul Khair's grave had by then all become houses of *zamindars*, the landholders.

The city has also grown over places that once lay outside it. Mozang, a former settlement outside the city, had become one of the mohallas of Lahore by the nineteenth century, and it is said to have been settled in connection with Pir Shirazi Baloch, to whom the Baloch people of Mozang came and went. Zinjani was buried in Chah Miran, which is now a suburb of Lahore. The shrine of Madho Lal Hussain stands in Baghbanpura, once on the outskirts of Lahore, a neighbourhood now within the expanding city just next to the Shalimar gardens. In Amar Sadhu, an old village now inside the Defence Housing Authority, stand Gurdwara Bibi Kaulan and the shrine of Baba Mastan Shah.

The Sufi geography of the city ran from the empty lands outside the city walls to the Ravi River, and beyond the city to the village of Babupur, far from Lahore. There were graves even inside the fort. In the nineteenth century a building in the fort of Lahore, facing the throne on its south side, held a grave, and common people called it the Nigar Khana.

The great burial ground of Miani lies west of Mozang. Many graveyards and graves lie in the land of the Miani tract. In Akbar's reign, it is said, the place was a village in which only learned men lived, and because Punjabis call the *ulama*, the learned men of religion, *mian*, it was called Miani. Its chief then was Hafiz Jan Muhammad, father of Hazrat Abu Muhammad Qadiri. After Hafiz Jan Muhammad's death Abu Muhammad Qadiri was made its head, but a split arose over a point of the *shariat*, the religious law, many left, and the village was deserted. Another man was then brought in, appointed by the king and settled there, the headship was shared between him and Abu Muhammad, and the place was settled again.

The gravediggers of Miani held that the village was inhabited in the time of Muqim Sahib of the Hujra. When Miani was at last deserted, the village became the graveyard it is now.

Other burial grounds belong to the modern city. A historical graveyard in Sultan Pura, the heart of Lahore, is the seat of the shrine of Ghore Shah. There families gather at the graves on a Sunday morning while children play cricket, and drug addicts "often end up finding a permanent abode in this land of the dead". There is no Tower of Silence in Lahore, and "the Parsi community buries its dead in a graveyard next to the Minar-e-Pakistan facing the ancient walled city of Lahore".

The great Mughal buildings were also settings for devotion. Aurangzeb, entering the city after the war of succession, prayed in congregation at the Wazir Khan mosque and camped in the Shalamar garden. The Wazir Khan Mosque was built in the seventeenth century by Wazir Khan, the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan's governor of Lahore. The Shalimar Gardens were built by Shah Jahan.

The fort and its gates stand at the centre of the Sikh memory of the city. Guru Arjan was tortured outside the Alamgiri gate of the Lahore fort. Guru Ram Das was born in Lahore, Guru Arjan spent much time in the city, where he was killed and his remains interred, and Guru Hargobind also spent much time there. The domes of the Badshahi Masjid face the Alamgiri gate, named after the imperial title of the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb. "The Roshani gate, one of the thirteen gateways that lead into the ancient walled city of Lahore, is between the Alamgiri Gate and the Royal Mosque". It was once reserved for the prince and the princess on their way from the fort into the city, and lamps were lit all along the passageway, which gave the gate its name.

Relics came and went with the regimes. At the Qadam-e Rasul in Begampura a stone once bore the print of the Prophet's foot, and no one knows who carried it off in the time of the Sikhs. At Sharaf un-Nisa Begum's tomb a Quran and a sword were laid out every day after the noon prayer, willed to remain in the tomb. In the Sikh period the Sikhs took them away; they were said to be worth some thousand rupees. At the Imambara, the Shia hall of mourning, the *taziya*, the model of the Imam's tomb carried in mourning processions, is borne on replica graves of Imam Hasan and Imam Husain.

Lahore has temples as well as shrines. A Hindu temple lies in the middle of a congested locality in the heart of the city. The Neela Gumbad Valmiki Mandir stands in the historical market of Anarkali and serves the city's Balmiki Hindus. Among the city's prominent temples are the Sitla Mandir at Shah Alami, the Jain Mandir at Anarkali and the Bhadra Kali Mandir at Thokar Niaz Baig, and among its functional ones the Krishna Mandir on the Ravi road and the Valmiki Mandir at Neela Gumbad. In Lahore, Muslims and Christians whose ancestors were once Hindus still visit a Hindu temple that their ancestors visited.

There are smaller sacred places too. A shrine of cats, the Billiyon Wala Mazaar, stands next to Bhatti gate, one of the entrances to the walled city, and the fact that it lies in the heart of a major metropolitan city is probably why the practice there has diminished much faster. Masjid Shuhada at the Regal Chowk junction is "a small white structure with an open courtyard"; on Fridays it is teeming with pilgrims, and mats are spread on the road for hundreds of men. The churches and the Baha'i centre of the city's other communities complete the landscape. On Mall Road, "the Lahore Cathedral built in the late nineteenth century is a model of the Gothic architectural tradition", and the Baha'i assembly meets at the Baha'i Centre near the Ganga Ram Hospital.

The city is also the resting place of modern figures. Muhammad Iqbal, the poet, who was granted a PhD by the University of Munich, is buried in Lahore, and a *maqam*, a memorial place, has been raised in his honour in the garden of Jalaluddin Rumi's tomb in Konya. Bullhe Shah, whose life ran from 1680 to 1752, lived near Lahore. Faruq Ahmad died in Lahore on 13 February 1945 after a long bout of pneumonia, and it is told that "Throughout the time of the funeral, a light rain fell from that cloud of mercy only above that spot in Lahore".

## A City of Saints: The Fabric of the Holy Places

Not everything that looks new in Lahore is new. In the Sikh period even temples of old foundation were built anew, so that their fabric looks fresh. One site shows the whole story. Its enclosure had belonged to the Wazir Khan mosque in the time of the Muslim kings, and its rents were spent on the mosque. When that rule passed, a man took up residence, obtained a lease from one of the rulers and built a small mud *shiwala*, a temple of Shiva, and Raja Dina Nath then built the present masonry temple at great expense in Ranjit Singh's time.

Several of the city's mosques replace an earlier mosque on the same ground. In the Mehr Das enclosure four of the shrines can be ranked by age, and the oldest is the first and most ancient building of all.

Many buildings carry their dates on their walls, sometimes in a chronogram, a verse whose letters, read as numbers, give the year. The Wazir Khan mosque records that it was completed in the reign of Shah Jahan, the second Lord of the Conjunction, and one of its inscriptions names Wazir Khan as the founder of a second House of God. The Sunehri Masjid is dated on its *mihrab*, the prayer niche, beneath the Ayat al-Kursi, the Throne Verse of the Quran. The mosque of Muhammad Salih Kambo is dated in the three niches over its gate. For the death of Sayyid Ishaq Gazruni the *bismillah*, the words "In the name of God", itself serves as the chronogram.

Other mosques carry dates too, among them the mosque of Takiya Sadhuwan, Moran's mosque, Bokan Khan's and Imam Shah Wali's, while those of Zabita Khan and Sayyid Amir Shah were rebuilt. Masjid Pholiyan Wali was rebuilt in 1282 AH, the year on its marble slab, and Nawab Imam ud Din Khan's mosque is dated 1266 AH. The dome of Data Ganj Bakhsh carries its date, and its mosque was rebuilt in 1868. A mosque's marble founding slab names its founder, a sister of Muhammad Sultan the contractor. Sarai Golian Wali was built by order of Jahangir. Gulabi Bagh carries 1066 as the date of the garden, and its tilework was said to look fresh after two hundred and thirty five years.

Hindu and Sikh foundations are dated in the Samvat, the Bikrami era of the Hindu calendar. The memorial shivala of Sukh Ram Das Khatri Kapur and the two Mehr Das shivalas are dated Samvat 1930. The Devi's mandir followed her appearance in Samvat 1931, which corresponds to 1875, and the donor's brick is inscribed February 1875. The later temples are dated with the same care. Chamna Das's mandir at Tali Sahib is dated Samvat 1898 Bikrami, and Dhani Nath was appointed in Samvat 1880 Bikrami. The Anarkali shiwala and the *thakurdwara*, a temple of Vishnu, of a Jangam, a Shaiva ascetic, are dated Samvat 1910 and Samvat 1937.

Pandit Bishnath's mandir is dated Samvat 1904 Bikrami, Chhujhi Mal's Samvat 1933, Atar Singh Subedar's Samvat 1934 Bikrami, and Dharm Chand's rebuilt shiwala Samvat 1935 Bikrami. Rai Singh Risaldar's thakurdwara carries on a marble slab over its east door its own name, the name of his father Karam Singh and Samvat 1938.

The *samadhs* of the Sikh nobility, their memorial tombs, carry the same era. Ram Singh's samadh dates from Samvat 1895 Bikrami and was built in Jamadar Khushhal Singh's own lifetime. Raja Teja Singh died in Samvat 1919, and Rani Partab Kunwar in Samvat 1914.

The holy places were carefully measured. Mian Mir's platform is nineteen by nineteen *zira*, an old cubit, square. Bahadur Khan's octagonal platform has *mihrabs* four and three quarter *gaz*, the old yard measure, high and two and a quarter wide, a gallery of two storeys in each face, two staircases, and eight corner domelets, of which one had fallen and seven stood in the 1880s. Nusrat Khan's tomb has eight *gaz* of platform clear on every side. Ali Mardan Khan's tomb is three storeys high, with a basement holding three masonry graves, and no tomb in Lahore is as tall.

Jahangir's tomb is measured in feet. It has a marble platform nine feet wide and a foot and a half high, forty cells and four minarets, and of its twelve tanks eleven survived, and of its four wells three. The marble platform of the Hazuri Bagh *baradari*, the pavilion of twelve doors, is eight feet wide and three feet above the ground, with fifteen red stone steps down to its basement and sixteen marble steps up to its third storey.

The measures reach the water too. The tank in the Wazir Khan courtyard is a hundred *gaz* square, and at Shah Muhammad Ghaus a tank ten by ten is always full of water. East of Government House a stepped tank, sixty by thirty feet, is kept full from the canal. One well is big enough for twelve Persian wheels to run on it with ease.

The gilded finial, the *kalas*, shows whether a building of the Sikh era was ever finished. Khushal Singh's samadh has none, and in another dome a fitting waits empty for a *kalas* to be raised. Golden finials gleam at Udham Singh's and Suchet Singh's samadhs. None had yet been raised at Shiv Gar's shivala in the 1880s. Bhauri Sarkar's has a brass one, and none was ever set on Sher Singh's dome. Gilded finials stand throughout at Rani Jindan's, and over each samadh at Kharak Singh's and Nau Nihal Singh's.

Several grand buildings stood unfinished. One remained incomplete, and another was left incomplete because of the revolution of the kingdom and by the 1880s lay heirless, ruined and decaying. After Raja Teja Singh's death, Mul Singh, the agent of the state, took charge of building his samadh, and by the 1880s Raja Harbans Singh, heir to the state, had gained full authority and might bring it to completion. At Khushal Singh's, on Teja Singh's death the property and the *jagir*, the land grant, were divided between Raja Harbans Singh and Sardar Bhagwan Singh, and the work passed to Bhagwan Singh, who neglected it.

Wills governed the fabric of graves, and were sometimes built against. Mian Wada's tomb has no dome and his grave is of unbaked earth, because he willed that his grave not be made of masonry. Faqir Sain Qutb Shah Qadri's grave was likewise left unpaved by his will, though the wheeled well there is masonry, and Shaikh Sadi Balkhari willed that no dome be built over his grave, so none was. By Sayyid Ishaq Gazruni's will his enclosure was made of masonry and his grave of earth, but when the Nawab built a mosque there the grave was raised in masonry too. Daryagar Sanyasi asked that his samadh be made of earth and not masonry, and so it was, yet a masonry shivala has also been built on the same spot. Sharaf un-Nisa's tomb was built without stairs and kept so by her will; the Begum climbed up every day by setting a wooden ladder against it.

Many tombs were built in their occupants' lifetimes or by their disciples. Mauj Darya's was built by Akbar's order in the saint's lifetime. Abul Muali laid out his own tomb and his son finished it. Shah Kamal's was built by the disciples of his family. Shaikh Mahmud Shah Naqshbandi's was built by his disciples in his lifetime, with a basement made first, into which his coffin was placed at his death and the door shut. Haji Nur's enclosure was built in his lifetime on the plan of the Kaaba. Fazl Shah's grave was made before his death by his patron, and Makhduma Begum had her own tomb built smaller than her husband's, out of respect.

The working fabric of the holy places was close and practical. The Kotwali shiwala has separate buildings for staff quarters, kitchens and linen stores. There are small rooms for the *pujaris*, the temple priests, at the Bhagat Ram shiwala and at Ragnath Mishr's, and masonry rooms for faqirs at the *dharamsala*, the pilgrims' rest house. The samadhs of *mahants*, the heads of temple establishments, are built in masonry inside the Shivala Tarpoliya's own halls. Two domed samadhs of mahants and the samadh of a *majzub* stand at the Wazir Khan shiwala, and many domed masonry samadhs of former successors and managers stand at Bhauri Sarkar's.

The Bhagat Ram shiwala has thirteen fountains and an underground cold room, and the Begampura mosque a great tank with a fountain. Bakhshi Bhagat Ram has a gilded samadh and a painted portrait inside his own temple courtyard, which those who come to worship look upon. A marble platform there, two *gaz* square, is called the Dharamshala because he sat on it to give alms to Brahmans. After a theft, the Shivala Tarpoliya's silver *jalhari*, the basin that holds the Shiva lingam, was remade by subscription from four hundred rupees' weight of silver.

The shrines built seats for their offices into the fabric. At Mian Mir a grand hall is the seat of the *mujawirs*, the attendants of the shrine, and a separate building the special seat of the *sajjada nashin*, the hereditary custodian who sits on the saint's prayer carpet. At Shah Muhammad Ghaus a windowed masonry seat for the *sajjada nashin* is built into the western side of the great enclosure wall, and outside the enclosure of Data Ganj Bakhsh the *mujawirs* have many seats.

There are wells at most sites, and bel trees at four. A well at Mian Mir took fifty years to make, and at Sayyid Abdul Wahab Qadri's tomb a great well deserves to be called a tank. There are a wheeled well and two bathhouses at Hamid Qari's *khanqah* and again at the Zakariya Khan mosque, and a well and a water house at the Muhammad Sultan mosque. A living pir mounted a Persian wheel on his own shrine's well, and at Shaikh Musa's enclosure a well is worked by the wooden wheel of the *zamindars* who farm there.

Water was part of the endowed fabric. The water of one low lying mosque goes nowhere outside, and soak pits are built on the spot. An ancient well survives where the tank of Masjid Chinian Wali has vanished. At the Thibi Wala shivala an ancient well serves as a soakaway for the water that drips on Shiv ji night and day, the one thing Raja Dina Nath left untouched when he rebuilt everything else. Over one Shivling water falls through a brass snake fed from the well terrace, and a soakage well takes it away; a brass pipe does the same at another shiwala. Against all these, Rai Singh's thakurdwara had no well, to the great hardship of those who lived in the temple.

Several holy places gave water to the public. There is a *sabil*, a place for giving water, at Mozang, and at the Multani Dharmsala a masonry room for giving water, where a Brahman lives to serve it. At the Sant Shah mandir a newly dug well and a red stone *sabil* serve high and low alike, the well head roofed and pierced so that people draw water and bathe there. The Chhote Lal shiwala's well can be drawn from outside as well, and the Akbari Mandi thakurdwara's well on the bazaar front is open to all for drawing water and bathing.

Canal water ran through the garden belt and the institutions. It enters Shalamar from the south and runs inside every one of its gardens, and Farah Bakhsh drains the upper gardens through the Sawan Bhadon. Bagh Raja Teja Singh owes its green to the canal reaching it, and Bagh Lala Ratan Chand has been perfectly green since water was brought to it from the canal. The contractor's famous cold well is fed with canal water through a channel. Mela Ram's public tank by the railway station is filled from the canal and has a women's ghat built in full purdah, and Diwan Ratan Chand's tank is filled with canal water. At the railway works the canal water is stored in two tanks, passed into two wells, raised by engine to an iron cistern on a very high masonry building, and distributed to the engines and to every workshop.

The tilework of the city's tombs and mosques goes by two names. At the Dai Anga mosque and on Jani Khan's dome it is called *kansi kar* work, in yellow. At the Begampura mosque, whose minarets are green and yellow, and at the Nila Gumbad, whose grand dome is green and turquoise, it is called *kashi kari*. The same word is used of Makhduma Begum's dome, of the inscriptions of the Zakariya Khan *mihrab* and of Miskin Shah's walls. On Sharaf un-Nisa's tomb the tilework forms great painted cypresses and gives the building its byname, the Cypress Tomb.

## Communities

In Aurangzeb's time Lahore was a gathering of peoples, where craftsmen of every land lived. By the 1880s Muslim, Hindu, Sikh, Brahmo and Christian foundations stood in the city side by side, and traffic between the communities ran in both directions.

In the 1880s the city's Muslims were divided four ways. The Ahl-e Sunnat wa Jamaat, the Sunnis, came first. The Shia Imamiya mourned greatly in the ten days of Muharram and made *taziyas*. The Wahhabiya Najdiya followed no imam and called themselves *muwahhid*, those who affirm the unity of God. And the Sufis bore enmity to no one, mostly listened to *sama*, Sufi music and spiritual listening, and were called the sect of peace with all and without prejudice. The Sufi orders included the Qadiri, Chishti, Suhrawardi, Naqshbandi and Naushahi.

Membership of a Sufi order reached into the professional classes. A physician's adopted son, of a Sayyid family, had taken the oath of allegiance in the Naushahi Qadiri line, and so joined the word *faqir* to his name that his descendants, for all their wealth, were still called Faqir. The Faqir family was known in Lahore as the family of the Faqir sahibs and of the physicians, and every notable of Lahore knew their nobility. A practising Lahore physician was also a pir, a Sufi master, through whom the grace of the Chishti and Qadiri lines flowed, and who had many disciples. A graveyard of Chishti faqirs was laid out on Zain Khan's open ground, with a masonry mosque. At Teel Pura a *khanqah* and *madrasa*, a religious school, survived the collapse of the quarter, its dervishes and students staying on, and the madrasa was still flourishing in the 1880s. Imam Ghulam Muhammad, a faqir of the Qadiri order, left as his memorial *Ganj-e Makhfi*, a book of Sufism in verse.

The titles for the holders of religious buildings follow the community that holds them. A *mahant* held Hindu and Sikh mandirs and dharamsalas, a *sarparast*, a patron, held a thakurdwara, a *pujari* the shivalas and thakurdwaras, and a *mutawalli*, the trustee of an endowment, an *imam* or a *mujawir* the mosques and mazars. Yet the titles crossed from one tradition to another. *Sajjada nashin* was used of the office at a Hindu samadh, Chhajju Bhagat's *chaubara*, and *gaddi nashin*, the one who sits on the seat, of the same office. *Gaddi* and *masnad* were used of a faqir's seat of the Sikh period, and *gaddi nashin* of a Sikh *mahant* at Mozang.

The vocabulary of office was wider still. There were the *khadim*, the servant, and the *muhtamim*, the manager, of a Kuka dharamsal. There was a trustee and helper of the congregation, *mutamid o muawin halqa*, at the gurdwara of Guru Ram Das, and a disciple successor, *chela janashin*, and a *gaddi nashin* at Baba Khuda Singh's dharamsala. One holder called himself a *bairagi*, an ascetic who has renounced the world. *Mujawir o sajjada nashin* served as one compound title, and a caretaker and priest was called *khabar gir o pujari*.

Some rites and holy places were shared. On Sundays Hindus and Muslims alike brought children suffering from an illness to Shah Dargahi's well and bathed them there. Hindus attended the Guru Ram Das gurdwara, and *qawwals*, singers of *qawwali*, the Sufi devotional music, sang *kafis* of divine unity there. Kashmiri Pandits staffed two Shaiva temples founded by officers of the Sikh state. The Baoli was chosen for any gathering of any kind in the Hindu religion, and all the Hindus of Anarkali came to one place to worship and make offerings. The shrine of Sitla Mata was the resort of both Hindus and Muslims, and Muslims too, after their children recovered from smallpox, brought them there and made offerings. The Hindus and Muslims of Lahore alike acknowledged the holiness of Bhai Rupa.

The calendar of shared gatherings was full. Hindus and Muslims attended Miran Badshah's mazar on Thursdays, both *melas*, the festival fairs, at Madho Lal Husain drew Hindus and Muslims, and Muslims too mostly went to the mela of Bhadar Kali. On the twenty eighth of Shaban, Hindus and Muslims held a mela together at Shah Bilawal's khanqah. At a Hindu bhagat's samadh *qawwali* was sung in the mystic raga on Mondays and Tuesdays, and at an Ekadashi assembly inside a thakurdwara *qawwals* sang ragas of divine unity.

Some of the city's crowds gathered at no shrine at all. The mouth of the contractor's cold water well was closed all winter and opened at the height of the hot season. Thousands of water skins were carried from it into the city, and Hindus, Muslims and Christians crowded there, so that for two months a mela of onlookers was held at it every day.

The communities also acted together. Hindus and Muslims together protested against the demolition of the mosque at Shah Muhammad Ghaus's mazar. Hindus, Muslims and Christians all gladly joined the subscription to repair the Badshahi Masjid. The Qizilbash household's Muharram food was given to all, to those of the Sunni and the Shia persuasion alike.

One exchange across communities founded a temple outright. A Muslim Turkish soldier had taken an image from a mandir the Turks demolished in Hindustan, and he bathed and dressed it every day because, it is said, whatever his heart desired came to him. He sold it for a great deal of money to the Hindus of the mohalla, who placed it in the *haveli*, the mansion, that became the Mata temple, and the soldier himself gave the image its name. Buildings also changed hands between communities, and a mosque could become a temple: Ranjit Singh renamed the Moti Masjid the Moti Mandir.

Two mosques passed to Hindu holy men under the Sikhs and were taken back under the English. In Ranjit Singh's time a Hindu *sanyasi jogi*, an ascetic, named Basant Gar took possession of Daya Lado's mosque, built a shivala on one side and a thakurdwara on the other, and lived there for nearly thirty years. Under English rule one Mehr Shah, a Qadiri faqir, took a few Muslims with him from Lahore and evicted the Hindu faqir by force. The sanyasi petitioned the British authorities, but nothing came of it. Mehr Shah built the outer enclosure wall, and by the 1880s the Muslims of the surrounding *kothas*, the houses, prayed in it.

Inside the city the same thing happened. Under the Maharaja a Hindu had made his home in a mosque and set images of the *thakurs*, the gods, in it, and under the English a Muslim faqir gathered a few Muslims and evicted him from the mosque by force. In the 1880s the mosque remained in that faqir's possession.

Possession could divide Muslims as well. One mosque still in use was held in the 1880s by the Muslims of the Wahhabi persuasion, who were opposed to both the Sunni and the Shia sects. The Wahhabis spent a great deal of money repairing it, and gave it a grand new door and a tank kept full from the tap. Another mosque stayed small because Dilawar Khan, a Muslim though he was, had built a haveli on the ground of its courtyard.

The city's elites drew lines of their own. Lahore had a Brahmin community with standing in the city, and it is not clear how Shah Hussain and Madho managed to overcome their conflict with Madho's family and the Brahmins of Lahore. Lahore judged its saints too: Shah Hussain's "open passion ruined his reputation throughout Lahore".

The communities also met in violence. Under Mir Mannu, in Muhammad Shah's reign, the Sikhs had grown in number and there was a general order to seize and kill them. During a disturbance in Lahore the Gilani household raised a flag so that the Sikhs would know theirs as a house of the sayyids, the descendants of the Prophet. When the Gilani hazrat moved into a donated haveli, the whole city was in uproar.

There was strife between Shia and Sunni as well. On the tenth of Muharram the Zuljanah, the model horse of Imam Husain, was led round the whole city and brought to the Imambara, a building of the Shia Imamiya whose patrons were Nawab Nawazish Ali Khan Qizilbash and Nawab Nasir Ali Khan. In 1849 there was a fierce quarrel between Shia and Sunni near Shah Alami Gate that came to blows. That day the Sunnis pulled down the enclosure's inner buildings, threw down the battlements of the tomb, filled the well with bricks and beat Gami Shah unconscious. Then the Deputy Commissioner summoned a troop of cavalry from the Anarkali cantonment, the crowd dispersed, and those arrested received some punishment.

A state prosecution emptied one religious building. When the Kukas, a Sikh reform sect, made a disturbance in Maler Kotla and were punished, the splendour of their dharamsal in Lahore departed too, and Ram Singh was sent to Rangoon.

The city knew separation as well as mixing. The settlement of the cow killing butchers was set apart by royal order because the Hindu subjects loathed them, and a magnificent masonry mosque was built there. The quarter was later abandoned and its people mutilated, and only a masonry mosque still stands. Under the British the honorary magistracy was a communal quota of eight of Lahore's notables, four Hindu and four Muslim, and a hereditary Chief Pandit was said to be distinguished by the honorary magistracy. The hospital kept four separate kitchens, one each for Hindu, Muslim, and Christian and European patients and a shopkeeper in the fourth, with Hindu and Muslim cooks and servants. Women's and men's ghats, the bathing steps, were built apart at Diwan Ratan Chand's tank, with a gate to the women's ghat where women in purdah bathe, and at the bathing tank of the cremation ground.

Some communities ran their own observances. Shaikh Musa was a blacksmith, many blacksmiths of his family were his disciples, and the blacksmiths also held his annual *ʿurs*. The Khoja community appeared as witnesses for their own mosque's hereditary *mutawalli*. The Hindus at Bhadar Kali at Niaz Beg served the pujaris with heart and soul and counted their service an honour. In the nineteenth century Lahore also had its Jains: most of the followers of the *panth*, the path, of Parasnath in the city came from one group, who did not tie the mouth cloth and did not marry, and anyone who married was expelled.

The gravediggers of Miani were a community of their own. Mian Murad Bakhsh, of the gravedigger people, tired of the work and took one Khwaja as his pupil, and in the nineteenth century that man's descendants were among the gravediggers. Those working there then included men named Rahmat, Nabi Bakhsh, Miran Bakhsh, Shahab al-Din, Nur Din and Chiragh Din.

Hindu, Sikh and Brahmo foundations stood beside the Muslim ones. Every Sunday followers of the Brahmo faith gathered at the Brahmo Samaj to worship the one God, and a paid *chowkidar*, a watchman, guarded the building. One preacher's discourse was directed against the new faiths, the Arya and the Brahmo, which are wholly opposed to the worship of images.

The Sikh establishments of the 1880s included the Baoli, with its shops, its *naqara*, the great kettledrum, and a building fund drawn from Khalsa salaries. There were Baba Khuda Singh's dharamsala with its *gaddi nashin*, the Kuka dharamsal, and the bhang dharamsal, kept with the government's permission. There were the Nihangs, the Sikh warrior order, of Shahid Ganj, the regular gathering at Mozang, and Tali Sahib, its stipend stopped and its tree felled. The royal samadhs were kept by a single salaried boy, and there were the eleven samadhs of the eleven ranis who became *sati*, burning themselves on the pyre, with Ranjit Singh.

The Christian foundations were counted with the rest. The Mian Mir church seats about eight hundred, and forty five thousand rupees of its cost came from the government and the rest from subscription. At another church a bell was rung not only for prayer but every day at twelve o'clock. The *padri*, the missionary, bought Bagh Bhai Mahan Singh, changed the form of the garden and built houses there in his own fashion, and the Christian women and men living there received stipends from the padris and were taught every day. There was a Mission School among the buildings near Fazl Shah's grave and the Rang Mahal.

Women founded and patronised holy places in all three traditions. Musammat Jwala Dai, wife of Ram Kishan, built her mandir and made it *waqf*, a religious endowment; she died once the building was finished, and by the 1880s her son in law Har Narain was its guardian, giving five to seven rupees a month. The mother of a man named Kanhaiya Lal built Chor Mor Wala with her own money in Ranjit Singh's time and gave it to Mahant Balram Das. The third shivala at Mehr Das was founded by a woman called Bhagtan, who kept a shop selling *paan* under Sikh rule.

Rani Lachhmi built a thakurdwara out of deep devotion and left it as her memorial in this transient world. Bhauri Sarkar took her name from her reddish hair, *bhura* being the Punjabi word for a red shading into black, and her work was carried out in the time of Maharaja Sher Singh. Rani Jindan built the shivala beside her thakurdwara while she held power, and laid the foundation of Sardar Jawahar Singh's samadh, the work continuing until she was exiled to Hindustan by order of the English government. Rani Gul Begum held the largest jagir of all, and twelve hundred rupees a month under the English.

Muslim women of the Mughal household and the city built mosques, tombs and quarters. The founder of the Masti Darwaza mosque was Maryam Zamani, wife of the emperor Jahangir. Dai Anga, the royal wet nurse, lived in Jahangir's household; Anga was her title, from *ang*, the body, for an *anga* is one with whom a person is one soul in two bodies. As Sultan Begum's wet nurse she was given a garden outright once it was finished, and her grave is in it. Daya Lado, wet nurse of Prince Salim, built a mosque in her own lifetime and made her grave in its courtyard, and after her death, while Muhammad Shakur looked after the mosque, a learned Maulvi Ismatullah taught there.

Zeb-un-Nisa Begum was owner and founder, a poet, the builder of her own tomb in her lifetime, and the builder of Shah Rustam Ghazi's red stone tomb and marble grave. Begampura is named after a woman: the mother of Nawab Khan Bahadur built all of it in her own lifetime, and so it is known as Begampura to this day.

Women outside the nobility appear too, and so do the limits placed on women. Moran the courtesan built the mosque that bears her name, a renowned mosque of Lahore. People held her order stronger than the Maharaja's, and in the 1880s her family still lived in the same bazaar, headed by a *chaudhri*, a headman, of the courtesans' community. Nawab Zakariya Khan built a haveli for Kallo Bai of the *mutriba* community, the singing women, because she could not enter the Nawab's harem. One mosque was the women's mosque of a palace, where the women of the household of the governor of Lahore came to pray by a path from inside the palace.

Sati is part of the city's fabric. There are eleven samadhs with Ranjit Singh's. At Jamadar Khushal Singh's stand the samadh of his wife, who became sati with him, and two more whose names are unknown, perhaps those of their slave women. There are others at Sher Singh's and for Sardar Bur Singh's wife, four at Kharak Singh's for the women who burned with the Maharaja's body, and two of the same kind for Nau Nihal Singh. At Bhai Wasti Ram's, the women's samadhs are of marble, his mother's marble from head to foot.

The Muftis and the Gardezis were old families of Lahore and Multan. One scholar of women's Sufi ritual traces her maternal family to the old walled city of Lahore, and counts among her ancestors Mufti Ghulam Sarwar, who wrote a history of the Punjab, *Tarikh-e Makhazan Panjab*. Her paternal grandfather was Mufti Muzaffar Din.

Today Lahore is a city of minorities as well as of the Muslim majority. Five religious communities live in and around it: Hindus, Christians, Sikhs, Baha'is and Zoroastrians, the Parsis.

Partition runs through their memories. One man was a teenager when the partition riots spread in Lahore, and his grandfather refused to leave his ancestral home. Bishna Ram recalls that friends and relatives told his family "that Lahore was no longer safe for us", while Shazia Waleed's father was "a prominent Hindu businessman from Lahore who continued living here despite partition". Mary, a widow of eighty three, recalls that her family lived "near Shahjamal on the canal that now divides Lahore into two parts". As partition came, "we would frequently sight mutilated bodies floating in the water", and her mother made the children wear a cross to show "to anyone who would come asking for our religion".

A Protestant poet puts it simply: "We Christians were living here before Lahore became a part of Pakistan". Alfred Woolner's statue outside the old campus of the University of the Punjab is "one of the few statues of pre-partition Lahore that has survived", defying the iconoclastic streak that emerged in the country after 1947.

The Hindus of Lahore are few and careful. Prem Gupta, a Hindu activist of forty from Lahore, lives in a Muslim neighbourhood where "theirs are the only Hindu houses. Unlike the majority of the Hindus, living in Lahore, they have refused to take up dual names". As he puts it, "If you want to see the lights of Diwali, go to Peshawar. You will find nothing in Lahore". Many prominent Hindus of Lahore went into hiding during the wars of 1965 and 1971. After the sixth of December 1992 the city's temples were attacked, and its functional temples on the Ravi road and at Neela Gumbad were burned down, and Shami Lal, another Hindu from Lahore, shut his business for several months.

The Neela Gumbad temple "serves a small community of Balmiki Hindus in Lahore, numbering only a few hundred, most of whom are Christians on record". Pilgrims went from the city to Katas in 2010, and in 2011 the Neela Gumbad priest took "a delegation of three hundred people from Lahore and neighbouring regions" to Ram Thamman.

Christians in Lahore meet an old prejudice. "Even in prominent cities like Lahore, several Muslims refuse to eat with Christians and consider utensils used by them to be impure", and many low end hotels and restaurants, in the cities as well as the countryside, keep separate utensils for Christians. Colonel Chaudary, a Christian of high caste who lives in Lahore, found that the Muslim labourers in his house refused his cook's food "because of the impurity associated with eating at a Christian's house". A school principal in Lahore was accused of blasphemy over a multiple choice question that listed his own name beside the Prophet's.

In one outbreak, "an angry mob of Muslims in Lahore had burned down 170 Christian houses", their rage "ignited by an argument between two friends, a Christian and a Muslim, while they were enjoying their evening drinks", while the accused Christian was already in police custody. The missionary colleges belong to this story too: Forman Christian College is "one of the oldest colleges set up by Christian missionaries", and one of the first institutes teaching in English, set up in the latter half of the nineteenth century.

The Sikhs, once the city's rulers, are now rare in it: "it is now rare that one runs into a Sikh in this city, a city which was once ruled by a Sikh and the seat of their political strength". Ranjit Singh built "a mosque at a mohalla called Gul Begum in Lahore", and Fakir Azizudin's "family still lives in Lahore in their ancestral house, located inside the walled city". A Sikh named Kartar was refused service at a restaurant in Model Town because they did not have "our" utensils. Only recently have the Sikh communities of Lahore been able to exert themselves, and Ghulab Singh became "the first Sikh police officer in the revamped traffic police of Lahore".

The Parsis of Lahore are "microscopic". Farah Desai, eighty two, recalls that at partition there were quite a few Parsis living in Lahore and Amritsar, all well to do, most of whom later emigrated: "There are only a handful of us left here", only thirty five Parsis in Lahore. Sadaf, born after partition, grew up in "a Lahore that was different from the multi-religious city that Farah grew up in", where the sari "had become the symbol of an Indian woman and wasn't encouraged in Lahore any more". Daria Boatwala, who moved to the city in the eighties, finds that "Lahore is much more monolithic and I would say religiously conservative". With no priest, there is no way to perform a traditional Parsi marriage in Lahore, and the same holds for the rites after death.

The Baha'is and the Ahmadiyya live with the same pressures. The Baha'i assembly at Lahore was established before partition, and about forty people attend its meetings out of the one hundred and fifty Baha'is registered with it. Usman Nawaz, a Baha'i of Shahdara, was injured with a stone by a Muslim worker at the Sohrab Cycle Factory and was eventually forced to resign because of the hostile attitude of his colleagues, while the management were afraid to take any action. "On 28th May 2010, two houses of worship of the Ahmadiyya community in Lahore were attacked, resulting in the death of 94 people", and all political leaders refrained from visiting the site or sympathising with the community.

Among the Muslim majority a public piety has risen. In 1935 the Deobandis, by then a formidable religious force in the city, opposed the first *Milad* procession, the procession for the Prophet's birthday. Today walkers in a middle class park in the heart of Lahore carry rosary beads, and some play a recitation of the Quran in Arabic with its Urdu translation, a public display of their religiosity. A visitor from Mumbai, by contrast, was surprised to see that hardly any women in Lahore were wearing a burqa. At the Lahore University of Management Sciences there have been rumours of the religious right slowly dominating the administration.

On a national day of protest against a film, at the old village of Hanjarwal, temporary gallows were erected on the road and an effigy dressed in a shirt with the US flag on it was dragged to them; days earlier, a group of lawyers with members of religious parties had attacked the US consulate in Lahore, taking down its flag and burning it. Raiwind is "a small village in the outskirts of Lahore", and during its annual congregation the cars heading there jam the traffic at a junction in the city.

The shrines matter to the women of the city. "In the major mosques in Pakistan, for instance, there is a small space where women can go and pray on Friday or on religious festivals such as the Eid." In the local neighbourhood mosques a woman cannot offer her ritual prayers: "Thus, the important spheres of religious and spiritual participation for women are the Sufi shrines".

Sufi devotion in Lahore is also domestic. A *murid*, a pledged disciple, of one Sufi order runs an informal religious school for women in the city. Two weekly circles of remembrance, *halqa-i zikr*, meet in private homes: a senior male *murid* hosts one in his home, and a female disciple of Shaykh Wahid Bakhsh Rabbani leads a separate session made up mostly of women.

Lahore is linked to the shrines of Sindh by the fakirs who move between them. One of them, Murad, relocated to Lahore after leaving a lodge at Sehwan and was reported at Bibiyan Pak Daman. The young woman he married was a pilgrim visiting from Lahore whom he had consulted at the shrine of Bodlo.

## Shrines, Endowments and the State

In the 1880s authority over a Lahore temple or shrine was often stated as ownership, and it rested with the man who served there rather than with any trust. Basant Gar Sanyasi held authority over the Thibi Wala shivala, a temple of Shiva; his chelas, or disciples, served there, and their daily expenses were his charge. At the Bhairo ji *asthan*, a holy seat, the distribution and spending of the offerings lay with its holder alone, and his four chelas drew their living from that income. Madho Das held a mandir with its wells, garden and land in proprietary possession, and Bawa Ram Das was both pujari, the temple priest, and owner of his temple. At the Gilani Sayyids' graveyard Pir Nizamuddin was simply its owner, its *malik*.

Mosques kept the prayer leader and the trustee apart. Mufti Jalal-ud-Din led prayers with the permission of Mufti Ghulam Sarwar, the mosque's *mutawalli*, the trustee of a religious endowment. Another *mutawalli*, Mirza Nur Ali, who also managed his mosque, had its repairs carried out in lime himself.

Custodians were paid by shares, by salaries and by stipends. At Data Ganj Bakhsh more than seventy *mujawirs*, the attendants who keep a shrine, divided the offerings in equal shares. Seven *bighas* of farmland with a running well belonged to the tomb of Shah Abu Ishaq Qadri, and its *mujawirs* lived on the income. At Mehr Das three custodians shared one enclosure: Bawa Chandi Nath performed the *puja*, the worship, of both its shivalas and took their income; the worship of the thakurdwara, a temple of Vishnu, belonged to Moti; and the Devi's mandir had no pujari of its own, the two serving it together. At the Baoli the offerings were divided by kind between the custodian and the servers.

Salaries also came from outside. A Brahmin serving at a *samadh*, a memorial raised over a cremation, drew his salary from the agent of the Jammu government; he came every morning, offered flowers on the *samadhs* and on Shiv ji, and went away. A whole royal *samadh* complex was staffed by a single boy, Nikka Singh, seventeen years old, appointed and paid a yearly sum by the government, with one Granth Sahib to read from and one mat. Private patrons paid priests too. At the Chhote Lal shiwala the Brahmin pujari and the other servants drew their salaries from Lala Chhote Lal's *kothi*, his mansion and business house; Rai Singh paid his own Brahmin pujari; and Pandits Prem Nath and Shiv Nath paid a salary to the Brahmin Keshav Ram.

State and princely stipends came and went. Bhai Nihal Singh held a *jagir*, an assignment of land revenue, of eight thousand rupees a year in return for service at the Baoli. For as long as he lived, Maharaja Kharak Singh kept up a mosque imam's allowance of ten rupees a month; he would sit in the courtyard of the mosque inside his own haveli, a great mansion, and tell the Muslims to give the call to prayer and to pray. The *mahant*, the head of the establishment, at Tali Sahib had one rupee a day in Ranjit Singh's time; by the 1880s it had stopped, though the offerings kept him comfortably off. Mauj Darya's khanqah, a Sufi lodge, received forty rupees a month under the Sikhs and nothing at all by the 1880s. At Jahangir's tomb there were *jagirs* worth thousands of rupees and salaried *hafiz*, men who knew the Quran by heart, kept to recite it at the grave in turns.

Funding often lapsed with its funder. Sardar Bhagwan Singh used to meet one shivala's expenses until he too died. Raja Dina Nath came without fail to the khanqah of Fazl Shah Majzub, a *majzub* being one spiritually intoxicated, absorbed in divine love, and gave thousands of rupees as *nazrana*, offerings to a holy man. He kept a *munshi*, a clerk, always in attendance to write down whatever the faqir said and report it to him nightly, made the building and the grave *pakka*, of fired brick, in the faqir's lifetime, and paid for the funeral in costly shawls. While Raja Dina Nath lived the *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death, was kept with great pomp, and devotees went on holding it after him. By the 1880s only an annual *fatiha*, a prayer for the dead, was left, and the family had sold its houses and havelis and died destitute.

Succession passed by discipleship, by heredity and by teaching lineage, line by line. At Chor Mor Wala, Mahant Balram Das was followed by Jai Ram Das, who in turn had a successor. At Jwala Dai, Salig Ram Bairagi was followed by his chela Aya Ram, and at Thibi Wala Basant Gar Sanyasi was the chela of Mangal Gar. At Chhajju Bhagat's the line ran from Parsotam Das and in time reached Dwarka Das. At the dharamsal, a lodging house for worship and travellers, its founder Goma Singh was succeeded by Daya Singh, and in the 1880s the holder was Nihang Singh. At one shivala by a well a *mahant* had held office for some generations.

Muslim imamat, the office of leading prayer, ran the same way. At Wazir Khan's mosque there were three generations: Imam Ghulam Muhammad; his son Imam-ud-Din Bakhsh, who was the mosque's imam all his life; and in the 1880s Imam Muhammad, his son, who led the prayers. At another mosque two sons of Maulvi Nizam-ud-Din were still its imams. An imamat could pass with a house: Aziz-ud-Din gave over his house together with the office of imam of the Baluchan mosque that went with it, and left on Hajj.

Teaching lineage could serve as a title deed. Mian Ahmad-ud-Din, the *sajjada nashin*, or hereditary head, of the khanqah of Mian Wadda, also held Hamid Qari's khanqah. When Sikh rule ended and the Qasab Khana mosque fell empty, he seized it on the claim that Mian Jan Muhammad, a teacher who had taught there while the mohalla, the neighbourhood, was inhabited, had been Mian Wadda's pupil and disciple. He had been *sajjada nashin* of his own khanqah since the coming of the English, and like his father he was a *hafiz* and a learned man; his predecessor, Mian Sharf-ud-Din, *mujawir* and *sajjada nashin*, was a most devout and orderly man.

A gift could turn into hereditary control of a mosque. After a sermon, Nura gave Maulvi Jan Muhammad cash, a robe of honour and the haveli he lived in, turning his own household out with nothing but the clothes they wore. The haveli, with goods, cash and jewellery worth thousands of rupees, passed to the maulvi, and in the 1880s his descendants still lived in it and held the mosque as well.

The state could appoint the head of a Hindu shrine. In the time of the three rulers of the city of Lahore, Bawa Parsotam Das came from Jaipur and they jointly made him *sajjada nashin* of a temple, when only an old *chaubara*, an upper room, and a boundary wall stood there; he then had the great temple with its gold work built. Its *mahants* were forbidden to marry. Their sect had its own *granth*, a separate scripture, and its rule forbade eating meat, drinking wine, lying, taking interest and dependence on anyone.

Sometimes heirs held everything. Shah Rahmatullah Qureshi's family kept both: the chain of *piri muridi*, the bond of master and disciple, still continued in his family, and the same people were guardians of the building. A grandson disciple of Shiv Gar took one shivala's income, owned its land, and lived in it with his wife as his home. At Shalamar a gift of land made an office hereditary: the gardener who owned the land gave it willingly to the emperor and would not take a price despite the emperor's insistence, so the gardenership was entrusted to him generation after generation, and his descendants were still its gardeners in the 1880s.

A transfer of *tauliyat*, trusteeship, could fail for want of an endowment. At a tomb in Sir Henry Lawrence's Residency, Khwaja Ahmad Kashmiri, a descendant of the man buried there, took permission from the British, took possession, spent much money, rebuilt the graves and repaired the mosque, and gave the trusteeship to Muhammad Bakhsh before leaving for Kashmir. Because no means had been set up for the building's necessary expenses, it fell desolate again as before.

One establishment might run several unrelated sites, and custodians might live far away. An unnamed thakurdwara belonged to the Banke Bihari mandir, whose *mahant* took its offerings and appointed its pujari. At the tomb of Abdul Jalil Qutb-e Alam the guardian was Khurshid Alam, son of Ghulam Muhi-ud-Din Shah, who lived in the village of Ratta Piran in Sialkot district and had two rooms at the tomb to stay in. The seat of a spiritual line could lie outside Lahore altogether: Shaikh Tahir's family lived at the small town of Batala, and so did a collateral line of Mauj Darya's.

Some custodians neglected their charge. At one temple the heir took no care of it at all. Rani Jindan's thakurdwara stood roofless, with no guardian, served only by a Brahmin named Murli who lived on whatever offerings were made. The sole servant of another temple, which had fourteen shops, spent their rent on his own keep while the building lay unrepaired and decayed.

Custodians also sold what they held, and this happened again and again. The fullest case is the Khoja mosque. After the city's *nazul*, the property that had passed to the state, had been recorded, a man filed a claim in the district court that his grandfather had been *mutawalli* of the mosque while the mohalla was inhabited, and asked for it so that he might restore it. Witnesses of the Khoja community declared that his grandfather had been their spiritual guide and imam, that they had built the mosque and given it to him, and that it was now the claimant's right, and the Deputy Commissioner gave him the mosque. He ran it as a mosque for a year or two, then made it a *kothi*, let it to English tenants, took the rent, and finally sold it.

*Mujawirs* sold shrine land. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar, a poet of Lahore, built a family burial enclosure inside the shrine enclosure of Sayyid Jan Muhammad Huzuri at his own expense, buying the land from the khanqah's *mujawirs*, with its door opening from inside the tomb's enclosure. At Shah Ismail's tomb the *mujawir* sold the land to the English, who joined it to their *kothi*, and the old well no longer belonged to the grave; the people of the *kothi* drew their water from it. At another mosque the slabs and lintels of the eastern gateway were taken out and the door collapsed, and then a greedy imam sold the land, where a barbers' shop and a *hammam*, a bathhouse, came to stand.

A graveyard could become rental property. When English rule came, Pir Nizamuddin, owner of the Gilani Sayyids' graveyard, settled many people in it, water carriers among them, and charged them rent. He was the same man, Sayyid Nizamuddin Gilani, known as Pir Bodiyan Wale, who had earlier tried hard to restore the place without its old splendour ever returning.

Endowment land was mortgaged and absorbed. At Sitla Mata all the outer land was held in mortgage from the *mahant*. At Chhajju Bhagat's *chaubara* the *mahant* built shops on the adjoining land outside. Rent was turned to the holder's own living: the *mutawalli* of the Sunehri Masjid spent its shop rents, through the Sikh period, on the mosque's upkeep and on himself. At Wazir Khan's mosque what survived of the *waqf*, the religious endowment, was held in the names of the mosque's servants, and even the man who filled the mosque's tank lived on the shop rents. At Chhujhi Mal Khatri's thakurdwara the two endowed shops had passed to the founder's brother, who took their rent and gave not a *habba*, the smallest of coins, towards the temple's expenses.

Possession was fought over, sometimes for decades. At Begampura the contest ran for fifty years under three regimes. Under Ranjit Singh an artillery officer named Khazana took the place and settled *zamindars*, landholders, there. Sansar Chand of Kangra was granted it and lived there for months, the land assessed at a hundred rupees a year, and then made it over to his Brahmins, who pulled down many graves, took away the stones and sold the bricks. Sardar Lehna Singh Majithia took it from them and compensated them with a *jagir* of a hundred rupees in the hills. A nawab came from Kabul whose genealogy truly matched that of the Khan Bahadur who had founded the building, took possession and dismissed the *zamindars*, but the case stood before Maharaja Ranjit Singh for some years and in the end he achieved nothing.

In Sambat 1895 of the Bikrami era, the Hindu calendar, the cantonment of General Gulab Singh was fixed near Salimpura, and the General made the mosque his home, expelling one *lambardar*, a village headman, and keeping another as his gardener. Under the English that same gardener claimed possession and became owner. The heirs brought suits, an order for a civil suit followed, and they got nowhere and fell silent. One heir won possession for some months in a summary suit and sold bricks worth hundreds of rupees before the *zamindars* were restored on appeal. Twice, possession beat inheritance.

Some custodians learned to work the new rulers. Nihang Singh of the dharamsal, known as the picture baba, always kept a portrait of a former Lieutenant Governor of the Punjab and produced it at Englishmen's bungalows; as a historian of the city put it in the 1880s, he had made the picture a means of his own advancement. At Than Bhairo, in the Sikh period, Dhani Nath Jogi cultivated the Sikh nobles of Lahore, bought out the old occupants at fifty rupees a year, and made himself master of the place. He had been appointed by Jawala Nath; his successor Ram Nath paid the fifty rupees for a few years and then stopped, while the descendants of the old holders went on living in Lahore.

Under the English that dispute reached the Settlement department, the office that recorded land rights and revenue. The claimant, Badri Das, pursued the case for some months, and the Settlement officer went to the spot, but the jogis' possession was not broken and the claim was dismissed. The place, which had once belonged to faqirs, stayed with the jogis.

Other claims were settled by the neighbourhood, the court or a builder. At the Sufi Wali Masjid, when the founder's son claimed the mosque and its attached house as his own, the people of the mohalla had him evicted through the court. One Dilawar Khan, superintendent of Kunwar Nau Nihal Singh's stables, forcibly took the land of a mosque courtyard from the imam and built his own haveli on it; the mosque's heirs, Mufti Ghulam Rasul and Mufti Ghulam Muhammad, appealed to Raja Kharak Singh, who sternly rebuked Dilawar Khan and had a rent deed written for the land in the imam's favour. That mosque fell in the rains of the early 1880s and was rebuilt by Nawab Abd al-Majid Khan, a leading citizen of Lahore. Under English rule, when Masjid Naqiban had lost its splendour and stood empty, a *naqib* claimed it and had it released. The land outside the Wilbagh Rai shivala belonged to an old wide well, and the people of the mohalla quarrelled over it endlessly until its builder settled the matter by putting up the shivala on it with his own money; the well then came to be counted as the shivala's.

These shrine disputes were part of a wider run of property cases in the city. Arain occupants defeated an owner's long eviction suit by sheer length of possession. Five named heirs went to law over a partition, and an *amin*, a court officer, was appointed. The descendants of Wazir Khan claimed the Pari Mahal and were refused, because the building had stood confiscated for a long time in the Sikh period. One man's many suits were turned away because the English government did not think it proper to alter the matter, and a *katra*, a market quarter, passed by a decree of Islamic law and the *qazi*'s fatwa, enforced by order of Maharaja Ranjit Singh.

Faqirs lodged on shrine premises as a matter of course. They lived at Ghore Shah's, at Shah Gada Lahori's and Shah Husain Zanjani's, at Hamid Qari's khanqah, at Taj Shah Majzub's, at Khwaja Muhammad Saeed's and at the Shia Imambara, the hall for the mourning of Muharram. At Shah Sarwani's a restorer of the British period built a room and seated a faqir in it, and at Mauj Darya's the buildings were for the servants of the khanqah.

They were found too in the Suthra courtyard, in the enclosure of Bokan Khan's mosque, at Moran's mosque, where rooms with lattice windows above the shops housed its dervishes, and in the masonry rooms for faqirs at the dharamsala. In the sixteen cells of Wazir Khan's mosque an illuminator worked in one, a scribe sat in another, and in others the *mujawirs* of the shrine were lodged.

How an endowment was made could decide whether it survived. Bakhshi Bhagat Ram set apart a village worth four hundred rupees a year from his own *jagir* for a shivala's expenses; but because he had made the grant on his own authority and not the government's, the village was confiscated along with his *jagir*, and house rent paid by his son took its place. The full story behind that loss ran from audit to ruin. In 1847 John Lawrence demanded his accounts; he delayed, his *jagir* was confiscated and he was suspended from office, five lakh rupees being found outstanding. After annexation no right was established; the Chief Commissioner fixed him a pension of twelve hundred rupees for life, and on his death that too was taken back.

The *samadh* of Haqiqat Rai shows the same thing in four stages. In Ranjit Singh's time a well with twenty *bighas* of land, a *bigha* being a local measure of land, was held free of revenue for the *samadh*, and two villages in Sialkot district, one of them Mandi, were held in *jagir*; but well and villages stood in the name of Sarup Singh, the *samadh*'s *khadim*, its servant. Under the English the *muafi*, the revenue free grant, was fixed for Sarup Singh's lifetime only, and on his death the whole was confiscated, leaving his grandson Nihal Singh without it. At last, after much effort, a yearly cash sum was released in the name of the *samadh* itself, Nihal Singh was settled as *khadim* and pujari, and the stipend was fixed in perpetuity. At another shivala revenue free farmland belonged to the *mansab*, the office, not to the man, and the *mahant* took its income.

Some grants were very large. Pandit Madsudan, a Brahman ritual specialist, held a *jagir* of nine thousand nine hundred and fifty five rupees and the village of Qila Gujjar Singh *muafi*, free of revenue, generation after generation; lakhs of rupees were given to Brahmins in charity through him, and he held the office by succeeding his father. That perpetual *jagir* then passed by *hiba*, a gift, to a youngest son by a second wife, against the claims of three elder sons. Raja Teja Singh had a *jagir* of ninety three thousand rupees fixed for him, Sialkot being exchanged for Batala and Sheikhupura held in *jagir* by his successor. Rani Gul Begum, whose *jagir* was the largest of all, received twelve hundred rupees a month under the English and built her own garden tomb.

The state also created a permanent estate for the Qizilbash Nawabs. An estate in Lahore district was granted to the family in perpetuity in exchange for a cash pension, with proprietary right and the revenue remitted, its succession written down and the upkeep of the whole family laid upon it; on it the grantee settled many villages and built splendid *kothis*, mosques and *imambaras*. The office of *ifta*, issuing legal opinions, had its own grants: land in pargana Qasur, a revenue subdivision of Lahore district, was held in the mufti's *jagir*; he bought land in mohalla Adil Khani Lohani and settled his own quarter there, still known in the 1880s as Kotli Muftian; and under Ahmad Shah Durrani came the robe of *ifta* and a royal *farman*, a decree, written in the month of Ramazan. After the Sikhs had looted the village of Miani, its resettled houses paid a rent of one rupee a year each, as they still did in the nineteenth century.

Shops built under and over mosques were the usual Muslim endowment. The Sunehri Masjid was raised a storey and shops were built beneath it on every side so that their rent might pay for the mosque. At Hafiz Chabuk Sawar's mosque the shop rents were made *waqf*. Mirza Muhammad endowed shops to the east and south to keep his mosque flourishing, and a *nashistgah*, a sitting house, as the imam's home. At Nura Iman Wala's mosque the shops beneath paid for its expenses; at the Sufi Wali Masjid two shops on the bazaar side paid their rent to the founder himself, Haji Mauli Dad; and at Moran's mosque shops flanked the stair and the imam took their rent.

Wazir Khan's endowment shows how much could be lost. The income of everything Nawab Wazir Khan built and endowed, from the mosque to the Delhi Gate on both sides of the bazaar, its shops, a *sarai*, an inn for travellers, and a *hammam*, belonged to the mosque. By the 1880s the *sarai* and the *hammam* were government property and much of the rest had passed into other people's names. The shops above the mosque, under its domed vestibule, had been the bookbinders' shops; in Sikh times they flourished and there were no bookbinding shops anywhere else in the city, but by the 1880s all the mosque's shops were in ruins and the binders' descendants worked in offices and printing presses.

A grave could come first and an endowment be built around it. Wazir Khan built a room over the grave of Sayyid Sarbuland, which stood there before him, and endowed it in the mosque's name. The tomb at Masjid Suf likewise stood before the mosque that came after it, and Wazir Khan kept the grave of Miran Badshah just as it was, counting it the ornament of his mosque.

*Waqf* could also be legal shelter. Warned that Aurangzeb had ordered his dismissal and the confiscation of his property, Sarfaraz Khan at once made all his immovable property *waqf*, began pulling down the haveli he lived in, which stood on his own land, and gave out that he was building a mosque on the spot. The emperor was much pleased to hear of it, ruled that so devout a man ought not to be dismissed, ordered him property in exchange for what he had endowed, and restored him to office.

Under British rule endowment was made anew, in a form that bore rent. After the army left the Badshahi Masjid, Khan Bahadur Muhammad Barkat Ali Khan, while serving as *tehsildar*, a revenue officer, gathered some property by subscription whose rent went to the mosque's expenses; a running well and tank were set going and an imam and a muezzin appointed. At the mosque of the released Kashmiri Bazaar shops, whose rents brought in five hundred rupees a year, a committee of notables managed the income; the mosque was well attended, its imam and muezzin drew salaries, repairs were made, and as the land grew the shops increased too. One low mosque had no endowment at all, and the care of the people of the mohalla kept it going and its mulla in his living.

Hindu temples were endowed too, often by purchase. Bhauri Sarkar had a mandir built in her lifetime, bought much farmland and endowed it for the temple's expenses, and planted a very fine garden that still went with it. Inside the city a woman founder, Musammat Jwala Dai, built a mandir and endowed it out of heartfelt devotion. Much property was endowed with Daya Anga's mosque. Pandit Bishnath, being childless, endowed a *pakka* haveli beside his thakurdwara, four shops in a bazaar and a second *pakka* haveli, from whose rents the temple ran comfortably and the pujari had a fair living, and left a will that the income should always go to the temple. At the Bhagwan Das thakurdwara the rents of the shops on the lower storey all went to the mandir, and fourteen more shops built on the east side were also *waqf* in its name.

Shop and house rent was the working income of many Hindu establishments. The Kotwali shiwala had tenants in shops beneath it; the heir of the Teja Singh thakurdwara collected the rent of its bazaar shops on the first storey and spent it on the temple; and at the Shivala Tarpoliya the adjoining shops on the north and west of the bazaar belonged to the mandir, their rent going to the *mahant*. The Baoli had shops and rented *kothris*, small rooms. At one mandir the founders took the rent of the tenanted shops below and spent it on its expenses; the Nihal Chand mandir had four shops to the north and three to the south; and at the Mori Gate three shops were let to wood sellers.

The Chhote Lal shiwala had eight shops facing the bazaar whose rent paid for the temple, and Rai Singh took the rent of his thakurdwara's shops and spent it on the temple. Another mandir owned a *pakka* haveli of three storeys let to tenants, its rent going to the temple's expenses. At the Dehona Das mandir temple land was let to trade: a wood seller kept a shop on it and paid his rent to the *mahant*.

Rents also showed the city's fortunes. While the fort was inhabited, every shop and *baithak*, a sitting room, in the bazaar on that side fetched twenty rupees a month; by the 1880s they stood empty. A son in law, Har Narain, gave five or seven rupees a month towards one mandir's expenses. Rising streets ate into rentable frontage: at Masjid Muhammad Salih Kambo the bazaar's ground had risen until the shops sank half into the earth, only five steps of plinth remaining, and at another mosque the lane had risen so far that the plinth was gone and the northern window that once stood a storey up cleared the lane by about two feet.

Land endowments were measured. Shah Sharf's khanqah held eight *bighas* of cultivated and seven of uncultivated land free of revenue. Hassu Teli's shrine had six *bighas* of cultivated land with a well and a wooden Persian wheel, whose yield met the shrine's expenses and its keepers' living every year. The Shiv Gar shivala had land of its own, and Rani Lachhmi's thakurdwara had land cultivated every harvest. The dharamsal had forty *bighas* and a running well, whose income Nihang Singh took and used to feed travellers. Than Bhairo had land and a well, revenue free since Maharaja Ranjit Singh's time, and eighteen *kanals*, a smaller land measure, remained at Tali Sahib after the railway took the rest.

A shiwala built in the Sikh period by a Kayath, of the scribe caste, had much land, four rooms and a well, and Shah Gada Lahori's grave had cultivated land of its own. Land was also taken by encroachment: Baba Dehona Das drew into his mandir much waste land lying on every side.

Grain, the treasury and the *rakh*, a government grazing reserve, also kept institutions. Mian Muhammad Sultan, the contractor, endowed much land from a *rakh* in the name of a khanqah, and all the grain it yielded went to the expenses of its madrasa, a religious school; in his time the madrasa's prosperity doubled and its income rose. Another *rakh*, granted by the government to the same contractor, later passed into mortgage and was pledged to the Maharaja of Jammu. Another madrasa was paid from the treasury of Lahore by order of the emperor.

Debt could swallow an endowment. After Bhai Nihal Singh's *jagir* at the Baoli, his successor took the monthly shop rents, and within a few years Bhai Jawahar Singh had fallen into debt; when the debt was put under administration a maintenance allowance was fixed for him, and that income too sank in the debt. Muhammad Sultan, owing six or seven lakh rupees, took the money from the Maharaja of Jammu and mortgaged all his property to him, every *kothi*, Landa Bazar, the Pari Mahal shops and the rest, keeping five hundred rupees a month for himself. The Maharaja of Jammu then collected Landa Bazar's shop rents, though the founder had once remitted them, and that remission had itself helped the bazaar to prosper. Muhammad Sultan died with no son of his own; Sirajuddin, son of Abdul Rahman, was declared his heir and also died, and in the 1880s two or three women received a stipend.

The courts of the Muslim kings had their own order. In their time Lahore had four muftis and one *qazi*, the judge; each mufti was reckoned the ruler of a quarter of the city, every case was completed in a mufti's court and then sent to the *qazi*, and the final order came from the *qazi*'s department. By the 1880s only the name of the old muftis was left. Office could be refused at a price. A king wished to make Pir Siraj-ud-Din the city's *qazi*; he refused, and for this disobedience was ordered beheaded in the crossroads bazaar, until the ulama, the learned men, who had brought him to court won his pardon with much pleading. From that day he closed his door to teaching, renounced the world and withdrew into seclusion.

The Mughal state was also the city's builder and keeper. Under Jahangir the royal palace of Lahore was built with many pleasant apartments, and Jahangir ordered a tall minaret at every *kos*, a road measure of about two miles, along the highway from Agra to Lahore, with baked brick wells along the way and two rows of trees; his officers built them and planted fruit trees. Under Aurangzeb the Ravi turned towards the city and damaged its buildings and gardens, and in the fourth year of his reign an embankment ten *kos* long was ordered, called the Alamgiri dam and likened to the dam of Alexander. The riverbank was laid out like a handsome tank, the nobles built pavilions over the water, and for more than forty years the imperial government repaired and rebuilt the work every year at great cost.

The state disciplined the city as well. Jahangir's decrees forbade tobacco as a reprehensible innovation, a *bidʿa*; those who smoked in defiance were paraded in disgrace through Lahore and some even had their lips cut, yet they did not stop, and the habit grew. In the case of Haqiqat Rai, the *qazi* of the city wrote a fatwa for his death and sent it to the governor of Lahore, Zakariya Khan, who told Haqiqat Rai that if he became a Muslim his life could be saved and otherwise he would be beheaded; he refused and was beheaded.

The city's monuments were at the mercy of its rulers. In the nineteenth century it was held that the elders of Lahore invited Ranjit Singh to take the city when Lehna Singh, as its ruler, began destroying the monuments of former kings. Yet in Ranjit Singh's own time the red, black and marble stone of the country around Lahore was torn down. The buildings of the earlier age had survived until his reign with no guardian, and when the cantonments of his armies were built the bricks of old tombs and houses were pulled out, so that thousands of tombs, domes, mosques, gardens and old havelis, memorials of the nobles of the past, were lost. In the Sikh period an oath on the Quran was court procedure, and when one such oath was found to be false the Maharaja had both the man's hands cut off.

The state's hand has also reached Lahore's shrines in other ways. Special trains such as the Jhule Lal Express were started to carry pilgrims on their annual journey between Sehwan and Lahore, the capital of Punjab, and in the city the Bukhari brothers organised pilgrimages to Sehwan, reserving entire trains for them; such private patronage both complemented the state's project at Sehwan and was moved by it. A terror attack at Lahore's most prominent shrine led to tighter security at major Sufi sites across the country, tightening that could be seen at Sehwan by 2011.

The Punjab government banned the use of drugs at a famous Sufi shrine in Lahore to control law and order there, a ban that, as one writer put it, permanently altered the culture of the shrine. Prominent architects of the city petitioned to save a historic Hindu temple in Lahore that the local people were encroaching on, and one of them asked whether it was un-Islamic to save a Hindu temple. Among the endowments of the minorities, the Dyal Singh Memorial Trust, a trust from before Partition based in Lahore, runs the book stall at Tambu Sahib.

The courts and the government have also acted on the city's moral and religious life. The Lahore High Court heard the Shaheed Ganj case under the British and again around 1957 and 1958. In February 2012 the lawyers of the Lahore High Court unofficially banned the sale and drinking of Shezan drinks on the court's premises, because the firm is owned by an Ahmadi family. Behind the Roshani gate lies the Heera-Mandi bazaar, once the area reserved for the city's prostitutes, before they scattered across Lahore after a crackdown under the Islamist government of General Zia-ul-Haq. The fort, once the emblem of Mughal power and wealth, is now a tourist destination, where for twenty Pakistani rupees one can tour even the private chambers of the emperor Jahangir.

## The Religious Economy

For the Hindu temples inside the walled city, the offering was the endowment. At one thakurdwara the temple's necessary expenses rested on offerings alone, with no *jagir* and no daily stipend fixed. At Bishno Devi the building's expenses were met from offerings and the pujari lived on the same, and at the Wilbagh Rai shivala the offering was enough for both the temple and its pujari. At the Thibi Wala shivala a great deal was offered, and on it the *mahant* and the pujaris lived; at Chor Mor Wala the *mahant* and pujaris lived on the offerings alone, and the mandir's expenses were met from them.

The house of Chhajju Bhagat lived the same way, and at Bhadar Kali at Niaz Beg the offerings were enough for the pujaris. At the Sitla Devi mandir the keepers lived on offerings, and at Atar Singh's the pujaris lived on the temple's income. At one temple no stipend or *jagir* was fixed by the government at all; devout Hindus came daily, bowed their foreheads and made their offerings, and the temple lived on what they gave. The Kotwali shiwala, by contrast, had three sources of income: some land, a stipend fixed by the government, and shop rent besides. One pujari lived partly by begging.

Elsewhere the custodian took offerings on top of rent and land. At Sitla Mata the *mahant* took all the rent, and the daily offerings came on top of it. At Bawa Mangal Das's thakurdwara farmland outside the enclosure yielded income to the *mahant*, who took the offerings too. At the Suthra house the *mahant*, a most courteous and hospitable man, served every traveller who came heart and soul and alone took the offerings. At Ganga Parbat one man performed the worship of the mandirs and took their income, and at the tomb of Abul Muali his descendants lived on *piri muridi* and on the tomb's offerings.

Where a temple stood decided much of what it received. Beside Sadhu's well, because the mandir stood on the bazaar, people came in great numbers to worship and much was offered. At the Bhairo ji *asthan* the *mahant* sat in the window and at once picked up whatever cash and goods were offered, because its platform stood on the road. At Mian Mir the five *melas*, festive fairs, and the large offerings were put down to the population of the cantonment.

Some charges were fixed for each occasion. In one Khatri brotherhood every family holding a wedding offered eight annas at the shivala, an anna being a sixteenth of a rupee: four annas when the wedding party set out and four when the bride was brought home, and the pujari took the sum. For a mosque's upkeep it was settled that four annas should be taken from the party to every *nikah*, the Muslim marriage contract, in the city, and the sum grew to a good one each year, alongside a committee of Muslim notables who watched over the mosque. At the Dharm Chand shiwala the whole mohalla contributed to its expenses, with the daily offerings besides. By command of the emperor Alamgir, Jhingar Shah Suthra had one paisa a shop a year fixed for him in every city. At the cremation ground a man took one anna for each body and helped with the burning, in the same *dalan*, a covered hall, where the government sepoy who guarded the ground lived.

A burial ground paid its people in the same way. In the nineteenth century, when a body came, the family of the dead gave the gravediggers their due for digging the grave and gave the *sajjada nashin* something besides, as they were able. A gravedigger kept his share whatever other work he did.

*Nazr*, the offering made to the holy, ran upwards and was sometimes refused. Ranjit Singh gave one holy man lakhs of rupees in cash as *nazr* and fixed *jagirs* and stipends for him. After his illness the Maharaja made an offering at the grave of Miran Badshah, bowed his forehead there and vowed that such an act would not happen again. The sultans of the time gave lakhs of rupees as *nazr* to Shah Chiragh's family. Against these, Mastan Shah threw away the *nazrs* of a thousand rupees that Ranjit Singh sent him, refusing to take even one.

One lender refused to be repaid. Haji Nur, of the Pahracha caste, lent Shah Jahan one crore rupees for a campaign: the emperor summoned him and said, Baba, lend me a crore of rupees, and he gave it at once. Years later he refused to take it back, saying that since the emperor had called him Baba, father, he had become his son.

*Nazr* also ran downwards, from patron to participants. For the ten days of Muharram the Qizilbash household kept the *taziya*, the replica of Imam Husain's tomb, at their house and gave lavish meals day and night to Sunni and Shia alike. On the seventh of Muharram, the day of the *mehndi* procession, all who carried the *alam*, the battle standard, came to the house, and the chief gave each of them a *nazr* of one to ten rupees; in all, not less than ten thousand rupees a year was spent. At Mubarak Haveli the seventh of Muharram drew the *mehndi* and *alam* processions of the whole city, and Nawab Nawazish Ali gave every one of them a cash *nazr* from his own purse.

*Chanda*, subscription, was the alternative to endowment. Temples were built by subscription and run on the offerings, one of them within the ten years before the 1880s. The image of Bishno Devi was brought from Amritsar and its shrine built by subscription. A silver *jalhari*, the basin that holds the lingam, weighing four hundred rupees, was remade by subscription after a theft. Under Sikh rule the people of the bazaar raised money and built a shivala beside a well, which still stood in the 1880s, and the people of one mohalla together bought a house where a shop had been and had a thakurdwara built in it.

A mosque was finished by subscription after its founder's death, the people of the mohalla collecting money, with a large donation for the sake of God from Sayyid Haidar Shah of the Canal Department. A shiwala was raised on money collected by begging, and British officials turned to subscription too.

Private money did public work. At the house of the Faqir brothers there were a *shifakhana*, a dispensary, and a madrasa where people were taught free. Nawab Abdul Majid Khan gave out English and native medicines worth hundreds of rupees from his own means, and built and repaired many mosques in Lahore, which won him the highest good name. Hajj and pilgrimage were spending for the elite: two Hajjs and a pilgrimage to Karbala, lakhs of rupees spent on the Hajj, and a body sent to Karbala. Charitable wells were even built from the proceeds of a theft: some time after the thief was hanged, the shopkeeper used the same goods to build several wells in Lahore, and the well named after Sadhu the thief is one of them.

Money moved at the scale of the court. On the day of Nur Jahan's bath of recovery the emperor gave her physician a robe of honour worth one lakh and seven lakh in cash; the queen gave all the jewellery she wore, and her attendant slave girls gave theirs as *tasadduq*, alms given for another's safety. The physician already headed the royal hospitals. Jawahar Singh courted the Sikh troops with a gold *kantha*, a necklace, worth twenty five rupees for each soldier, and robes, gold bangles, pearl necklaces and *jagirs* for the officers, but even so his time as vizier did not last. Of Moran, the Maharaja struck coin in her name, which circulated through the whole Punjab, and in the 1880s her coins were still kept as keepsakes.

## Religious Property under British Rule

In the 1880s the state's repairs to Lahore's buildings ran through its Executive Engineer, Kanhaiya Lal, the city's historian. He administered an annual repair grant for Shalamar Bagh; the supervision and annual repair of the Tehsil building were his; Montgomery Hall was under his supervision, its annual repairs carried out through him by the Municipal Committee of Lahore, and his replacement roof was ready at the end of 1865; and the repair and supervision of the women's jail were still his. He also urged that if the administration would turn to the Badshahi Masjid once more, its repair could be completed.

Under the British, *nazul* and auction were the means by which religious property changed hands: property was entered in the *nazul* register and auctioned on application. Shaikh Muhtaram's *rauza*, a tomb with its garden, was auctioned at an Englishman's request and made into a *kothi*, its graves demolished. Abul Hasan Khan's tomb was auctioned and quarried for its stone. Nusrat Khan's tomb passed to *nazul* and was then sold to Lala Mela Ram. The Dai Anga mosque was entered in the *nazul* register, and the *deorhi*, the gatehouse, of Jani Khan's garden was bought from the government by a *zamindar*.

Nawab Mian Khan's tomb garden, without an owner, was taken by Raja Suchet Singh and then bought by Nawab Ali Raza Khan Qizilbash for two thousand two hundred rupees. Sultan the contractor bought the Pari Mahal from the government, rooted it out to its foundations, and used its bricks to build Landa Bazar and a *sarai*; the *sarai*'s land too was taken from the government, because it held the chowk of Prince Dara Shikoh and its bricks. The well and garden of the Kallo Bai haveli were auctioned to Diwan Ajudhia Prasad. *Nazul* land could also found a temple: the government auctioned land that had lain vacant since the Sikh period, and Nihal Chand bought it and began to build his mandir on it. State land had been bought for a temple before, in Sambat 1910 of the Bikrami era.

The state sold and demolished monuments itself. The walls of Mahtabi Bagh survived, decayed, into the English period, until the government sold them to Mela Ram, and the ground went under mango trees and cultivation. The government pulled down Maharaja Kharak Singh's haveli and sold its bricks and stones, and people carried off the materials of that splendid house. The royal buildings of the fort were pulled down and quarters built in their place for the British soldiers. At the Badshahi Masjid the eastern cells were pulled down in the English period and the eastern wall razed; British troops were quartered in the mosque, windows were cut in the cells for the English, and new verandahs were built. At Nur Jahan's tomb the ebony coffins were taken from their stone vault and the bodies buried in the ground; the tomb was repaired in brick and lime and the basement left open, so that people went in and fouled it.

Tombs and mosques were cleared for fortification, roads, pipes and rails, and this began before the British. For Ranjit Singh's moat and double gates an open field was needed before the Bhati Gate, so both a mosque and a tomb were pulled down. Shah Sharaf's coffin was taken out and placed, through Faqir Aziz-ud-Din, in the enclosure of Haji Muhammad Saeed's grave, and by the Maharaja's order a small masonry platform was built over it, which still stood in the 1880s. When the Assistant Commissioner began the present Kotwali and the widening of the bazaar was approved, land was taken from the shivala there too, and its southern wall was pulled down and set back. At the Shivala Tarpoliya a sitting place on the bazaar side was demolished by order of the officials.

In the early 1880s the government resolved to straighten every large bazaar of the city along one side and to lay water pipes, and it paid settlements. The Sufi Wali Masjid lost part of its fabric to the road, for which the government paid five hundred rupees, and Haji Maula Dad added much of his own. At Nura's first mosque the staircase, both *hujras*, the small cells, part of the courtyard and a row of shops went into the road, and the government's money rebuilt the staircase and a *hujra* above it and widened the courtyard with red stone slabs. About half the land of the second Nur Muhammad mosque was taken for the water pipes, and the compensation paid for a smaller rebuilding under Fazl Haq. When the bazaar's western line was straightened for Lahore's water supply, much of Rai Singh's mandir's land and its well went into the bazaar, and he added his own money to what the government paid.

A road to the hospital crossed temple land at Sitla Mata: the government took land from it, cut a straight road, and in return gave cash and other land in exchange. The railway was the largest taker of all. At one shivala the grand western door stayed shut because the railway bought the adjoining land on that side and paid five hundred rupees in compensation, and worshippers used the northern way instead. At Tali Sahib the land came inside the railway's yard, and only eighteen *kanals* on the north side within the boundary wall remained. The *tahli* tree there, which had stood since the time of Sri Chand, which gave the place its name and was worshipped, the railway cut down; its root greened again and grew back into a tree.

The railway's reach went further. It took the Dai Anga mosque and paid its private occupant; it kept its material in the Shahjahani *sarai*; it lodged its men in a converted tomb and its workshop officer in a converted mosque; and it brought crowds to the *melas* from Amritsar and distant cities. In one *sarai* the shops between its northern gate and the bazaar were occupied by prostitutes, and a government police post stood inside it.

One temple's claim against the state ran its full course. A government water pipe in the lane let water into the foundations of the Bhagat Ram shiwala and cracked its walls; the founder's son petitioned, an inquiry was held, the Deputy Commissioner and other officers came to the spot, the pipe was dug up and the cause proved, and five hundred rupees in cash were paid for the repair, with which he mended it. The telegraph office was built on the land of an old well, for which the *zamindars* of the village of Mozang had already been compensated, and a confiscated haveli was given to Raja Tej Singh in exchange for the *kothi* that became Government House, the alternatives offered him being cash at valuation or another government house.

Restitution came through named officers, and sometimes went beyond what the mosque owned. The Masti Darwaza mosque had been entered and recorded as *nazul* by Qazi Faqih-ud-Din of that department, but Major MacGregor, Deputy Commissioner of Lahore, released it to the Muslims with great generosity, together with a *baithak* and shops that did not belong to it. At Dalip Singh's exile the army was taken out of the Badshahi Masjid and the government handed the mosque to the Muslims, and Sayyid Buzurg Shah Hakim, whose forebears had been its imams in the royal period, was made its *mutawalli*, though no revival followed.

At the Nila Gumbad, after the mess left, Munshi Najm-ud-Din petitioned the government, had the mosque and tomb released and paid for the mosque's repair himself; the mosque revived, came to be called Najm-ud-Din's mosque, and its imamat was given to Maulvi Nur Ahmad. Another mosque, empty for some years, revived when Maulvi Ghulam Qadir petitioned the government and obtained an order restoring it to use, and Imam Bakhsh the butcher, who held the contract for supplying meat to the British troops, spent much money on its repair and whitewashing. The Sunehri Masjid was seized by the Akalis, an order of armed Sikh devotees, settled by a *mochalka*, a written bond, kept under *nazul*, and at last released under Egerton.

Under every regime the use of holy buildings changed: tombs and mosques became barracks, *kothis*, offices, kitchens, stores, a treasury, a mess, a police station, a mission school and an asylum. Shah Chiragh's mosque served as officers' quarters and then as the Accountant's office. The Nila Gumbad was whitewashed into a mess. Bahadur Khan's tomb was converted when the Mian Mir cantonment was made, men of the Railway department lived in it, and the grave was demolished without trace. Ali Mardan Khan's *deorhi* became a Sikh colonel's home, and Abul Hasan Khan's tomb was occupied by a French general.

Nusrat Khan's tomb was absorbed into General Court's *kothi* with its old mosque, and a floor laid over the grave. The Kambo tomb was made a *kothi* by an Englishman, and an adjoining dome turned into a kitchen. A mosque at Asaf Jah's tomb was turned into a *kothi* by a railway employee, and an Englishman made his *kothi* of the mosque and well at Pir Hadi's shrine. The Shahjahani *sarai* held the railway's coal and timber, and the Moti Masjid, renamed the Moti Mandir by Ranjit Singh, served as the treasury under the sultanate and again under the English.

The buildings of Fazl Shah and the Rang Mahal served first as a *thana*, a police station, and then as a mission school. A *sarai* built as a Sikh general's cantonment became, under the English, the asylum for the insane without being pulled down. A Mughal *sarai* at Ichhra lost its travellers and was filled with cannonballs for years, which gave it the name Goliyan Wali, the place of the balls; it then became family property, and lodged government prisoners before the Lahore jail was built. Sayyid Chiragh Shah's mosque was in government hands, the English made Dai Anga's mosque a *kothi*, and in the mosque of Dara Shikoh an Englishman had made himself a home.

The fullest single case is the Dai Anga mosque, which passed through five occupiers, the money going to the occupant and not to the endowment. Endowed as *waqf* under the Mughals, it held powder and shot for years under Ranjit Singh, was emptied and put on the *nazul* register, and then Mr Cope, superintendent of the Lahore press, got government permission to make it his *kothi*. When the railway works were set up on the spot, the railway took the mosque and paid Cope its price, and an officer of the ironworks came to live in it; in the words of a historian of the city in the 1880s, the *kothi* that ought to be called a mosque was now the railway's. The English kept its arches of enamelled tile, *kansi kar*, with Quranic verses and blessings on the Prophet in *naskh* script, intact for their beauty, since such coloured work would be hard to make again.

The furthest any holy place passed into state hands was Gilani's tomb. Desolated by Sikh plunder, its dome became a wrestlers' arena, and a *jamadar*, an officer, demolished the grave and built an octagonal house round it. The house was lived in by British Deputy Commissioners and a Commissioner and was then taken over as the Lieutenant Governor's residence; the old tomb remained as it was in the lower storey, with the doorway of one dome blocked.

Popular devotion could defeat a security objection. The authorities set about pulling down the Suthra house several times, because anyone who wished could climb from its walls and the branches of its trees into the fort, but each time it was spared, because thousands revered the place. Clearance could even give a shrine a new building. Under English rule, when the houses inside the chowk were demolished and the square opened out, Muhammad Sultan the contractor built a *rauza* over the grave there, and its dome added to the grave's splendour and doubled the beauty of the chowk. A century before, in Mir Mannu's time, when the founder of the Sunehri Masjid straightened the bazaar to set off his mosque, a grave came to stand in the road, and he rebuilt it as a masonry room with an arched doorway.

The public square itself passed through three regimes. Chowk Wazir Khan was open under the Chughtais, the Mughals; in the Sikh period shops and sitting rooms were built inside it; in the English period all those buildings were demolished and the ground opened out as before, and a contractor built a khanqah inside it, beside a well built by Raja Dina Nath.

Two fatwas governed mosque land in the chowk, and a British officer later did what one of them forbade. The founder of the Sunehri Masjid asked the governor for the chowk's land; the governor could not grant it on his own authority and asked the ulama for a fatwa, and they gave leave, holding that the land is God's property and that whatever of it is given for building a house of God is most fitting. But when he wanted to absorb the small older mosque in the chowk, the ulama refused, writing that where people have once prayed it is not lawful to build the stair, the door or the washroom of another mosque; so the Nawab gave his mosque a road on two sides and built stairs on both, which were most unsightly. Later Captain Nisbet, Deputy Commissioner of Lahore, had the old small mosque, by then unused, pulled down, built the mosque's eastern door with a stair planned on the east, and pulled down the old stairs on the two sides, adding their ground to the mosque.

Grants and houses were confiscated. Raja Suchet Singh's haveli was confiscated and used again by the Tehsil department, and Diwan Hakim Rai's haveli had been confiscated in the last Sikh disturbance. With them went Bakhshi Bhagat Ram's *jagir* and the temple village inside it, the Bhai's *jagir* and property lost to debt, and the Haqiqat Rai grant taken back at the grantee's death.

The British also repaired and preserved. One tomb's name had already been entered in the register for repair by the government. The English kept Jahangir's tomb in repair from the first, and when the river came near they spent thousands of rupees on an embankment to protect it. Municipal drainage works at government expense filled the great tank at Haveli Mian Khan, whose stagnant water had spread a stench through the mohalla. The Wazir Khan *baradari*, a pavilion with twelve doors, in very bad condition in Sikh times, was repaired by the English so well that it seemed built anew. The enclosure of the *marghat*, the cremation ground, was built by English order, and the unfinished *samadh* of Kharak Singh and Nau Nihal Singh was completed.

Descendants restored their forebears' tombs. Ghulam Muhi-ud-Din Shah, of his line, built the outer enclosure of Abdul Jalil Qutb-e Alam's tomb. Nawab Zakariya Khan Khan Bahadur built the *maqbara*, the tomb, of Khawand Mahmud because he was one of the saint's descendants, and settled the mohalla of Mughalpura on all four sides of the grave. At Shah Sarwani's tomb, after the Sikhs had stripped the whole building, its garden and half the bricks of its well, a descendant came to Lahore with the Superintendent, repaired and whitewashed the tomb, built a new verandah, set the well working again, built a *kotha*, a room, and seated a faqir in it, and planted trees on the tomb's land.

The repair of the Badshahi Masjid was raised at a Deputy Commissioner's *darbar*, a public assembly, with a government grant and subscriptions from every community. Nawab Nawazish Ali Khan, Muhammad Barkat Ali Khan and Nawab Abdul Majid Khan petitioned the Deputy Commissioner of Lahore, who held a *darbar*, gathered Hindus and Muslims from inside and outside the city and urged them to subscribe, gave money from his own purse and obtained a good sum from the government. The mosque was repaired, but in the 1880s the turrets of its four minarets, the courtyard floor and many stone slabs were still waiting.

Under colonial rule hereditary claim gave way to qualification. Shams-ud-Din drew up a petition and had the seals of the people, elders, chiefs and gentry of Lahore set on it, but the officials preferred a qualified jurist, another man was made *qazi*, and the court took its fatwas from the blind Hafiz Wali Allah. Teaching passed from the mosques to the government. Under the Sikhs there was no government madrasa, but there were four madrasas in mosques and faqirs' lodges, one of them teaching thousands of Hindus and Muslims, and boys were taught in every mosque besides; the course was Persian, Arabic, grammar, syntax, logic, rhetoric, hadith and Quranic commentary, and the first lesson was the Quran.

Under the English, government schools were opened everywhere. A single state college taught Hindu law, the *shastri*, and Muslim *sharia* questions under Maulvi Abd al-Hakim, beside English law, and in one school boys were made to learn by heart *ghazals*, love lyrics, in praise of Jesus Christ.

At Moran's mosque the change can be seen in one family. By Maharaja Ranjit Singh's order the imamat of the mosque was given to Maulana Ghulam Rasul and Ghulam Allah, because there was then no greater teacher in Lahore and they were the master teachers of the whole Punjab. A madrasa began with them, the mosque's fame spread to other lands and students came from far away; but when both had died and government schools opened under English rule, the madrasa's line was broken. Of Ghulam Allah's five sons, Imam al-Din had died; Nizam al-Din taught without payment, for the sake of God, in Bombay; Ahmad Bakhsh taught in the government school at Jhelum; Khalifa Ghulam Muhyi al-Din taught in the Jalandhar district; and Khalifa Hamid al-Din taught in the Lahore Government School and still held the imamat of the mosque. Within two generations three of the five sons held state teaching posts.

The mosque and shrine schools that survived stood beside this. Wazir Khan's mosque had sixteen great cells round its wall for scholars and students to live in, and the Badshahi Masjid had cells for dervishes and students, where a Madrasa Islamiya was started with its own imam and preacher. A madrasa was attached to a Suhrawardi tomb, and Maulvi Muhammad Bakhsh taught in the cell of one mosque. The madrasa of Mian Wadda, finished in Akbar's reign, taught the Quran, commentary, hadith and jurisprudence, kept the blind and the lame by the hundred, and was endowed on the grain of a *rakh*. Craft and copying went on in the holy places too: Miran Bakhsh the illuminator, famed for illumination in printing ink and for gold work, worked in a room of a mosque.

The colonial state licensed, administered and suppressed. The *chiraghan* mela, the festival of lamps held inside a garden, used to last one day; the government made it two, and merchandise came there for sale. Outside the Delhi Gate stood the house of the Rasul Shahi order, among whom the drinking of wine came before every act of worship and a still ran day and night. On the recommendation of Raja Dina Nath the British licensed them to distil for their own drinking only; for fifteen or sixteen years the house flourished as before, and then the government grew suspicious on information it received and closed the still, and from that day no one came to the house. At the bhang dharamsal, built for Sikhs to lodge the night and drink bhang, a drink made from cannabis, bhang was ground day and night and its drinkers gathered with the government's permission. The garden of the man who held the *sair*, the duty on goods traded in the whole city, gave way to the government hospital, and on its one surviving well the same wooden Persian wheel was kept.

The colonial state reached into shrine succession through the city's schools as well. In the 1930s there was a fierce debate in Chishti circles over whether the British could compel a minor who had become *diwan*, the hereditary head, of the shrine at Pakpattan to be educated at a British college in Lahore. The provincial government, with the governor of Punjab, sat in Lahore.

## Festivals and Practice

By the 1880s Lahore kept a crowded calendar of festivals, and Hindus and Muslims shared some of it. On the twenty eighth of Shaban Hindus and Muslims together held a *mela*, a fair or festival gathering, at Shah Bilawal's *khanqah*, the Sufi lodge where disciples gather. Mian Mir had five melas a year: the *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death, which lasted two days and two nights, and four more on the four Wednesdays of Sawan, with offerings in abundance. Ghore Shah had an annual mela, and at Shaikh Musa's grave the blacksmiths, many of them pledged disciples, or *murids*, of his family, kept the yearly *ʿurs*.

Many other saints had their days. At Hamid Qari's khanqah the *ʿurs* fell in Jumada al-Thani, on the date of his death, and Shaikh Mahmud Shah Naqshbandi's tomb had its annual *ʿurs* too. Hassu Teli's fell on the third of Shawwal, Fateh Shah Mast's on the seventh of Shawwal and Shah Chiragh's on the seventeenth of Rabi us-Sani. Faqir Sain Qutb Shah Qadri's *ʿurs* came twice a year, with *qawwali*, the devotional music of the shrines, all day, and Mauj Darya's annual *ʿurs* drew a great crowd. At Shah Abu Ishaq Qadri's shrine, on the fifth of Muharram, the Quran was read all night.

Abul Muali's shrine had three melas a year besides both Eids, with illumination and dancing courtesans on the first night and *qawwali* on the second. At the *rauza*, the tomb, of Shah Abu al-Maali there were melas on both Eids, offerings and an annual *ʿurs*. Taj Shah Majzub's *ʿurs* fell on the date of his death, and it was paid for privately: its managers, Nur Muhammad and his son Bota, held the annual mela on his date at their own expense, in fulfilment of a prayer that had been granted.

At Data Ganj Bakhsh there was a mela every Friday, the *ʿurs* in Safar, the mela of the Last Wednesday and a vigil every Thursday night. Muhammad Fazil's *ʿurs* fell in Rajab and Shah Sharf's in Safar, while Shah Sarwani's was kept twice, on the ninth of Safar and the ninth of Rabi al-Awwal. Shah Yaqub Zanjani's shrine had once held a Thursday mela and had a great mela in Rajab. The Huzuri khanqah held a two day annual mela, with lamps on the first day and *qawwali* on the second, and at a Qadiri and Shattari *mazar*, a saint's tomb, Sufis gathered every year for a mela to hear *sama*, spiritual listening to music, and fell into ecstasy.

Muharram was kept in the great houses. In the Qizilbash household there was *taʿziyadari*, the mourning observance of Muharram, for the ten days, with food given out day and night to Sunni and Shia alike. On the seventh, the day of the *mehndi*, the henna procession, the *alam* bearers, who carry the standards, came to the house and each received from one to ten rupees. At Mubarak Haveli the seventh of Muharram drew the *mehndi* and *alam* processions of the whole city. Under Ranjit Singh it had been proclaimed in the city that whoever brought out a *taʿzia*, the model of a tomb carried in the Muharram procession, would lose his head.

Congregations could be large. Six or seven thousand worshippers came together for the Eid prayers at the Badshahi Masjid, and the church at Mian Mir had about eight hundred seats.

The Hindu and Sikh calendars were just as full. On every Ekadashi, the eleventh day of the lunar fortnight, one temple held a great mela: devotees gathered in numbers, bhagats sang *bhajans*, devotional hymns, all night, and offerings were made in abundance. At an Ekadashi assembly inside a *thakurdwara*, a temple of Vishnu or Krishna, *qawwals* came and delighted the listeners with songs of divine unity. At the Kali Mata *mandir*, or temple, a mela gathered on every one of the nine days of Navratri; hundreds of Hindus came to bow their foreheads, Hindus from other lands came too and were blessed, and offerings were many. On the day of Basant, the spring festival, a mela was held at the *samadh*, the memorial shrine, of Haqiqat Rai, and its offerings brought a good income.

On the night of the fourteenth of Phagan a great mela was held at a *shivala*, a temple of Shiva, beside a well, and countless people came from the city to worship. The Devi Dwara of Rajo and Dharman held two annual melas, each on an Ashtami, the eighth day of the lunar fortnight, when great crowds gathered and offerings were abundant, and through the year it kept vigils at which the bhagats sang the hymns of the Devi, the goddess, all night. The Mata temple held eight vigils a month, among them one every Tuesday and one on the *panchami*, the fifth day of the fortnight. At Mozang the Sikhs gathered on the fifth day of every bright half of the moon, when *prashad*, consecrated food, was distributed and lamps were lit.

The greatest mela of the city was Bhadar Kali's at Niaz Beg, held every year in the month of Jeth; no mela in Lahore was greater, and Muslims too, for the most part, attended. Because it fell in the hot season people suffered. Stalls of sharbat, a sweet cold drink, of water and of pounded bhang, a drink made from hemp, were set up, and sharbat was given to all alike; there was little shade, and tents were pitched. The crowd stayed a night and a day, and shopkeepers moved their shops there in advance and prepared sweets. Inside the walls, on the same day, the city temple of Bhadr Kali drew an immense gathering of women and men, those who did not go out to the village coming there to worship.

Than Bhairo's great mela fell on a Sunday at the new moon of Bhadon, with offerings and an all night vigil of *bhajans*, and besides it hundreds of people came from the city every Sunday to bow their heads there.

By the 1880s many melas, *ʿurs* and *sama* gatherings were in decline. At the *mazar* of Miran Badshah inside the Wazir Khan mosque, in the time of the Sikhs, a mela had been held every Thursday where thousands gathered, and the annual *ʿurs* and *sama* were held with great pomp; Hindus and Muslims still came on Thursdays and the *ʿurs* was still kept, but the *sama* belonged to the past. At Imam Ghulam Muhammad's tomb the annual mela and the *sama* gathering had stopped entirely. At Sayyid Sarbuland's *mazar* crowds had gathered on Thursdays in the Sikh period and the annual *ʿurs* had been kept with pomp, but that brisk trade was no more, and the same was true of the tomb at the Masjid Suf and of the bookbinders' shops. Fateh Shah Mast's great mela of Sikh times no longer drew such a crowd, and at Shah Husain Zanjani's *mazar* the great annual mela of the Sikh period had little by little lost its splendour.

Shah Yaqub Zanjani's melas were killed by the building around them: on two sides Lala Ratan Chand's *sarai*, a travellers' inn, and the buildings of his tank hid the khanqah, it was hemmed in on all four sides, and its graveyard too had been closed. At Fazl Shah Majzub's khanqah the *ʿurs*, kept with great pomp in Raja Dina Nath's lifetime, had dwindled to a yearly *fatiha*, the short prayer for the dead, shrinking to that with the death of its patron. Two melas grew instead: Mian Mir's, with the population of the garrison, and the *chiraghan* mela, the festival of lamps, whose crowds came from Lahore, Amritsar and distant cities because of the railway. The anniversary of Madho Lal is still celebrated in Lahore near the Shalimar Garden on the last Saturday in March. It is famous as *Melā Chirāghān*, the Fair of Lamps, and is held every year at the Shalimar Gardens.

Vows were tied to sickness, and some of these rites were shared across the traditions. On Sundays Hindus and Muslims alike took children suffering from a sickness to Shah Dargahi's well and bathed them there. Each person brought four sweet loaves and four salted ones, and after the bath the zamindar of the well, its landholder, took the loaves together with one paisa for the lamp. At Sitla Mata worship went on all the time, but the special worship came when smallpox was raging: while the sickness was at its height people gave offerings, each as much as he could, and thousands whose children had recovered came in joy. At the Sitla Devi mandir, after the bath of recovery from smallpox, cash and a goat were offered.

Offerings were also made in kind. At Ghore Shah clay horses were offered at the *mazar* in thousands on the day of the mela, so that heaps of them always lay on the parapets of the tomb. At one grave a vow of bread was current, told as a *karamat*, a miracle of the saint. Whoever came to the grave in need vowed a number of loaves, and when at last by God's grace his work was brought to completion, he cooked the loaves, brought them to the grave and shared them out.

Daily and weekly worship kept its hours. At the Kotwali shiwala the *arti*, the worship with lamps, was performed at four in the morning and seven in the evening, and again whenever a notable came, so that no hour was empty of worship, with special worship on Mondays. Its instruments were the great kettledrum, the pipe and the gong, and the pipe, the gong and the conch sounded so loudly that no one could hear anyone else speak. *Arti* was performed twice daily at the Bhagat Ram shiwala, and worship twice daily at the Radha Kishan thakurdwara and in Chhajju Bhagat's house, whose great hall was counted a shivala; the Granth, the Sikh scripture, was read twice daily at the Guru Ram Das gurdwara. At Chor Mor Wala the *mahant*, the head of the establishment, being a learned man, himself gave the *katha*, the recitation and exposition of scripture, every evening.

At the Ram Dwara the *katha* of the Bhagavata, the Ramayana and other holy texts was recited twice a day. There every attender was handed a rosary of *tulsi*, holy basil, by the mahant and told to sing the praise of Ram; a heap of rosaries lay in the middle of the circle, and one man was appointed solely to look after them. A lamp burned night and day at the Bhairo ji *asthan*, or seat, and at Than Bhairo's fire place it never went out. A *naqara*, a kettledrum, was played at two times of day at the Baoli and at *arti* time at Banke Bihari, and mystic songs were sung in the evening at the Kuka *dharamsal*, a Sikh place of worship and shelter. At a Hindu bhagat's samadh, every Monday and Tuesday, Hindu devotees, women and men, attended while *qawwali* was sung in the mystic raga.

Some rites gathered around ordinary places. A saint's shop became a second site of his cult: a flag still flew over it, a lamp was lit there every day, and on Thursdays many lamps were lit. Boys played all day at Pir Bhola's grave and climbed up on it, and every Friday the boys of the city made a *mazar* of Pir Bhola in lane after lane, offering flowers on it and lighting lamps.

The *langar*, the free communal kitchen, fed the poor across the traditions. By old custom any poor person who came to the Ram Dwara was fed, and any traveller too, and its offerings went to two heads only, the necessary expenses of Pandit Kanshi Ram and the cost of the daily *langar*. Shah Bilawal Qadri spent thousands of rupees a day on a public *langar*, and a public *langar* ran continuously at Mauj Darya's house. At Than Bhairo the temple's mahant fed the *faqirs*, holy mendicants, and the jogis, wandering ascetics, who gathered for the mela, while lay visitors cooked their own food. At both of Shah Sharf's melas faqirs gathered and food was given out; at Shah Sarwani's tomb faqirs gathered on both *ʿurs* dates to eat and to hear *qawwali*; and at Mubarak Haveli the food given to the poor and the faqirs during the ten days of Muharram was cooked in one part of the building.

Land and mills stood behind the kitchens. One *dharamsal* had forty *bighas*, a bigha being a measure of land, and a running well, whose income Nihang Singh took and with which he fed travellers, and a mill was built there to grind flour for them. A mill ran continuously inside Mian Wada's enclosure, and another was built for grinding flour on the Bhauri Sarkar estate. Mian Wada's *madrasa*, a religious school, was a charity for the disabled with a headcount and a curriculum. Under the Sikhs the blind, the lame and the crippled, a hundred dervishes and faqirs, and students in their hundreds received bread and clothing from it; by the 1880s the khanqah maintained about two hundred blind and lame people and five dervishes, all were taught the Quran, and the blind were made to learn it by heart.

At one establishment hospitality was the mahant's rule. One dharamsala lived wholly on offerings: its faqirs were supported only by the service and gifts of devotees, *bhajans* were sung daily in the third watch, offerings were made on the Granth, and any travelling faqir was given food. Before moving the image of Thakur ji from Rangilpur to Lahore with great honour, Hari Narayan first spent a great deal of money there feeding sadhus, Hindu holy men, and Hindus.

Bhang had its place in shrine practice. At the samadh of Guru Arjan a building held a stone mortar sunk in the ground and a pestle for grinding bhang. At Shahid Ganj the Nihang Sikhs, the order of Sikh warriors, ground bhang all day in a roadside hall where its cauldrons stood, and the stalls of the Bhadar Kali mela served it pounded. One mahant's own offering, his *nazr*, was a little bhang that he called *prashad*.

*Sama* and music ran through the city's devotion, held at some shrines and fallen silent at others. In the assemblies Punjabi *kafis*, *barah mah* and *abyat* were sung: the *kafi* is a mystic lyric, the *barah mah* a poem of the twelve months, and *abyat* are couplets. In the sixteenth century, it is told, a man of Lahore came and said that in his time there was no one worthy of listening to music, *samaʿ*: "In this time there is no one worthy". Women musicians performed *hazrī*, literally "presence", at the Sufi shrines of Lahore, above all at the time of the *ʿurs*, and the same word was used of the qawwals who sang at the city's shrines both before Partition and after it. In recent fieldwork a grassroots musician, Zarina Khatoon, was interviewed in Lahore, and a discography of Sufi music lists four recordings by qawwal parties from the city, those of Kausar Jabin, of Mubarak Ali and Fateh Ali, of Muhammad Siddiq and of Alaf Din Pathan.

A modern Sufi order keeps a quieter practice in the city. Its *zikr* circles, gatherings for the remembrance of God by repeated recitation, meet in Lahore, and their full repertoire of remembrance was recorded at a gathering on a Thursday evening in October 2000. Hospitality is refused at these gatherings: "That is why serving any food or even a cup of tea is strictly forbidden, because some people then might come just to socialize".

Lahore drew pilgrims in and sent them out. Pilgrims passing through visited its shrines: Fakhr-ud-Din, on his first journey towards Ajmer, visited all the *mazars* of Lahore. At the time of a saint's festival, a fakir arriving at Data Darbar found that "countless other fakirs had gathered in the city". In the autumn of 2011 a visiting fakir from Lahore was at Sehwan "to perform his duty at the shrine of Lal", keeping his retreat by the river there. A Lahore woman of Shahdara is a regular at the Maryabad festival, where on the second day the bishop comes from Lahore.

The Milad procession, in honour of the Prophet's birthday, began in Lahore in a single year at a single mosque. In 1935 a group of about a hundred people left the Wazir Khan Mosque singing praises of the Prophet and walked around the alleys of the walled city. The mosque's prayer leader, Maulana Syed Dedar Shah, had argued that if Hindus could hold festivals and processions for what he called their fake gods, Muslims could do the same for their Prophet, though most of his followers rejected the idea. Soon afterwards Karam Elahi, also of Lahore, organised his own little procession, and Inayatullah Qadri, another religious scholar, began holding an annual Mehafal-e-Millad, a gathering in the Prophet's honour. The event crossed the boundaries of the walled city and spread to neighbouring villages and towns; in time the procession had to be moved outside Delhi Gate, where a permanent platform was built, and the junction where it converged every year took the name Millad Chowk.

The city's religious minorities keep their own calendar. Lahore's year includes Shri Valmiki's birthday and Krishna Janamashtami, Palm Sunday and Good Friday at the Sacred Heart Cathedral, Easter at the Cathedral Church of Resurrection, Navroz and Hazrat Bab's birthday, and the commemorations of Guru Arjan's martyrdom at Gurdwara Dera Sahib and of Maharaja Ranjit Singh's death anniversary. "On religious festivals like Holi, Diwali and Shri Valmiki's birthday, the Neela Gumbad Mandir becomes an island of religious syncretism in Lahore". Before 9/11, a Catholic remembers, a huge procession used to leave the Don Bosco School on Railway Road for the Sacred Heart Cathedral, and all the Catholic churches of the city merged into it. The city's yearly religious conferences of about three thousand people were stopped at the government's request, following threats of rising terrorism.

Easter breakfast at the Cathedral Church of Resurrection is "halwa puri and channa, typical Lahori nashta", the city's own breakfast. At Navroz, Daria Boatwala says, "Nothing really happens here as you can see", and tambola is the only game the community plays in Lahore now. On a Sunday on Mall Road, cricket in all the grounds is a usual Sunday feature.

Older and humbler customs linger at the edges. A man in Lahore claimed to have spent forty days inside a grave. In the nineteenth century the Shakir Shahi *konda*, a clay bowl, was famous in Lahore, made by Shakir Shah Kahar at two annas a *konda*. Urdu almanacs printed in Bombay and Lahore still prominently feature the death anniversaries of the Sufi saints of the subcontinent, and copies of al-Busiri's *Burda*, the devotional ode, are still sold in the shops of Lahore. And once the army of Khalilullah Khan and Raja Jai Singh camped outside the city on the day of Eid al-Adha.

## Poetry

Lahore was a city of Persian poets under the Ghaznavid kings. Mas'ud-i-Sa'd-i-Salman's family came from Hamadan in Persia, but he himself was born at Lahore, and in several passages of his poems he speaks of it as his native place. He was probably born about 440 AH (1048 to 1049) and died about 515 AH (1121 to 1122). He was the panegyrist, the poet of praise, of five Ghaznavid kings, Ibrahim, Mas'ud III, Shirzad, Arslan and Bahramshah, and his special patron was Sultan Ibrahim's son Mahmud, who was made governor of India in 469 AH (1076 to 1077).

He shared his master's disgrace and was imprisoned; once freed, he returned to his father's estate in India. When Mas'ud III gave the government of India to his son Shirzad, the commander in chief made the poet governor of Jalandar, a dependency of Lahore. Imprisoned again, he was released about 500 AH and, being then sixty, retired from public life and spent the rest of his days in seclusion. Sana'i, who first collected and edited his poetry, was among those who bore witness to his pre-eminence, and the poet Rashidi was his contemporary and rival.

Another poet of the age, Abu'l-Faraj-i-Runi, is tied to the city by his name. Runa, from which he took his *nisba*, the part of a name that tells where a person comes from, was a place near Lahore, though accounts differ and some put it in Khurasan.

Madho Lal Hussain is Lahore's own poet, pictured roaming the streets of Lahore drunkenly in the company of fellow carousers. His verse was first printed in the city: a collection in Gurmukhi, the script of Punjabi used by Sikhs, made by an unknown Sindhi editor, was published in Lahore around 1900, and the Punjab University library holds a manuscript of his verse, number 374, of 1804. Najm Hosain Syed's play *Takht L'hore*, the Throne of Lahore, carries the city in its title.

The Mughal court spoke verse in the city too. In the thirty seventh year of his reign, on his court's second journey from Lahore to Kashmir, the emperor Akbar set out in the rains, and as he crossed the Ravi below the fort of Lahore he asked about whom this Persian couplet had been written:

کلاه خسروی و تاج شاہی
بہر گل کی رسد حاشا و کلا

The blinded Shahryar, a prisoner in the fort of Lahore, spoke a quatrain whose last line says that the eye of the sun has gone blind, and Jahangir's palace at Lahore was praised in a *rubaʿi*, a Persian quatrain.

The mosques of the city carry verse in their inscriptions. Over the gate of the mosque of Muhammad Salih Kambo, three niches hold two lines that name its founder:

بانی این مسجد زیبا نگار
بنائے آل محمد صالح است

The outer gateway of Masjid Chinian Wali carries a chronogram couplet, a verse whose letters, read as numbers, give a year. One chronogram, a quatrain, was the work of Farid, a poet of Lahore, and his line is written beneath it. Of four chronograms on one mosque, three call it a second Kaaba, and all four put the date in the mouth of the *hatif*, the unseen voice.

At Roshnai Gate a parapet once fell suddenly on two men and dashed out their brains. The accident was read as recompense, in the words of a Persian couplet that warns against heedlessness of the requital of deeds.

## Lahore in Books

Two older accounts describe Lahore as a seat of poets and of rulers. In the Ghaznavid age it was the birthplace and native place of Persian poets, and the seat on which Jalandar depended. To a Mughal chronicler it was a great city and a mighty metropolis, where craftsmen of every land lived and the goods of the seven climes and the wares of sea and land were sold. He called it the seat of the sultanate and the capital of the realms, and Aram Shah's Lahore a land that delights the heart. His city was a capital of rulers, a store of treasure and arms, and a place that Islam Shah feared for its very greatness.

In the nineteenth century *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* was written on the orders of the commissioner, who asked for a detailed account of the buildings, *mazars*, tombs and mosques of the environs of Lahore. Its author called the task very hard, because the history of hundreds of years had to be gathered. He held that no book had yet been written or printed that covered the antiquities of Lahore in full, and described his own as reporting on the tombs of the Muslim elders, on other buildings and on the old customs of Lahore, while being at root a history of the saints. A modern translator gives its title as "Tehqiqat-i-Chisti: Tarikh-i-Lahore ka Encyclopedia (Researches of Chisti: Encyclopedia of Lahore's History)".

Its author wrote in conversation, and in argument, with earlier writers on the city. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar had long sought an account of the elders of Lahore's environs and could not obtain it. Lala Amin Chand's *Safarnama*, printed by Koh Nur, gave an account of the city of Lahore that the author disputed, and Amin Chand's work on the buildings and people of Lahore was cut short when the same commissioner left for Shahjahanabad. The author was also a witness: he saw with his own eyes what was done in the city with the heads of Raja Hira Singh and Pandit Jalla.

*Tarikh-e Lahore*, the history of 1884, was written by a resident of the city who was also an engineer and a historian of property, and his hand shows in it. He reconstructed vanished features: when a mosque was built its founder must have kept the plinth very high, and there must have been shops beneath its northern wall. Where a building had no vocabulary of its own he supplied one, again and again saying that a thing should be called so: the door of a veranda should be called the outer door of the temple, one part the fifth degree, another a three arched seat. A house may rightly be called a collection of temples, and a great hall should be thought of as a *shivala*.

His judgements of beauty were either backed by European opinion or delivered flat. The Wazir Khan mosque, in his eyes, was famous among Europeans and might rightly be called the pride and glory of Lahore; great painters learned their art from it, and travellers from far away came to Lahore and carried off copies of its niches. The Badshahi Masjid was larger than the Jami Masjid of Delhi, and it was a pity that the dome of the Sufi Wali mosque was never built, for its beauty would have been doubled. One parapet was shaped so that from a distance it looked like domes, and the unrepaired Shiv Gar shivala in truth needed no repair, so solidly was it built. At Sher Singh's modest *samadh* he gave a moral reading: the visitor's tears fall unbidden, and he becomes certain that the world is transient.

He reported a private donor's work with an engineer's interest. Nura Iman Wala repaired the Wazir Khan mosque at a cost of thousands of rupees, and of the eight openings above the dome of each of its four minarets he had four closed and four left open for greater strength. At Nawab Mian Khan's tomb, when a mosque was built on the west a matching one was built on the east, purely for beauty.

He dated many buildings by the years that had passed since their founding, counting back from his own day. The Teja Singh thakurdwara had been built under English rule twenty five years before, on the site of a demolished old *haveli*, a great mansion; Baikunth Das's thakurdwara had been raised by subscription ten years before; the Shivala Ragnath Mishr was forty years old and the Kuka dharamsal fifteen. Bishno Devi's *devi dwara*, a temple of the goddess, was forty years old, Chor Mor Wala had been founded fifty years before in Ranjit Singh's time, Jwala Dai's mandir nine years before, Mehr Das's own mandir not less than two hundred years before, and the Chhajju Bhagat thakurdwara ten or twelve years before. The Wazir Khan mosque was two hundred and fifty one years old, two hundred years had passed since Shah Bilawal's exhumation, and the Pir Sabz tree was nearly two centuries old. Khuda Singh had died eighteen years before, Shiv Gar forty and Daryagar ten.

Where he had no year he dated by reign: the time of the Tughluq sultanate and of Sultan Muhammad Tughluq, the Lodi sultanate, Akbar's time, when the foundation was laid of a mandir that still stood and drew devotees in numbers for its antiquity, the reign of Shah Jahan, the Alamgiri period, the reign of Ahmad Shah, and the Sikh period. In his closing pages the Chughtai kings, the kings of old, the Sikh government and the English government stand side by side without comment, and both 1857 and the fall of the Sikhs are called only a disturbance, *mufsida*. He framed the city's history as a sequence of destructions: thirteen numbered shocks, *sadme*, of plunder and demolition, followed by the present form of the city of Lahore.

He cast Sikh rule as the time of loss and English rule as the time of restitution, in four cases close together: the confiscated shops, the powder magazine, the stripped gateway and the encroached chowk. Elsewhere he reported both regimes in the same register with different verbs: the English demolishing cells for some reason of expediency and cutting windows for their own troops, while also convening *darbars*, public assemblies, subscribing and restoring mosques by name and date; the Sikh regime confiscating, quartering artillery, prising up flooring and transferring endowments. He drew no general conclusion from the contrast. On the city's defences he gave the administrations' own reasons, that so high a wall was thought superfluous, that the work was done to remove a nuisance, and that the time of the English peace had come, without endorsing or contesting them.

Where he vouched for the state's fairness, the words were his own. In his telling the government, as a matter of justice, paid full compensation for every loss; land taken from houses was paid for to the owners' satisfaction, so that no one was displeased; flood damage was paid to the last penny; and for the rebuilt tank the government paid the full price of the houses, cleared the ground and began building. This was a compulsory acquisition inside the walled city, and he recorded no dissent. He was also a party to what he reported. State repairs ran through him as Executive Engineer, the annual repair grant for the Shalamar Bagh among them, and for the Badshahi Masjid, a building in his own division, he asked the administration publicly, in writing, for a further grant, saying that if it turned its attention to the mosque once more, the mosque would be repaired in full.

His economic argument was a counterfactual: had the income of its property not been attached to the Wazir Khan mosque, the very form of its habitation would have been lost. He explained a shrine's prosperity by the man in charge, not by the saint. Dola Wari kept its standing because some holy man or other always lived there, and Bhai Prem Singh, a blessed and courteous man, kept his place well peopled. The Teja Singh thakurdwara's splendour was doubled on its sadhu's account until he left, and a Brahman kept one shivala in great splendour. One mosque was in full splendour while its founder lived and had little splendour now, and at a lecture platform, after the death of the pandit in whose time it had flourished, Pandit Gopi Nath, editor of the *Akhbar-e-Aam*, lectured there, but that splendour did not return.

One passage is his judgement rather than description, a hostile portrait of the community that supported a mosque. Of the Sadhus of Takiya Sadhuwan, Muslim Kashmiris who came under Sikh rule, he wrote that they had all once been tenants and had become owners of property and wealthy; that they plied no visible trade or calling, their business being to bring in money by every stratagem; and that wherever they went they changed their names, one calling himself Sufi Shah, another Shatranji Shah, another Miskin Shah, one becoming a faqir on the path, another a *majzub*, one absorbed in divine love, another a prince. It is an accusation that a Lahore community impersonated faqirs for a living, not a description of it.

He named the texts he relied on, and where they failed him. *Tuhfat al-Wasilin* was his authority twice, and it was silent where he needed it: of one grave, no one knew the real name of the person buried there, and *Tuhfat al-Wasilin* had not recorded it either. Munshi Inayatullah, author of *Bahar-e Danish*, was one man's maternal uncle and father in law. For the marble of Bahadur Khan's tomb he had only the testimony of old people.

Lahore is also a city of manuscripts and collectors. *Haqiqat al-Fuqara*, by Pir Muhammad, is a Persian verse life of Lal Husain written by an eyewitness; the Punjab University Library holds its unpublished original manuscript, under the shelfmark 3253/248 Farsi, and a printing said to date from 1966 was not found in the libraries of Lahore that were searched. Mr Khalil al-Rahman Dawoodi of Lahore supplied modern scholars of Sufism with manuscripts, among them one copied at Taunsa.

Modern scholars have seen Lahore from very different distances. For a student of a modern Sufi order it was home in the field: he spent the academic year of 1995 and 1996 studying Urdu in the city, and in Pakistan was "based in the cultural capital of Lahore", and a photograph of the dead shaykh circulated among friends there. The translator of Madho Lal Hussain signed his introduction "Lahore / August 2015". A scholar of women's voices in Sufi ritual worked from Lahore too: she started from the city on a spring afternoon, measured Kasur as seventy miles from it and placed Jhang Siyal to its southwest, and noted that landlords' sons now live in large urban centres like Karachi, Lahore or Islamabad. Khalida and Jaleel Naqvi, custodians of the shrine of Hazrat Pir Sabir at Paran Kalyar, took part in her fieldwork in Lahore and Kasur and were interviewed in Lahore.

The city's saints have their own local literature, among it M. Latif Malik's *Aulīyā-e Lahore*, Samina Qureshi's *Lahore: The City Within*, a Lahore reprint of *Tarikh-e Lahore*, and Tahir Lahori's essay *Aulīyā ke Urs*, published in *Sohna Shehr Lahore*.

Other studies name the city only in passing. Lahore marks the far edge of the area from which volunteers come to serve at the *ʿurs* at Ghamkol Sharif; it is the city to which one woman, Rabi‘a, returns, and its neighbourhood of Samnabad is named. A study of the goddess Hinglaj names Lahore as a Punjabi city in its survey of Pakistani Hinduism, and as the home of an informant of the Nath sect. The twentieth century Chishti Zauqi Shah worked as a journalist in Peshawar, Lahore, Hyderabad in Sind and Karachi.

Lahore is a city of print. A Sufi Foundation founded by a senior *murid* published translations and original writings by Wahid Bakhsh during the 1980s; Bazm-i Ittehad al-Muslimin was founded in 1984 by the shaykh's nephew; and from 1996 Talifat-i Shaheedi, set up by another *murid* of Shahidullah Faridi, published four more texts, three of them in English. Commercial houses carry the rest, among them Ziya al-Qur'an, Qausin Publishers and al-Faisal Publishers, and some fifty different texts of the order are produced and distributed from a number of publishing houses, reaching readers through the Urdu bookstores of Karachi and Lahore. The city has its press too, among it the English language weekly *The Friday Times*, and from Lahore Pakpattan is measured as 120 miles to the southwest.

Older scholarship knows Lahore as a place of publication and nothing more. Lahore houses printed mystical poetry, among them Ashraf, the University of the Punjab, Urdu Dunya and Allah wale ki qaumi dukan, whose numbered chapbook series carried Panjabi mystical verse: a Panjabi version of the *Burda*, *Qasida-i Burda Panjabi*, a commentary on Bullhe Shah, and a collection of Panjabi mystical verse with no author named. Two Panjabi verse translations were published at Lahore, by Pir Imam Shah and by Maulana Muhammad Shahuddin, the second in 1939. Lahore publishers also issued the literature on *qawwali*, among them Sheikh Muhammad Ashraf and the Institute of Islamic Culture.

For a writer based in the city, Lahore is home and field: "Based as I am in Lahore, much of my fieldwork took place in the city itself and its vicinity. This is bound to have had its limitations." On a Sunday, Mall Road, "one of the busiest boulevards in the city", is deserted, and a drive along it is "a journey through the colonial era", past the Lahore High Court, combining European and Eastern architectural techniques. The National College of Arts was established in Lahore in 1857 and is considered the most prestigious art college in the country.

He watches the city change. The Anarkali market, named after the legendary lover of the Mughal emperor Jahangir, has over the years become the largest tyre market in the city, and the Pak Tea House a few shops away, once famed for the gatherings of the city's leading intelligentsia, is on its way to becoming yet another tyre shop. For one resident, Rettigan Road was a prominent locality of colonial Lahore, now taken over by small businesses and shops and no longer the clean, spacious area it once was. Behind the Roshnai Gate the musicians remain, and it is still one of the largest markets for traditional instruments in the city. The Ravi, which once flowed next to the fort, has changed its course and moved a few kilometres away, and today the mighty Ravi of history flows like a stream in Lahore.

The metropolis swallows its villages. The Defence Housing Authority, the neatest and most expensive residential area of Lahore, sprawls across a vast tract of land, eating into the small historic villages of the area, whose people, having sold their abundant land for very little to the housing authority, are left congested and poor behind a wall. Hanjarwal is a village subsumed by the ever growing metropolis of Lahore. Iqbal Qaiser was working on an encyclopedia of Lahore, the first of its kind, in five parts of about a thousand pages each, drawn from land revenue records and government archives, until administrative changes at the Punjab Cultural Institute put an untimely end to the project.

Lahore is also a crossroads and a city of crafts. The Grand Trunk Road connected with Lahore and then Delhi, the caravanserai route ran through Lahore, Satgaraha, Deepalpur, Harrapa, Multan and Uch Sharif, and the Bedian road is the old route to Amritsar. The Kashmiri binders who used to live in Lahore were known for their meticulous binding, and Bhai Ram Singh was Principal of the Mayo School of Arts in Lahore. Rohtas was not the kind of fort found in Lahore, where only royalty stayed.

In a lecture on Sikh architecture in Lahore, Iqbal Qaiser argued that "like the Sikh religion, which picks up from both Hinduism and Islam, Sikh architecture draws inspiration from both Hindu and Muslim architecture", and that domes once part of Muslim buildings, made smaller by the Sikhs, are now used in Muslim buildings in Pakistan too. New apartments have been built to meet the growing demands of urban Lahore, and indigenous trees are being replaced by imported ones in its developed areas.
