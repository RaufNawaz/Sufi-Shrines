## Overview

Sindh is the country of the lower Indus valley, the southern part of present day Pakistan. By the end of the seventeenth century it was the *wilayat*, or realm, of Sindh, known as Thatta, and a Mughal *suba*, or province, the *suba* of Thatta, made up of four *sarkars*, or divisions: Thatta, Siwistan, Nasirpur and Umarkot. Under Akbar the country had been made part of the *suba* of Multan, with *sarkars* at Bhakkar in the north and at Thatta in the Indus delta, and Upper and Lower Sindh were never definitely united until a much later day; even in 1843 their union was not complete. The name has never had fixed edges: in the Middle Ages the Siraiki speaking area of southern Punjab was part of Sindh, and in Baluch poetry the word means not the modern political division but the valley of the Indus.

Under the British, Sindh was governed from Bombay rather than Calcutta, and Sindhi nationalism grew out of the struggle for independence from the Bombay Presidency in the 1920s and 1930s. Today Sindh is the province of Pakistan with the most significant Hindu population.

Its earliest capitals were Brahmanabad, then Adpur, then Dewal, and by the end of the seventeenth century Thatta and Debal were the seat of government. The Arabs reached Sindh in 711 and laid the foundation of a Muslim rule; the country was under Arab governors for nearly three hundred years, though the Arabs left no permanent mark upon the land. Local dynasties followed, the Sumra and then the Samma, and from 1520 the Turkish Arghun and then the Tarkhan, under whom Sindh fell to the Mughal Empire. Akbar annexed it in 1592.

In the eighteenth century the Kalhora rose from governors to partial independence, paid tribute to Persia after Nadir Shah's invasion and passed under the overlordship of the Pathan kingdom at Kabul. The Talpurs who overthrew them ruled the country until the British conquered it in 1843. Beneath these changes ran a deep continuity: the Sindh that Sir Charles Napier conquered was not very different in essentials from the Sindh of Akbar, Jahangir, Shah Jahan and Aurangzeb. When Persian was given up as the official language in the 1850s, Sindhi and English became the two languages of administration, and at Partition the departure of the Hindu elite and the arrival of Muslim refugees from India changed the make up of the province's people.

After the Arab conquest Sindh never ceased to be a centre of Islamic studies and a seat of Islamic mysticism. Hallaj travelled throughout it in 905, and from the thirteenth century, when Lal Shahbaz Qalandar settled in Sehwan and Baha'uddin Zakariya made Multan a centre of the Suhrawardi order, Sindh produced an immense number of mystics, whose shrines are scattered all through the Indus valley. One observer found the country priest ridden and said to possess a hundred thousand tombs of saints, and its social religion was built round the reverence of the disciple for the master.

The orders dominant among the Hindu Sufis of Sindh were the Qadiriyya, the Qalandariyya and the Suhrawardiyya, while its *faqirs*, the wandering holy men, were divided into two great orders, the Jelali and the Jemali dervishes. The river had a saint of its own, Khwajo Khizr, whose cult was linked with the worship of the Indus and supported by Muslim and Hindu alike. Wandering yogis crossed Sindh on pilgrimage to the goddess at Hinglaj in Baluchistan, and the south of Pakistan, Sindh above all, is crossed by a network of pilgrimage routes visited by different religious communities. The ties of the province's Hindus to Sufism are of three kinds: a tomb visited by Muslims and Hindus alike, such as Bhit Shah; a saint with a double identity, such as Udero Lal; and a Sufi *darbar*, a saint's shrine and court, managed by Hindus, such as that of Pithoro Pir.

In the eighteenth century Sindh was a Muslim state whose people were for the most part converted Jats, the majority Sunni but with very influential Shia sections. The Baluchis had lived in the country for many generations before the Kalhora called them in, and their political importance dates from that moment. The Hindus advanced money to the *zamindars*, the landholders, financed trade, farmed the revenue and owned all the petty shops in the villages. The Lohanas made up half of the whole Hindu population and were divided into Amils and Bhaibands, and in the nineteenth century the Nanakpanth, the path of those who follow Guru Nanak, was probably the dominant religious path among Hindu Sindhis.

Before the census, people had identified themselves by their locality, caste and ethnicity rather than by the great religions. The Sidis, first brought as slaves from Africa, now speak Sindhi as their native language. Sindh is home to the majority of Pakistan's Hindus, over 90 per cent of whom belong to underprivileged tribes or subcastes such as the Menghwar, the Bhil and the Kohli, and Nanakpanthi Sikhs live in the province in very large numbers.

In Sindh the Sufis made the spoken language into a literary one, though Persian was the court language of the ruling houses until the British conquest. Siraiki, the dialect of northern Sindh spoken especially among its Baluchi population, gave many of its Sufi poets a second language. Folk tales were turned to mystical purposes more widely here than elsewhere, from the verse of Qadi Qadan in the first half of the sixteenth century to the *Risalo*, the collected poems, of Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit in the eighteenth. By 1940 the *Risalo* could be called the only classic the language of Sindh had yet produced; it is universally acknowledged as the greatest classic of Sindhi literature, and it is understood and loved in Sindh by Hindus as much as by Muslims.

Its printing in 1866 was the main event in the meeting of print and Sufism in the province, and in the late nineteenth century Hindu Sindhis played a leading part in publishing Sufi books. Most of the musicians of Sindh have been Hindus. There can be no ritual there without the Manganhar, the community musicians, and *sufiana kalam*, Sufi poetry sung in the vernacular, is a feature of the shrines of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar and of Shah Abdul Latif at Bhit. Most of the Naqshbandis in Sindh, by contrast, are strongly opposed to music.

Holy places and power were long bound together. Since the time of the Sumra the government had a strong theocratic base, with much real power in the hands of the Sayyids, the descendants of the Prophet, and under the Kalhora the reverence for *pirs*, Sufi masters, Sayyids and holy men became the leading feature of eighteenth century Sindh. Under the native governments, as one writer saw it, Sufism was a formidable political engine. British policy in the Punjab and Sindh treated the hereditary saints much as it treated landlords and tribal leaders, and the *hurr*, the obedient disciples of the Pir Pagaro, joined the struggle against the British and played a minor part even in the war of 1971.

The state's Sindhi publications hardly mentioned Sehwan until the late 1960s, while regularly featuring other saints of Sindh, and in the decade before 2018 many temples were renovated in step with changes in the political landscape. Sindhi nationalists joined the province to Sufism, and G. M. Syed found Sufism in its purest form there; yet the notion that the history of Sindh holds no religious tension between Hindus and Muslims is a utopian ideal of the nationalists. Despite the spread of Islamist terrorism in Sindh, the Sufi culture still pervades Sindhi society.

## History: From the Earliest Capitals to the Mughal Conquest

Sindh has been named and bounded in many ways, and never as a modern map would draw it. Arab historians spoke of Hind and Sindh separately: Sindh was bounded by Multan, Jaisalmer and Gujarat, and Hind, or Hindustan, began where Sindh ended. The Chach Nameh is counted one of the earliest histories of Sindh. The two names run together in poetry too: in the romance of Hir and Ranjha told by Waris Shah, Ranjha blesses a Raja that he may rule over horses, camels, elephants, Hindustan and Sindh.

For the years of Shah Abdul Latif's life, which are given as 1689 to 1752, the history of Sindh is above all a history of Mughal power in decline, for the growing weakness of Delhi's overlordship produced local events which shaped the destiny of the province in a manner of its own. That history is hard to write. Reliable records of the years 1690 to 1760 are almost wholly absent, and the East India Company's records for the poet's lifetime are totally devoid of any reference to Sindh. From the year 1000 onwards Sindh receives hardly any mention in Indian history, and a historian of the Mughal economy found it impossible to include Sindh in his reconstruction.

The land itself shaped this history. Sindh is difficult country for the historian, poor in informative historical monuments: an alluvial tract over which the Indus has wandered through the centuries in a multitude of shifting channels, whose buildings were made mostly of mud, sticks or wattles, as they still were outside all the large towns in 1940. For six months of the year it is as hot as anywhere in India and cursed with dust storms, a shifting plain liable to flood, surrounded on every side but the sea by forbidding desert or barren hills. For the most part Sindh stood apart from the crucial events of neighbouring Asia; its history is episodic and isolated, and it kept to a late date the theocratic foundation of Islamic society. Between 1659 and 1758 no event of prime importance graced its annals, which were monotonous and turned in on themselves, cut off by deserts and barren land on all sides and by a climate that allowed campaigning only a few months a year.

By the end of the seventeenth century the country was called the *wilayat* of Sindh, the realm of Sindh and the lands of Thatta, and it was the Mughal *suba* of Thatta. Its history began with ancient capitals. Brahmanabad was a great city and the seat of the throne, and its fort had fourteen hundred towers, each a rope's length from the next. After it Adpur became the capital, and many traces of its towers and walls remain; after that came Dewal, and in the late seventeenth century Thatta and Debal were the seat of government.

The Arabs came first as traders and then as conquerors. Arab trade had begun in Gujarat and Sindh, and a commander was sent to attack Sindh. In the time of Uthman the Arabs attacked the lands along the river of Sindh, and in the time of Ali they conquered part of the country, which was given up after his death; under Walid they conquered Sindh and reached the Ganges. In 711 the Arabs reached Sindh and laid the foundation of a Muslim rule that still continues in Pakistan, conquering Sindh and the provinces northward up to Multan. Muslim navies sailed to the coasts of western India and occupied the densely populated Hindu and Buddhist society of Sindh, beginning the long encounter between Islamic and Indic civilisations. In one view the conquest, by opening a direct and regular contact between India and the Fertile Crescent, threw western Asia, Africa and Europe open to the agricultural treasures of India and drew them for the first time into a single agricultural universe.

The story that the conquest began as a punitive expedition against the ruling Raja Dahir is a myth. It was begun by Hajjaj, Walid's governor in Iraq, as one part of his policy of expansion, which also took in Transoxiana. Muhammad bin Qasim al-Saqafi came by the land route from Iran, began his advance through Makran and Baluchistan in 710, and conquered Sindh between 711 and 712. Sindh was under Arab governors for nearly three hundred years, and under Arab influence for longer still, while the early dynasties of Multan and Mansura held Upper and Lower Sindh until nearly the end of the tenth century. Yet the Arabs left no permanent mark upon the land; they were merely a military garrison living among strangers.

The early conquerors settled their families in large numbers in the towns of Sindh. Brahmans in high office converted to keep their positions, and Buddhists, hostile to Brahman domination, converted as well. Hindu and Buddhist scholars from Sindh were sent to the Abbasid court, and in the eighth century a scholar of *hadith*, the reported sayings of the Prophet, and a poet from Sindh were leading literary figures. The pious of these western provinces seem in the early centuries to have been interested mainly in collecting *hadith* and in passing scientific knowledge from India to the central Muslim lands. When Arabs replaced Brahmans as rulers in the eighth century, they continued the earlier practice of requiring the Jats to associate themselves with dogs, so affirming their lowly status.

The internal administration of the country was left largely in the hands of Hindu landlords paying the land tax, *kharaj*, and the poll tax, *jizya*. Arab merchants carried the trade between Sindh and the neighbouring countries of India, bringing the produce of China and Ceylon to the seaports of Sindh and carrying it on by way of Multan to Turkistan and Khurasan.

The tenth century was the age of the Isma'ilis. Towards its end Sindh was touched by a wave of Qarmatian heresy from Egypt, and the Qarmatians ruled not only Bahrain but also northern Sindh and Multan. Little is known of Sufis in Sindh in these centuries, for two reasons: the land routes between Khurasan, Transoxiana and Sindh were slow and arduous, and the rise of Sufism came in the time of the Isma'ili Fatimid Caliphate, so that Mansura, the capital of Sindh, accepted the overlordship of the Egyptian Caliphs, and Sufism there was cut off from other lands; accounts differ on which dynasty held Mansura at the time. When Sindh ceased to receive Arab governors from the Umayyad empire, independent dynasties arose, among which the Sumra, a local dynasty of Rajput origin, soon became prominent. Southern Sindh was never thoroughly conquered by the house of Ghazni.

The house of Ghazni reached Sindh at the edge of its campaigns. Sultan Mahmud led a great army towards Multan and Sindh and had as many as four thousand boats built, each fitted with three iron spikes, one at the prow and one on either side; his enemies sent their families away to the islands and fought, and most of them were killed or drowned. On another of Mahmud's campaigns a raja abandoned his army out of faintheartedness and poor judgement and made for Sindh, and the Sultan's troops pursued and caught him. Mahmud set out to punish a group who had behaved insolently towards his soldiers on the bank of the river Sindh as he returned from Somnath.

The Sumra, a Sindhi tribe that was at one time ruler of Sindh, held the country for centuries. A Sumra Rajput line of thirty six princes is said to have reigned for five hundred years, and government stayed in the Sumra family until it passed to the Samma. The Sumra ruled Lower Sindh until the middle of the fourteenth century, while after the end of the twelfth century Upper Sindh came under Sunni rule. A Sumra king, Chanesar, reigned in Lower Sindh for about eighteen years around 1228. Upper and Lower Sindh have had very different histories, and how far Sumra authority reached over each is confused.

Arab rule in Sindh having weakened and died, it was left to Persianised Turks to establish a permanent Muslim presence in India from the thirteenth century, and that century tied the province to the sultans of Delhi and to the great Suhrawardi centre at Multan. On his fourth campaign Shihab al-Din went against Dewal, which is known as Thatta, brought the whole country along the river Sindh under his control, and returned with the rarities of that land and great wealth. Sultan Shihab al-Din Ghori gave Taj al-Din Yalduz a wide realm that took in Kech, Makran and Sindh, and Yalduz was called king of Kech, Makran, Sindh and Multan.

In the reign of Iltutmish the fugitive Jalal al-Din fled towards Sindh and Siwistan, and seven years after November 1221 Iltutmish routed his rival Nasiru'd-Din Qubacha from Sindh. Shaikh Baha'uddin Zakariya openly sided with Iltutmish when he wished to add Multan and Sindh to the Delhi Sultanate, and after Iltutmish annexed Multan and Sindh in 1228 the Sultan and the Shaikh grew more intimate. Under Balban the realm of Sindh, with all its dependencies, was fixed as the *jagir*, a grant of land revenue, of Prince Muhammad Sultan, who was posted to Multan.

The fourteenth century, the century of Pithoro Pir, was turbulent, a time of passage from the Sumra to the Samma. The Samma officially took power in 1351, but the Sumra kept control of certain outlying areas of Sindh, among them the area of Umarkot. In 1351 Muhammad Shah Tughluq, sultan of Delhi, arrived in Sindh in pursuit of a rebel; in 1367 Firuz Shah Tughluq came back to lay siege to Thatta, and Jam Juna II, who then controlled Sindh, sought alliances with the sultan of Gujarat. In the same century the governors of Sindh sought the help of Makhdum Jahaniyan.

The Samma rulers bore the title Jam, given to their headman and chief, and the first of them was Jam Anzar. Sultan Firuz Shah of Delhi marched three times against the Jam of the day, at last took the *wilayat* of Sindh and brought the Jam to Delhi, and then, pleased with his service, gave the country back to him. The line of the Jams ran on, through many rulers, to Jam Nanda and Jam Firuz, the last of them. The Kalhora, centuries later, were the first Sindhi dynasty to wield lasting power since the fall of the Samma.

Sindh became a Muslim land late. Its people in the eighth century were Hindu races speaking a Sanskritic language, and they were converted not by the Arabs but by the Central Asian peoples who took up Muslim penetration of India from the twelfth century onwards, through the reigns of the Sumra and the Samma. Most of the population was converted in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries; under Jam Nizamuddin, about 1461, Islam spread widely over Sindh and the mass of the common people became Muslim. By the time of the Lodi Afghans in the sixteenth century the farming population and the landholding class were almost entirely Muslim, and the Hindus were confined to the business of writing and to trade and commerce, as they remained down to the British annexation. So Sindh became Muslim when Central Asian and Persian influences were at full strength, and the late conversion of most of its people is one of the province's peculiar circumstances.

Even so, before the eighteenth century the people who called themselves Muslims were few, probably because the name was kept for the Sayyids and their dependents. The Muslim conquests of northern India extended the Persianate cultural world to Sindh, where Persian remained the chief literary language of the Muslim elite down to the Mughal period and beyond, and the Mughal capital of Thatta and the other towns of Sindh were homes of Persian poetry written by the educated elite.

In the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries Sindh was ruled first from within and then from outside. The quarrels of the Jams reached beyond Sindh: Jam Bayazid and Jam Ibrahim, offended with Jam Nanda, ruler of Thatta, went over to Sultan Husain Langah and were given a *jagir*, and Jam Bayazid later helped the Sultan kill his vizier Imad al-Mulk. When Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus settled at Uch, a coming dated to the year 887 of the Hijra, the Islamic era, Sultan Husain Mirza, the ruler of Sindh, and Sultan Sikandar Lodi, king of Hind, became his disciples.

The Samma line ended in a quarrel within the family. Jam Salahuddin, a kinsman of Jam Firuz whose sister was married to Sultan Muzaffar of Gujarat, twice raised armies from Gujarat and won; Jam Firuz called in Mirza Shah Beg Arghun from Qandahar, Salahuddin was killed, and Jam Firuz ruled Sindh alone. Then Mirza Shah Beg Arghun led an army from Qandahar and took Thatta, and Jam Firuz went to Gujarat, entered the service of Sultan Bahadur Shah, gave him his daughter in marriage and died there. With that the rule of the Samma was cut off.

Turkish influence prevailed in Sindh from 1520, when the Turkish dynasty of the Arghun, coming down from Afghanistan, overcame the last native Sindhi rulers; the Arghun were followed by the Turkish family of the Tarkhan, under whom Sindh fell to the Mughal Empire. Mirza Shah Beg took Sindh by strength and courage and died after a rule of three years, and Mirza Shah Husain fortified Bhakkar anew, took Multan and ruled for thirty two years. When Babur came, his *farman*, a royal order, went to Mirza Shah Husain Arghun, ruler of Thatta, who came up from Thatta to fight for Multan.

Then Mirza Isa Tarkhan, one of Mirza Shah Beg's amirs, at Thatta, and Sultan Mahmud at Bhakkar each claimed independence, sometimes at peace with each other and sometimes at war, and Isa Tarkhan died after thirteen years. Mirza Muhammad Baqi overcame his brother Khan Baba and kept up his father's dealings with Sultan Mahmud of Bhakkar. With the Tarkhans and the Arghuns, two Central Asian dynasties, the Naqshbandi order came to Sindh from the sixteenth century onwards.

Sindh's independent rule ended when Akbar added it to the Mughal Empire in 1592, though accounts of the year differ. The imperial order was first to take Thatta and after that to turn to the conquest of Qandahar. After ruling for eight years, Mirza Jani Beg, son of Painda Muhammad son of Muhammad Baqi, came to court with the Khankhanan, Mirza Abd al-Rahim, whose deeds included the conquest of Gujarat, Thatta and the Deccan, and the country entered the protected dominions of the empire. Jani Beg was given the rank of three thousand and Thatta as his *jagir*, and Lahari Bandar, the port on the Thatta side, was made crown land.

The Khankhanan, Abd al-Rahim, one of Akbar's chief nobles, was travelling with an army towards Sindh when he stopped in Lahore to receive the blessing of Shah Hussein; the Mughal armies then conquered Thatta, and Hussein's reputation spread. Under the empire representatives of the Delhi court came to Sindh, but the native rulers enjoyed much freedom.

## History: Mughal, Kalhora, Talpur and British Rule, to the Present

The Mughal unit was not Sindh alone. Under Akbar the country as a whole was made part of the *suba* of Multan, with two *sarkars*, Bhakkar for Upper Sindh and Thatta for Lower Sindh. At the time of Nadir Shah's invasion of Hindustan and sack of Delhi, Upper Sindh still formed part of the *suba* of Multan, and its Mughal portion, Sukkur, Bhakkar and Shikarpur, was then annexed to the Durrani kingdom. From the time of Shah Jahan until the first decade of the eighteenth century the emperor's authority was wielded by officials sent from other parts of the empire; with the Kalhora, the emperor's representative was a native of Sindh. While the Mughal government flourished, Sindh was a province of the empire, an honorary appointment given each year to some person of rank whose expenses generally exceeded the revenues.

The governorship of Thatta was an office given to the emperor's men. On Shah Jahan's march from the Deccan to his accession, during a halt of seven days at Ahmadabad, a Mirza Isa Tarkhan received the rank of four thousand *zat*, the personal rank of an imperial officer, and two thousand horse, with the governorship of the *suba* of Thatta. In Sarmad's day travellers from Iran came to Hindustan by way of Sindh, and Sarmad came by the same road and reached Thatta.

The Mughal century ran its course in Sindh through rebellion and disorder. In 1605 the Daudpota rose in Shikarpur and Upper Sindh; from 1648 to 1651 Aurangzeb was governor of Multan and Sindh; in 1658 the civil war between Aurangzeb and Dara Shikoh ended in Dara Shikoh's defeat and his death after a pursuit through Sindh; and the Kalhora began to oppose the Mughal troops. For a hundred and fifty years after Akbar's annexation, Sindh showed the characteristic failings of Mughal government as clearly as any part of the empire, and its history under the Mughals is full of conflicts between local and imperial demands for revenue.

In the early seventeenth century part of Sindh was in disorder, and Mughal control was weak beyond easy reach of the larger towns. The Sameja rebelled against the Mughal governor in 1640, disturbing the peace of central Sindh. In 1644 the people were so exceedingly oppressed and kept so poor that, though the soil was fertile and could produce large quantities of good indigo, they had neither the will nor the means to manure and sow the ground. Trade was harder to finance in Upper Sindh than in Lower Sindh, and in 1647 a conflict of authority brought much unsettlement to Upper Sindh.

The disorder continued through the century. In 1652 the bad government of Upper Sindh and the corruption of its petty officials drew caustic comment, and in 1656 the Sameja were pillaging in Middle Sindh, while the Baluchi population lay outside the effective control of the Mughal officers. In 1659 and the year after, Sindh was ravaged by famine and plague; as one observer wrote, never did famine rage worse anywhere, the living being hardly able to bury the dead. By 1699 travelling in Sindh was still not to be lightly undertaken, the countryside infested by bands of robbers, and the later Mughal empire in Sindh was no better than the early one at keeping law and order. At the end of the seventeenth century there was lawlessness in the countryside, the absolute autocracy of the governor and a thriving river trade, yet near the centre of government the administration was, within its limits, not lacking in practical efficiency.

The eighteenth century loosened the empire's hold and brought the Kalhora to power. They were a Sindhi tribe of obscure origin, of the type now classed as Jamot, original inhabitants of the Indus valley who are not Baluch. The struggle for power in the lower Indus valley first showed itself plainly in 1717, in the conflict around the Sufi Shah Inayat. The Kalhora clan from Upper Sindh, particularly active in the struggle against him, soon afterwards became independent in Sindh, and the army sent against Jhok came from various places in Sindh and Hind.

In religion, a strong Naqshbandi influence at the beginning of the eighteenth century changed the spiritual climate of southern Sindh to a certain extent. Makhdum Muhammad Hashim of Thatta and his relatives attacked the Sufis who defended the emotional side of religion, and their Naqshbandi group helped to promote a more sober mystical piety.

The century passed through clear stages: Kalhora governors from 1701; partial independence by 1736; Persian overlordship and tribute after the invasion of Nadir Shah in 1739, though accounts of the year differ; about 1747 the passing of overlordship to the Pathan kingdom at Kabul; and from 1778 civil war between the Kalhora and the Talpurs. Sindh remained an integral part of the empire until the disastrous coming of Nadir Shah, when it fell first under Persian and then, in 1747, under Afghan dominance. The Mughal governors sent from Hindustan gave way to Sindhi governors who bowed the knee as little as they dared to Mughal, Persian and Afghan in turn, and by 1760 the ruling Kalhora dynasty was independent almost in fact but not at all in theory. The Talpurs after them were never in fact completely independent of their Afghan overlords until the first decade of the nineteenth century.

After Aurangzeb's death the native Kalhora dynasty took over the rule of Upper and later of Lower Sindh, only to become the vassals of the invading Nadir Shah, who conquered Sindh and the Punjab before he reached Delhi in 1739. The influence on Sindh of Ahmad Shah Abdali, also called Durrani, was not as important as it was in the northern areas of present day Pakistan, yet by 8 April 1758 the Pathans had subdued the whole principality of Sindh and made it tributary to them, as it had been to the Persians since Nadir Shah's conquest. In Shah Abdul Latif's own lifetime Nadir Shah made Sindh tributary to Persia and Ahmad Shah Abdali made it subject to Kabul. The tribute fell from reign to reign under the Afghans and was always in arrears, and the government of Sindh regarded the Afghan government at Kabul as a form of unwarranted alien oppression.

Within the Kalhora house power passed through many hands. Yar Muhammad Kalhoro obtained a grant from Aurangzeb, defeated the Daudpota in Upper Sindh and extended his power there. Nur Muhammad Kalhoro became Subedar, the governor, of Sindh; when Ahmad Shah Abdali invaded Sindh, Nur Muhammad fled and died, and commotion and civil war ended in the ascendancy of Ghulam Shah Kalhoro. Then came misgovernment, the deposition of Sarfaraz Khan Kalhoro for misgovernment, the rise of Ghulam Nabi Khan Kalhoro as ruler of Sindh, the civil war between the Kalhora and the Talpurs from 1778, and at last the establishment of Talpur ascendancy.

From 1756 to 1758 Sindh was in a pitiable condition of unrest, though its troubles from 1754 to 1758 were typical of what was happening elsewhere, and in 1758 Sindh no less than the rest of India was in confusion. In 1760, as one observer wrote, it would have profited the East India Company if Sindh were under as good and quiet a government, and as plentiful in grain, as it had been only a few years before. In 1775 Sindh was described as a small tract of country, naturally fertile, rich and populous when it fell under the government of its ruling family about thirty five years before, but declining ever since, and its chief political ties were with the Pathans and the Sikhs.

The Kalhora fell to the Talpurs. It was the misfortune of the mass of the people that a dynasty of their own proved so poor a substitute for the government of the Mughals, while the Talpurs did succeed in giving a distracted land peace for forty years. The Kalhora were overthrown by the former disciples of their saintly ancestor, Adam Shah Kalhoro, and the Talpurs, who came to power in 1781, ruled the country until the British conquered it in 1843. In 1781 too most of the scholar Makhdum Abdur Rahim Girhori's books were destroyed in the Afghan raids in Upper Sindh.

The Baluchis, settled in Sindh in considerable numbers, helped the Talpurs destroy the failing power of the Sindhi ruling family. The earlier Kalhora had encouraged them to settle, they had provided the bulk of the Kalhora troops since the eighteenth century, and by then they were the strongest military force in the land; under the Talpurs they stood at the top of local society and held *jagirs*. Under the Kalhora the government became a sort of partnership between the ruling house and the chief Baluchi tribes. The Talpurs welded Sindh into a more complete unity than it had ever known, but only by asserting a Baluch supremacy that bore hard on the rest of the population, especially the Hindus. Under the Mughals and the Kalhora, Sindh was never at peace; under the Talpurs it was, and the price of that peace was the tyranny of a Baluch minority.

The power of the *pir* was reinforced in the eighteenth century when the Kalhora developed the irrigation system. Before 1843 most Muslims were Sunnis, but the sovereigns, the Talpurs, were themselves Shia. They were on friendly terms with Fath Ali Shah of Persia, and in one view the first portrait of Ali was drawn in Sindh and sent to him as a gift. Under Talpur rule the Amils, a class of scribes, almost monopolised the post of *kardar*, the local revenue officer, and when the British conquered Sindh they had already formed a separate social class.

The settled rule of the Kalhora brought new business openings for merchants, one reason why the Lohanas were the most important trading community of Sindh, Kutch and Saurashtra in the nineteenth century. Sindh's place between trading routes connecting Afghanistan, Khurasan, Arabia, Persia, Turkey and other lands made the province appealing to merchants from the entire continent.

Between them the Kalhora and the Talpurs made Sindh a petty Muslim state which kept, until the British conquest in 1843, the characteristic features of Mughal administration, adding to them a peculiar Islamic form of rule close to theocracy and a policy of splendid isolation. From 1701 onwards the aim of all Sindh policy was to make the country independent of Mughal, Persian and Pathan, to reduce the tribute and to keep the land closed to foreigners of every sort, so that in 1843 Sindh was to all intents and purposes a sovereign petty Muslim state. The two houses kept Sindh shut to the influences of the outside world for a hundred and fifty years.

Beneath these changes ran a deep continuity. The peoples of Sindh in the first half of the eighteenth century lived much as Sir Charles Napier found them in 1843, and the main structure of government remained largely unaltered from the days of Shah Jahan to the days of Napier. The economic foundation changed little, even though in the second half of the eighteenth century the four large towns of modern Sindh, Karachi, Hyderabad, Sukkur and Shikarpur, rose and displaced Thatta, Nasarpur, Rohri, Sehwan and Kandiaro. The Sindh of Napier was not very different in essentials from the Sindh of Ghulam Shah Kalhoro, which in turn did not differ from the Sindh of Akbar, Jahangir, Shah Jahan and Aurangzeb; the framework of Mughal administration in Sindh remained little altered in essentials from 1600 to 1840, though the change in spirit was considerable.

The British entered on Sindh as if it were almost an unknown land. Only with their conquest of 1843 did the literary culture of the Sufi tradition come to be overlaid by the new patterns of modernity, and Sindh was governed from Bombay rather than Calcutta. At the turn of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the region was dominated by British colonisation, and its shared culture rested on the interaction between new local elites and colonial administrators from as early as the middle of the nineteenth century. The first European movement to reach Sindh was Freemasonry: Sir Charles Napier, the conqueror and first governor of Sindh, built a Masonic lodge in Karachi in 1843.

The new government changed the languages of the province. When Persian was given up as the official language in the 1850s, Sindhi and English became the two languages of administration, and the Amils led the great campaign of translation launched by Sir Bartle Frere, the commissioner in Sindh. Sindhi continued to flourish after the British occupation, and with the introduction of a new Arabic alphabet in 1852 a considerable literature grew up, mystical poetry and prose among it. The province's main social structure remained an almost feudal system, with a class of civil servants and the groups involved in trade, but the policy of the British commissioners, Sir Bartle Frere above all, gave birth to the Sindhi intelligentsia.

After the uprising of 1857 the Sufi Imdadullah fled through the Punjab and Sindh, visiting Sufi shrines, and sailed from Karachi into exile in Mecca, and many Kayasths went to work in the princely states, among them the Khairpur emirate in Sindh. With the growth of new trading centres in Sindh, Karachi above all, the Lohanas invested in agricultural land, mainly cotton, and by the end of the nineteenth century had become powerful landowners, known locally as *zamindars*.

Figures for Sindh from the early 1870s count 1,718,688 Muslims, 393,092 Hindus, 5,034 Christians and 86,663 persons of other castes such as Sikhs, Parsis and Jews. Sindh was a largely Muslim country, Muslims making up about 75 per cent of the whole population, where power had been in Muslim hands for centuries. In 1941 the Hindus made up about 21 per cent of the population of Sindh, including 6 per cent of the scheduled castes, so that they would have numbered under a million, about 0.9 million.

The twentieth century brought a politics of the province's own and a breach in its communal peace. Sindhi nationalism grew from the struggle for independence from the Bombay Presidency in the 1920s and 1930s, and a book published in 1947, *Save Sind, Save the Continent* by Ibrahim Joyo, was important to its later growth. From the 1920s onwards riots between Hindus and Muslims increased, a growing tension that culminated with Partition. It is a common claim that Sindh did not suffer the violence the Punjab knew, but reports from the Punjab were reaching the province, and there was a precedent in the Sukkur riots, which spread beyond the city to Rohri and to other parts of Sindh such as Lakki. Even if the violence never reached the level of the Punjab's, there was communal violence in Sindh.

In the telling of Rochaldas, the violence spread through Sindh not because of the Muslim Sindhis but because of the Muhajirs, the Muslim refugees from India, who wanted the houses of the Hindus, at least in Karachi; some Hindus had already sent their wives and children to India while they themselves stayed on in Sindh.

Independence in 1947 brought the mass emigration of the Hindu population from Pakistan to India. By early November 1947, 246,000 Hindus had already left Sindh by sea, air or rail; by 1 January 1948 nearly 500,000 people were estimated to have sought refuge in India, and Hindus from interior Sindh travelled in special trains chartered by the Sindh government. In December, with an added train, 140,000 Hindus crossed the border, and on 14 January the government of India appointed a director general of evacuation to help those who were not Muslims and wished to leave Sindh for India. The Hindu Sindhis left above all after the Karachi riots of January 1948. Estimates of their number differ: the general consensus is about a million, though it may have been closer to 0.8 million, if not fewer.

Partition brought major changes to the province of Sindh. The departure of the Hindu elite and the arrival of Muslim refugees from India changed its people, and the Sindhi community came to define itself by a sense of being shut out and subjugated by Muhajir power. Even so, Sindh is the province of Pakistan with the most significant Hindu population, and it now belongs to Pakistan, a country that has been through different steps of Islamisation.

## The Land and Its Livelihood

Sindh is the creation of a river, the slow work of the Indus laying down land over the ages. Because the river made it, the province is not seriously troubled when the monsoon fails, though its rains are scanty and never regular; its calamities have come instead from the river, in unchecked floods, drifting sand and channels that change their course. Within those limits the land has always fed its people in modest comfort, and it was once thought a sort of Arabia Felix, a happy Arabia, with a quiet, industrious and intelligent people living apart from the rest of India. Away from the river and its large branches the country is arid and greenery is rare. It has little building stone and no metal at all.

Famine, which devastated less fortunate parts of India, ordinarily held no terrors for the Sindhi peasant, and the province was relatively immune from it. Health was another matter. Under the Mughals, Aurangzeb's *jagir*, a grant of land revenue, in Sindh yielded little because drought, locusts, plague and floods struck in three successive seasons between 1648 and 1652, and plague returned in 1696. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries public health in Sindh was not good. Lower Sindh was described as unhealthy, with its epidemics, agues and fevers, and ophthalmia, an inflammation of the eyes, caused by the dust.

Irrigation has always been the key to the province, for in a land of so little rain its place cannot be exaggerated. The canals, Persian wheels and wells of Sindh were built by the people of the land without any help from the empire of Delhi; skilful irrigation has existed there since the earliest days, and Sindh has always been a pioneer of irrigation in India. Irrigation under the Kalhora rulers was probably better than under the Talpurs who came after them, yet in 1853 the whole canal system could still be called one of makeshifts and expedients. The old ways of lifting water were still in use all over Sindh in 1940, and the canals of the Mughal and Kalhora days must, within their limits, have been a very important source of the country's wealth. Several of the biggest were dug by *begari*, forced labour, which had always been practised in Sindh.

The crops followed the water. In 1847 bajri and jowari, kinds of millet, were commoner in the upper part of the country and wheat and barley in the lower, while rice grew most widely in the low southern parts. The Chandookah and the Lar were then, as in 1940, the two great rice producing areas, and cultivators were thought better off than in most parts of India. Sindh has always been noted for its fish, both fresh water and salt; the *Ain-i-Akbari* gives rice and fish as the staple food of Lower Sindh. The coast of Lower Sindh was, with Gujarat, Kanara and Malabar, one of the great sources of India's seafaring people.

In the seventeenth century Sindh was a place of trade. With the Indus as its inland waterway, its easy connexion with Lahore and Multan, its thriving cotton industry, its vast emporium at Thatta and the busy port of Laribunder, it was soon seen as a profitable centre of trade with Europe. In 1636 it was said that of all Indian goods none were in such request as those of Sindh. That year the English set up a factory, a trading post, at Thatta; they gave up their factory in Sindh in 1662, opened one at Thatta again in 1758 and gave it up in 1775. After a letter of 13 October 1657 the Company's papers fall blank on Sindh until 25 November 1744, and mentions of the province stay uncommon until the factory reopened in 1758; from August 1762 to July 1764 the Sindh factory kept a regular record. None of the Company's covenanted servants between 1712 and 1738 lived in Sindh. The Company was always very much the timid guest of the Sindh government, and its customs and transit dues were as unreasonable and oppressive as they had been in Mughal days.

Inside Sindh all goods went by pack animals, mostly camels, and wheeled traffic was practically unknown. Yet trade by land and by water was very well organised, the business soundly established and the government's supervision very thorough. Pirates infested the coasts of Gujarat, Kathiawar and Sindh and kept shippers in continual anxiety, and a Company letter of 1758 called the whole coast rogues on both sides. Three kinds of rupee circulated in Sindh, and the *lari*, a coin, was in common use in southern Persia, Sindh and western Asia.

Under the Mughals and the Kalhora the chief industries were cotton goods, saltpetre and indigo, leather and fish, and hand milling of flour, oil pressing and the potter's craft could still be seen in Sindh in 1940 exactly as they were then. The popular modern idea of Sindh as a desert is as mistaken as the view that it was a purely agricultural country. It supported half a dozen thriving industries, camels and buffaloes kept much of its population, and camels were bred in every part of Sindh. Thatta and Nasarpur were the main centres of the cotton industry. Saltpetre production was put at up to 10,000 maunds a year, the maund being a measure of weight, but Sindh's supply was never more than a useful addition to the main supply from Bengal and Bihar, and indigo, grown mostly in Middle Sindh, was of inferior quality.

Sindh has always had large numbers of buffaloes and oxen and a plentiful supply of hides, and its saddlery and ornamental leatherwork were famous. In a Muslim land, three quarters of the people did not share the prejudice against leather. Skilled hands were harder to find. In the 1760s the Company complained of how few skilled workmen Sindh had and how unable it was to provide suitable artisans; Sindhi craftsmen able to make the articles it needed could not be found, nor could artificers for the saltpetre coppers.

The trade declined. By 1770 Sindh suffered with the rest of cotton manufacturing India, and the later Kalhora made it a self contained Muslim state closed to Englishmen for nearly three quarters of a century, so that it sank into lethargy until after 1843. The decline of its trade from 1775 was not, however, due to the incapacity of the Kalhora or the parochialism of the Talpurs. By the time of the British annexation, after half a century of Talpur rule, Sindh had for nearly a hundred years ceased to supply cottons to the European market, and was importing English chintzes and calicoes instead of exporting its own. Its exports were rice, ghi, opium, indigo, dried fish and leather, and Upper Sindh sent dry grains, ghi, cotton and indigo down to Lower Sindh and received rice, fish, leather goods and arms in return.

The people were far fewer than in modern times. In the Mughal and Kalhora days Sindh supported not a quarter of the population it supported in 1940, and its people then can hardly have numbered more than a million. In 1828 and 1832 the population was put at between 850,000 and about a million, the lower estimate counting about a quarter of them Hindus. After 1740 the chief concentration of people shifted from Thatta to Shikarpur, Hyderabad and Karachi. Society was agricultural and pastoral, with cottage industry spread widely and town life, as it is understood today, found in Thatta only.

The village changed little. The farming and herding people lived in huts that could be put up and moved with the floods, and nineteenth century descriptions still matched the farming and herding districts of Sindh in 1940. The Talpurs, like true Baluchis, set little store by magnificent buildings, and Baluch influence is thought partly responsible for a decline in the standard of building. The clay houses of Lower Sindh as they were in 1842 were still recognisable in 1940, and wind scoops were still in universal use in Lower Sindh to catch the south west wind. The Sindhi village must have recalled the villages of early medieval Europe, as the small villages of rural Sindh still did in 1940, with Hindu traders in the bazaar and the Muslim cultivators in their *landhis*, clusters of huts, round it.

The lot of the poor altered as little as their houses. Labourers, artisans and peasants were relatively little worse off in Mughal and Kalhora times than in 1940. The moneylender advanced money against a share of the crop, a system that still prevailed, and children herding cattle were still one of the chief obstacles to educating the peasants. Yet throughout the Mughal and Kalhora periods circumstances continually conspired to grind the poor in their poverty.

Government rested on the land. At the time of the British occupation the system did not differ greatly from that of the Mughals: the state took its share in kind, and taxation as a whole was extraordinarily comprehensive. Sindh was by then approaching a natural and self contained economy, a sign of a great increase in administrative efficiency from 1740 onwards. Local cesses were not abolished under the Kalhora, and under the Talpurs their collection was tightened. Tax farming, the farmers being mostly Hindus, was one of the great curses of Mughal, Kalhora and Talpur rule alike, and a poll tax, the *sar shumari*, fell on Muslims who were not Baluchis, the Jats among them. Backed by the Baluch soldiery, this taxation made the government far from popular with the common man and enriched the ruling house.

Land tenure in Sindh, so far as is known, has always been feudal. In 1847 land held in *jagir* or *inam*, grants free of revenue, came to a tenth of the government land. The jagirs were held mostly by Baluch chiefs, and the *puttadaris*, leased holdings found in Upper Sindh especially, mostly by Afghan settlers dating from the time of Nadir Shah and earlier. The representative of the zamindar, the landholder, was called Mukhadam or Arbab, a title that had not died out by 1940.

## Saints and Pilgrimage

The Sufi history of Sindh opens with a traveller. The mystic Hallaj crossed the province shortly after 900 to call people to God, wandering from Gujarat through Sindh, the lower Indus valley, which had been part of the Muslim empire since 711. In 905 he travelled widely there and probably discussed theological questions with the sages of the country, and the seeds he sowed grew in later centuries into the mystical poetry of the province. Hallaj is still a very popular figure in Sindh, and many Sufi poets name him in their verses. The Sufi traditions are held to have entered the subcontinent through Sindh, a long line of mystics coming after Muhammad bin Qasim's conquest by way of Baluchistan, Makran, Multan and Sindh itself; accounts differ on how far Sufism spread in those early centuries, for no growth like that of Transoxiana and Khurasan is known there.

Sindh drew contemplative and religious people to it during the middle age of Islam, and this is the foundation of its school of Sufi thought. Sufi mysticism, brought in centuries ago, found a congenial home there and has been very prominent at various times, and the several skilled exponents of Sufi thought in medieval Sindh account for the mastery of Sufi expression in the poetry of Shah Abdul Latif. From the thirteenth century onwards there was a great immigration into Sindh of learned and religious men from the centre of the Muslim world, and the great ascendancy of the Sayyids, the descendants of the Prophet, dates from about that time. Most of these Sayyids were Shia, and Sindh may for a time have been a congenial home for them. Down the centuries the Sayyids of Sindh have produced a number of men famous for their learning and their saintliness.

The course of Sufism itself is hard to follow. How *tasawwuf*, Sufism, progressed in Sindh cannot be traced in detail, and between the thirteenth century, when Lal Shahbaz Qalandar flourished, and the eighteenth, very little about the Sufi poets of the province is securely established. Accounts differ on how steadily Sufism grew there; as in other regions of South Asia, a strong Sufi presence is said to have been rapidly established across Sindh with the arrival of charismatic figures, often linked with one of the main Sufi orders, such as the Suhrawardis and the Qadiris.

The landscape of shrines took shape from the thirteenth century, from the day Lal Shahbaz Qalandar settled in Sehwan, and from Baha'uddin Zakariya's Suhrawardi centre at Multan, on the northernmost border of the country. From then on Sindh produced an immense number of mystics, whose shrines are scattered all through the Indus valley, and who made short songs in their mother tongue to express the mysteries of love and union. The Sumra of Lower Sindh, who were Isma'ilis, are believed to have turned to Sunni Islam under the influence of the Sayyid Makhdum Jahaniyan, and a Jat chief of Sindh, later named ʿAbdullah, is said to have embraced Islam through a miracle performed by Makhdum Jahaniyan. Under the Mughals, at their conquest of the country, a great plague broke out in those parts; dervishes dreamt that it came from the rebelliousness of its people, and everyone made vows.

In the sixteenth century a group of mystics migrated from Sindh to Burhanpur in central India, where they founded a new branch of the Qadiri order, and they are said to have used Sindhi verses in their *sama*, the Sufi sessions of spiritual listening to music. From that time on the native tradition of Sindh holds countless stories of saints, above all of those buried on Makli Hill near Thatta.

Three orders were dominant among the Hindu Sufis of Sindh. The Qadiriyya was the most influential; the Qalandariyya, in Sindh, reflects the radiance of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar and the pilgrimage centre of Sehwan Sharif; and the Suhrawardiyya, the oldest order to settle in Multan, was predominant in the Indus valley from the thirteenth century onwards. Perhaps because of the Suhrawardi hold on Sindh, the Chishtiyya never spread there as it did in the rest of South Asia, and the Suhrawardis and the Qadiris remain the brotherhoods that dominate the landscape of Sindh. The Naqshbandiyya has several important centres in the province, above all Lowari Sharif near Badin, in Southern Sindh.

The *faqirs*, the wandering mystics, of Sindh belonged to the two great orders of Jelali and Jemali dervishes. The Jemali faqirs belonged to four orders, the Qadiri, the Naqshbandi, the Suhrawardi and the Chishti, and beside them went the Qalandars and the so called Sufi beggars who wandered far and wide over Sindh, taking their toll of the superstitious country people. In Sindh, however, Sufi practice rested more on personal allegiance to a master than on membership of an order, and a Sufi could belong to several orders at once.

Shah Abdul Latif himself came from one of the many lineages of hereditary saints established in the Sindh countryside, where they have always enjoyed great prestige and power as *pirs*, holy men with a special spiritual authority. Yet unlike many of the pirs of Sindh, the poet was not formally linked through a chain of spiritual descent to any of the great Sufi orders. His *Risalo*, the collection of his poetry, names none of the great orders, nor any of the sanctuaries to which the people of Sindh flocked on many occasions.

The pir stood at the centre of religious life in the countryside, and the Sufism of the common people was a very different thing from the refined system of the poets and theologians. The saints of Sindh were so numerous that scarcely a village was without its tombs of holy men. The pirs held positions of great personal authority, and the countryside became the home of an immense number of their petty realms. A sainted mendicant, such as the Kalhora claimed for their ancestor, has always appealed vividly to the Muslims of the lower Indus valley. The belief that the saints sleep in their tombs is the solid foundation of the extraordinary devotion paid in Sindh to the tombs of saints, pirs and holy men, and it is bound up with the bond of pir and disciple.

Travellers found the reverence and pampering of holy men in Sindh almost beyond belief. A Persian proverb said that Multan consisted of heat, dust, beggars and tombs, and it applied equally to Sindh, whose social religion was built round the reverence of the *murid*, the disciple, for the *murshid*, the master. One traveller called it the most priest ridden country on earth, said to possess no fewer than one hundred thousand tombs of saints, and another found its mendicants more numerous than in any other country in Asia. More than a hundred thousand Sufi shrines are still said to stand in the province. The tendency grew more pronounced under the Kalhora; yet the real form and meaning of Islam in Sindh is to be read not in these outbursts but in the beautiful spiritual poems of Shah Abdul Latif.

The tombs were places of trade as well as prayer. Markets and fairs on saints' days at saints' tombs were well established, so that in this the commerce of Sindh followed the old medieval pattern, and every kind of popular superstition could be found in the province. Still today the trees near Sindhi shrines are hung with many pieces of cloth, preferably red, in the shape of little flags or tiny cradles, tied there for the sake of a vow, as is usual almost everywhere in the Islamic world.

The cult of ʿAbd al-Qadir Gilani runs through the province. Old Sindhi songs describe how his spiritual realm extends from Istanbul to Delhi. About 1850 there were some hundred large trees in Sindh, all named after Gilani, each with a pole and a flag hung on it to fulfil a vow made in adversity or sickness; sweetmeats were given to the poor in the saint's honour, and not even cattle were allowed to touch the fruit and leaves of the trees. Names such as Ghauth Bakhsh, gift of the help, in Sindh and Baluchistan show how popular he was. Hymns to Gilani are found in both Sindh and the Punjab, and one of the first geographical poems in Sindhi was composed in his honour.

The river had its own saint. Under the name of Khwajo Khizr he has always held great power in Sindh, and popular belief has made him the spirit of the waters and the deity of the river Indus. His cult was linked with the worship of the Indus and supported by Muslim and Hindu alike, and in Sindh Khizr is also called Zinda Pir, the living saint. The Daryapanthis of Sindh, the followers of the river path, believe in the sanctity of the River God or the God of Waters, a belief that touches Hindu and Muslim alike. Udero Lal is the figure most often merged with Khizr in Sindh, and offerings to the Indus can still be seen in the wider region.

The piety of the province lived in books as well. Some mystics in Sindh are said to have kept only three books for spiritual nourishment: the Quran, the Mathnawi of Rumi and the Diwan of Hafiz. Many pious people in Sindh kept the marketplace of union warm by reciting the Mathnawi, and Rumi's work was quoted by stern Naqshbandis as much as by members of the more emotional orders. At Shah Abdul Latif's own tomb today dervishes perform his music on Thursday nights, and the melodies are well known all over Sindh.

Women have their place in this devotion. Sindh, famous for its saint worship, is particularly rich in legends of women saints, and the Sindhis have not refused to admit the religious merit of women. The *faqirani*, the woman faqir, could occasionally rise to the high rank of *murshid*. The women of Sindh's religious life include Bibi Fatima Hajram, a *hafiza*, one who knew the whole Quran by heart, and in Sindh, as elsewhere, there are whole groups of women saints, such as the Seven Chaste and the veiled women in the rank of the *abdal*, a high order of saints. Women in Sindh often address the saints with endearing diminutives, in poems in honour of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar of Sehwan and, in more recent times, in verses devoted to the Pir Pagaro. One scholar of Islam held that she had learned more about Islamic piety from women in Anatolia and Sindh than from most of her learned colleagues or from sophisticated books.

Women's singing at the shrines has a geography of its own. The *lotevali* gypsy women sang only at the shrine of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar, the one place in Sindh where they still came, and no such tradition was seen at Bhit Shah, at the shrine of Sachal Sarmast, at Rikhail Shah's or at any other Sufi *dargah*, or shrine, in Sindh. Asked whether women's singing at Sufi shrines is above all a tradition of Sindh, the singer Reshma said that it was so at the shrines, less so in Rajasthan, but most of all at the shrine of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar.

Hindu pilgrims and renunciants crossed Sindh too. Wandering yogis traversed the province on pilgrimage to the shrine of the goddess at Hinglaj in Baluchistan, and three of the *surs*, the chapters, of Shah Abdul Latif's *Risalo* are devoted to them; as a young man the poet is believed to have spent time with them. Sur 18, *Ramakali*, praises the bands of Hindu yogis who wandered from place to place in Sindh or beyond as ideal practitioners of the spiritual life, and the many places in and around Sindh named in the *Risalo* support the belief that the poet travelled widely in his youth. The Nathpanthis, followers of the Nath yogis, known locally as Verangins or Berangins, played a part in the province in many forms, and the poet's own words for the yogis show that they were an important religious community in eighteenth century Sindh. *Sami* is the name generally given in Sindh to a group of wandering faqirs. Raja Bharthari, who gave up his throne and made a hermitage at Sehwan, is also known to the Hindus of Sindh as Raja Virag.

The south of Pakistan, and Sindh above all, offers a whole range of places of salvation visited by different religious communities, forming a network of pilgrimage routes and ritual journeys. Legends that link Shah Abdul Latif with the Goddess join Hindu and Muslim sacred spaces in Sindh, and show that such imagined landscapes need not be divided by clear religious categories. Most visitors to the festival of the Hinglaj goddess are Kohlis, Menghwar and Bhil, which reflects the Hindu population of Sindh as a whole. The deified heroes most popular in Sindh were Rama Pir, Pithoro Pir, Pabuji and Guga Pir; Ramdeo Pir is still known in the province as Rama Pir, and has now been taken into the temples of the gods worshipped across India.

The Hindus of Sindh are tied to Sufism in three ways. Most common is a Sufi tomb visited by both Muslims and Hindus, such as Bhit Shah; fairly common too is a saint with a double identity, Muslim and Hindu, such as Udero Lal; and the third is a Sufi *darbar*, a saint's shrine and seat, managed by Hindus, such as that of Pithoro Pir. A number of sacred figures in Sindh have this double identity, the Muslim side always Sufi and the Hindu side usually belonging to the Nathpanth, the path of the Nath yogis. Hindus have long come to the Sufi mausoleums of Sindh, though accounts differ on when they began. The great majority of Hindus used to visit the Sufi shrines; this did not make them Sufis, but it does mean that for them the Sufis are part of their religious legacy.

Pithoro Pir shows how varied these meetings could be. Among his Muslim disciples in Sindh are Sindhis such as the Sammas and Panhwars and Baluchis such as the Maris and the Legharis, and in Sindh his disciples mix, whereas in Kutch the untouchable disciples worship apart. His cult shows the variety of the negotiations between Sufism and Hinduism in Sindh.

The holy places of the province are its *darbars*. In Sindh this Persian word was mostly used, and the darbar was the sacred ground in which Sufi culture was at work, a term used by Sikhs, Muslims and Hindus alike. Colonial rule brought a renewal of religion as a composite middle class spread, marked by the rise of the darbars and a darbar culture. Some darbars of the Nanakpanth, the path of Guru Nanak, had been founded in the late eighteenth century, but there was a renaissance of Nanakpanthi darbars in the second half of the nineteenth, with many new ones built all over Sindh. Hindu Sufis from Shikarpur and Rohri, in Northern Sindh, suggest that the devotional centres of the province may have shifted north some time after the British conquest.

Guru Nanak's own passage is remembered as well. A temple at Clifton has been identified as the one Nanak visited on his tour of Sindh, and tradition has it that he visited Uch Sharif on his way to Sindh, towards the end of his travels, after he had already visited the east, south, north and west.

Jhulelal has a geography of his own in the province. Some groups among the Hindus of Sindh already worshipped him, though most of them were Nanakpanthis. Before Partition his temples in Sindh were built with a dome, as were some Sikh *samadhis*, memorials raised over the place of a cremation, and his priests, the Thakurs, lived in several cities of the province until they migrated to India from 1947 onwards. In colonial Sindh there was an attempt to create a single central path devoted to Jhulelal, then known as Amar Lal or Udero Lal, and a meeting of several Hindu *panchayats*, community councils, of Sindh was held at Udero Lal. Shrines devoted to Jhulelal are found across Southern Sindh. Thano Bula Khan lies in the Kohistan, the western part of Sindh that stands close to Baluchistan.

Seen from India, Sindh remains the source of holiness. The darbars that Sindhi migrants founded in India are not centred on tombs, unlike those of Sindh, and in them a photographic portrait carries the sacredness of the space that the tomb carries in the dargahs of Sindh. The *shahnai*, a reed pipe, is very common in any religious ritual performed in Sindh, while in India some typical Sufi rituals of Sindh, such as the *alam*, the raised standard, and the *dhamal*, the ecstatic dance, have been suppressed. In Sindh the *bayragins*, the crutches of renunciants, are kept as relics and mark renunciation, once again whatever the religion of their owner.

The Indian darbars keep their ties to the old country. The darbar of Rochaldas is made an extension of an original sacred territory in Sindh, the holy city of Sehwan Sharif, and invoking Jhok Sharif builds a network of sacred places all located in Sindh. The *mehndi*, the henna rite, of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar keeps a direct ritual link with the lost territory of Sindh, and the living link is with the master who stays in Sindh, now in Pakistan. The spiritual masters, the *sajjada nashins*, hereditary heads of the shrines, or *pirs*, of Daraza, Tando Jahaniya and Jhok Sharif are still in Sindh.

## Communities

Sindh is one of the western wings of India that became regions with a Muslim majority. There, from the fourteenth century through the Mughal period, the growth of Muslim societies went with the adoption of settled agriculture, in a frontier society not yet deeply touched by Brahmanical values, and the shrines of great Sufis attracted and absorbed pastoral clans who had not yet taken to farming and were not Muslim. Along the rivers of Sindh lived groups who gave themselves wholly to tending cattle and had no masters. Called Jatts, they lived in the wastes of the Indus valley with no social hierarchy among them, and between the seventh and eleventh centuries these pastoral Jats were moving up from Sindh into the Multan area, a migration that carried them north into the Punjab.

In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries a great gulf yawned between the ideal of Islam and its practice in Sindh, a Muslim state that struggled to a measure of independence in the middle of the eighteenth century. Most of its people were converted Jats. The majority followed the Sunna, the way of the Prophet, but very influential sections held Shia beliefs, and the Sayyids were nearly all Shia. The mass conversions blurred the clear line between Sunni and Shia and left the Islam of the common people much shaped by Hindu ways, with Hindus visiting Muslim shrines and making votive offerings, and Sindhi orthodoxy easygoing in character. This popular Islam owed much to the religion of the indigenous Sindhis, sometimes called Jats, who were the majority of the population.

From a simple belief in the tenets of a tolerant Sufism flowed a Sufi school of thought, a more pantheistic view of Islamic doctrine than elsewhere, an absence of bitterness between Sunni and Shia, and a kind of meeting between the deeper mystical ideas of Muslim and Hindu thought. Hindu sharing in the Muslim orders is more visible in Sindh than in the Punjab. Hindu writers composed mystical poems in Muslim imagery and poems in honour of the Prophet, and even wrote *taʿziya*, laments, for the Muharram mourning of the Shia community of Sindh, or ballads on the fate of famous Sufi martyrs.

Makhdum Abdur Rahim Girhori was not typical of Sindhi Sufism. On the whole Muslim and Hindu alike shared a common understanding of the mystical message of Islam, beside an almost incredible veneration for Sayyids as such and a great growth of superstitious practice among the people in eighteenth century Sindh. This understanding may have been eased by the Ismaili missionaries who came to Sindh in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. Neither Hindus nor Muslims are orthodox in Sindh: still in the 1920s numerous Hindus, among them some of the best minds of Sindh, were Sufi by religion, and a number of Hindus became devoted disciples of Muslim saints. Shah Abdul Latif, like his Punjabi contemporaries and his successors in Sindh, accepted the Hindus who strove on the way to perfection as true pilgrims in the eternal Light.

Under the Mughals, at the end of the seventeenth century, the peoples of the *suba*, or province, followed its landscape. North of a line from Multan and Uch to Thatta and Kech Makran lay hills of hard rock, home of the Baluchis and some Afghans; to the south, from Uch to Gujarat, were hills of sand and the Bhati, whose chief had his seat at Jaisalmer, with other Rajput tribes; and from Bhakkar to Nasirpur and Umarkot lived the Jareja, among other peoples. Society was also seen as three classes: the privileged, that is the Amirs, chiefs, Baluchis, Sayyids and religious mendicants; the pastoral, farming and other Muslim tribes who made up the majority; and the Hindus, who were oppressed in the most open way.

By 1843 Muslim society was divided along ethnic lines between the Ashrafs, of noble descent, and the Ajlafs, the common people. The Baluchis stood at the top. The Sayyids were mostly landowners, *zamindars*, but were also pirs, and the pirs of Sindh played the part of mediators between the tribes, and between the rulers and the ruled. Apart from a few groups of Afghans and Pathans in the north west, the bulk of the Muslims were Sindhis, most of them landless farmers, *haris*; the trading Muslims were the Memon, the Khojah and the Bohrah; and excluded groups such as the Shikaris and the Sidis stood at the bottom. Arabic, it was said, was known only to Muslim scholars.

The Baluchis have been in Sindh for many generations. Tribes of them settled in various parts of the country long before the Kalhora called them in during the eighteenth century to stiffen the forces of the prince of Sindh, the moment from which their political importance dates. In 1613 the Baluchis of the lower country had no good name for keeping the law, and Upper Sindh drew them more than Lower Sindh; by 1940 most of the Baluchi population lived in the Upper Sind Frontier, Larkana and Sukkur districts, the first still a kind of Baluchi preserve, and the Baluchi preferred the open fields to a house in a street. Baluchis and Sindhis had lived together long enough for their poetry to share common ground, and the Doms, the minstrels, visited the villages of Sindh wherever there were Baluchis to listen to them. As a class the Baluchis were not at all intolerant; intolerance in Sindh is traced rather to the Kalhora, as fanatical Muslims. The Mazaris are among the tribes strongly represented in Sindh, and Hindu peoples who came from the Punjab form an important part of the population.

The Hindus kept the money and the trade of the province. Even in the 1640s big business was in the hands of Hindu traders, some of whom grew very wealthy; the brokers, agents and *dalals*, or middlemen, were mostly Hindus, who could not display their wealth, and the Company's agents relied on many substantial Hindu traders. Under the Kalhora the true Sindhis, the Jats and camelmen, the fishermen and hunters, were fully exploited, and the position of the Hindus grew worse; yet they stayed in Sindh, lending money to the zamindars, financing trade, farming the revenue and owning all the petty shops in the villages.

Every year the Hindu traders of Sindh made long voyages by sea to places like Aden, the port in Yemen that was their regular destination, Gujarat and Lanka, through seas where Portuguese pirates had an evil name. It was the custom for their wives to make offerings to the water deity for their husbands' safe return. One distant place was famous for the wealth that drew sailors from far away Sindh, and the Hindu festival of lamps in the autumn marked the beginning of the trading season.

The British knew mostly the trading castes among the Hindus, and from this arose two common views, that Sindh had no real caste system and that the Brahmans played a small role. The Saraswat Brahmans accepted food from the Lohanas, who made up half the whole Hindu population and were divided into Amils and Bhaibands. In Eastern Sindh the Rajputs had kept their feudal privileges under the Talpurs, and the British restored them in return for loyalty. The *panchayat*, the caste council, prevailed among the Hindus of Sindh, and in rural Sindh the Lohanas' panchayat could hold authority over other castes.

The Sodhas, the dominant Rajput group, came by their own tradition from Ujjain in Malwa in 1226, and their chief, Parmar Sodha, was established as king, *rano*, at Umarkot. Most of them became landowners and farmers, and in 1907 they were described as worshippers of Shiva. Contrary to popular belief, there were many untouchables in Sindh, among them the Bhils or Kohlis and the Menghwar, mostly settled around Umarkot. At the first census many were found to have come from Gujarat or Rajasthan between 1843 and 1872; most worked in agriculture, and crafts were largely in Muslim hands.

The Lohanas have long been the centre of Hindu Sindh. By 1851 a portrait of the Lohanas was held to be a general portrait of the Hindus of Sindh. Over the past few centuries the community has absorbed many other groups and clans, and it now takes in a large share of the upper classes of Pakistan's Hindus; the merging is explained by the small number of Sindhis who were not Muslims drawing together against the majority. Around the eighteenth or nineteenth century the merchant groups of Sindh divided: Lohanas who worked for the local rulers became Amils, at first 10 to 15 per cent of them, and those who went on trading were Bhaibands.

The Amils, probably in the administration from the eighteenth century onwards, were the subcaste of the Lohanas who became the official scribes of the Kalhora and the Talpurs. The Lohanas of Sindh are too varied for any claim to hold for the whole community. Once the dominant Hindu community of Sindh before Partition, they are still mostly traders, and many of them, the Amils above all, have taken a leading part in making Sufism a main part of Sindhi identity.

Two paths dominated among the Lohanas and related merchant groups, the Nanakpanth and the Daryapanth. The Daryapanth, in all probability the older of the two, rests on the deification of the Indus and is served by a priestly caste, the Thakurs. The Lohanas practised animal sacrifice at Navratri, the festival of nine nights, and for Khetrapal, a guardian deity, while the Rajputs followed neither path. Saffron, which in Sindh means a bright red, is for the Daryapanthis a symbol both of religion and of marriage.

In nineteenth century Sindh the Nanakpanth was probably the dominant religious path among the Hindu Sindhis, and many Nanakpanthis belonged to a subgroup known as the Udasis. After the Singh Sabha reforms the Sindhi Nanakpanthis were not included in the Sikh community, and they saw themselves as a Hindu religious sect. The census shows the same blurring: many Lohanas followed Guru Nanak's teachings, so that in 1881 Lohanas from Shikarpur and Hyderabad were counted as Sikhs and in 1891 as Hindus. Today many middle and upper class Hindus in Sindh follow the Nanakpanth, and it is usual to cover one's head before entering a *tikana*, the temple that holds the Guru Granth Sahib.

Belonging in Sindh was long a matter of place rather than creed. Certain populations could be counted neither Muslim nor Hindu, recognised by neither, and this matched the creed of the Sufis of Sindh. As early as 1851 the worship of the intercessors was seen as the common religious language of Hindus and Muslims. In rural Sindh Muslims worshipped Sitala, the smallpox goddess, when epidemics struck the village, and devotion to a spiritual master counted for more than affiliation to an organised religion.

Colonial rule changed this. With new factors such as the census, people had more and more to name themselves by one of the great religions, where before they had named themselves by locality, caste and ethnicity. The Sindhis of the late nineteenth century increasingly named themselves by a religion the colonial power would recognise, and the migrants, coming from a region where Muslims dominated, identified more and more as Hindu in the later censuses. The Sindhi Hindus were the first to take an active part in the colonial system, and much of the new scholarly writing was the work of Hindu scholars, until the mass emigration of the Hindu population from Pakistan to India after independence in 1947.

The Hindu Sufis were the meeting point. Sufism entered the religion of the Hindu Sindhis in the nineteenth century, carried mainly by the Amils, the literary class, who made up the bulk of the province's literati. Hindus took Muslim Sufi masters through a cultural Islamisation that worked beyond religious affiliation, to which they surrendered without knowing it, and because the Muslim Sufi market was far more developed than the Hindu one; for a Hindu, to be a Sufi was to take full part in building a modern Sindhi province. No formal tie is known between Sindh and the Hindu branch of the Naqshbandiyya.

Krishna and the Vedanta entered the same meeting. Worship of Krishna had grown among the Amils by the late nineteenth century, and some of them joined the Vallabhachari sect. Sufis of the period such as Sachal, Qutub Ali Shah and Bedil brought Krishna into their poetry, so that, though Sindh was dominated by Muslims, Krishnaite *bhakti*, loving devotion to God, influenced the *wujudi* Sufis, who held the unity of all being. Wall paintings of Krishna in Hindu temples may be a sign of a Krishnaite revival among the Sindhi Hindus in the nineteenth century. In turn, the Muslim Sufis of Sindh were drawn mostly to the Vishnuite school of Hinduism. A main force in the religious renewal of the province was the Neo-Vedanta of Ramakrishna, and before Partition many stories were told of businessmen who gave up all their worldly goods and work to become renunciants.

Some communities stood low. The fishing castes named in Sur 16 of the *Risalo*, *Kamod*, among them the Mangar, the Me and the Muhano, were traditionally ranked as the lowest of the low in Sindhi society, and the Mohana likewise belonged to the lowest social strata in Sindh. Professional musicians in Sindh come mostly from the minstrel class, which is held in low esteem.

The Kohlis are the indigenous peasant communities of Sindh, sometimes migratory and usually temporary farm labourers on the large feudal holdings. They are not Muslims, and together with the Bhils they are the native people of the country that runs from Sindh into Rajasthan. The Bhils and Kohlis are aboriginal, landless, roving peasants who work on the large landholdings of Sindh and Indian Gujarat. Because Shah Abdul Latif wrote in the Sindhi vernacular, Kohli and Bhil peasants, men and women, can understand his poetry and relate to it.

The Sidis came to the province from Africa, first brought as slaves to work in the subcontinent. They are free now, though some are still attached to the feudal households of Sindh, and their native language is Sindhi. Africans are present in Sindh in large numbers because of the slave trade; they were freed there too when slavery was abolished, and they had been coming through the ages. The Sidis of Sindh and Kutch claim descent from Hazrat Bilal, and they are also known as Sheedi or Syedi in Sindh and Gujarat. Their *mugarman* drum is held sacred by the Sidi communities of Sindh, Baluchistan, Rajasthan and Gujarat, who use it in their shrines and rituals. A visitor to the Sidis of Karachi Mori was served fish cooked in curry, a traditional Sindhi lunch eaten with rice and flatbread.

The common life of the country was shared. The peasants of Sindh were in 1940, and always had been, fond of laughter and amusement, of conversation and of listening to songs and stories, and they kept Id, Holi and Diwali with whole hearts. Wrestling was as popular in earlier days as in 1940, when it could claim to be the most popular sport of the country; Sindh had, and in 1940 still had, a great variety of folk dances; and polo seems never to have been played there. Drink and drugs were undoubtedly a curse of the country and very widespread.

Kin and custom shape security and the place of women. In rural Sindh above all, the clan or *baradari*, the brotherhood of kin, guarantees security rather than the nation state. Among local people the greatest conflicts can be resolved if one feuding party can get a *niyani*, the little sister of Sindhi society, to intervene for it, and for this reason women faqirs could be seen as perfect instruments of intercession. In parts of Sindh and Punjab unmarried girls in their teens and early twenties have been found the most vulnerable, facing discriminatory food portions within the family and limited access to health care.

Some communities carry their histories as legends. In the Brahmakshatriya story, Parashurama arrives in Sindh to kill the Kshatriyas, the warrior caste, and the mighty king Ratnasena, ruler of Sindh, flees. In the Charan story of Avar, famine drives her family to Sindh, then ruled by Umar Sumra, a recent convert to Islam known as Hamir; the Samma and Bhati Rajput kings, who had long wanted the country, help Avar kill him and then divide Sindh between them, and another story has Avar kill fifty two Hun demons and so save the people of Sindh. The Vagris tell of moving back and forth between Sindh and Gujarat for many generations until Partition forced them to settle on one side of the border; with no written account, the details of the clan's history can only be guessed at.

Most Hindus of present day Sindh belong to the lowest castes. Sindh is home to the majority of Pakistan's Hindus, whose number estimates put at between 6 and 10 million, the figure varying widely, and over 90 per cent of them belong to underprivileged tribes or subcastes such as the Menghwar, the Bhil and the Kohli, who often work as manual labourers for the powerful landowning elite. In Sindh the Kohli community is mainly a disempowered scheduled caste whose members work as peasants on the fields of the province's feudal elite. Despite the mass migration, the Sindhi Hindus of Pakistan still number several millions, most of them from the scheduled castes, mostly Menghwars, Bhils and Kohlis.

Some call them Dalits, and among the Hindus of Sindh there is now a debate on whether the term fits; some even claim that they are not Hindu. Yet the Sufi legacy is still alive among the Dalits of Sindh, and the way it is taken up depends on social milieu, on caste and on whether people live in town or country.

Most of the non Muslims who remain in Pakistan are Hindus living in the outlying regions of Southern Punjab, inner Sindh and Baluchistan. By the census of 1998 the Hindus were the largest minority in the country, with Christians second. In Sindh and Southern Punjab the Hindu community counts both lower and upper castes and is part of the social fabric of the area. After the demolition of the Babri Mosque on 6 December 1992, Hindu communities in Sindh and Quetta were attacked.

The Nanakpanthi Sikhs belong to South Punjab and Sindh, and they are found in very large numbers in the province of Sindh; their temples in Sindh and Southern Punjab hold portraits of Guru Nanak beside Hindu deities. Thousands of such Sikhs live in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Punjab and Sindh. The communities who refused the Khalsa, the order of baptised Sikhs, and called themselves followers of Guru Nanak still exist in Sindh and in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa; baptised Sikhs look down on them, and at Manora they are merging into the larger groups of Sikhs. There are said to be several shrines in the Multan region and in Sindh where people still practise Hinduism and give it the name of Islam.

Caste remains contested in the province. It is often said that the caste system is almost absent in Sindh, or was never rigid there because the Brahmans were so few, and Sindh may indeed have had a social fabric of its own, though colonial rule renewed it deeply there as everywhere in India. That picture is tied to the assimilating role of the Lohanas and to nationalist portraits of Sindh as a place of religious harmony. Devoted Sindhi Hindu nationalists hold that caste discrimination exists today only in India or in Pakistan's other provinces, while many upper caste Sindhi Hindus deny or ignore acts of segregation, which Dalit organisations fiercely oppose.

The words of caste had their own Sindhi uses. In Sindh the term Vaishiya was used, unusually, for operatives and mechanics as against merchants and shopkeepers, and the Saraswat Brahmans were devoted to the Devi, the Goddess, who in most areas of Sindh was called Hinglaj.

Rites and formulas both mark the communities off and draw them together. Reformers set their ideas of progress against low caste rites in Sindh such as the sacrifice of animals or the drinking of alcohol as *prasad*, the blessed offering, and many other Hindu groups in Sindh, and all over Pakistan, sacrifice animals for the fulfilment of wishes or at rites of passage. Among the Hindus of Sindh the formula that all have one Lord, *Sab ka malik ek hai*, has for some years been very popular and marks a new policy of theirs, and the Hindu *dargah* shows how strongly the middle and upper Hindu classes of Sindh hold to their Sufi legacy.

## Language, Poetry and Music

Poetry is the heart of religion in Sindh: "Sufi poetry is the backbone of Sufism in Sindh, including for the Hindu Sufis". It was the Sufis who made the spoken language of the province into a literary one. Sindh had, and still has, "a big treasure of folk poetry, ballads, and working songs", with folk tales at home in every part of the country, from the hill slopes of Baluchistan and the desert of Thar Parkar to the fertile plains watered by the Indus and the shifting creeks near its mouth. Mystical poetry in the mother tongue begins in Sindh, as in the Punjab, in the fifteenth century, and in Sindh too the eighteenth century was the most important period for the making of mystical poetry and prose.

Shah Abdul Latif did not begin this tradition. Before him there was already a settled culture of Sufi poetry in the spoken language, with poets such as Shah Abdul Karim, who died in 1623, and Shah Inat Rizvi, who died in 1711, though that earlier poetry tends to be overshadowed by Shah Abdul Latif's unique reputation.

The works of the Sufis of Sindh, known as *kalams*, devotional poems, "constituted the foundations of Sufi culture". Apart from the Naqshbandis, Sufism in Sindh is dominated by poetry of *wahdat al-wujud*, the unity of being, the teaching that all existence is one in God. The Hindus of Sindh went to Sufi shrines and shared their passion for Sufi poetry with Muslim Sindhis, above all through Shah Abdul Latif's *Shah jo Risalo*. The Sufis to whom the Hindus look belong mostly to Upper Sindh, where Siraiki is used for Sufi poetry besides Sindhi, and all of them belong to this *wujudi* school, leaving out the Naqshbandis. This trend seems to have grown in the nineteenth century, under colonial rule.

The province's religious culture was made in its own language. Manuscripts and early lithographed books show that it had been vernacularised, carried into the speech of the people, and the first step was usually a translation from Arabic and Persian into Sindhi. In the early eighteenth century Naqshbandi scholars from Lowari Sharif wrote treatises showing that the Muslim Sindhis did not know the Quran; they wrote paraphrases and then a Sindhi translation of it, and condemned as un-Islamic some rituals they called Hindu or Shia. Their later treatises concluded that the Sindhis, even when officially Muslim, did not practise orthodox Islam.

"Devotional literature in Sindhi thus formed the basis of a culture that transcended social, religious and ethnic divisions." It was carried to the villages not by the *ulema*, the Muslim scholars, nor by the *pandits*, the Hindu learned men, but by the caste musicians, the Manganhar or *mirasi*. The Shia devotional poetry of the *marsiya*, the lament for the martyrs of Karbala, is cherished by both Muslims and Hindus, and Hindus have been its performers as well as its authors.

The learned languages of the province were not its spoken one. The five native histories of Sindh are all in Persian, "which was the Court language of the ruling houses of the country up to the days of the British conquest". The learned men of Mughal and Kalhora Sindh were not few, but they wrote "in Persian and Arabic and not in the language of the countryside". Most of them were Sayyids, Kazis and Mullas, or men who taught the Quran, and the writers came largely from families that had immigrated into Sindh: "They were not Sindhis by birth".

Learned men had long been drawn to the province. In the days of the Afghans before the Mughals, and earlier, "Sind proved a favourite home for learned men", attracted by its fame for Sufi philosophy. The age of the Arghuns and Tarkhans was especially notable for the arrival of "this kind of intellectual aristocracy", which had "but little effect upon the unlettered cultivator class". In this, Sindh was merely typical of Muslim India generally.

Arabic and Urdu left little mark on the province's verse. Arabic has always been known and spoken in Sindh by a few learned men, and the Quran "is known by heart by large numbers of devout Muslims in the province", but Arabic's effect on the poetry of Sindh is negligible. "Urdu has never been a language which has taken kindly to Sind soil", and the literary currents of Sindh came from Persian and Arabic sources. The Sindhi language itself has been seen as suited to "a peace-loving agricultural and pastoral culture elaborated by an industrious and non-martial people". The province went on producing mystics who wrote either in Persian or in their mother tongue.

Learning reached few. Sindh has always had men called *hafiz*, one who has memorised the whole Quran, but that does not mean they knew Arabic as scholars. In Mughal days Sindh showed simply the working of the Islamic system of education, and "The great mass of the people remained illiterate". The province has never been known for learning, and in medieval times could have had few chances of keeping anything like a Muslim university.

Teaching seems never to have been held in great respect. Teachers were seldom paid more than half a rupee a month by each pupil. In the time of the Talpurs Sindh had six *madrasas*, schools of religious learning, among them those at Sehwan, at Trippat, at Matiari, at Mohar or Walhari near Umarkot, and at Chothiyari on the Nara. Sindhi was much neglected as a language of teaching, except by Hindus, who wrote it in a script other than the Arabic, and conditions were unlikely to have been better under the Mughals and the Kalhora.

Northern Sindh had a second language of verse. Siraiki is the Sindhi dialect spoken mostly in Northern Sindh, especially among the Baluch, and it is also a language of Southern Punjab. Many Sindhi Sufi poets, especially from the north and mainly those of Baluch origin, composed in several languages, Siraiki among them. The imagery of the two language areas is closely related, as one would expect among the people of mainly rural provinces, and the mystical literature of Sindh and the Punjab is closely alike, down to the motif of spinning and weaving "in a cotton-growing country". On the road between Bahawalpur and Uch "the cultures of Punjab and Sindh merge": a Punjab Hotel stands beside a Sindh Hotel, Siraiki is a blend of the two languages, and the Siraiki of Uch is closer to Sindhi.

The province's Hindus shared in this poetry. By one account the Bhakti, the devotional movement of Hindu India, reached Sindh only in the sixteenth century, because of the province's isolation. One scholar called Shah Abdul Latif and Sachal "the late participaters in the Bhakti movement which had a major impact in Sindh through the spread of Guru Nanak's teaching". The *slokas*, short verses, of Sami were probably widespread among the Hindus of Northern Sindh.

Sachal Sarmast had many followers among the Hindu trading castes of Northern Sindh, and some of them took their love of singing his poetry to India when they migrated after Partition. For the Sindhis who left, singing Sindhi Sufi poetry keeps "a link, through language and music, with Sindh as a fatherland".

Above this tradition stands one book, the *Risalo* of Shah Abdul Latif. By 1940 it was called "the only classic which the language of Sind has yet produced in the realm of deeply imaginative literature", its poems held in universal and popular esteem and known in some form to all classes. Today it "is universally acknowledged to be the greatest classic of Sindhi literature in both Sindh itself and other parts of Pakistan, as well as among the Sindhi émigré population in India and the wider diaspora".

Hindus love it as much as Muslims do. The poetry "exercises over Hindus in Sind a spell almost as complete as that exercised by it over Muslims", and its appeal lies "deep in the hearts of all classes of Sind's population, Muslim and Hindu, lettered and unlettered". The poet's achievement came when Sindh was still medieval in outlook, and in 1940 it could be said that "the probabilities are all against Sind's producing a second Shah Abdul Latif", though his poems "still bind Sind with a powerful spell of love and admiration". His poetry is "the only gem cast up from the sea of Sind's vernacular literature".

The poem's place has been explained by the province's history. The cultural heritage of Sindh came to fruit in Shah Abdul Latif's poetry just as the Mughal Empire was showing clear signs of breaking up. On this reading the province had two peculiar circumstances: most of its people had come late to Islam, and there was no large body of native literature in Sindhi, because its learned class came mostly from the privileged, many of them immigrants rather than people of the land; accounts differ, though, on how much Sindhi literature came before Shah Abdul Latif. Under Muslim rule from the fourteenth century on, the thought and religion of the Hindus found little place in the literary forms of the day, while the Sufi strain of thought has always appealed to Hindus in Sindh.

The stories of the poem have been read as Hindu in origin. Apart from the tales of the Punjab and Kutch, they are, on this reading, stories of Lower Sindh told among the Hindu peoples on the edges of the Thar before their general conversion to Islam. The Hindus of Sindh in 1940 felt that their culture, no less than that of Islam, went into the making of the poem.

Accounts of when the stories are set differ; the heroines' stories are loosely set in the time before the Mughals, when Sindh was ruled by the Rajput dynasties of the Sumra and the Samma, and other *surs*, the poem's chapters, are built on male heroes "from the heroic Rajput period of Sindhi history". These legends give the province a heroic past. Lakho Phulani was "a legendary bandit from the heroic age of Sindh, who is remembered for his fearless attacks on the ruling Jarejo Samos", and Jakhiro "a Rajput ruler of medieval Sindh famous for his bravery and generosity", the verses on whom are read as a long praise of the Prophet. In a famous episode of these legends, Jakhiro resisted the invasion of Sultan Alauddin, the Muslim emperor of Delhi, who died in 1316, and took under his protection the women of his fellow Rajputs, the Sumra.

The great tales of the province are love stories, and its poets made them teach. The mystical poets of Sindh and the Punjab developed "the art of utilizing simple folk tales as media for mystical instruction", and in Sindh the range of tales used in this way is even wider. The first hints appear, veiled, in the verse of Qadi Qadan in the first half of the sixteenth century, grow clearer in the short verses of Shah Abdul Karim of Bulri, and come to full bloom in the eighteenth century in the *Risalo* of his great grandson Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit. Since everyone knew the tales, the poet usually begins his telling at the most dramatic moment.

The tales have their own history. The story of Lila and Chanesar goes back to the time of Jam Chanesar, one of the Samma rulers of Sindh in the fourteenth century, and the tale of Mumal and Rano began in Lower Sindh some time in the fifteenth. "Sohni is one of the favorite folk tales in both Sind and the Panjab", and the Sohni myth is sung in Sufi poetry in every region of Sindh and in the Siraiki speaking area of the Punjab.

"Sassi-Punnoo is a story of old Sind." Sassi belonged to Bhambore, an old city, and Punnoo was a Baluch chief, and the romances of Sohni and Sassi travelled beyond their first setting to become popular in both the old Punjab and Sindh. The Sassi myth is told in the lands of Sindh and Baluchistan, sung in the oral traditions with all the feeling of her grief, and performers love to sing it in the languages and dialects of Sindh, Baluchistan and the Siraiki speaking regions. Sassi's run through the desert at sunset, in Shah Abdul Latif's verse, is "a scene which can be appreciated by those who know the glowing evenings at the western border of Sind, when all of a sudden, darkness overtakes the wayfarer, and nothing is left but the sounds of a few animals".

The *Risalo* makes a world out of Sindh. It "draws upon a wide-ranging set of interlocking references to the scenery, society, and legends of Sindh to create a whole new imaginative world". In its twenty second *sur*, *Sarang*, the monsoon rains are a sign of divine power and grace, in Sindh, where they transform the normally dry countryside, and across the rest of the world. The cloud of mercy rises over the desert of Eastern Sindh, the Thar Parkar, and is seen floating from Africa to China.

After naming the places the rains reach, ending "Some have passed over Umarkot and have made the ground green", the verse asks God to make Sindh flourish forever and closes: "Dear friend and sweet beloved, make the whole world prosperous" (translated by Christopher Shackle). Coming closer to his home, the poet prays, in another rendering: "My Lord, be kind over dear Sind, / Friend! Sweet darling! You may make the whole world happy!" This last blessing on Sindh is one of the most often quoted verses of the *Risalo*.

The countryside fills the poem. In the eighteenth *sur*, *Ramakali*, the yogi's horn is praised: "There is nothing like it in the north, in Sindh, or in Hind", Hind meaning the Gangetic region, as against Sindh and the Indus valley. The crane, *kūṉja*, comes to Sindh as a winter migrant, and in the *sur* *Dahar* its plaintive cry is heard as the pain of separation from the flock. Few countries are as rich as Sindh in bird life, and there the crow, *kang*, is a bringer of good news, the typical messenger bird of the province as the pigeon was in high Persian poetry.

The poem is also a picture of a time. The spinning of cotton, one of the most important occupations of women, is a frequent theme of the poetry of Sindh and the Punjab. Shah Abdul Latif "offers in his verse a vivid picture of the conditions of the Sind countryside during the early 18th century". The image of the swan must have been popular in Sindh for a long time, and Lila's swinging bed recalls the coloured lacquer work beds that are still a speciality of the province.

Other habits mark the province's verse. Sindhi girls who never learned what a pen looks like and knew not a single letter still complain, in song, that no letter has come from their friends; the complaint is general among the Sindhis, common in wedding songs, and in the folk poetry of Sindh the heroines always make it. In the Golden Alphabets, poems that run through the letters in order, words with the same letters were joined in one verse, since alliteration was a favourite device, particularly in Sindh. The mystical bards of Sindh and the Punjab knew the tradition of *Ahmad bila mim*, Ahmad without the letter *m*.

An Arabic saying was very popular in eighteenth century Sindh: "Affliction is for saintliness the same as are flames for the gold". Even the poetry of Shah Abdul Latif, in the remote province of Sindh, calls the reader to admire Iblis, Satan, as the one true lover.

The Prophet is a theme of the province's poets. Poets in the plains of Sindh and the Punjab wrote tender little poems or instructive Golden Alphabets to fill the villagers with love for him, and "The great mystical poets of Sind have devoted wonderful songs to the Prophet, who appears as the dawn between the night of created human life and the Divine day". *Virahini* poetry, in which the devotee is a young bride waiting for her bridegroom, who is the Prophet Muhammad, is sung in Sindhi devotional settings such as the *mauluds*, gatherings that celebrate the Prophet's birth.

The poet names the province in lament as well as in prayer. In the lament of Marui he says that Marui is no more in Malir, nor Hamir; that "Punhun is no longer in Kech", nor is there an Amir in Sindh; and that there is no Pir in the convents. The singer Alan Faqir recited the line "Punnu is not in the Kec / Sind is not the amir", and read it politically: Sindh does not lead the land, it is not in the position of leadership or amirship.

Under British rule the poem gained a scholarship and a place on the syllabus. In the days of the Kalhora and the Talpurs the poems lived as sung verse, and only after the settled days of British rule was there born "that type of literary curiosity which calls forth books of comment and criticism". Writing on the poet grew largely out of English education in the eighty years before 1940, Trumpp working on it when Bartle Frere was Commissioner in Sindh, and the great growth of general education under British rule spurred the study of the poems.

After 1843 Persian was quite quickly replaced as the language of schooling, government and elite literature by English and by Sindhi, whose growth the colonial authorities in Bombay actively encouraged. The *Risalo*, a classic that had always appealed to every section of the Sindhi people, "was pressed into service for the examinations prescribed by the new education syllabus". Because Sindh was governed from Bombay rather than Calcutta, Urdu was not installed as Persian's immediate successor. Of Sindhi's many dialects, that of Hyderabad became the standard, it is said, when in 1853 the British asked the scholars of Hyderabad to write the textbooks for the whole of Sindh. Colonial Sindh also saw an attempt to impose a script of its own, the Khudawadi.

Print and the colonial state remade this heritage. A printing press was brought to Karachi in the 1850s, but the great event for Sufism was the printing of the *Shah jo Risalo* in 1866, followed in the 1890s by a rationalisation of the poetry begun by Hindu men of letters. Hindu Sindhis led the publishing of Sufi books in the late nineteenth century, and the Hindus of Sindh played a key part in handing down the Sufi legacy up to Partition.

For most Sindhi intellectuals before Partition, Hindu or Muslim, the Theosophical Society, a modern religious movement, gave the frame in which the Sufi legacy of Sindh was read anew. In the late nineteenth century that legacy was built on the *wujudi* teaching, recast in Theosophical terms through its likeness to *advaita vedanta*, the Hindu teaching that reality is one. The lectures of the Reverend Bambridge in Karachi led to a revival of religious and theological studies in Sindh, and Annie Besant was known in the province as Basanti Devi.

Shah Abdul Latif stands at the centre of the province's modern image of itself. Accounts of his dates differ; he is most often dated 1689 to 1752. Sindhi nationalist narratives are built around the "Sindhi language, the poetry of [national poet] Shah Abdul Latif, the Indus, the peasant or hari, and above all, Sufism and mysticism", with the link to Mohenjo-Daro besides. G. M. Syed is held chiefly responsible for making Shah Abdul Latif the most important poet of Sindh.

A Sindhi writer, Abdul Qadir Mangi, put it plainly: "Every Sindhi, of whatever faith he may be, believes in the greatness of Bhitai. From a Kohli Bheel to a Syed, every one considers Bhitai his spiritual guide". Sindhi nationalists see Shah Abdul Latif as the ideal Sindhi, the perfect example of the province's tolerant and mixed history, while more orthodox writers try to explain away his journey as the recklessness of youth.

Music was everywhere, and it came early. The *Ain-i-Akbari* describes the music of Sindh as *kami*, amatory. Whatever the reason the Chishti order spread so little in the province, "music appeared very early in the Sufism of Sindh", and Shah Abdul Latif's poetry shows how much music mattered to him and to the region, and the role of the musician in its society. The Naqshbandis stand apart: most Naqshbandis in Sindh are strongly opposed to music and to the singing of devotional songs.

"Most of the musicians in Sind have been Hindus and not Mussulmans", one reason, it has been thought, why the poetry holds so strong a spell over those of the land who are not Muslims. There is nothing distinctive in the music of Sindh, whose instruments are chiefly the *ektar*, the sitar, the *sarangi*, the *tambur* and the pipes and drums used elsewhere in India, yet "Sind has long had a reputation for proficiency in musical execution prevailing amongst all classes". As one observer put it, "The Sindians are excessively fond of singing".

The poetry lives in its singing. In remote corners of Sindh illiterate folk musicians repeat the name of the mystic Hallaj in ecstatic songs and claim "that he was the true lover who paid with his life for his love". The moving mystical songs of Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit and the ardent poems of Sachal Sarmast, who died in 1826, were in the 1970s almost as popular as they had been a hundred and fifty years before. "Even to this day, most Punjabis and Sindhis are more likely to have heard a kafi than read one", the *kafi* being a short sung lyric.

Shah Abdul Latif and other Sufi poets of the region composed in the spoken languages and dialects and sang them as roving mendicants within an Islamic frame. "For fear of the clergy mystic poetry sung to music remained confined to the minstrel class." *Sufiana kalam*, Sufi poetry sung to music, is usually sung in the spoken language and is a feature of the shrines of Sindh, especially those of Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar and of Shah Abdul Latif at Bhit. Because large parts of the Quran are quoted in the *Risalo*, musicians in Sindh treat the written book with the utmost veneration and never let it lie on the floor.

"In Sind there can be no ritual without the Manganhar, be it a birth, a wedding, or a death. They are the designated community musicians, and each community has its own Manganhars." The Manganhar are hereditary professional musicians of Sindh and Rajasthan, and a woman musician there is called *mangiari* or *mangti*. The Sidis, of African descent, and the Manganhar sing the ceremonial devotional narratives. There were roving minstrels too in the lands of Sindh and Rajasthan, among them the *jogi* snake charmers and flute players.

The musicians have their own forms. Choir singing in Sindh is called *sung*, and speech and song together create ecstasy, a very special setting of some of the province's shrines. In one school of Sindhi Sufi poetry, also called *sung*, the lover sings to win back the estranged beloved, which is called *manana*. The singer Abida Parvin uses the *dohra* or *bait*, a couplet, within her narrative, a style she learned from her male teachers, and it has been claimed that no other woman musician in Sindh has this style, "which is essentially a unique male style".

Women sing too, often for women. In the shrines of Sindh women singers and musicians are sometimes called the *faqiriani*. The Sidi, Mohana and Manganhar women attached to the landed households of Sindh sing devotional Sufi poetry at their mentor's shrine only on special occasions such as his *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of a saint's death; there they perform only before women, and their women patrons pay them well. In the Sayyid households of Sindh these musicians sing to women only, and Sidi women give ritual musical service in the province's households. The singer Taj Mastani deals with television stations and with clients from the wealthy landed households of Sindh, who call on her for ritual ceremonies. In the Siraiki belt around Multan, Bahawalpur, Mianwali and northern Sindh, women's voices are again prominent in the Sufi poetry of the oral traditions.

The province belongs to a wider world of song. "The mystical lore of the region that includes Sind, Baluchistan, and the coastal belt of Iran shares myths, melodies, and linguistic codes", among them the famous *Mast Qalandar dhun*, a tune said to be sung all along the lands from Rajasthan through Sindh and Baluchistan to Iran: "Almost every folksinger in the region can sing it". The Indus and the Baluchistan coast, back through Persia, seem to have been a route used for centuries.

The province has its own ceremonial music. *Gic* are songs usually sung by women at births and weddings; *jamalo* is a folk tune and also a dance; *kapati* are the songs of the cotton spinners; *olang* is a drum rhythm played at the bridegroom's home; and *sadmana* is a melody for ceremonial occasions. *Candar* is both a Sindhi melody for happy occasions and the wooden castanets that keep the rhythm in Sindhi folk music. *Haswari* is a ceremonial tune played on drums and bagpipes, which Sindhi musicians say they play for the *pirs*, spiritual masters, and *murshids*, spiritual guides, when they appear at festive ceremonies such as a wedding, and *laon* is a Sindhi wedding song. *Dhammal* is the vigorous ritual dance performed at Sufi shrines in Punjab and Sindh.

## Shrines and the State

For centuries religion and government were bound together in Sindh. Since the time of the Sumra in the thirteenth century "the land has been predominantly Muslim in population and the government had a strong theocratic basis with much virtual power in the hands of the priestly class of Sayids". Saints and rulers were tied to each other from early on: Baha'uddin Zakariya supported Iltutmish's annexation, governors sought the help of Makhdum Jahaniyan, and a ruler of Sindh became the disciple of Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus.

The Kalhora deferred to the religious class, and this "led to the extraordinary reverence for pirs, Sayids and holy men, which may be said to have been the predominant characteristic of eighteenth century Sind". By one estimate the expense of religion took a third of the gross revenue. Under the Kalhora the true Sindhis were exploited and the position of the Hindus grew worse, the only form, it has been judged, that a state so nearly theocratic could have taken.

The saints were both a treasure of the state and an instrument of it. Diwan Gidumal, the minister of the Kalhora princes, offered an invader two bags which, he said, "contain the most valuable of Sind's gifts; they contain the holy dust from the tombs of numerous saints and Pirs of Sind". Accounts differ on which invader it was; in one telling it was Nadir Shah, in 1739. The story is told as a mark of the country's pride in its mystical heritage. In the nineteenth century one writer saw Sufism in Sindh as a political power: *tasawwuf*, Sufism, "under the native governments was as formidable a political engine as most of the secret confraternities recorded in history". Even among the mild Sindhis, he wrote, a noted Pir might once safely order a disciple to murder an enemy, "Yet the native princes encouraged it partly from superstition and partly because the price of every Pir was well known to them".

The law was the law of a Muslim state. "Sind was a Muslim state and in theory justice was administered according to the tenets of the Koran", though in Mughal and Kalhora days every man was to some degree the defender of his own rights and the police were sadly too few. The collective responsibility of the village, as officers described it in the nineteenth century, may well have held in Mughal and Kalhora days too. Apart from cattle stealing and the wildness of the Baluch tribes, crime was comparatively rare, though the country held many expert thieves and equally expert trackers. Trial by ordeal was practised, and under the Talpurs there were no regular civil courts. As the state religion of Sindh came from the Quran, so did its law; Hindus settled their disputes "by panchayats", councils of elders, and the Baluch seemed generally to take the law into their own hands.

Learning, too, lived on religious property. No school in Sindh appears to have owed its founding to any act of an emperor of Delhi. The chief support of the learned came from revenues granted as *inams*, grants of land or revenue, to mosques and to the *maktabs*, elementary schools, attached to them.

Under British rule the *pirs* became landlords in the eyes of the state. British policy, "especially in the Punjab and Sind, was to treat them in essentially the same way that landlords and tribal leaders were treated", meaning the hereditary saints, the *sajjada nishin*, the keepers of the shrines. The cult of holy men is most evident in the countryside, where great landlords and large holdings of land are crucial, especially in Punjab and Sindh, and there "the local pîr represents the local landlord or is at least closely related to him".

Under indirect rule, Sufis were usually content to keep the peace so long as they could continue their religious routines and keep their estates and authority. The exceptions were the two *hurr* rebellions against the British in Sindh, raised by the followers of the Pir Pagaro, a landlord, political leader and hereditary head of a Sufi brotherhood who in the late 1990s still held great authority in Pakistan. The *hurr*, the Free, the loyal and absolutely obedient disciples of the Pir Pagaro, were first gathered to support Ahmad Brelwi's struggle against the Sikhs between 1829 and 1831 and later took an active part in the struggle against the British. "The hurr even played a minor role in the Indian-Pakistani war in 1971", and this was not the first time that mystical leaders in Sindh had taken part in politics or social reform.

The tie between land and shrine reaches the lives of musicians and *faqirs*, holy mendicants. A *vadera* is a landed lord in Sindh, and the singer Alan Faqir said that the *vaderas* quarrel with one another and come to the *faqir* for a prayer. *Saeen* is a title used for a landed lord or a spiritual leader from lower Punjab and Sindh, where the culture of the shrines thrives.

The wealth of the shrines has drawn criticism and reform. Most people continued, and many still continue, to venerate their saints, as hundreds of simple folk songs and popular verses show. The exaggerated veneration of a saint's tomb has been the target of some of the best modern Sindhi short stories, and the gap between the wealth of the shrines and their guardians and the poverty of the people is a powerful theme of social and religious criticism, in Sindh as elsewhere in the Muslim world. In Sindh as in Bengal, the Naqshbandiyya and some more militant groups of mystical inspiration tried to curb the overflowing emotion of the folk songs.

In the present the reformers come from outside the shrines. Tableeghi preachers "focus on southern Punjab and rural Sindh where syncretistic religion held sway", and in recent years the Deoband school has turned its attention to South Punjab and Sindh, "where there is an ubiquitous saint and shrine culture".

Partition separated the keepers from many of their followers. "The darbars in Sindh were most of the time preserved with different arrangements regarding their management", often with *gaddi nashins*, the seated heads of a line, or followers in India paying the keepers, and despite the changing relations between Pakistan and India it was always possible to cross the border. Yet Molchand worried after Partition that it had become hard for Hindu Sindhi Sufis to visit the Sufi places of Sindh, now in Pakistan. In Sindh the groups of Sufis and Nanakpanthis, followers of Guru Nanak's path, had no central body ruling over all their followers.

The Pakistani state has shaped which saints the province celebrates. The state's Sindhi publications hardly mentioned the saint or the shrine of Sehwan until the late 1960s, while "other saints of Sindh (especially Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai, Sachal Sarmast, and Shah Inayat) were regularly featured during this time, and their annual fairs were marked by special editions". Only from the 1960s onward did Urdu and Sindhi newspapers report the annual fair of Sehwan in detail. The publications were two prominent state journals, *Nayin Zindagi*, published by the Information Department of Sindh, and *Mehran*, published by the Sindhi Adabi Board. Today state bodies such as the Department of Culture and the Sindhi Adabi Board routinely publish books and brochures on the life of the saint of Sehwan and on Sehwan's history and heritage.

The Sufi Shaykh Wahid Bakhsh, who died in 1995, warned against nationalist slogans, among them "Sindhu Desh", the country of the ethnic Sindhis: "They do not realize that four smaller countries, which they wish to create, will be swallowed by India overnight". In his view the divisions of ethnic rivalry at home were as destructive as any enemy from outside.

The modern state also shows in representation and money. The Sindh province "has 9 minority seats out of 168". Government money for the places of minority communities shows in a wave of temple restorations across Sindh, many temples having been renovated within a decade, at the same time as changes in the political landscape. Violence against a temple could be settled outside the state: in one of the rumours about the attack on a temple at Larkana in March 2014, "According to tradition in Sindh, the culprits took the Quran and asked the Hindu family for remission". The instability of the country and "the feudal environment in Sindh" are thought to have kept scholars away from the province.

Some see a growing threat. The Hindus of Pakistan, Sindh included, are, it seems, more and more under threat from the country's radical Muslims, and Islamist terrorism has spread in Sindh as elsewhere in Pakistan; yet "the Sufi culture still pervades Sindhi society". Sufism does not of itself mean tolerance. It has been argued that "the most active people working for the conversion of young Hindu girls to Islam are well-known Sufi pirs", and "the Sufi dargahs are now targets of Islamist terrorism", a *dargah* being a saint's shrine.

## The Province in Books

Under the Mughals, at the end of the seventeenth century, the province was the *suba*, or imperial province, of Thatta. It measured two hundred and fifty *kos*, a measure of distance, in length, from Bhakkar to Kech Makran, and one hundred *kos* in breadth, from the town of Badin to the port of Lahari. It was bounded on the east by Gujarat Ahmadabad, on the west by Kech Makran, on the north by Bhakkar and on the south by the salt sea. Four *sarkars*, districts, made up the *suba*, Thatta, Siwistan, Nasirpur and Umarkot, with fifty seven *mahals*, revenue divisions, and five ports. In this country a third share was taken from the cultivator.

The province lay at the edge of Hindustan. The realm of Hindustan then adjoined Bengal to the east and the Deccan to the south, and on the west Thatta reached the salt sea. Kutch was a separate country, two hundred and fifty *kos* in length and breadth, with Sindh to its west. The *wilayat*, or country, of Sindh known as Thatta had, like Gujarat Ahmadabad, passed at various times out of the hands of Delhi's rulers and come under an independent ruler of its own, and a history of those rulers, the *Tarikh-i Bahadurshahi*, covered them both.

The province lived on its river. The greatest river was the Sindh. Merchants from Multan and Bhakkar shipped their goods down it to Thatta, travellers and even heavy armies went by water, and large armies seldom went by land because of the jungle and the want of water. Most things turned on the boat, and there must have been forty thousand boats, large and small.

Salt and iron mines were plentiful, and there was a quarry of yellow stone that could be cut to any size for building. There were wild ass, hare, hog deer, pig and fish, and the people lived on rice, curds and fish. Dried fish was shipped to the ports, and one fish came up from the salt sea into the Sindh, was caught in nets, and was found in no other country. Curds kept for four months, there were flowers and many fruits, the mango above all, and small melons grew in the desert.

Much was said of the province's liver eaters, the *jigar khwara*, more famous here than anywhere around. By a spell, it was said, they took the livers of people, especially of children and especially at mealtimes; some said a trance came over them; they shared the liver with their fellows, and the victim died. They made others like themselves by feeding them a piece and teaching them the spell, and they rode the hyena and brought news from far away. When one was caught, those who knew the art cut open the calf of the leg and took out a seed like a pomegranate seed, which was fed to the victim, who recovered.

A liver eater, it was said, would not sink with a millstone tied to the neck, nor burn in fire. To cure one, the experts branded both temples, filled the eyes with salt, hung the person up in a house for forty days, gave food without salt and recited spells. Most of them were women, some were men, and they were found in every land, but more in the lands of Thatta.

In 1940 a book on Shah Abdul Latif was dedicated "TO THE PEOPLE OF SIND" with a verse on a peace that followed an age of war: "The drums of peacefulness are struck", runs one line, and the next, "Your land a full abundance yields". To its author, eighteenth century Sindh was a survival of an older world. When the poet's verses were composed, "Islam in Sind preserved the true native form of its characteristic power", and the ideas of popular thought went much deeper into the Muslim mind than in the Sindh of 1940, when that rule over the land had been lost. The Muslim India of the Middle Ages, "of which eighteenth century Sind was a relic", recalled medieval Christian Europe in some of its features.

The Sindh from which the poetry sprang was, in this view, more like a "Merrie England" of villages that sufficed for themselves: "It was a society of landholders, petty cultivators, herdsmen, craftsmen and traders, knowing little of the outside world except what could be seen of it at markets and fairs, in the gatherings of strangers at tombs on saints' days or in the company of fellow pilgrims". In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries Sindh "was a land of cultivators and herdsmen, keepers of camels, buffaloes, sheep, goats and oxen, of tombs and Sayids, of dust and heat, of the annual rise and fall of the waters of the Indus but inefficiently controlled by a crude system of irrigation". Of the poem's rustic scenes, "No one familiar with Sind will dispute how true a portrayal this is of a simple rustic civilization of husbandmen and cattle herds", and the poems are loved by all classes in Sindh.

The same author argued with the nineteenth century writers, holding that "many of the criticisms of Victorian writers are unjust". Burton despised Sindh for what he thought its lax Islam, and his view that Sindh came into British hands almost exhausted cannot be accepted "without considerable qualification". Against such critics stands the assistant surgeon Heddle, in May 1836: "The large masses of the indigenous population are particularly industrious", the merchants of Sindh are active and intelligent, and "The people are orderly and obedient and the laws are respected". "The peoples of Sind, whether indigenous or immigrant, have never shown any military genius", ran the verdict of 1940, and "The people as a whole have always been peaceful and industrious". Lieutenant James, on the other hand, reporting in 1847 on the Chandookah *pargana*, a subdivision, saw Sindh as one long battlefield, the scene of usurpation, tyranny and bloodshed, "till finally the British bayonet gave the worn-out country peace".

The word itself is not always the province. Baluch poetry speaks often of Sindh, but the word there means not the modern political division of that name but the valley of the Indus, though some places it names are indeed Sindhi places in the country south of Multan that runs far down the river towards the sea. One Baluch bard sings that the drunkards "are the young men of the Sindh country", since "there is much water and bhang is cheap". Bayezid's master is said to have been Abu ʿAli as-Sindi, "from Sind", but the name may point to the lower Indus valley or to a village called Sind close to Bistam; and even in the first case, "not every man from Sind could be expected to know all the intricacies of Hindu monistic philosophy".

Above all, Sindh has been seen as a mystical country. The Arabs conquered it in 711, "and this province never ceased to be a center of Islamic studies and a seat of Islamic mysticism". "Sind has always been praised as the country with a particularly strong inclination towards mysticism", and a Sindhi author, J. Parsram, wrote: "Sind has been flattened and has been in a state of negation. This is the cause of the many disadvantages from which it suffers; but it has been also the cause of a great blessing", the blessing being the mystical tradition, something of which India is in need. The country had always been proud of its mystical heritage.

One portrait of the province ends in its light: "the villages in Sind are covered with a veil of golden dust; the walls, built of unbaked clay, look like bronze, and the blue and white tiles which cover a lonely saint's tomb match the purity of the blue sky", and the ways of Dard and Shah Abdul Latif are "as different as are the sunsets in their native provinces". Other voices were harsher. Trumpp, introducing the *sur* *Sorathi*, wrote of "how much even the strong monotheistic deism of Islam has become modified and corrupted in Sind, as in other parts of India, by its contact with Hinduism", and the province's veneration of Sayyids and its growth of superstitious practice have also drawn censure.

Later writers of the province saw it as a meeting of civilisations. Jethmal Parsram, in *Sind and its Sufis* of 1924, summed up the many religions that had flourished in Sindh, Buddhism, Jainism and Hinduism, to which Sikhism and Sufism had more recently been added, and wrote that "there is hardly a country in the whole of Asia, including India, in which the mystic thought of two great civilizations, the Indian and the Arabic-Iranian, is seen as in so beautiful a union as in Sind". Ram Panjwani was already sure, while still in Sindh, "that Sufism was the core of Sindhi culture, a culture shared by all creeds and social milieus". He set out to acquaint the Sindhis "with their past and the glorious cultural heritage of Sind which was inhabited by their forefathers", his Sindh was "a land of the mystic poets, who are humanists as well", and by the religious culture of Sindh he meant the spirit of Sufism.

Sindh, it has been argued, "hosted a specific shared religious culture resulting from a process of vernacularization", though some of its features are found in neighbouring provinces too. Its Sufi culture was the most shared product of a society that was still highly fragmented. One explanation finds the cause in Sindh's place as a frontier, with a constant flow of migrants, far from the main imperial centres, and as both an isolated land and a buffer zone Sindh "has hosted many unorthodox versions of normative religions, especially Hinduism and Islam". British rule had a double effect: it made Sufism into an object, "enthroning it as the fundamental of Sindhi culture at large", as in most regions of India, and it renewed religion through a mixed middle class. Yet for centuries the province had drawn a kind of religion of its own based on devotion, which put faith beyond formal belonging to any one institutional religion.

The same idea has a nationalist form. In nationalist writing "on both sides of the border", Sindh is presented "as a distinct entity lacking any cultural links to other provinces or the state of Pakistan generally". K. R. Malkani, a Sindhi Hindu who left Hyderabad for New Delhi after Partition, wrote: "Sindh has always been there, Pakistan is a passing show", and "A Sufi Sindh and an Islamic Pakistan cannot coexist". Nationalist writers also blame British rule for wronging Sindh. Hussain Soomro writes that the province was once so prosperous that it was said to flow with milk and honey, and in such narratives Pakistan and the British become "the culprits in the case of lost harmony and affluence in Sindh". Sindhi nationalism ties Hinglaj itself to a real Sindh that prospered before outsiders began to suppress it, and makes the shrine a hallmark of the province's golden past.

The nationalist case joins the province to Sufism. G. M. Syed, a politician who spent many years in prison for his separatist views, "directly linked the province of Sindh with a tradition of Sufism", found Sufism in its purest form there, and took as proof the shrines of Sufi saints that are "to this day, visited by Hindus and Muslims alike". In this reading, "both Sindhi Hindus and Muslims were influenced by mysticism and religious tolerance. This was the Sindhi essence that both Hindus and Muslims shared". Ishtiaq Ansari ties Mohenjo-Daro directly to today's Sindhis, claims that the Veda was composed on the shores of the Indus, and holds that Shiva and the Mother Goddess "were already known and honored in the ancient civilization of Sindh". Badar Abro defines the real Sindh, *asal sindh*, as the valleys between Rohri and eastern Baluchistan where the Sindhis once lived in prosperity, and argues that because Sindh stayed under the sway of Sufi mysticism the area is, in his English word, secular, so that its religious places belong to the shared heritage of that real Sindh. Khadim Soomro writes: "A Sindhi has a special place in human civilization because he stands for good and liberal attitude and behavior".

This picture has its limits. The common belief among Sindhis today "that in the history of Sindh, there is no religious tension between the two communities" has been called "merely a nationalist utopian ideal". The fifteenth *sur* of the *Risalo*, *Marui*, allows a nativist reading that exalts the authenticity of the values and landscape of Sindh, but a caution has been sounded against seeing Shah Abdul Latif "too narrowly" as "an authentic spokesman of the Sindhi folk tradition or as an advocate of Sindhi nationalism".

The province also belongs to a larger cultural region: "the Sind-Rajasthan continuum", and the lands that include Sindh, Baluchistan and the coastal belt of Iran, to which the Sassi myth belongs. The singer Alan Faqir spoke of Sindh as a land invaded again and again, when the Mongols, the Tartars and the Sassanids came, looted, plundered and assaulted, which yet goes on and will not die: "Sindhri is beaten", he said, and yet Sindhri goes on, Sindhri being a name for Sindh in the poetic traditions. From Manora one looks out at "the Makran mountain range, the boundary line between Sindh and Baluchistan".
