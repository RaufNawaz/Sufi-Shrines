## Overview

Umerkot is a town of Sindh whose area was contested ground. It was fought over by Rajputs, clans of a warrior caste, and stood at the centre of conflicts of local, regional and central interests, and its fortress is where an emperor halted after crossing the desert and where Prince Muhammad Akbar was born. Between Mirpur Khas and Umerkot lies the hamlet of Pithoro, with the tomb of the saint Pithoro Pir, *pir* being a title of a Sufi spiritual guide. The country around is home to the Menghwar, and pilgrims from a village near the town have walked to the Hindu temple of Hinglaj.

## History

The area of Umerkot was a place of rough combat between two groups of Rajputs, the Somras and the Sodhas. Although power officially changed hands in 1351, the Somras kept their control of certain outlying areas of Sindh, and the area of Umerkot was one of them. A chief named Parmar Sodha is thought to have been established as king, or *rano*, at Umerkot.

The fortress also received an emperor. After crossing the desert, the emperor's party came after thousands of hardships and toil to the fortress of Umerkot, and Rana Parshad, its ruler, counted their arrival his own good fortune and performed the duties of service. There, on the fifth of Rajab, the seventh month of the Islamic year, Prince Muhammad Akbar was born.

His mother was Hamida Banu Begum, whose descent was traced to Ahmad Jam, and whom the emperor had married after reaching the neighbourhood of Thatta. The astrologers foretold a long reign and a long life for the child. The emperor stayed some while in that land and then left, meaning to go to Qandahar and to leave the women of his household there.

## Communities

The Menghwar, an important group among those held as untouchable, outside and below the Hindu caste order, are mostly settled around Umerkot. Part of their devotional literature was, and still is, in Sindhi. The Rajput lineages of the Somras and the Sodhas belong to the same ground. The gazetteer of 1907, a descriptive handbook of the region, described the people of Parmar Sodha as worshippers of Shiva.

Other peoples lived in the country around. In Aurangzeb's time, the stretch from Bhakkar to Nasirpur and Umerkot, described with the province, or *suba*, of Thatta and its four *sarkars*, or districts, was home to the Jarija and others.

## Holy Places and Pilgrimage

The tomb of Pithoro Pir stands in the hamlet of Pithoro, between Mirpur Khas and Umerkot. The hamlet lies on the railway line which formerly connected Hyderabad, in Sindh, to Barmer and Jodhpur, in Rajasthan. As with many other saints, the site of the sanctuary bears the saint's name.

Devotion also went out from the district on foot. A group of pilgrims from a village near Umerkot covered around 600 kilometres on foot to Hinglaj, the temple of the goddess Hinglaj Devi.

## Umerkot in Books

The town's name is also written Umarkot and Omarkot. To a Persian chronicle of Aurangzeb's reign, which calls it Amarkot, Umerkot is a fortress on a royal journey, the birthplace of a prince, and a place in the description of the province of Thatta. To a modern study of the Hindu Sufis of South Asia, it is the setting of Pithoro Pir's tomb in a country of Rajput rivalries, and the home of a community whose devotional literature is partly in Sindhi. To a study of the temple of Hinglaj Devi, it is the home district of pilgrims who walked there.

The town also belongs to romance. In the Mumal myth, Umerkot is the seat of the vizier Rano. Omar of Omarkot, too, is a figure of romance, not a saint.
