## Overview

Balochistan is a dry land of desert, mountains and coast. In the mid 1960s a traveller could go by train from Iran across the Baluchistan desert and the Sulaiman Mountains, down through the Bolan Pass to the green strip of the Indus Valley. It was one of the roads by which Sufi mystics came into the region, and it holds Hinglaj, a centre of pilgrimage in the part of the province where Sindhi is spoken.

Its history is bound up with its neighbours. Muhammad bin Qasim began his advance through Makran and Baluchistan in 710, the offspring of men from its hills came to sit on the throne of Sindh, and in Mughal times Rustam Mirza held Multan and Baluchistan together as his grant. Since the establishment of Pakistan it has been a province in a state of latent insurgency, and in the 2010s Indian pilgrims needed an additional visa to travel there.

It shares myths, melodies and linguistic codes with Sindh and the coastal belt of Iran, and the tale of Sassi is told in Sindh and Balochistan alike. It is one of the regions where Pakistan's Hindus live, and Sidi communities, who hold a drum sacred, and the Brahui live there too.

## The Province in History

In 710 Muhammad bin Qasim al-Saqafi began his advance through Makran and Baluchistan, and between 711 and 712 he conquered Sindh.

Sindh, its neighbour, had its own trouble with dynasties that did not last, with civil wars and with the depredations of hill hordes from Balochistan. Mian Mir Mahomed, the first prince of the Kalhoro dynasty, sent to the Baluchi country and induced two of his *murids*, his pledged disciples, men of the mountains, to leave their barren hills and settle in the low country, a step one history of Sindh called "the fatal mistake". In time, with the rise of the Talpurs, the offspring of "mendicants and shepherds" from the hills of Balochistan sat one after another upon the throne of Sindh.

In Mughal times Baluchistan had chiefs of its own. During the emperor's campaign against Qandahar, among the orders of the Khankhanan, the khan of khans, was that it would be better if the chiefs of Baluchistan showed the submission and obedience required of them and joined in the campaign. Rustam Mirza held as his *jagir*, a grant of land revenue, the *wilayat*, or territory, of Multan and Baluchistan, which yielded more than the revenue of Qandahar.

Since the establishment of Pakistan, Balochistan has been a province in a state of latent insurgency.

## Land and Routes

Balochistan is a land of hard seasons. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries the camel *kafilas*, the caravans that went from Bakhar to Afghanistan and Persia, kept regular seasons for travel, which avoided the worst of the hot weather and the inundation in Upper Sindh and the intense winter cold of the highlands of Balochistan and Persia. Its pastoral tribes move between the plains in the wet winter season and the mountains in the dry season, unlike the pastoralists of the western Punjab, who stayed on the plains.

One historian remembered his first journey to the Subcontinent, in the mid 1960s, "travelling by train from Iran across the Baluchistan desert and Sulaiman Mountains, down through the Bolan Pass until the fertile, green strip of the Indus Valley hove into view". A route from the Indus along the coast of Balochistan and back through Persia seems to have been used for centuries.

Rain is scarce. Because the average rainfall is low, the mud of the province's mud cones is not subject to rapid erosion, and the cones last. Unlike many other rivers in Balochistan, the Hingol flows throughout the year. Travellers of the past came through a dry and barren landscape, and in the season of the spring gatherings the heat is extreme.

Its edges meet Sindh. From Manora one can see the Makran mountain range, the boundary line between Sindh and Balochistan. The Kohistan is the western part of Sindh that stands close to Balochistan, and the village of Fathepur lies in the Kachhi plain about 100 kilometres from Jacobabad, but in the Sindhi speaking part of Balochistan.

In 2001 the first part of the Makran Coastal Highway, the N 10, was completed, and the area around the River Hingol was joined to National Highway 25, a heavily travelled road that links Karachi with Quetta. The highway was meant to boost the economic development of the Balochistan coast, and it reshaped the pilgrimage, which had been a journey of hardship.

## Saints and Pilgrimage

Balochistan was one of the roads of the Sufis. After Muhammad bin Qasim's conquest, a long line of Sufi mystics came to the region through Baluchistan, Mekran, Multan and Sindh itself.

Names tell of devotion too. Names like Ghauth Bakhsh, "gift of the help", are found in Sindh and Balochistan and point to the popularity of Gilani there.

Hinglaj is a centre of pilgrimage in the Sindhi speaking area of Balochistan. The province's shrines have also known violence: a terrible bomb blast at Lahoot la Makan in November 2016, which killed more than fifty people, curbed the growing influx of visitors and created a volatile situation for Balochistan's shrines.

Families will often bring women who are emotionally disturbed to take part in the *dhammāl*, the ecstatic devotional dance, which devotees claim has healing powers. In Balochistan such healing ceremonies are called *guāṭi*, though accounts of the word differ.

## Communities

Balochistan is one of the regions where Pakistan's Hindus live. Most of the non-Muslims who remain in Pakistan are Hindus living in the "faraway" and "peripheral" regions of southern Punjab, inner Sindh and Balochistan, and at the census of 1998 Balochistan was among the provinces across which most of the Hindus were scattered.

Part of the province speaks Sindhi. Both Hinglaj and the village of Fathepur lie in the part of Balochistan where Sindhi is spoken.

The Sidi communities of Sindh, Balochistan, Rajasthan and Gujarat hold the *mugarmān* drum sacred and use it in their shrines and rituals. The Brahui also live in Balochistan; assuming there were matrilineal structures among them, the myth of Sassi expresses the conceptual worldview of a community.

Its people lived beyond its hills. There must, it is reckoned, have been many Mekranis and other people of the southern Baluch country in the Kohistan and Kotri localities, near the residence of a poet of eighteenth century Sindh.

## Language, Poetry and Music

The poetry of Sindh and that of Balochistan have certain elements in common. One student of the poetry of the Baluchis found in it "no ghazals, no artificial arrangements of poems in diwans, none of the pedantry of Persian prosody", that is, no *ghazal*, the lyric of love, and no *diwan*, the collected volume of a poet's verse. He wrote too: "As might be expected in a parched-up land where water is scarce and rain seldom falls, the poets delight in describing the vivid thunderstorms." That poetry is wholly oral, sung by professional minstrels called Doms or Dombs at the gatherings of the clans.

Balochistan belongs to a wider world of song. The mystical lore of the region that includes Sindh, Balochistan and the coastal belt of Iran shares myths, melodies and linguistic codes. The "Mast Qalandar" *dhun*, or melody, is reported to be sung all along the Rajasthan, Sindh, Balochistan and Iran continuum.

The tale of Sassi belongs to the province. It is told in the lands of Sindh and Balochistan and sung in the regional languages and dialects of Sindh, Balochistan and the Siraiki speaking regions. There is evidence of the female power principle in the Sassi myth, which originated in Balochistan and Sindh, and Sassi is said to have been a ruler.

The folk tales of Sindh are set in several parts of the country, and among them are the hill slopes of Balochistan, beside Thar Parkar, the Indus plains and the mouth of the Indus.

## Pilgrimage and the State

For Indian pilgrims Balochistan was hard to reach. In the 2010s they had to obtain an additional visa allowing travel to the province, and access was further limited by legal ambiguity: Non-Objection Certificates had to be obtained from the government, while the legal status of an application for one was unclear. As one writer on the pilgrimage put it, Pakistan "actually does not issue visas for the journey to Balochistan", so that "the journey has no proper legal status and thus is likely to involve bribery and nepotism". Part of the province was a restricted area, the status of visas for the province was legally ambiguous, and there was no solid legal base for the journey into it.

The province also figures in the politics of the nation. In the national elections of October 2002 the MMA gained the upper hand as the largest party in Balochistan. Among the slogans of the nationalist parties, Wahid Bakhsh listed "Azad Balochistan", a free home for the ethnic Baluchis, and their aim, he said, would leave "four smaller countries" to "be swallowed by India overnight".

At the time of a protest in September 2012, a local *maulvi*, a Muslim cleric, might preach a sermon praying for the demise of the United States, Israel and India, which he held responsible for everything wrong in the country, among it a separatist movement in Balochistan.

## Balochistan in Books

To a Mughal chronicler at the end of the seventeenth century, Baluchistan was a *wilayat* that could be given with Multan as a *jagir*, and its chiefs were men to be brought to submission and obedience. He did not call it a *suba*, a province of the empire, and gave it no bounds.

To a writer on eighteenth century Sindh, in 1940, Balochistan was hill country: the home of the hill hordes, of the disciples a Kalhoro prince called down to the low country, of highlands of intense winter cold, and of a poetry that shares certain elements with that of Sindh.

For a scholar of the 1970s, the hill slopes of Balochistan lay within the folk tale country of Sindh. At the turn of the twenty first century it was seen as part of one region of shared lore with Sindh and the coastal belt of Iran.

For a historian who first saw it in the mid 1960s, it was a desert crossed on the way to the Indus. In the 2010s it was described as a land of low rainfall and extreme heat, as a restricted area, and as one of the "faraway" and "peripheral" regions where Pakistan's Hindus live. One writer claims an ancient past in which the "real Sindh" covered "the valleys between Rohri and east Balochistan".
