## Overview

Sehwan Sharif is a town in central Sindh, the *dargah* town of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar. A *dargah* is a Sufi shrine complex, and Sehwan is a very reputed centre of Sufi pilgrimage, whose narrow streets are almost impossible to walk in during the *urs*, the yearly commemoration of a saint's death. Histories also know the town as Siwistan, a fort on the river.

Its shrine is one of the most important Muslim shrines in Sindh. Before partition about half of the town's people were Hindus, and it had two main Hindu temples, one devoted to Shiva and one to Jhulelal. From 1947 almost all of its Hindus migrated to India, where Sehwanis in Bombay still have a community council.

## History

Sehwan is said to be a historic city built within a large fortress from the time of Alexander the Great. Its fort changed hands more than once. Under the Samma rulers, in the time of Jam Nizam al-Din, known as Jam Nanda, Mirza Shah Beg, the ruler of Qandahar, sent Mirza Isa Tarkhan against the Jam and then came himself. He took the fort of Bhakkar, then took the fort of Sehwan as well, and went back to Qandahar.

The fort fell again in the campaign of the Khankhanan, a title meaning khan of khans, against Thatta. His army first conquered the fort of Sehwan and then moved on. Mirza Jani Beg made peace and brought the country of Sehwan into the imperial dominions, and gave his daughter in marriage to Mirza Iraj, the Khankhanan's son.

The fort of Siwistan also stood in the path of Dara's flight to Thatta. On the twelfth of Safar, as the story runs, Saf Shikan Khan reached a point thirteen *kos*, an old measure of distance, from Siwistan. Muhammad Salih, the *faujdar*, or military commander, and keeper of the fort, wrote that Dara had come within five *kos* of the fort and that his treasure boats were following.

Saf Shikan Khan sent a thousand horsemen with musketeers and camel guns, together with diggers and water carriers, to build a battery near the fort at a spot where the river was narrower. The guns fired on the boats, but the distance was too great for them to do any harm, and the boats passed the fort unhindered. Muhammad Salih was judged to have failed in his service: had he blocked the way, Dara would probably have been taken. Dara got past Siwistan, a place of dread and of mortal danger, and reached Thatta.

Siwistan was also a place of flight in another story. A Jalal al-Din, defeated before Lahore, escaped toward Sind and Siwistan.

The town had a place in the history of the Sufis, the Muslim mystics, too. Shaikh ʿArif was posted to Siwistan. Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq appointed Makhdum-i Jahaniyan Shaikhu'l-Islam, the chief religious dignitary, and made him the head of a *khanqah*, a Sufi lodge, in Siwistan, known as the Khanqah-i Muhammadi, with several villages attached to it.

## The Town and Its Country

Siwistan lay on the river. After the fort of Bhakkar, the Indus entered the districts of Thatta by way of the country of Siwistan. The province of Multan ran four hundred *kos* in length, from Firozpur to Siwistan, and the *sarkar*, or district, of Siwistan itself, on the bank of the river Sind, was one of the four *sarkars* of the province of Thatta.

In these parts of the province lay a great lake, two days' journey long. Fishermen lived there, mostly by fishing.

The mountains of Sehwan are a feature of its landscape. The town lies in central Sindh, where Vicholi was spoken, a Sindhi dialect which the British proclaimed the official Sindhi language, and to be born there was different from being born in Hyderabad. It is a small city, in contrast to a big one such as Karachi.

## Holy Places

Sehwan is the *dargah* town of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar, a very reputed centre of Sufi pilgrimage with a strong Sufi ambiance. The shrine of Sehwan Sharif, one of the most important Muslim shrines in Sindh, is held to have once been a Hindu temple dedicated to Lord Shiva. Siwistan also held the Khanqah-i Muhammadi of Makhdum-i Jahaniyan, to which several villages were attached.

Before partition the town also had two main Hindu temples of its own. The first was devoted to Shiva and stood in the northeast of the town; the other was devoted to Jhulelal, a deity revered by Sindhi Hindus, and stood on its northwestern side.

## Communities

Before partition the Hindus made up about half of the town's people. From 1947 onwards almost all of them migrated to India.

Among them were the Thakurs, the priestly caste of Jhulelal, who ran Jhulelal's temple in the northwestern part of the town. In Sehwan they could have been the most powerful Hindu group. All the Thakurs left Sindh to settle in India, especially in Bombay and Ulhasnagar. In Bombay there is still a Sehwani *panchayat*, a community council, which has published several directories of the Sehwanis in India and overseas since partition, and for some time now it has had a Facebook page.

Nandirani Merani was an Amil, a member of one of the Hindu communities of Sindh, from Sehwan Sharif, who translated many books on history, geography and other subjects. Through his family Mulchand was extremely well integrated into the mystical network of Sehwan Sharif, and the Shahi Bazar in Sehwan figures in his story; Sehwan Sharif was where he decided to stay. Paru Chawla was born a Lalwani in Sehwan Sharif in 1932, and despite the strong Sufi ambiance of the city she left at about fifteen years old.

## Festivals, Music and Pilgrimage

The *urs* lasts for more than a week. Women who live around the heavily populated area of the shrine say that during the celebrations it is almost impossible to walk in the narrow streets of the city. Large numbers of women musicians converge on Sehwan to earn a living. The singer Alan Faqir is said to have sung on the buses and trains between Rohri and Sehwan Sharif in his youth. Sehwan also has its *malangs*, wandering dervishes, and a fair whose dates fall in Shawwal, the tenth month of the Islamic calendar.

Sehwan is both a goal and a starting point of journeys. Bedil went to Sehwan Sharif in his teens, where he is said to have been granted spiritual visions. Raj Kumar gave up a journey to Sehwan in order to go instead to the temple of the Hindu goddess Hinglaj Devi, and in 2010 a pilgrim at Lahoot la Makan had set out from Sehwan.

## Sehwan in Books

Writers have seen Sehwan from very different distances. In the Persian chronicle written in Aurangzeb's reign, Sehwan or Siwistan is a fort taken in campaigns, a *sarkar* of the province of Thatta, and a country through which the Indus passes, with no word of any shrine or saint there. In a modern history of Sufism in India it is "Siwistan (Sehwan) in Sind", a place to which Shaikh ʿArif was posted and where a *khanqah* held villages.

Modern writers see a town of pilgrimage. A study of Islamic mysticism speaks of its *malangs*, its fair, a sanctuary of Shiva and a lingam, the emblem under which Shiva is worshipped. A study of women's voices in Sufi ritual, drawing on two informants at Sehwan Sharif, one of them a medical doctor, sees a historic fortress city crowded at the *urs* and full of women musicians.

A modern writer calls its shrine one of the most important Muslim shrines in Sindh, once a Hindu temple dedicated to Shiva. For a study of the Hindu temple of Hinglaj Devi, it is the *dargah* town of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar, a destination one pilgrim gave up for Hinglaj, and the place another pilgrim set out from.

In a study of the Sindhis in India the town appears again and again: as a small city of central Sindh, as a very reputed centre of Sufi pilgrimage, as a town whose Hindus were about half its people before partition, and as the origin of the Thakurs and of the Sehwani *panchayat* of Bombay.
