## Overview

The Punjab, the land of the five rivers, has never been one fixed unit. At the end of the seventeenth century it was the Mughal *suba*, or province, of Lahore, a country of five *doabs*, the tracts of land between two rivers, with the *suba* of Multan beside it; in 1875 it meant all the country from Delhi to Peshawar then attached to Punjab. Today it is Pakistani Punjab, the most prosperous province of Pakistan. Its Siraiki speaking south, with Multan as its capital, was a part of Sindh during the Middle Ages.

Mahmud of Ghazni established Ghaznavid rule over the Punjab, which became the last refuge of the house of Ghazni, and Mongol armies came again and again, bringing plunder, famine and deserted villages. Under the Mughals the Punjab was a prince's *jagir*, a grant of land revenue, and a *suba*. The eighteenth century made it a battlefield of Afghans, Sikhs and Marathas; Ranjit Singh then held the whole region and ruled it for forty years, and after his successors it passed under British rule, which joined the five rivers with canals. In the end the province was divided between India and Pakistan.

The Punjab is first of all a land of saints' tombs. Its Sufi settlement began with the Ghaznavid conquest, when Hujwiri reached Lahore in about 1035. The Chishti order is dominant in Punjab, and by Guru Nanak's day the Qadiris had made the Punjab the main centre of their activities. The province is also the home ground of Guru Nanak's journeys, and today southern Punjab is believed to be the hub of the country's shrine culture.

Hindus and Muslims served the same state, and the village of the old Punjab was regarded as one social unit irrespective of caste or creed. Today the province has a Christian community with a formidable presence, a significant Hindu population still living in its south, and Sikhs, many of them Pathans who came from the tribal areas.

Above all the Punjab is a land of mystical poetry in its own tongue, carried by the *kafi*, the Punjabi Sufi lyric, to people who heard rather than read it, and reaching its height with Bulleh Shah. The love of Hir and Ranjha, sung in the version of Waris Shah wherever Punjabis meet, is the province's own story, and the Punjab is one of the two principal regions of *qawwali*, the Sufi devotional music. Rulers bound themselves to its holy places: the Delhi sultans and the Mughal emperors granted land for the ceremonies of the great shrines, Ranjit Singh attached vast tracts of land to the gurdwaras, the Sikh places of worship, and the British treated the hereditary saints as they treated landlords and tribal leaders.

## History: From the Earliest Times to Babur

The name Punjab has been given to more than one unit. At the end of the seventeenth century it was the Mughal *suba* of Lahore, beside the *suba* of Multan, and in accounts of earlier reigns it was the *wilayat*, or territory, of Punjab. The Siraiki speaking south was a part of Sindh during the Middle Ages.

The province's history begins long before Islam. Tradition holds that after Yudhishthira gave up his throne at the coming of the Kaljug, the dark age of Hindu reckoning, the five Pandava brothers and Draupadi travelled through the lands of Multan and Punjab on their way to the mountain of Badrinath. One tradition holds that Raja Deb Chand of Delhi gave the Punjab in *jagir* to his brother Lohar Chand, who made Lahore his capital, though accounts of the city's beginnings differ.

In the centuries before Guru Nanak the Punjab was the land through which every invader came, from the Ghaznavids, Ghurids and Tughluqs after the beginning of the eleventh century to Timur in 1398. As one retelling of the Guru's life puts it, "Since Punjab in the north was the gateway to India, it bore the brunt of all these invasions". In the *wilayat* of Multan, Islam began in 99 AH, the year of the Islamic calendar, with Muhammad Qasim. The Muslim conquest of northern India extended the Persianate cultural world to the Punjab, where Persian remained the dominant literary language of the ruling elite through the Mughal period and beyond.

The pattern of conquest began in 986, when the Ghaznavid Sultan Sabuktigin defeated the Hindu Shahi raja who controlled the region between Kabul and the north west Punjab. When Sabuktigin and Mahmud of Ghazni attacked the Punjab, Raja Jaipal held throne and crown at Lahore, and after the sack of the city Malik Ayaz became ruler of the Punjab by Mahmud's order. Under Mahmud's successors the province passed between princes. When Masud came to the throne his young son Majdud was given the Punjab as his governorship; after Masud's death Majdud rebelled against the new sultan, Maudud, and died suddenly on the day of Eid during the siege of Lahore.

The Punjab became the dynasty's refuge. The destruction of Ghazni by Ala al-Din Husain of Ghur in 1150 to 1151, followed six years later by the invasion of the Ghuzz, drove a large number of people to the Punjab for safety. When the Ghurids drove the dynasty out of Ghazni, Khusrau Shah set up his government in the Punjab, and his son Khusrau Malik succeeded him on the throne of Lahore. Accounts of how and when his rule ended differ; the Ghaznavid government came to its end with his surrender to Sultan Shihab al-Din Ghori.

In 1175 Mu'izzu'd-Din Muhammad bin Sam seized Multan, establishing Sunni rule over the southern part of the Punjab, and in a rising in the reign of Iltutmish, Taj al-Din Yalduz took Multan, came to Lahore and plundered the Punjab without restraint. The armies of Chingiz Khan's line came next. Sultan Jalal al-Din Khwarazmi crossed the river of Sindh into the Punjab, and after his defeat in November 1221 and Chingiz Khan's march back to the west, Iltutmish consolidated his power in the Punjab.

The Mongols shaped the province for two centuries. Their pressure forced waves of Turkish settlers into the towns of the Punjab, Lahore, Dipalpur and Multan among them, and in the middle of the thirteenth century Balban, to build a defence against Mongol raids, strengthened and peopled Lahore, Multan, Sirhind, Bhatinda and Dipalpur with large garrisons of Turks. In Jalal al-Din Khalji's reign the leader of a Mongol army that had set out for Punjab, a kinsman of Hulagu Khan, made peace, became a Muslim with some other amirs, and was settled with them at Ghiyaspur, which came to be called Mughalpura.

Blow after blow still fell on the province. In the time of Sultan Jalal al-Din Firuz Shah Khalji a Mongol army came into the Punjab while there was famine there, and plunder left villages and towns deserted; after his victory on the Sutlej the sultan resettled the villages that had been left empty and without a lamp. Under Sultan Muhammad Tughluq a Tatar army plundered Dipalpur and Lahore, and Timur's invasion of 1398 to 1399 devastated both the Punjab and Delhi.

Under the Lodis Tatar Khan was governor of Punjab, appointed by Sultan Bahlul Lodi. In the fifteenth century the Punjab enjoyed a peaceful period, as the Afghans helped to resettle it and build its towns, founding many new ones and promoting trade. Multan meanwhile went its own way: its governor declared his independence, and it is said that in the reign of Sultan Ala al-Din the Second of Delhi the *wilayat* passed to the Langah people.

Babur ended that peace. Guru Nanak came back from eastern India about 1520 to find the Punjab plunged into a number of significant political crises. Babur invaded the Punjab a third time in 926 AH, 1520, reaching as far as Sialkot, and Daulat Khan, who had in time become governor of the Punjab, then invited him to invade India. Guru Nanak's *Babur Bani* is a long poem written at the time Babur attacked Punjab, destroying the cities of Eminabad and Lahore on the way. In Babur's reign the *wilayat* of Multan, long lost to the sultans of Delhi, was conquered by Kamran Mirza's good management and sword.

## History: Under the Mughals

Under the Mughal emperors the Punjab was a prince's *jagir* and a *suba*. Humayun's brother Prince Kamran held Lahore and Multan in *jagir*, and the struggles between the Mughals and the Surs passed through the province. On Humayun's return Sultan Sikandar came out of the hills, seized the *parganas*, the revenue districts, of Punjab and began to collect their revenue. Akbar was on his way to Punjab against him when the news of Humayun's death reached him at Kalanaur, and he came to the throne in 963 AH; in the second year of the reign Sikandar was driven out and security returned to the *wilayat* of Punjab.

Muhammad Hakim Mirza crossed the Indus many times to trouble the people of Punjab, and Zain Khan Koka brought the *zamindars*, the landholding chiefs, of the hills to obedience. Raja Todar Mal, the chief *diwan*, confirmed the *wilayat* of Multan to the Baluch as a firm barrier between Khurasan and Hindustan, and in Akbar's empire the Punjab was counted as two provinces, the *subas* of Lahore and Multan.

The reign of Jahangir was a time when the Mughals enjoyed effective authority in the Punjab. In Shah Jahan's reign Prince Dara Shikoh, *jagirdar* of the Punjab, laid out the Chauk Dara Shikoh in Lahore, and when the government of the Punjab was entrusted to him he put up a great many buildings. A Hindu amir served as *diwan*, the chief revenue officer, of the *suba*, and the canal enterprise of the seventeenth century in the Punjab was an exception to the general rule that Mughal public money went to tombs and palaces rather than public works.

The war of succession was fought partly across the province. Dara Shikoh agreed with Shuja that he would prepare a revolt in Punjab while Shuja marched from Bengal, and at Lahore Dara sent letters through the *suba* urging its soldiers to enlist with him. The emperor set out towards Punjab in pursuit of Dara in the rains, against the advice of most of his nobles, and halted at Lahore to put the affairs of the *suba* in order. By the time the Guruship passed to Harkrishan, the Sikh community had spread beyond the confines of the Punjab to places as far as Kabul.

## History: Decline, Sikh Rule, the British and Partition

The eighteenth century was a time of ruin, and the Punjab was its battlefield. The beginning of its misfortunes dates to the reign of Muhammad Shah, when the decay of Mughal administration gave rise to small local tyrannies which lasted into the nineteenth century. Nadir Shah first conquered Sindh and the Punjab before he reached Delhi in 1739, and after his invasion marauding Sikh bands formed themselves into robber baronies and imposed their own system of extortion on the Punjab farmer.

Ahmad Shah Abdali, the Afghan king whose capital was Kandahar, began in 1748 a series of invasions of India through the Punjab, and his treatment of the province delivered its *doabs* into the hands of the Sikh confederacy of the *misls*, the Sikh fighting bands. Mir Muin al-Mulk, popularly known as Meer Manu, was governor of Punjab, constantly harried by the *misls*, and Abdali had agreed a yearly tax that the governor was to pay him for the right of governing Punjab. Waris Shah's lament comes in the epilogue of *Hir and Ranjha*, dated 1180 Hijri: "Of all the wretched Punjab I am most concerned for Kasur".

Bulleh Shah, who died in 1758, lived at a time of great social upheaval in the Punjab, as the declining Mughal authority came under challenge from armed rebellion by the Sikhs and from local notables who set up their own power, and those unsettled conditions are sometimes reflected in his poetry. One of his *kafis* speaks of the disturbed state of Punjabi society in the twelfth century of the Hijri era, the eighteenth century of the common era. Of that new century the poet says:

*"The door of doom and torment has opened, and Panjab is in a bad state. It has been struck by fear of the pit of hell." (translated by Christopher Shackle)*

This critique is only a very small, if vividly expressed, part of his poetry. In these years the Sikhs rose in the Punjab, and in 1758 the Marathas occupied it. When the Sikh period came, a severe famine struck the Punjab.

Ranjit Singh then brought the whole region of the Punjab into his hands, with Kashmir, the Derajat and other lands besides, and ruled it for forty years. He lived from 1780 to 1839 and was crowned Ruler of Punjab in 1801, and in a land ruled for over a thousand years by Afghans, Mughals and others who were not Punjabi he is called the first Punjabi to rule Punjab. He had complete faith in the *majzub* Mastan Shah, a holy man absorbed in divine love, who refused the thousands of rupees he offered.

On the day Ranjit Singh died, it is said, the *majzub* Taj Shah foretold that the kingdom of the Sikhs would last nine years more and that then the Firangis, the Europeans, would be masters of Punjab. His son Kharak Singh took the throne, and after the deaths of Kharak Singh and Kanwar Nau Nihal Singh, Rani Chand Kaur was seated on the *gaddi*, the throne; Maharaja Sher Singh ruled the region for two and a half years. The Sikh state ended under Maharaja Dalip Singh, when a disturbance ran through Multan, the country of Hazara and four hard battles, and then the whole country entered the British dominion. Dalip Singh was the last Maharaja of Punjab.

The British came late to the Punjab, in the middle of the nineteenth century, almost a century after Bengal, which is why English is less rooted here. With their conquest of the 1840s Persian was quite rapidly replaced by Urdu and English as the language of education, administration and elite literature. By the 1880s Lahore was the capital of the *suba* of the Punjab, the seat of its Lieutenant Governor and its courts. Colonel Lawrence held the administration of the Punjab together at the time of the Delhi rising while he kept raising a large army and sending it to Delhi, and the Lahore museum building was put up in 1864, when the government first held an exhibition of the wonders of the Punjab.

The colonial state remade the land. In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries the British connected the five rivers with each other through canals, turning jungle into farmland. Thousands of new villages, called *chaks*, were founded and farming families from all over Punjab were invited to settle, while the former inhabitants, mostly nomads, could not prove their ownership of the land, and it was given to the newcomers.

In the twentieth century three closely related languages, Urdu, Hindi and Punjabi, were current at once, and their differences were used to sharpen the rival cultural identities of Muslims, Hindus and Sikhs. Then the Punjab was divided between India and Pakistan, and a second wave of migration followed. In the south the violence of Partition never reached the heights it did elsewhere.

## Saints and Pilgrimage

The Punjab is first of all a land of saints' tombs. Its Sufi settlement began with the Ghaznavid conquest: when Mahmud of Ghazni annexed the Punjab, many Sufis were prompted to settle there. Khattali ordered his disciple Shaikh Husain Zinjani to move to the Punjab and later sent Hujwiri after him, and Hujwiri reached Lahore in about 1035. Tradition holds that in the time of Sultan Masud of Ghazni, Shaikh Muhammad Ismail Muhaddith Lahori, a Bukhari *sayyid*, a descendant of the Prophet, settled in Lahore, and he is said to have been the first of the callers to Islam to come to the city. It is said, too, that the Bibi Pak Daman, whose shrine is in Lahore, involved the men in spreading Islam among the people of the Punjab.

The family of Baba Farid came from Kabul and migrated to the Punjab in the middle of the twelfth century, and Farid's settling at Ajudhan was the religious side of a very slow change in the Punjab's cities from a Hindu to a Turkish and Islamic orientation. From the fourteenth century through the Mughal period the shrines of the great Sufis of Punjab and Sindh drew pastoral clans, who had not yet taken up farming and were not Muslim, into their ritual, social, economic and political orbit, and the descendants of the Sufis married into the leading families of the clans. As an army major wrote in 1911 of the Muslim saints of the western Punjab, "Thus, all human beings require an intervener between them and God".

The orders spread across the province. The Chishti order spread in Hind and Punjab from Khwaja Muin al-Din Hasan Sijzi of Ajmer and is dominant in Punjab, and in the time of Makhdum-i Jahaniyan the Suhrawardi order spread from Uch to Gujarat, the Punjab and Kashmir. The first of the Qadiri line to come to Punjab was Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus Uchchi Jili Gilani, who is said to have entered Uch in 887 AH, and by Guru Nanak's day the Qadiris had made the Punjab the main centre of their work. The Naushahi order, which began with Hazrat Naushah Ganj Bakhsh, also flourished, its *faqirs*, the wandering Sufis, numbering in the thousands.

The Chishtis rose again in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. Khwaja Sulayman Taunsawi, who died in 1850, made the order strong by founding the academy at Taunsa on the upper Indus and other religious schools throughout the Punjab, and Muhammad 'Aqil and Gul Muhammad Ahmadpuri founded religious schools in the southern Punjab that gave free teaching and lodging to hundreds of students.

The Punjab is also the home ground of Guru Nanak's journeys as Sikh tradition tells them. By the time he returned to Talwandi, it is said, there were very few in the Punjab who did not know of him and his teachings. On his second journey he crossed Rajputana and the south west Punjab until he came to Multan, and his fifth, of 1523 to 1524, covered the towns and regions within Punjab.

Madho Lal Hussein still commands great reverence in Punjab more than four hundred years after his death. In popular belief in the countryside, *pirs*, Sufi masters, and *jogis*, Hindu ascetics, can by the force of their piety make the Moving Finger of Destiny cancel what it has written. Countless shrines to unknown saints stand on the outskirts of Punjabi villages, Khidr is often called Zinda pir, the living saint, in Punjab, and at the shrines of Punjab and Sindh devotees perform the *dhammal*, a vigorous ritual dance.

Today the heart of shrine devotion is placed in the south. In his will Shahidullah Faridi wrote that Wahid Bakhsh should spread the *silsila*, the Sufi lineage, in Bahawalpur only, which has been read as marking out the southern Punjab as Wahid Bakhsh's spiritual domain. Southern Punjab is believed to be the hub of shrine culture in the country, and the southern region, where Uch Sharif lies, holds more traces of religious and cultural syncretism, the blending of traditions, than the northern and central regions.

## Communities

The meeting of the province's faiths has been seen in very different ways. In one view, for over three hundred years in the medieval Punjab Islam and Hinduism lived side by side in a state of constant conflict. Yet Islam took root much more readily in the Punjab and Bengal than in upper India, the region with the deepest traditions of Muslim rule and patronage. In both Bengal and the western wing of Punjab and Sindh the growth of Muslim societies went together with the spread of settled farming, and by the eighteenth century large communities of Muslim peasants had appeared in both the Punjab and Bengal.

The Jats stand at the centre of this story. Between the thirteenth and nineteenth centuries, as the Jat tribesmen of the western Punjab gave up pastoral nomadism for settled farming, they gradually took on an Islamic identity. In Akbar's statistics, of the 186 Punjabi *parganas* whose dominant *zamindars* are known, 82, nearly half, were held by Jat groups. The Jats whom al-Biruni had called low Sudras in the eleventh century had become the dominant farming caste in nearly half of the Punjab, and by the 1650s the word Jat had come to mean almost the same as peasant farmer. The change has been explained by the coming of the Persian wheel, the water wheel turned by animals, which made much of the arid western Punjab able to grow food crops.

Of the Jat and Rajput groups that became Muslim in the medieval period, the great majority claim to have been converted either by Baba Farid or by his contemporary Bahauddin Zakariya, who died in 1263 and whose tomb is in Multan. The Punjabi Muslim clans that claim Baba Farid include the Bhatti, Chhina, Dhudhi, Dogar, Gondal, Hans, Jo'iya, Khokhar, Siyal, Tiwana, Watt, Kharral and Ara'in. Yet the genealogies of the Siyals of Jhang District show that conversion among Punjabi clans was very slow indeed, and probably unconscious as well.

The province's peoples moved across it. Under the Langah sultans Malik Sohrab Dodai came with his tribe from the country of Kech and Makran and was given a *jagir*, and many more Baluch followed, and the rest of the country along the Indus was assigned to them. A few years before 1884 famine fell on Kashmir, and thousands of starving Kashmiris came into the Punjab.

Hindus and Muslims served the same state. Under the Mughals a Hindu *amir*, a nobleman, served as *diwan* of the *suba*, and when Nawab Zakariya Khan Bahadur governed Lahore the brothers Diwan Lakhpat Rai and Jaspat Rai were diwans of the government of the Punjab. When Meer Manu cast the battle between the state and the insurgents as a war between Muslims and Sikhs, there were in fact several Muslims in the Sikh groups and many soldiers who were not Muslim in his own army. Under Sikh rule the Fakir sahibs, pleading with Ranjit Singh for the mosque by the Baoli, the stepwell, told him that nowhere in the mosques of the whole Punjab did the *mullas*, the prayer leaders, give the call to prayer aloud. Shaikh Sulayman Taunsawi urged his followers to join the resistance against Sikh aggression in the Punjab, yet he also worked to keep cordial relations with the Punjabi Hindus.

In other ways the boundaries were porous. Hindus shared in the Muslim orders, more visibly in Sindh than in the Punjab, and some saints were claimed by both communities. In the village life of the old Punjab the whole community was regarded as one social unit regardless of caste or creed, and it was by no means unusual for a Muslim family to depend on Hindu Brahmans to fix a marriage. The *nirguni* tradition of *bhakti*, devotion to God without form, was influential especially in Punjab, where the Sikh tradition grew from it.

The land's people are known by caste and clan. Ranjha is still a well known Jat caste of the old Punjab, and the Gujars, who keep animals and sell their milk, gave their name to towns such as Gujranwala, Gujrat and Gujar Khan. Hir's own people, the Siyal, were powerful landowners of the Punjab and one of the few tribes in the province that allow women to inherit property under customary law.

Village life had its own pattern. The *charkha*, the spinning wheel, was the constant companion of every woman in the rural Punjab, and spinning parties, where young girls competed in spinning skill and in scandalous talk, were a famous institution of the village. A traditional Punjab wedding still keeps the symbolism of marriage by capture, and the *arsi*, a thumb ring set with a mirror, was a favourite ornament of the bride. *Gur*, raw sugar, is a well known ceremonial offering, and rice cooked in ghee and sprinkled with sugar is the usual ceremonial dish.

Reform movements have pressed on the shrine culture of the countryside. In the early twentieth century Muhammad Ilyas, founder of the Tableeghi Jamaat, worked in undivided rural Punjab, urging Muslims to "purify" their religious practices, and Tableeghi preachers focus on southern Punjab and rural Sindh. In recent years the Deobandi school has focused on South Punjab and Sindh, where saints and shrines are everywhere, and organisations of the Ahl-e-Hadith school, opening seminaries in the countryside of southern Punjab, target first the shrines and their guardians, who in their eyes have grown immensely rich feeding off the oppressed classes.

Partition changed who lived beside whom. Local people tend to be more accepting of the practices of other faiths than the migrants who came after the division of the Punjab, and several gurdwaras and temples have been taken over and destroyed by the migrants. Southern Punjab has been historically more tolerant than Central Punjab towards other faiths, which is why a significant Hindu population still lives there. In the Punjab itself Christians have a formidable presence and are the overwhelming majority among the minorities, while Hindus are a smaller community there, and many Hindus in Punjab pass as Muslims or Christians by taking names that are not Hindu, a way of surviving in a hostile place. In most villages Muslims are the landowners, and Christians are confined to menial work and treated as untouchables. Attacks on Christians came at Sangla Hill in 2005 and at Gojra and at Bahmniwala in Kasur in 2009.

Nanak Panthi Sikhs belong to South Punjab and Sindh, where their temples stand, and the thousands of devotees of Guru Nanak in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Punjab and Sindh who did not join Guru Gobind Singh's Khalsa are called Nanak Panthi Hindus, with a picture of Guru Nanak beside pictures of Hindu deities in their temples. Many of the province's Sikhs are Pathans who came from the tribal areas, and moving to the heart of Punjab most of them could not even speak its language.

The province's shared festivals belong to its farming year. Lohri, like Vaisakhi and Basant, is an ancient festival of the Punjab that marks the change of season, kept on the 13th of January with a village bonfire, dry fruit and folk songs. Once kept on a large scale throughout the province, it died down after the creation of Pakistan, when the country's elite gave up festivals like Vaisakhi, Lohri and Basant, and it is now being revived by a group of Pathan men living in the province.

## Language, Poetry and Music

The Punjab is above all a land of mystical poetry in its own tongue. Reliable tradition traces the use of the native language for mystical poetry back to the fifteenth century, and popular mystical poetry may have emerged as early as the days of Fariduddin Shakarganj, the Chishti saint of Pakpattan who died in 1265. Madho Lal Hussein's allusions to the stories of Hir and of Sohni and Mahiwal seem to begin, or to carry on, a tradition typical of the religious poetry of the Punjab and Sindh: the setting of folk tales inside mystical thought.

The *kafi*, a stanzaic poem built on folk themes and techniques, was easy to understand and to memorise, and so the ideal way to spread mystical teaching among people who could not read; even today most Punjabis and Sindhis are more likely to have heard a kafi than to have read one. The golden age of Muslim Punjabi poetry came in the late seventeenth and the eighteenth century, when the province was invaded one time after another by Marathas, Sikhs and Muslim armies. Punjabi mystical folk poetry reached its peak with Bulleh Shah in exactly the years when Shah Abdul Latif was writing in Sindh.

The Punjab and Sindh nourished the same kinds of mystical writing, sung in forms closely tied to the native musical modes rather than in the Persian *ghazal*, the classical lyric of love. Poets in both provinces loved the motif of spinning and weaving, natural in a country that grows cotton, and pictured the longing soul as a woman, so that the tragic heroines of the folk tales, Hir, Sassi, Sohni and many others, stand for the human soul in search of the beloved. In the south the language shades into its neighbour's: Siraiki is spoken in northern Sindh and southern Punjab, mainly in southern Punjab, with Multan as its capital.

The love of Hir and Ranjha is the province's own story. By the 1880s the tale was on the lips of good and bad, young and old, rich and *faqir*, the poor holy man, across the country of the Punjab. The romance was worked into more than a hundred versions in Punjabi, Urdu, Sindhi and Persian and reached its final form in the late eighteenth century in the verse of Waris Shah, which is sung wherever Punjabis meet, and it became a favourite Sufi way of conveying the value of passionate love in spiritual life. Hir's lament as she is carried off in the palanquin is traditionally sung at almost every Punjabi wedding as the bride leaves for her husband's home. Sohni and Mahiwal, set in the old Punjab, is popular in both the old Punjab and Sindh, and Mirza Sahiban is another famous love legend of the province.

The poets made the Punjab itself a figure in their verse. Waris Shah's similes draw on the invasions of the province, as if armies from Kandahar had swept over the Punjab. Bulleh Shah's lament for the province is the best known of such lines, and its full bitterness comes through only against that backdrop of upheaval. In the poem *Come See Me Once in a While* the lines run as follows.

*Now hell has opened its bogs. / Punjab has gone to the dogs. / This hell is the nethermost hell.* (translated by Taufiq Rafat)

The *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, the lives of the Punjab's saints written in 1875, closes with a chronogram, a verse whose letters give a date, for the year 1292 AH by its author's son, Mufti Ghulam Safdar Lahori, which speaks of the province's saints.

جو خدا کے دوست تھے پنجاب میں
حال ان کا اس سے ہوتا ہے عیان

*A chronogram for the book, on the friends of God in Punjab whose lives it makes known.*

The province's minstrels carried the songs of every faith. In the time of Guru Nanak and Mardana the Mirasis, a community of minstrels, went from city to city singing of local chieftains, and Mardana knew by heart several folk stories of Hindu deities and Muslim saints that he would sing at the festivals of the Punjab. The Punjab is one of the two principal regions of the *qawwali* tradition, beside Uttar Pradesh: tunes from Uttar Pradesh rest mainly on the length of the syllables, while Punjabi tunes tend to be organised by stress, the drum marking the metre more strongly.

In Sikh times no printed book came from Hindustan into the Punjab, and most of the lyrics of Bulleh Shah and the other Sufi poets were gathered into printed collections only in the late nineteenth century, when publishers in Lahore brought the older Sufi poetry into the new print culture. After 1800 Muslims rarely carried on the literary heritage of Punjabi, which became almost entirely the literary language of the Sikhs, while Punjabi Muslims turned to Urdu, though they still enjoy the simple mystical verses and ballads of their spoken mother tongue. In Pakistani Punjab the language is looked down upon as the speech of the boorish and the uneducated, and the educated elite end by disowning, even ridiculing, anything Punjabi, the poetry of Baba Farid, Guru Nanak, Madho Lal Hussein, Waris Shah and Bulleh Shah among it.

## Shrines and the State

Rulers tied themselves to the province's shrines and its land from early on. The Delhi sultans and the Mughal emperors granted huge tracts of rich land for the ritual ceremonies of the great Sufi shrines of Punjab and Sindh. Akbar used the Chishti saints, Shaikh Salim and Shaikh Fariduddin Shakarganj in the Punjab, to rule his provinces, and through this doubled Chishti loyalty he could anchor his imperial legitimacy in the Punjab and Rajasthan.

The Sikh years were hard on the shrines as one eyewitness saw them: the Mufti who wrote the *Hadiqat al-Awliya* saw the disgrace of the notables of Punjab at Sikh hands and the destruction of the tombs of the shaikhs. Under Ranjit Singh the Moti Masjid in the Lahore fort was renamed the Moti Mandir and held the treasury of the state. The Sikh state endowed the gurdwaras: Ranjit Singh spent large sums renovating and enlarging many gurdwaras all over Punjab and attached vast tracts of land to them so that the revenue could pay for their running. With sectarian divisions among the Sikh communities and the British takeover of Punjab, a large number of important gurdwaras came to be run by Hindu *mahants*, the heads of temples and religious houses.

The British saw the hereditary saints chiefly in terms of their economic power, and their policy, especially in the Punjab and Sindh, was to treat them much as landlords and tribal leaders were treated, securing the loyalty of the *pirs* by reinforcing their economic positions. In central Punjab the colonial state did not try to control shrines directly; the state after independence has gone further, driven by a wish to harness the plural traditions of the shrines in favour of a single Islam.

After Partition hundreds of temples and gurdwaras all over Pakistani Punjab were taken over by refugees and turned into households. The Punjab has 8 reserved minority seats out of 371 in its assembly. On 4 January 2011 the Governor of Punjab, Salman Taseer, was shot dead by one of his bodyguards, Mumtaz Qadri, for supporting the cause of Aasia Bibi, a Christian woman accused of blasphemy, and Qadri became an instant hero, hailed by the lawyers' community.

## The Province in Books

Under the Mughals the Punjab was drawn as a *suba*. At the end of the seventeenth century the *suba* of Lahore was a country of five *doabs* divided by its six rivers, and the five rivers of Punjab join the Indus beyond Multan, and from there the whole stream is called the Sindh. It was a country of good water and air, with very hot summers and cold winters, rice better than Bengal's and sugarcane better than the Deccan's, and most of its irrigation depended on wells worked by a wheel of small pots turned by a pair of oxen. Beside it lay the *suba* of Multan, called the abode of safety. The Mughal Punjab was a frontier made safe by union with Kabul: since Hind and Kabul came under one ruler, this union has been the cause of the prosperity of Punjab and the security of all Hind.

*Tarikh-e Lahore*, the history of 1884, calls the Punjab by many names: the territory given in *jagir*, the Mughal *suba* with its *diwan*, the region that Ranjit Singh ruled, the country that the English annexed, and the *suba* whose capital in its author's day was Lahore. Its author, Kanhaiya Lal, was also a servant of the province: the women's prison of the Punjab was built under his supervision, and he described its governors, courts and jails as they stood in his own day.

The *Hadiqat al-Awliya* of 1875 defines its own province: the land of Punjab, meaning all the territory from Delhi to Peshawar then attached to it, where the tombs of famous saints lie. It holds the lives of the Sufis of Punjab in seven *chamans*, or gardens, from Sultan Mahmud of Ghazni to 1292 AH, 1875. To its modern editor the Punjab is in this respect the most unfortunate of regions, for no regular and researched history of it has yet been written, and no comprehensive *tazkira*, or memoir, of the Sufis of Punjab is known to him.

The translators of the province's poets draw it in their own ways. The world of Waris Shah's poem is the old Punjab, with its *bars*, a kind of jungle, such as the Nili Bar in the Montgomery area, now renamed Sahiwal. One historian of Islamic mysticism most often speaks of Sindh and the Punjab together, as the two provinces of one tradition of religious poetry. Seen from eighteenth century Sindh, in a study of 1940, the Punjab is the theatre of the armies of Afghans, Sikhs and Marathas and a source of migrants and tales.

Modern travellers draw the Punjab to the scale of their journeys. *In Search of Shiva* kept its research to the country around its author's hometown of Lahore, so that only parts of central Punjab are included, and *A White Trail* was researched in central Punjab, where Hindus live in ghettos and where, in its author's perception, the conditions of Hindu life are the harshest of any region. The author of *Walking with Nanak*, born and brought up in Pakistani Punjab, found that its Muslim dominant culture offered him no way to explore Guru Nanak's personality. The modern ethnographers work with parts of the province: central Punjab, the southern Punjab as a Sufi master's domain, and a southern Punjab that was once part of Sindh.
