## Overview

Sialkot is an ancient city of the Punjab, set in the Rachna Doab, a *doab* being a tract of land between two rivers, in the foothills of the Himalayas. It lies right on the border, and historically it gave the Punjab its connection to Kashmir. In former times it was the seat of government of the province, and under the Mughals it was one of the centres of Islamic scholarship. It was known for its paper, its silk embroidery and its blades.

Its earliest recorded mention is in ancient Greek texts, where it is called Sagala, and legend gives it more than one royal founder. In the time of Guru Nanak, the founder of the Sikh faith, it was a city inhabited mostly by Muslims, with a powerful Hindu trading presence, and the stories of Guru Nanak's journeys run through it. Legend ties it to Puran Bhagat, whose well lay a little distance from the city, and by the early twenty first century Sialkot had become one of the most important cities in Pakistan, with an airport its own business community financed.

## History

Sialkot has gone by older names. It was called Shalkot, and also Salkot, and the city is said to be mentioned in the Mahabharat, the great Hindu epic, which in Aurangzeb's time was held to have been composed nearly five thousand years earlier. Its earliest recorded mention is found in ancient Greek texts going as far back as 322 BC, where it is called Sagala.

Who founded the city is a matter of legend, and there is no settled account. One tradition holds that it was founded by Raja Sela, an uncle of the Pandavas, the heroic brothers of the Mahabharat, and rebuilt by Raja Satya Vachan when it became part of the kingdom of Kashmir. Another attributes it to Raja Salbahan, whose memorial is said to be a fort of baked brick. It is also believed to have been founded by Raja Salivahan, the king of Sialkot whom legend makes the father of Puran Bhagat.

In former times Sialkot was the seat of government of the province of the Panjab, with its settlement stretching over two or three *kos*, an old measure of distance. When Lahore fell into ruin in early times, the seat of government passed to Sialkot, and it stayed there until Sultan Mahmud's conquest and Malik Ayaz's rebuilding of Lahore. In Aurangzeb's time Sialkot still exceeded every other town of the province in population.

The fort of Sialkot is old. On his fifth campaign Shihab al-Din Muhammad Ghori, between the Ravi and the Chenab, renewed and repaired the fort, whose pillars had fallen into disrepair, left his deputy there and turned back. Khusraw Malik, with the Khokhar tribe, then besieged the fort for a time and went back to Lahore without gaining his end. It is also told that, having failed at Lahore, Shihab al-Din came to Sialkot, rebuilt the old fort and left his army there. Accounts differ on when the town passed to him; by one, Sialkot became a part of the Muslim Sultanate when Ghori captured Punjab in 1192, and a garrison was left there.

Shahi Khan, who later became Sultan Zain al-Abidin, once came out of Kashmir to Sialkot. There he joined an ally who had escaped from Timur's custody in the Punjab, and marched against Ali Shah.

Babur's wars reached the town too. Daulat Khan and Ghazi Khan gathered forty thousand horse and foot, took Kalanaur, and seized Sialkot as well from the emperor's men. Babur camped on the Chenab near the town of Bahlolpur, and an order was given that Sialkot be laid waste and its people settled in Bahlolpur. Alam Khan and others came over to him when they heard that the royal standards had arrived near Sialkot, and from there Babur went on to Kalanaur. Accounts of the town under Babur differ, however: by another, during his third incursion into India, Sialkot offered no resistance and was spared the usual massacre and plunder.

Under Akbar the land that later became the *pargana*, or revenue district, of Gujrat had been part of Sialkot. The Mughal governors kept up the fort. Raja Man Singh of Akbar's time, *faujdar*, or military governor, of Jammu and *jagirdar* of Sialkot, the holder of its revenues as a grant, repaired the fort and improved the city. After him Safdar Khan of Jahangir's time, also *faujdar* of Jammu and holding the *pargana* as a grant, rebuilt the fort and its tower, and most of the governors after them made repairs as well.

## The City and Its Country

Sialkot was a bustling city in the foothills of the Himalayas. It lies right on the border, and historically it provided the Punjab with a connection to Kashmir. The Chenab passes the villages of the *pargana* of Sialkot, and the villages of Gujrat were taken out of it. Eminabad is not very far away, and Pasrur lies about thirty kilometres off, on a road that is perhaps one of the worst.

A stream rises in the hills of Jammu and flows below the city, and ten *kos* beyond it takes another name and spreads out until it loses its name altogether. In Aurangzeb's time, when it flooded in the rains, all the people of Sialkot, great and small, played in the water, naked but for a loincloth and carrying leather water skins, and anyone from the city who was far away remembered it with pleasure.

Gardens surrounded the city, above all the garden of Nazar Muhammad. The buildings of some of its *qanungos*, the keepers of the land records, and of some others, were exceedingly pleasing and delightful.

Sialkot's paper, the Mansinghi and the kinds called *hariri* and *khassa jahangiri*, was white and durable and was sent to other regions. The city's *karchikan* embroidery in silk and *kalabatun*, gold or silver thread, was worked on the *futa*, a cloth wrap, the *suzani*, an embroidered cover, the *dastarkhwan*, the cloth spread for a meal, and the *khwanposh*, the cover for a tray of food. Every year close to a lakh, a hundred thousand, of rupees came in from the buying and selling of this embroidery, and it went all over the world, though Gujrat produced still more of it.

The *jamdhar* and the *katari*, both daggers, and the *barchhi*, a spear, were well made in Sialkot. The city was famous for its silk, and it was an important post on the trade route between West Asia and Delhi.

By the early twenty first century a newly built motorway connected the Grand Trunk Road with Sialkot, and Nanak too, it is thought, followed a similar route. Sialkot had become one of the most important cities in Pakistan, and its business community had financed its own airport, which handled international flights, and the highway as well. On the way into the city stood several factories and many mounds, probably archaeological ruins, that had been turned into graveyards.

## A Seat of Learning

Under the Mughals Sialkot was one of the centres of Islamic scholarship, and Ahmad Sirhindi studied there. It was praised as an abode of learning, a gathering place of scholars, a mine of excellence and a dwelling of the learned.

In the reign of Akbar, Maulana Kamal al-Din Husain, aggrieved with the Khan who ruled Kashmir, came to Sialkot in the year 971 of the Hijra, in the Islamic calendar, and established teaching there. He was praised as the most excellent of the excellent and the most perfect of scholars, famous in his day for the excellence of his dervish life, and he left Kashmir in the reign of Husain Khan to give himself to teaching at Sialkot.

Under Shah Jahan, Maulvi Abd al-Hakim spread the sciences in Sialkot and wrote glosses, or explanatory commentaries, and students came to his blessed *madrasa*, a school of Islamic learning. His son Maulvi Abdullah ran the *madrasa* after him and was called the imam of the age.

The city also had a Sikh writer: the author of *Tarikh Guru Khalsa* belonged to Sialkot.

## Holy Places and Legends

The holy place most told of in Sialkot is the well of Puran Bhagat, which was a little distance from the city. Devotees raised a temple around it, "the most important temple in the ancient city of Sialkot". Tradition holds that Puran, his hands and feet cut off, was thrown into this well, and in the nineteenth century the well was still to be seen at Sialkot. Later, the story goes, Puran came to Sialkot at a command, and a garden that had dried up came back to life on his arrival.

Guru Nanak's journeys run through the city in the *janamsakhis*, the traditional stories of his life. In the Puratan tradition his first *udasi*, or long journey, ends at Saidpur, Pasrur and Sialkot, and his third *udasi* both starts and ends at Sialkot.

One story tells of Hamza Ghaus, a *faqir*, or wandering Sufi, who held Sialkot in fear. The quarrel began when a Hindu man broke a promise to give his firstborn son, and Hamza Ghaus told the people of Sialkot that he would pray for forty days. The story sets his curse in the centre of the market square.

Another story tells of Maula Karar, a vendor in the city, who told Nanak of the various shrines there. Karar later hid from Nanak, it is said, and was bitten by a snake that was escaping the harsh winter outside. He died on the spot, and Nanak and Mardana took part in his final rites.

The city has a place in the life of a Sufi saint as well. Shah Daulah, owing to some disputes with the local people of Sialkot, migrated from there and after some years moved to Gujrat. Shah Daula's first master lived in Sialkot.

## Communities

Many Sufi missionaries settled in the region, making it a predominantly Muslim one with a rich Sufi tradition. In Guru Nanak's time Sialkot was a city inhabited predominantly by Muslims, with a powerful Hindu trading presence.

Its past is also contested. The words "Allah O Akbar", God is greatest, set on barren rocks, are more common in places with a contested history such as Sialkot, where there used to be a major Sikh shrine.

## Sialkot in Books

The Persian chronicle written in Aurangzeb's reign gives the city the most room. In its account of the province it covers Sialkot's names and founders, its time as a seat of government, its fort and governors, its gardens and buildings, its paper, embroidery and blades, its stream and its scholars, and elsewhere it meets the town in campaigns and in the offices of the men who held it.

A modern retelling of the *janamsakhis* gives the city a short history of its own, from its legendary founding to the Sultanate and the Sufis, as the setting for Guru Nanak's visits. A modern traveller following Nanak's route sees a border city of factories and an airport, whose past is contested.

For a nineteenth century work, Sialkot is the scene of the Puran legend, and it names the city only there. A study of Islamic mysticism names it as a centre of scholarship under the Mughals, in a single sentence about Ahmad Sirhindi. A study of Sufi cults names it once, as the place Shah Daulah left, and a history of Sufism in India names the town only once, in Guru Nanak's itinerary.
