# Darbar Ghamkol Sharif (Zinda Pir)
darbar-ghamkol-sharif-zinda-pir / researched 16 August 2026 / batch G

## Verified findings

- **Location: a few miles from Kohat, North-West Frontier Province (now Khyber Pakhtunkhwa).**
  "Ghamkol Sharif, the lodge he founded when he left Abbotabad, is located only a few miles
  from Kohat, a large British garrison town or cantonment which was taken over by the Pakistan
  army at Independence." — Pnina Werbner, "Transnationalism and Regional Cults: The Dialectics
  of Sufism in the Plurivocal Muslim World" (conference paper, Keele University, May 2009), PDF
  fetched directly and OCR-extracted from the underlying file. Reliability: academic
  (anthropologist's own conference paper). Accessed 16 August 2026.
- **Founding narrative and date (1948).** "He had founded the cult centre in 1948, when he
  first secluded himself, according to the legend, in a cave on the hill of Ghamkol. At the
  time the place was a wilderness." — same source as above.
- **Naqshbandi affiliation, with a distinct branch name.** "They spoke of the cult as a
  tariqa, but to distinguish it from the wider Naqshbandi order to which it was affiliated, it
  was known as tariqa Naqshbandiyya Ghamkolia." — same source as above.
- **The saint's title and the scale of the cult.** "The saint at the head of the order,
  Zindapir, (the living saint), was by the time of my study the head of a vast, transnational
  regional cult, stretching throughout Pakistan to the Gulf, Britain, Afghanistan and Southern
  Africa." — same source as above.
- **Daily life at the lodge and the 'urs festival.** "Every day truckloads of supplicants
  arrive at Ghamkol Sharif, the lodge and now shrine of Zindapir, to see the saint and seek
  redress and blessing... On Fridays, holidays and 'urs festivals the stream turned into a
  veritable flood as mini-buses ferry supplicants from the neighbouring town..." — same source.
  Also: "the langar, as it is known in South Asia" is central to the lodge's economy; supplicants
  are fed before an audience with the Shaikh rather than asked for payment.
- **Founder biography and book existence.** Pnina Werbner, *Pilgrims of Love: The Anthropology
  of a Global Sufi Cult* — publisher pages fetched directly. Hurst Publishers
  (https://www.hurstpublishers.com/book/pilgrims-of-love/, accessed 16 August 2026): paperback,
  January 2004, ISBN 9781850656517, 360 pp.; "a Sufi Naqshbandi order founded by a living
  saint, Zindapir, whose cult originated in Pakistan and has extended globally to Britain,
  Europe, the Middle East, and Southern Africa," with "a celebration in which tens of thousands
  of people gather at the saint's lodge in Pakistan and in streets in Britain." Indiana
  University Press (https://iupress.org/9780253028853/pilgrims-of-love/, accessed 16 August
  2026): eBook ISBN 9780253028853, December 2016, 350 pp., "based on 12 years of fieldwork in
  Pakistan and Great Britain." Neither publisher page names Ghamkol Sharif or Kohat directly by
  name (they refer only to "the saint's lodge in Pakistan").
- **The mosque/charity's own account: location and founding date (1952).** "The roots of our
  Charity, go back as far as 1952, to an area, now known as Ghamkol Sharif." "Ghamkol Sharif is
  located in a valley, just off the outskirts, of a local town called Kohat (approximately 3
  miles) in the Khyber Pakhtunkhwa province." "(1912 – 1999)" [Zinda Pir's lifespan, given in
  the page's opening]. "The Urus Mubarak festival, is an annual three-day celebration, held at
  Ghamkol Sharif, Kohat, Pakistan, which commemorates the death anniversary of Hadhrat Zinda Pir
  Saab." — "Our Roots," Central Jamia Mosque Ghamkol Sharif (UK), https://gsmosque.org/our-roots/,
  accessed 16 August 2026. Reliability: primary/self-published — this is the affiliated UK
  mosque and charity's own history page, not independent scholarship, so treat as a
  corroborating primary account rather than independent verification.

## Conflicts

- **Founding date: 1948 vs. 1952.** Werbner's academic paper dates Zindapir's seclusion and the
  founding of the cult centre to 1948. The UK mosque/charity's own "Our Roots" page dates its
  own history "back as far as 1952." Neither source is reconciled here; both are reported as
  found.

## Verdict

STRONG — two independent, directly-fetched sources corroborate the core facts (location a few
miles from Kohat; Naqshbandi affiliation; the shrine/lodge founded by Zindapir; the annual 'urs):
Pnina Werbner's 2009 academic conference paper (fetched and quoted directly) and the affiliated
UK mosque's own "Our Roots" history page (fetched and quoted directly, but self-published and
lower-tier). The two disagree on the founding year (1948 vs. 1952), which is reported above as
an open conflict rather than resolved.
