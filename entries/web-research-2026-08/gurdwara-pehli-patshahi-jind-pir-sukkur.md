# Gurdwara Pehli Patshahi (Jind Pir), Sukkur
gurdwara-pehli-patshahi-jind-pir-sukkur / researched 16 August 2026 / batch B

Sheet location on file: near the Sukkur–Rohri (Lansdowne/Ayub) railway bridge, Sukkur, Sindh, Pakistan (27.6905, 68.857).

## Verified findings

1. **Location, founding legend, and current religious use.** "GURDWARA FIRST GURU JIND PIR, SUKKUR. Sukkur is a famous city of Sindh province. It is located on the Lahore-Karachi railway line… Just after crossing the bridge a road goes down at right hand. This road keeps turning along the bridge and reaches Gurdwara Jind Pir… Sat Guru Nanak Dev Ji had graced this place during his third itinerary. It was from here that he visited Sadhu Bela, an islet in the river Indus. Jhulay Lai Udasi sadhu was its first priest. The shrine is in the possession of Nankpanthi Hindus. Prakash of Guru Granth Sahib takes place and the idols are also worshipped here. A lamp lit by the Guru Dev Ji is kept burning round the clock." — Iqbal Qaiser, *Historical Sikh Shrines in Pakistan* (Lahore: Punjabi History Board, 1998), full text, p. 120 of the scan, at https://archive.org/download/HistoricalSikhShrinesInPakistan/HistoricalSikhShrinesInPakistan_djvu.txt, accessed 16 August 2026. Reliability class: published book (citable).

2. **The place-name "Jind Pir"/"Zinda Pir" is independently attested as belonging to a much older, separate Hindu-Muslim river shrine at Sukkur, with no Sikh gurdwara mentioned.** "In Sukkur, Hindus and Muslims worshipped together at Zindapir's island shrine until the late nineteenth century," with the 1874 Gazetteer of Sindh cited for the "non-antagonistic character of the common worship," and by the 1919 Gazetteer, "about twenty years ago… the Hindus abandoned their claim and set up a shrine of their own to Jinda Pir on the Sukkur bank." — Alice Albinia, excerpt from *Empires of the Indus*, published as "Life Around The Indus: Tracing River Worship In Sindh," Outlook Traveller, 30 September 2014, https://www.outlooktraveller.com/experiences/heritage/indus-river-worship-in-sindh-pakistan, accessed 16 August 2026. Reliability class: established press/published author (citable). This article makes no mention of any Sikh Gurdwara or Guru Nanak connection to the site it describes.

## Conflicts

- **Possible naming overlap between the Sikh gurdwara and the older Zinda Pir shrine.** Qaiser's book places the Sikh "Gurdwara Jind Pir" on the Sukkur side, reached by road after crossing the Sukkur–Rohri bridge, in the possession of "Nankpanthi Hindus" who worship both the Guru Granth Sahib and idols there. Albinia's account (via the 1874 and 1919 Gazetteers of Sindh) describes a shrine of the same or a very similar name ("Zindapir"/"Jinda Pir") on an island in the Indus at Sukkur, historically shared by Hindus and Muslims and dedicated to Khwaja Khizr/the river saint, with the Hindu congregation eventually founding "a shrine of their own to Jinda Pir on the Sukkur bank of the river" around the turn of the 20th century — which is geographically consistent with where Qaiser locates the Sikh-associated gurdwara. Neither source explicitly connects the two, and Albinia's detailed, gazetteer-sourced account does not mention Sikhs, Guru Nanak, or a gurdwara at all. This file does not resolve whether "Gurdwara Pehli Patshahi (Jind Pir)" is the same physical structure as the Hindu shrine descended from the shared Zinda Pir/Jinda Pir site, a distinct building that merely borrowed the name from its proximity, or something else — it is reported as an open question.
- Non-citable sources (SikhiWiki, All About Sikhs) summarize essentially the same content as the Qaiser book (their pages read as close paraphrases), so they are not treated as independent corroboration.

## Verdict

PARTIAL — one independent, directly-quoting published-book source (Qaiser) gives a specific, on-the-ground account of the gurdwara; a second independent, citable source (Albinia/Outlook Traveller, itself resting on 19th/early-20th-century gazetteers) corroborates the place-name and general river-shrine setting but does not confirm — and may complicate — the Sikh gurdwara's identity and relationship to the older Zinda Pir shrine.
