## Overview

The Punjab of these books is not one unit but several, each drawn by its own author in his own time: for Sujan Rai Bhandari, writing about 1107 AH, it is the Mughal *suba* of Lahore, a country of five *doabs* whose rivers join the Indus beyond Multan, with the *suba* of Multan beside it, while in his narrative of earlier reigns it is the *wilayat* of Punjab (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 510; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 513; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 405). For Kanhaiya Lal, whose *Tarikh-e Lahore* is a history of one city, it is by turns a territory given in *jagir*, the Mughal *suba* with its *diwan*, the region that Ranjit Singh ruled, the country that the English annexed and the *suba* whose capital in his own day is Lahore (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 14, folio 9; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 152, folio 155; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 9, folio 4; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 68, folio 71; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 60, folio 63). For Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori, writing in 1875, it is all the country from Delhi to Peshawar then attached to Punjab, where the tombs of famous saints lie (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 37, folio 25), and for Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti it is the land of Punjab that the British conquered and governed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 137). The modern translators and annotators write of "the Punjab" or "the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 3, folio 2; Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 16, folio xvi), and Michel Boivin reminds the reader that the Siraiki speaking south, with Multan as its capital, was a part of Sindh during the Middle Ages (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 216, folio 203). Haroon Khalid writes of the modern province, Pakistani Punjab, which he calls the most prosperous province of Pakistan (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 15, folio not stated; Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated). Lahori's modern editor holds that no regular and researched history of Punjab has yet been written (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 23, folio 11).

The province's history in these books begins before Islam: following the *Tuhfat al-Wasilin*, Kanhaiya Lal relates that Raja Deb Chand of Delhi gave the territory of the Punjab in *jagir* to his brother Lohar Chand, who made the city of Lahore his capital (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 14, folio 9). Mahmud of Ghazni, in Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi's account, established Ghaznavid rule over the Punjab (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 83, folio 67), and the province became the last refuge of the house of Ghazni, whose government, in Kanhaiya Lal's account, ended with Khusrau Malik's surrender to Sultan Shihab al-Din Ghori (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 24, folio 19). Mongol armies came again and again, and Kanhaiya Lal counts the blows of plunder, famine and deserted villages one after another (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 26, folio 29), while Richard M. Eaton describes how Mongol pressures drove waves of Turkish settlers into the province's towns, Lahore, Dipalpur and Multan among them (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 217, folio 205). In the fifteenth century, Rizvi writes, the Punjab enjoyed a peaceful period under Afghan rule, until Babur invaded it (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 408, folio 384; Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 409, folio 385). Under the Mughals it was a prince's *jagir* and a *suba*, governed for a time by Dara Shikoh, who put up a great many buildings there (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 76, folio 79; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 139, folio 142). The eighteenth century is a time of ruin, the Punjab a battlefield of Afghans, Sikhs and Marathas, and the introduction to Rafat's selection dates the beginning of its misfortunes to the period of Muhammad Shah (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 46, folio 37; Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, pp. 26–27, folios 6–7). Ranjit Singh then held the whole region of the Punjab, with Kashmir and the Derajat besides, and ruled it, in Kanhaiya Lal's account, for forty years (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 112, folio 115; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220). On 29 March 1849, the date uncertainly printed, the whole country entered the British dominion (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 592), and in Kanhaiya Lal's own day Lahore is the capital of the *suba* of the Punjab, the seat of its Lieutenant-Governor and its courts (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 60, folio 63). The colonial state connected the five rivers with canals and settled farming families on the new land, Khalid writes, and the province's history in these books ends with its division between India and Pakistan (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated).

For Lahori the province is first of all a land of saints' tombs, and he includes a saint when his tomb lies in the soil of Punjab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 127, folio 115). Rizvi makes the Ghaznavid conquest the beginning of its Sufi settlement, with Hujwiri reaching Lahore in about 1035 (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112). Baba Farid's settling at Ajudhan was, for Eaton, the religious side of a very slow change of the Punjab's cities from a Hindu to a Turkish-Islamic orientation (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 217, folio 205), and the great shrines of Punjab and Sindh drew pastoral clans into their ritual, social, economic and political orbits (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 48–49, folios 36–37). The Chishti order is dominant in Punjab, in Boivin's account (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 217, folio 204), the Qadiris had made the Punjab the main centre of their activities by Guru Nanak's day (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 420, folio 396), the Naushahi faqirs in Punjab number in the thousands (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 70, folio 58), and Shaikh Sulayman Taunsawi, who died in 1850, consolidated the Chishti order through religious schools throughout the Punjab (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, pp. 121–122, folios 108–109). The province is also the home ground of Guru Nanak's journeys, and by his return to Talwandi in 1506, a year imperfectly printed, there were very few in the Punjab who did not know of him, as Harish Dhillon retells the Janamsakhis (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 123, folio 137). Not every holy place belongs to a named saint: there are countless shrines to unknown saints on the outskirts of Punjabi villages (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 23, folio 12). Today southern Punjab is believed, Khalid writes, to be the hub of the country's shrine culture (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).

The books weigh the meeting of the province's faiths differently: Dhillon describes Islam and Hinduism existing side by side in the Punjab for over three hundred years in a state of constant conflict (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 16, folio 18), while Eaton traces a very slow, probably unconscious conversion of the Punjabi clans, many of which claim to have been converted by Baba Farid (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 234–235, folios 222–223; Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 227, folio 215). In Kanhaiya Lal's Punjab Hindus and Muslims served the same state, a Hindu amir serving as *diwan* of the Mughal *suba* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 336, folio 343). The annotator of Waris Shah describes the village of the old Punjab as one social unit irrespective of caste or creed (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 20, folio 19), and Annemarie Schimmel notes that Hindus shared in the Muslim orders, though more visibly in Sindh than in the Punjab (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 418, folio 387). Khalid's travels find a Christian community with a formidable presence in the Punjab (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 87, folio not stated), a significant Hindu population still living in Southern Punjab (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 31, folio not stated), and Sikhs, many of them Pathans who came from the tribal areas (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 203, folio not stated). After Partition, he writes, he has seen gurdwaras and temples taken over and destroyed by the migrants (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated), and among the country's elite the farming festivals of Vaisakhi, Lohri and Basant were given up (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).

The Punjab of these books is above all a land of mystical poetry in its own tongue, which Schimmel traces in one book to the fifteenth century and in another suggests may have emerged as early as the days of Fariduddin Shakarganj (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384; Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 16, folio 12). The *kafi*, easy to understand and memorise, carried the mystics' teaching to people who heard rather than read it (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 15, folio 15). Its golden age, for Schimmel, was the late seventeenth and the eighteenth century, when the province was invaded one time after the other, and it reached its apex with Bulleh Shah (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 17, folio 13; Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 154, folio 154), whose lament that Punjab has gone to the dogs is the best known of the verses in which the province itself is a figure (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 16, folio xvi). The love of Hir and Ranjha is the province's own story, on the lips of everyone in the country of the Punjab in Kanhaiya Lal's day (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 10, folio 5), and Waris Shah's version is sung, Schimmel writes, wherever Punjabis meet (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 171, folio 153). The Punjab is one of the two principal regions of the qawwali tradition in Regula Burckhardt Qureshi's study (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 73, folio 53), and in the south its speech shades into Siraiki, spoken in Northern Sindh and Southern Punjab (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 220, folio 207). With the British conquest of the 1840s, Christopher Shackle writes, Persian was quite rapidly replaced by Urdu and English (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, pp. 11–12, folios x–xi); Punjabi became, in Schimmel's account, a literary medium used almost exclusively by the Sikhs (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396), and Khalid finds it looked down upon by the educated elite of Punjab (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 128, folio 110).

Rulers bound themselves to the province's holy places: the Delhi sultans and Mughal emperors granted huge tracts of rich land for the ceremonies of the great shrines of Punjab and Sindh (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 48–49, folios 36–37), and Akbar, in Carl W. Ernst and Bruce B. Lawrence's reading, anchored his imperial legitimacy in the Punjab through Chishti loyalty (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, pp. 112–113, folios 99–100). Lahori's editor credits the Mufti with recording, as an eyewitness, the destruction of the tombs of the shaikhs at the hands of the Sikhs (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 33, folio 21), while Ranjit Singh attached vast tracts of land to the gurdwaras (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16), and under him the Moti Masjid in the fort was renamed the Moti Mandir and held the treasury of the state (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 339, folio 346). The British, Robert Rozehnal writes after Katherine Ewing, treated the hereditary saints of the Punjab and Sindh as they treated landlords and tribal leaders (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 33, folio 23), and many important gurdwaras came to be administered by Hindu mahants (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 189, folio not stated). After Partition hundreds of temples and gurdwaras all over Pakistani Punjab were taken over by refugees and converted into households (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 58, folio not stated), and Omar Kasmani, after Umber bin Ibad, sets the colonial state, which did not try to control shrines directly, against a postcolonial state that has gone further (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 69, folio 52). Reformist movements, the Tableeghi Jamaat and the Deobandi school among them, have turned to the shrine culture of the south (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 10, ch. 4, folio not stated; Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated), and the killing of the Governor of Punjab, Salman Taseer, on 4 January 2011 runs through Khalid's books (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 19, Notes, note 44, folio not stated).

## The Province in History: From the Earliest Times to Babur

The books give the name Punjab to more than one unit, and the history that follows is told in each author's own unit. For Sujan Rai Bhandari, describing his own time, about 1107 AH, it is the Mughal *suba* of Lahore, beside the *suba* of Multan, and in his narrative of earlier reigns it is the *wilayat* of Punjab, or simply Punjab under a governor (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 510; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 405; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 228). For Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori, writing in 1875, Punjab is all the country from Delhi to Peshawar that was then attached to it (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 37, folio 25). For Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti it is the land of Punjab that the British conquered and governed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 137). For the modern translators and annotators it is "the Punjab" or "the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 3, folio 2; Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 16, folio xvi). Kanhaiya Lal's *Tarikh-e Lahore* is a history of one city, but the Punjab runs through it as the larger unit to which the city belongs, the country whose capital Lahore became and lost and became again.

Michel Boivin reminds the reader that the southern Punjab has not always been reckoned to the Punjab. Of the Siraiki speaking south, with Multan as its capital, he writes that "this area was a part of Sindh during the Middle Ages" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 216, folio 203), and he describes a shared Sindhian culture, a set of social, religious and cultural traits that existed in the Sindhi area "at the time when it encompassed the southern present Punjab" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 53, folio 40). He does not say whether by present Punjab he means the Pakistani province (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 53, folio 40).

The province's history in these books begins long before Islam. Haroon Khalid compares the Indus Valley, "an agriculture-intensive society", to present day Punjab, Pakistan, where in his reading the fertility cult has particular significance (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated). Alexander's historians, Chishti writes, name Porus and a second king whose name is uncertainly printed, both of the Chandar Bansi line, of whom one lived at Hastinapur and the other in Punjab; he does not say which lived where (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 799). Bhandari relates that after Yudhishthira gave up his throne, at the coming of the Kaljug age, the five Pandava brothers and Draupadi travelled through the lands of Thatta, as the name is probably to be read, Multan and Punjab on their way to the mountain of Badrinath; he says nothing more of these lands (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 479). Kanhaiya Lal's Punjab, too, begins before Islam. Following Shaikh Ahmad Zanjani, author of the treatise *Tuhfat al-Wasilin*, an account he sets against that of the author of the *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, he relates that when Raja Deb Chand sat on the throne of Delhi he gave the territory of the Punjab in *jagir* to his brother Lohar Chand, a portion of the passage being illegible in the printed text, and that once his hold over the Punjab was complete he made the city of Lahore his capital (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 14, folio 9).

For Harish Dhillon, retelling the Janamsakhis, the Punjab of the centuries before Guru Nanak was the land through which every invader came, from the Ghaznavids, Ghurids and Tughluqs after the beginning of the eleventh century to Timur in 1398: "Since Punjab in the north was the gateway to India, it bore the brunt of all these invasions" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 15, folio 17). Once the conquerors had settled down in the Punjab region, he writes, they turned to converting the non-believers or destroying them (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 16, folio 18). H. T. Sorley explains Sindh's own isolation by the Punjab's exposure: in his account the invaders who poured over the frontier barriers of the north west passed Sindh by and moved rapidly on to the more promising fields of the Punjab rivers and the Gangetic plain, until the rise of the kingdom of Afghanistan and of the Sikhs closed those barriers in the middle of the eighteenth century (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 22, folio 13).

In Bhandari's history of the *wilayat* of Multan, which he says he found in certain histories, Islam began there in 99 AH with Muhammad Qasim, in the time of Hajjaj bin Yusuf (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 298). Sultan Mahmud of Ghazni, he goes on, took the *wilayat* from the Qarmatian heretics and made Islam current there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 298). For Christopher Shackle the Punjab's Muslim history begins as an extension of a wider world: "The Muslim conquest of northern India extended this Persianate cultural world to Panjab, where Persian remained the dominant literary language of the ruling elite through the Mughal period and beyond" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 11, folio x). In the same passage he writes that "A strong Sufi presence was rapidly established" in the region with the arrival of charismatic figures associated with the main Sufi orders (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 11, folio x).

Richard M. Eaton begins the pattern of conquest he traces in 986, "when the Ghaznavid Sultan Sabuktigin (r. 977-97) attacked and defeated the Hindu Shahi raja who controlled the region between Kabul and northwest Punjab" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 110, folio 98). Kanhaiya Lal tells the Muslim conquest of the province through its rulers at Lahore: when Sultan Mahmud and Sabuktigin of Ghazni attacked the Punjab, Raja Jaipal, a Brahman and ruler of the Punjab, held throne and crown at Lahore (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 14, folio 9). Of Raja Jaipal, Bhandari observes that the historians of Hind do not count him among the rulers of Delhi, but that since his seat of government was a fort whose name is uncertainly printed, with another reading in the editor's footnote, it appears that the lands of Samana and Punjab and others, apart from Delhi, were in his hands (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 427). He dates Sultan Nasir al-Din's accession to 367 AH, the year uncertainly printed, and explains that the raids of that time succeeded because Hindustan had then fallen into petty kingdoms and no one ruler held independent power (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 426–427).

Kanhaiya Lal dates the first attack on Lahore to the year 391 AH, the reading of the year uncertain in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 22, folio 17). Mahmud, he writes, wished to drive Raja Jaipal out of Lahore and bring the territory of the Punjab under his own rule, and after the sack of the city Malik Ayaz, his name uncertainly printed, was named to the administration of the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 17, folio 12). The book returns to this later, when Malik Ayaz became ruler of the Punjab by the king's order (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 23, folio 18). In Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi's history, Mahmud "established Ghaznawid rule over Khurasan, Afghanistan and the Panjab" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 83, folio 67), and Khalid places Al-Beruni's coming to India in "the first half of the 11th century, after the Muslim invader Shah Mahmood Ghaznvi attacked Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 44, folio not stated).

Lahori tells of Punjab under Sultan Mahmud's successors. When Masud came to the throne after Sultan Mahmud's death, his young son Majdud was given Punjab as his governorship, with Ayaz appointed as his tutor (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 193, folio 181). After Masud died and Maudud took the throne, Majdud rebelled and set up a kingdom of his own in Hindustan; Maudud marched against him, Majdud died suddenly on the day of Eid during the siege of Lahore, and Maudud established his hold over Punjab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 193, folio 181). From the middle of the eleventh century, in Eaton's account, Mahmud's successors "became progressively more provincial, their kingdom focused around their capital of Ghazni in eastern Afghanistan with extensions into the Punjab", and their raids "appear to have been less destructive and more sporadic than those of Sabuktigin and Mahmud" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 111, folio 99).

Retelling *Tuhfat al-Wasilin* under the Shahid Ganj entry, Lahori writes that when the Ghaznavid sultans came to rule Punjab and Lahore became the capital, their rule held well in Punjab for a long time, until in Bahram Shah's time their quarrels among themselves left the government of Punjab altogether weak; Raja Anangpal then took Lahore, and there was Hindu rule there for a time, its length uncertainly printed, until an army came again from Ghazni (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 264, folio 252). His modern editor calls *Tuhfat al-Wasilin* a fabricated tazkira (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 191–192, folios 179–180). The Punjab became the dynasty's refuge: after two defeats by 'Ala'u'd-Din of Ghur, Rizvi writes, "Bahram Shah was forced to flee to the Panjab" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 83, folio 67). The destruction of Ghazni by "'Ala'u'd-Din Husain of Ghur in 1150-51, followed by the Ghuzz invasion six years later" in turn "drove a large number of people from that region to the Panjab for safety", and Rizvi speaks of "The Ghaznawid Sultan of the Panjab" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 157, folio 139).

The province was the last refuge of the house of Ghazni. When the Ghurids drove the dynasty out of Ghazni, Khusrau Shah, father of Khusrau Malik, set up his government in the Punjab; Khusrau Malik sat on the throne of Lahore and his rule was established over the Punjab, some words of the sentence being illegible; and with his surrender to Sultan Shihab al-Din Ghori, the Ghurid sultanate in Hind began and the Ghaznavid government came to its end (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 24, folio 19). Bhandari writes that Khusrau Shah, having taken Lahore, governed the *wilayat* of Punjab until the end of his life, dying in 555, and that his son Khusrau Malik succeeded him in the government of the *wilayat*, his rule ending when he went to Ghazni in 582 AH (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 405).

The Ghurids followed. "A hundred and fifty years later in 1175 Mu'izzu'd-Din Muhammad bin Sam seized Multan, establishing firm control and Sunni rule over the southern part of the Panjab", in Rizvi's words (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 128, folio 110). In Bhandari's history of Multan, when Shihab al-Din Ghori conquered Hindustan, Multan too came into his possession (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 298). Dhillon writes that "Shahabuddin Muhammad Ghori captured Punjab in 1192" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 136, folio 151). After Shihab al-Din's death Taj al-Din Yalduz seized Ghazni and then came to the Punjab to take the throne of Hind from Qutb al-Din; in a second rising, in the reign of Sultan Shams al-Din Iltutmish, he took Multan, came to Lahore, plundered the Punjab without restraint, and crossed the Sutlej to advance on Delhi (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 25, folio 20).

The armies of Chingiz Khan's line follow. Kanhaiya Lal reports the author of the *Tuhfat al-Wasilin* as saying that Sultan Jalal al-Din Khwarazmi crossed the river of Sindh and entered the Punjab, and that when Chingiz Khan's grandson Quli Khan invaded the Punjab, the army of Delhi and the Mongols fought at Lahore (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 165, folio 168). Mughal is the book's own word for these armies. Lahori records, from a book he calls *Tazkira-e Hamidiya*, that Chingiz Khan's army entered Punjab in pursuit of the prince Jalal al-Din Khwarazmi and plundered the whole land of Punjab, and that the people of Lahore fought them for two months; he adds that the account is not well known and perhaps no one will believe it, but that it would be no wonder if it had happened (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 248, folio 236). After Jalal al-Din's defeat in November 1221 and Chingiz Khan's march back to the west, Rizvi writes, the Mongol's absence let Iltutmish (the name is misprinted in the text) consolidate power in the Punjab, and seven years later Iltutmish routed his rival Nasiru'd-Din Qubacha from Sindh (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 152, folio 134).

The Mongols shaped the province for two centuries. Eaton describes how, from the late tenth century onward and culminating in the thirteenth, Mongol pressures "forced waves of Turkish settlers into the subcontinent, many of them settling permanently in the urban centres of the Punjab, such as Lahore, Dipalpur, and Multan" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 217, folio 205). In the middle of the thirteenth century Balban, "in order to build a defensive bulwark against Mongol incursions, pursued the policy of strengthening and populating certain cities in the Punjab with large garrisons of Turkish elements", which added to Muslim settlement in the province's towns not only soldiers "but also thousands of refugees fleeing before the advancing Mongols"; Eaton's footnote names the garrisoned cities as "Lahore, Multan, Sirhind, Bhatinda, and Dipalpur" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 222–223, folios 210–211). Urged to conquer Gujarat and Malwa, Balban answered, in Bhandari's account, that since the Mongols were always raiding the *wilayat* of Punjab it was not right to go from Delhi to distant lands, and that to keep one's own kingdom strong and at peace was better than to raid the kingdoms of others (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 388). Chishti writes that Balban's nephew Sher Khan, posted to guard the Indus, saved Punjab from the Mongols' attacks and drove them out of Ghazni (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 810).

In Jalal al-Din Khalji's reign, in a year printed as 693 and uncertain, the Chingizid Mongols set out for Punjab with a great army; the sultan marched against them, they made peace, and their leader, a kinsman of Hulagu Khan, came in and became a Muslim with some other amirs; the sultan called him son, gave him a daughter and settled them at Ghiyaspur, which came to be called Mughalpura (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 370). Bhandari does not say where in Punjab the Mongols arrived (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 370). Kanhaiya Lal counts the blows that fell on the province one after another. The fourth came in the time of Sultan Jalal al-Din Firuz Shah Khalji, when a Mongol army sent by a Chaghatai Khan, ruler of Transoxiana, his name partly illegible, came into the Punjab; there was famine in the Punjab at the time, plunder and pillage ruined the whole region, and villages and towns were left deserted (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 26, folio 29). The sultan, named in the same passage, in an uncertain reading, as Shams al-Din Iltutmish, was away on the Gujarat campaign and could do nothing to help the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 26, folio 29). After his victory on the Sutlej the sultan resettled the villages of the Punjab that had been left empty and without a lamp, distributed the Tatars' loot among the poor subjects of the Punjab, and posted a large army on the frontier (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 27, folio 30). At the end of the Khaljis, Chishti writes, Ghazi Beg Tughluq, governor of Multan, gathered a great army in Punjab against Khusrau Malik (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 814).

The fifth blow fell in the time of Sultan Muhammad Tughluq. A Tatar army came from the west into the Punjab and both Dipalpur and Lahore were plundered; the rulers of the Punjab made every effort but fled to Delhi; when the army had gone the king came to the Punjab, worked hard to resettle its people, and beheaded the governor of Multan, whose name is printed uncertainly as Bahram, because the Tatar army had come into the Punjab at his signal (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 27, folio 30). Before Sultan Muhammad Tughluq's scheme of 1327, Rizvi writes, the Sultan raised "the slogan of jihad against the Mongols, who in previous years had devastated the Panjab and the region around Delhi" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 193, folio 175). The sixth blow came in the reign of Firuz Shah Tughluq, when the Tatar Mongol army arrived in the Punjab to plunder; the king, then besieging a place whose printed name cannot be read with certainty, came back to the Punjab and after a bloody battle drove the Tatar army out of it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 27–28, folios 30–31).

The seventh came in the reign of Sultan Muhammad Shah son of Firuz Shah, the rest of his name uncertain in the printed text, and with Amir Timur's invasion. A man named Sikha, of the Gakkhar tribe, both names uncertainly printed, came with a great host from the country belonging to Hazara, again an uncertain reading, entered the Punjab, drove the royal officers out of the country and laid waste thousands of settlements; Muhammad Shah sent Prince Humayun with an army to the Punjab; Amir Timur passed through the foothills of the Punjab and entered Delhi; and on his return the people of the Punjab, the word before the name partly illegible, laid before the Amir their complaint of Sikha's killing and plunder (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 28, folio 31). "Timur's invasion in 1398-99 devastated both the Panjab and Delhi", in Rizvi's words (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 290, folio 270). In Timur's time, Bhandari records, Jasrath Khokhar, the name uncertainly printed, escaped from Timur's captivity and reached Punjab (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 191). The eighth blow came in the reign of Sultan Mubarak Shah son of Khizr Khan, after the year 820 AH, the reading of the year uncertain, when Jasrath, Sikha Gakkhar's brother, also raised great disorder in the Punjab, and the king ordered Sultan Shah Lodi, governor of Sirhind, to take his army and drive Jasrath out of the Punjab; there the sentence breaks off at the turn of the page (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 29, folio 32).

Bhandari's account of the fifteenth century turns on the governorship of Tatar Khan. Before Tatar Khan's governorship, it is said, a flood surged through Punjab so that all the land from the Sutlej to the Chenab lay under water and towns and villages were destroyed, and the Mughals of Balkh and Kabul raided Punjab every year, so that the country lay waste and its produce was small (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 523). Under the Lodis, Tatar Khan was governor of Punjab, appointed by Sultan Bahlul Lodi; for fear of him the son of the raja of Jammu, his name uncertainly printed, left Jammu for Kashmir, and Sultan Muhammad Shah and then Sultan Fath Shah of Kashmir each sought his help (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 190). When Tatar Khan became governor of Lahore in the time of Sultan Bahlul Lodi, Rai Ram Deo Bhatti took the whole of Punjab from him in farm for five lakh *tankas*, the first numeral uncertainly printed (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 522–523). Recovery came under the Afghans, in Rizvi's account: "In the fifteenth century, the Panjab enjoyed a peaceful period due to Afghan rule. The Mongol invasions between the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries had devastated portions of the Panjab and the Afghans had successfully contributed to its re-settlement and urbanization by founding many new towns and promoting trade and commerce" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 408, folio 384). In the days of Guru Nanak's parents, Khalid writes in his reconstruction, "The jungles of Punjab were infested with thugs and dacoits who attacked innocent travellers" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 22, folio 4).

Meanwhile Multan went its own way. From the beginning of the year 600 AH to 855 AH, both numerals uncertainly printed, the *wilayat* remained in the hands of the sultans of Delhi, until in the reign of Sultan Muhammad Shah, of Khizr Khan's line, Hindustan fell into petty kingdoms and the governor of Multan declared his independence (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 298). It is said, Bhandari writes, that in the reign of Sultan Ala al-Din the Second of Delhi the *wilayat* of Multan passed out of his control to the Langah people (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525). Sultan Husain Langah took the fort of Shor from Ghazi Khan, and Chiniot, and in a short time held the country as far as Karor Kot and Dhankot, the names uncertainly printed (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 296). Tatar Khan, governor of Punjab, was sent against him by Sultan Bahlul Lodi together with Bahlul's son (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 296). After Bahlul, Sultan Sikandar, ruler of Delhi, made peace with Sultan Husain on the terms that each side would rest content with its own *wilayat* and not raid the other's border (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 295).

Babur ended that peace. Guru Nanak, Rizvi writes, came back from eastern India about 1520 "only to find the Panjab plunged into a number of significant political crises"; Babur "started unsuccessful negotiations with Ibrahim to annex west Panjab to Kabul", and "After two minor raids around the region between the Indus and Jhelum, Babur invaded a third time over the Panjab in 926/1520 reaching as far as Sialkot" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 409, folio 385). A footnote on the same page says that Daulat Khan "Gradually he also became the governor of the Panjab" and then invited Babur to invade India, in a year the printed text gives as 1523 with an uncertain reading (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 409, folio 385). Khalid writes that Guru Nanak's Babur Bani is "a long poem that Nanak wrote at the time the Mughal marauder Babur attacked Punjab, destroying the cities of Eminabad and Lahore on the way" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 15, folio not stated). Before the battle with Rana Sanga, Babur's ministers advised him to strengthen a few forts and withdraw to stay in the *wilayat* of Punjab; he refused (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 299).

Multan, too, came into the new dynasty's hands. Babur, after reaching Punjab, had granted Multan to Arghun with the charge that he strive for the prosperity of the country and the well being of its subjects (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 295). In a damaged passage Bhandari writes that Mirza Shah Husain Arghun ruled the *wilayat* for four years, and that for a period of eighty years up to 936 AH, the year uncertainly printed, it had been out of the hands of Delhi's rulers, after which it entered the dominion of Delhi and Prince Kamran Mirza took possession of it (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 294). In Babur's reign, he writes, the *wilayat* of Multan, long lost to the sultans of Delhi, was conquered by Kamran Mirza's good management and sword (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 298).

## The Province in History: Under the Mughals

Under the Mughal emperors the Punjab is a prince's *jagir* and a *suba*. Humayun attached Langar Khan to his brother Prince Kamran, in whose *jagir* the territory of the Punjab lay (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 76, folio 79), and in Bhandari's account Kamran's *jagir* was Lahore and Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 294). The struggles between the Mughals and the Sur passed through Punjab. Sher Khan by deceit gave Kamran hope that the *wilayat* of Lahore would be confirmed to him, so that Kamran withdrew from aiding Humayun (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 286). Later Kamran went to Salim Khan, son of Sher Khan, who had taken the throne of Hindustan after his father's death and was at that time in Punjab, the word for his presence there uncertainly printed; Kamran arrived at a town whose name is uncertain, and Salim Khan sent his son and Maulana Abdullah Sultanpuri to meet him (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 275).

In Islam Shah's reign, whose accession Bhandari gives as 952, uncertainly printed (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 265), the Niazis revolted: Azam Humayun defeated Khwaja Wais and pursued him, then laid hands on the towns and countryside of Punjab, plundering the cattle and goods of its inhabitants, so that a great disturbance arose in Punjab and a strange state befell those who lived there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 262). Islam Shah marched from Delhi with a heavy army and much artillery, and the Niazis held out in Dhankot until they were defeated (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 262). On Humayun's return to Hindustan, which Bhandari dates to 962, uncertainly printed (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 256), Shah Abul Maali was posted towards Lahore to manage the affairs of the *wilayat* of Punjab (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 255). Sultan Sikandar came out of the hills, seized the *parganas* of Punjab and began to collect their revenue as far as two *parganas* whose names are uncertainly printed; Shah Abul Maali, who treated his own soldiers badly, failed to repel him, and Akbar and Bairam Khan were sent (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 255). Akbar was on his way to Punjab against Sikandar when the news of Humayun's death reached him at Kalanaur, and Bhandari dates the accession to 963 AH (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 252).

The early years of the new reign were spent securing the province. Sultan Sikandar came out of the hills and began to collect revenue in the *wilayat* of Punjab, and disorder broke out there, so the emperor marched from Delhi towards Punjab (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 248). In the second year of the reign Mankot fell and Sikandar was driven out, and security returned to the *wilayat* of Punjab (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 247). Mir Muhammad Khan Kalan, brother of Atga Khan, and the other amirs posted in Punjab were sent against Sultan Adam the Gakkhar (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 238). At his fall, which Bhandari places in the sixth year of the reign, Bairam Khan took the road to Punjab and, after Bikaner, turned towards Punjab and reached it by a route whose names are uncertainly printed (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 245; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 246). After Bairam Khan's fall Muzaffar Khan became revenue officer of the *pargana* of Parsarur, which Bhandari calls a dependency of Punjab (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 205).

Zahur ul-Hasan Sharib records that the emperor Akbar, on his way to Punjab to put down a revolt, halted at Thanesar on Saturday 2 Muharram 980 AH; the name of the rebel is printed in a form that cannot be read with certainty (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 243, folio 239). His footnote gives the Persian *Anwar al-Arifin* as his source (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 245, folio 241). Bhandari speaks of a governor of Punjab in these years. Ibrahim Husain Mirza and Masud Husain Mirza turned towards Punjab by way of Dipalpur, the name uncertainly printed, and Khan Jahan, governor of Punjab, who was close to finishing his campaign at Nagarkot, made peace with the raja there and marched against them (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 228). When the imperial standards moved towards Punjab, the Mirzas came out of Sambhal, plundered and besieged the fort of Delhi, and the emperor went from Punjab towards Delhi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 231).

Around 985, the year uncertainly printed, Yusuf Shah of Kashmir fled by way of Jammu to Mirza Yusuf Khan, governor of Punjab, and with him and Raja Man Singh reached Fatehpur (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 186). Muhammad Hakim Mirza, Bhandari writes, crossed the Indus many times to trouble and harm the people of Punjab (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 203). Emboldened by the revolt of the amirs of Bengal, he came from Kabul with an army to Punjab, stirred up disorder and harmed the people of that country, but on the imperial advance he could not stay in Punjab and returned to Kabul (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 202). The emperor set out towards Punjab to put an end to Muhammad Hakim Mirza's disturbance (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 203), and Bhandari dates these years by the reign, among them the twenty sixth and twenty eighth regnal years (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 201; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 203).

During the emperor's residence at Lahore, Zain Khan Koka, sent to chastise the *zamindars* of the hills, brought all their rajas and rais to obedience by the force of the sword and the strength of good counsel and brought several of the rajas to court (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 170). Raja Basu, a *zamindar* of a place uncertainly printed that was dependent on Punjab, later rebelled (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 170). Bhandari speaks of these mountains as the hills of Punjab, but nowhere says where they lie (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 146; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 170). In Akbar's reign, he writes, Raja Todar Mal, the chief *diwan*, confirmed the *wilayat* of Multan to the Baluch, making it a strong vanguard between Khurasan and Hindustan and setting a firm barrier on the borders of both countries (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525). The Punjab appears in Eaton as two of Akbar's provinces: he reads Abu al-Fazl's statistics for the Lahore and Multan subas, compiled about 1595, though the opening of his sentence is damaged in the printed text and the scholar's name is misprinted there (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 225, folio 213). Annemarie Schimmel, writing of the Mughal age, finds that interest in Rumi's work "seems to have been common to all classes of society", for "the Mogul emperors (especially Akbar) were as fond of him as the simple villagers in Sind and Punjab" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 358, folio 327).

In Bhandari's narrative of the next reign, eight months after an accession that he dates to 1014 AH, uncertainly printed, Prince Khusrau rebelled (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 147; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 148). Dilawar Khan, governor of Lahore, held the fort against him, and Raja Basu, one of the chief *zamindars* of the hills of Punjab, who had just joined the emperor, was sent after the prince (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 146). The prince was captured at the end of Muharram 1015, the year uncertainly printed (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 145). Eaton calls the reign of Jahangir a time "when the Mughals enjoyed effective authority in the Punjab" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 231, folio 219). Khalid reports "some claims" that Guru Hargobind took service with Jahangir, serving him in the Punjab, the Guru's name being misprinted (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 234, folio 208).

In Shah Jahan's reign Prince Dara Shikoh, *jagirdar* of the Punjab, laid out the Chauk Dara Shikoh in Lahore (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 91, folio 94), and when the government of the Punjab was entrusted to him he put up a great many buildings (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 139, folio 142). The *suba* had its *diwans*: in Shah Jahan's reign, while Nawab Wazir Khan held the governorship, the Andhi Haveli was built by a Hindu amir who was *diwan* of the *suba* of the Punjab, and his descendants held it until the end of the Chaghatai rule (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 336, folio 343). Sorley, writing of Sindh under the Mughals, records two things of its northern neighbour in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries: that, as Sarkar remarked, "with the Punjab, Sind was the home of the leather industry" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 107, folio 98), and that against the general rule that Mughal public money went to tombs and palaces rather than to public works, the seventeenth century canal enterprise in the Punjab was an exception, though he does not say where in the Punjab it lay (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 122, folio 113).

The war of succession, in years printed as 1068 and 1069, both uncertainly, was fought partly across the province (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 64–66). Dara Shikoh had agreed with Shuja that while he himself prepared the means of revolt in Punjab, Shuja would march from Bengal as far as Allahabad, and that after success they would divide the kingdom and its wealth equally (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 76). Dara told Sulaiman Shikoh to reach Punjab by a road whose name is uncertainly printed, and the Barha sayyids advised going to Punjab by way of Saharanpur (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 74). At Lahore Dara sent letters of conciliation, full of kindness and promises of favour, through all the surrounding parts of the *suba*, urging the soldiers of that land, of every place, people and tribe, to enlist with him (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 77). The emperor's march towards the *wilayat* of Punjab came in the rains: crossing the Sutlej and the Beas without boats or fords was judged all but impossible, and most of the nobles did not advise moving in that season, yet he set out on the seventh of Dhu'l Qa'da, which Bhandari makes the seventeenth of Amardad, both numbers uncertainly printed (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 70). While the standards were in Punjab in pursuit of Dara, Shuja took his chance and set out for Allahabad (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 61).

The halt at Lahore was made to put the affairs of the *suba* of Punjab in order and to give the army rest and comfort (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 64). There Khalilullah Khan was appointed governor of Lahore with a reward of one crore *dams*, the figure uncertainly printed, and Lashkar Khan was moved from the *suba* of Kashmir to be governor of Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 64; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 65). The *faujdari* of the hills of Punjab went first to Khanjar Khan, *faujdar* of Bhera, the name uncertainly printed, after he left Dara (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 67), and then, at the Lahore halt, to Mir Miran, the name and his descent uncertainly printed, who had received the title of Mir Khan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 64). At the weighing festival of those months the governor of Lahore was honoured with robes of honour, the words of the gift uncertainly printed, and the page break leaves his name apart from his title (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 62; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 63). By the time the Guruship passed to Harkrishan, Khalid writes, "the Sikh community had expanded beyond the confines of the Punjab" into places as far as Kabul and a city whose printed name, perhaps Ghazni, is uncertain, which made central control by the Guru "increasingly difficult" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 181, folio 155).

## The Province in History: Decline, Sikh Rule, the British and Partition

The eighteenth century is, in every one of these books, a time of ruin, and they see the Punjab as a battlefield. Taufiq Rafat's translator's note places Bulleh Shah's life in "a time of great upheaval. The Mughal Empire was breaking up", when "the Punjab was being persistently rent by petty chiefs and marauders" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 16, folio xvi). The introduction to the selection writes that "After Aurangzeb, the halcyon days that the Punjab had enjoyed quickly came to an end", and that the badly defended North Western region of the empire became increasingly subject to foreign invasions and inner quarrels (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 26, folio 6). It dates "the beginning of the misfortunes of the Punjab" to the period of Muhammad Shah, when the decay of Mughal administration "gave rise to small local tyrannies which lasted into the 19th century" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, pp. 26–27, folios 6–7). "Ever since the fall of the Mughal Empire conditions had deteriorated in Punjab", Khalid writes, and Ahmad Shah Abdali, the Afghan king, "had already completed two raids of Punjab and beyond" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). The annotator of Charles Frederick Usborne's translation of Waris Shah writes that "The Punjab has often been the battleground of invading forces from the North", that the campaigns of Mahmud of Ghazni are famous, and that the poet may be thinking of Ahmad Shah Abdali, whose capital was Kandahar and who in 1748 began a series of invasions of India through the Punjab (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 8, folio 7). In his epilogue, which he dates to the year 1180 Hijri, Waris Shah writes: "Of all the wretched Punjab I am most concerned for Kasur" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 56, folio 55). The annotator glosses the lament: "The latter half of the eighteenth century, to which Waris Shah is referring here, marked the decline of the Mughal Empire in India, and was a period of great confusion and disturbance" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 57, folio 56).

In Kanhaiya Lal's account the *suba* still had its Mughal rulers in these decades. Nawab Zakariya Khan Bahadur, Shahnawaz Khan and other rulers of the Punjab lived in Mughalpura (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 90, folio 93), and Mir Sayyid Bhikari Khan, his name uncertainly printed, was raised by Muhammad Shah's order to the office of deputy of the *suba* of the region of the Punjab, together with a second office whose title cannot be read with certainty (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 146, folio 149). The last Mughal decades are told through the province's defence against Ahmad Shah Abdali. In Muhammad Shah's time Jani Khan, with his brother Mir Muin al-Mulk, defeated Ahmad Shah Abdali, who went back to Kabul; after he had gone Mir Muin al-Mulk ran the administration of the Punjab in his own way, and Jani Khan remained commander of the army of the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 270, folio 273). Kanhaiya Lal gives this account under the tomb where Jani Khan is buried, and prints his death in the year 1194, the reading uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 270, folio 273). Mir Muin al-Mulk, "popularly known as Meer Manu", "was the governor of Punjab", Khalid writes, and was "constantly pestered by the Sikh insurgent groups Misl, which had sprung up all over Punjab"; Abdali had "agreed an annual tax stipulation which the governor was responsible of paying to him, for the right of governing Punjab" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).

Schimmel writes that Nadir Shah "first conquered Sind and the Panjab before he reached Delhi in 1739", and that the influence of Ahmad Shah Abdali was greater in "the northern areas of present day Pakistan" than in Sindh (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 30, folio not stated). After Nadir Shah's invasion, Rafat's introduction says, "Marauding Sikh bands formed themselves into robber baronies and imposed their own system of extortion on the Punjab farmer", and Ahmad Shah Abdali's "treatment of the Punjab weakened the government in Lahore and delivered the various doabs into the hands of the Sikh" confederacy of the misls (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 27, folio 7). As the Mughal empire broke into pieces around 1750, in Schimmel's account, the "Sikh threatened the Panjab", and "Nadir Shah's successor, Ahmad Shah Durrani, invaded the Panjab several times, coming from his native Afghanistan" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 50, folio 48). Sorley quotes Sir Alfred Lyall's account of how Ahmad Shah of the Abdali Afghans, after Nadir Shah's assassination, took Afghanistan and from that base seized the whole of the Punjab between 1748 and a closing year that the printed text renders imperfectly, read as 1751 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 17, folio 8). Bulleh Shah, the introduction continues, "was fated to see a second invasion of the Punjab by Ahmad Shah Abdali in 1748 and then a third in 1752" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 27, folio 7). Lahori's modern editor writes that Ahmad Shah Abdali began his various attacks on Punjab in 1161 AH, 1748, and died in 1187 AH, 1773 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 277, folio 265).

The Persian and Urdu books tell the same years through the lives of saints and scholars. Chishti writes that in the reign of Alamgir the Second the Afghans attacked Punjab and took Multan and Lahore, while the strength of the Sikhs began to grow, and that Ghazi al-Din then resolved to conquer Punjab again (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 851). When Ahmad Shah Abdali first came to take Lahore, Lahori relates, and after plundering Punjab the king had gone back to Kabul, a man of Lahore told the saint Haji Muhammad Said Lahori that the Afghans had looted his house and carried his daughter off to Kabul (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 214, folio 202). Ghulam Murtaza Qasuri, the editor writes, wearied of the Sikh disorders in Punjab and migrated to Peshawar, and when Ahmad Shah Abdali came into Punjab he put a legal question to its scholars, Ghulam Murtaza among those who answered (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 152, folio 140). When the plundering Sikhs stirred up their pillage in Punjab, Shaikh Karam Shah Qureshi, who had lived in Lahore, went away to Lucknow, and on his way back he was killed by bandits near Shahjahanpur (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 190, folio 178).

Shackle reads the same century through Bulleh Shah, who died in 1758 and lived "at a time of great social upheaval" in the Punjab, "as the central authority of the declining Mughal empire came under increasing challenge from armed rebellion by the Sikhs and from local notables" establishing their autonomy; these unsettled conditions, he writes, are occasionally reflected in the poetry (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 8, folio vii). "In the years to come, when plunder became routine in the Punjab, Bulleh Shah had no choice of factions", Rafat's volume says, and the Sikh marauders under Banda Bairagi "were to lay waste the whole of the province excluding Lahore and Qasur", taxing the common peasant to raise armies on both sides (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, pp. 42–43, folios 22–23). Shackle glosses one verse as "Taken to be an allusion to the Sikhs, whose guerilla horsemen ultimately destroyed the Mughal authority in Panjab" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 193, folio 168), and remarks that "The Ranghars were a tribe of Muslim Rajputs who achieved prominence in eighteenth-century Panjab, and who were proverbial for their arrogance" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 179, folio 154). He reads the allusive verses of one *kafi* as appearing "to refer to a separation of Bullhe Shah from Shah Inayat due to the troubled conditions then prevailing in Panjab", and points to another *kafi* "for the allusions to the disturbed state of contemporary Panjabi society"; the twelfth century of the Hijri era of which the poem speaks, he explains, corresponds to the eighteenth century of the common era (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, pp. 165–166, folios 140–141). In that poem, in Shackle's translation, the poet says of the new century: "The door of doom and torment has opened, and Panjab is in a bad state. It has been struck by fear of the pit of hell." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 52, folio 27). Shackle also warns that this critique of the social upheavals of the early eighteenth century, which so interests modern readers, "represents only a very small, if vividly expressed, part of his poetry" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 15, folio xiv). "As Bulleh Shah lay dying in 1758 at the age of 78, the rape of the Punjab was proceeding apace", in the words of Rafat's introduction (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 28, folio 8).

Sorley follows the same years from Sindh. When the East India Company re-entered Sindh in 1758, "Afghans, Sikhs and Marathas were at each other's throats" in the plains of the Punjab (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 46, folio 37), and in 1760 "The Afghan and Maratha armies were threatening each other in the Punjab" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 70, folio 61). His chronological table of events in Indian history records under 1756 to 1758, a span whose digits are broken by a space in the printed text, that the "Sikhs rise in the Punjab" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 201, folio 192), and under 1758 that the "Marathas occupy Punjab" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 202, folio 193). The end of Muslim rule came with famine and plunder. When the rule of Islam passed from the Punjab and the Sikhs gained strength, Kanhaiya Lal writes, the first place the Sikhs plundered was the butchers' quarter of Lahore (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 89–90, folios 92–93). When the Sikh period came, a severe famine struck the Punjab, after which the descendants of the Andhi Haveli's builder left Lahore (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 336, folio 343). When the turn of the age overturned the Islamic state and the Sikhs took Lahore, the whole region of the Punjab was laid waste by famine, after which Mir Jawad's descendants left Lahore and the officials took over his haveli (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 342, folio 349).

"In 1797, when the grandson of Ahmad Shah Abdali, Shah Zaman Durrani, following the footsteps of his grandfather, attacked Punjab", Khalid writes, a Sikh band "met him on the banks of the Chenab" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). Ranjit Singh's Punjab is the province at its widest in Kanhaiya Lal's book. His preface calls him the Maharaja, ruler of the region of the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 9, folio 4), and under his government the whole region of the Punjab came into his hands (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 112, folio 115). He ruled the Punjab for forty years, and besides the Punjab the country of Kashmir, the Derajat and other lands were also in his possession (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220). Ranjit Singh is for Khalid "Maharaja Ranjit Singh (1780-1839), the erstwhile ruler of Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 42, folio not stated), "the Sikh ruler of Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 159, 196, folio not stated) and "the Sikh king who ruled Punjab before the British took over" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 206, folio not stated), "crowned as the Ruler of Punjab" in 1801 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated). In a land ruled, he writes, for over a millennium by Afghans, Mughals and other non-Punjabis, "Ranjit Singh was the first Punjabi to rule Punjab", and he brought stability "in Punjab which lasted a good forty years" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 208, folio not stated). Schimmel, writing of a later generation, remembers Shah Ismaʿil Shahid and Sayyid Ahmad of Bareilly as "known for their brave fight against the Sikh, who had occupied the whole of the Punjab and part of the northwestern frontier" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 403, folio 372).

The infantry of Maharaja Ranjit Singh, ruler of the Punjab, whose number the printed text gives as not less than seventy thousand in wording that is not clean, drew its pay through Bakhshi Bhagat Ram (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 96, folio 99). Ranjit Singh struck coin in the name of Moran, the courtesan, and her coin ran through the whole of the Punjab; Moran Shahi rupees and paisa are still kept as keepsakes (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 179, folio 182). The book says the same in another place: her stamp was struck on rupees and paisa, and Moran Shahi paisa and rupees still exist (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 47, folio 50).

The books remember Ranjit Singh's Punjab through its saints. It was well known throughout Punjab, Chishti writes of the saint Subah Shah, that the outward king was Maharaja Ranjit Singh (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 85). Lahori calls him Maharaja Ranjit Singh, ruler of Punjab, and writes that he had complete faith in the *majzub* Mastan Shah and offered him thousands of rupees, which Mastan Shah refused (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 246, folio 234). Chishti writes of Jaswant Rao Holkar and Amir Khan and Punjab in 1805, the year uncertainly printed, but the verb is garbled in the printed text, so it is not certain that he says they came into Punjab (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 580). All the people of Lahore, and indeed of Punjab, knew the quarter of the Faqir family by a name that the printed text gives uncertainly (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 585).

On the day Raja Ranjit Singh died, Lahori relates, the *majzub* Taj Shah foretold the ruin of the kingdom of the Sikhs: it would last nine years more, and then the Firangis would be masters of Punjab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 244, folio 232). After Ranjit Singh's death his own son, Maharaja Kharak Singh, sat on the throne of the government of the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 221, folio 224; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 586). After the deaths of Maharaja Kharak Singh and Kanwar Nau Nihal Singh, Rani Chand Kaur became mistress of the kingdom of the Punjab and was seated on the *gaddi* on the proposal of the Sandhanwalia sardars, who became the managers of the state, while Raja Dhian Singh, the wazir, went off displeased to Jammu (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 443, folio 450). Dhian Singh told Sher Singh that, God willing, he would soon be ruler of Punjab (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 588). Maharaja Sher Singh, ruler of the Punjab, whose reign lasted two and a half years, is in Kanhaiya Lal's words that emperor of the region of the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 210, folio 213).

Chishti tells the end of the Sikh state under Maharaja Dalip Singh. In 1848, the year uncertainly printed, a disturbance broke out in Punjab once more, and he follows it through Multan, the country of Hazara and four hard battles; on 29 March 1849, the date uncertainly printed, the whole country entered the British dominion (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 592). Then, Kanhaiya Lal writes, the English became masters of the Punjab, after the end of Sikh rule (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 33, folio 36). Bakhshi Bhagat Ram's *jagir* had not yet been released when the government annexed the country of the Punjab, after which the Chief Commissioner fixed his pension (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 68, folio 71). When Maharaja Dalip Singh was exiled and English rule over the Punjab was complete, the troops left the Badshahi Mosque (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 141, folio 144). For Khalid, "Punjab became a British colony almost immediately after his death eventually leading to its division at the time of Partition", and Dalip Singh was "the last Maharaja of Punjab, before the British took over" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 209, folio not stated).

The British came late to the Punjab. Khalid dates their "arrival, which in Punjab was in the middle of the nineteenth century", and calls the nineteenth century the one "which is also known as the Sikh period" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 57, folio not stated); "Punjab was colonized almost a century after the colonization of Bengal by the British, as a result of which English language is not as institutionalized here" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 105, folio not stated). For Shackle the colonial conquest marks the next turn: "Only with the British conquest of Panjab in the 1840s did the literary culture of the Sufi tradition come to be overlaid by new patterns of modernity", as Persian was quite rapidly replaced by Urdu and English as the language of education, administration and elite literature (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, pp. 11–12, folios x–xi). Rajab Ali Khan was Mir Munshi at the time of the conquest of the land of Punjab by the British government, with the title Mir Munshi of the dominions of Punjab, and he presented himself to the Lieutenant Governor of Punjab (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 137, 141; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 142). With British rule a legal settlement was established in Punjab (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 777).

In Kanhaiya Lal's own day Lahore is the capital of the *suba* of the Punjab. The court of the Lieutenant-Governor and his secretaries sits there, and its authority runs over the whole *suba* of the Punjab: chiefs, rajas and maharajas, commissioners and deputy commissioners are all subordinate to it; the second court is that of the Financial Commissioner and Collector of the Punjab; and the Chief Court gives the final decision in civil and criminal cases, hears the appeals from the commissioners of the *suba*, and must sanction every capital sentence (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 60, folio 63). The book names Egerton as Lieutenant-Governor of the territories of the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 148, folio 151) and McLeod, his name uncertainly printed, as a former Lieutenant-Governor of the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 213, folio 216). Robert Montgomery, Lieutenant-Governor of the territories of the Punjab, was long a ruler of the Punjab and rose from the office of commissioner to Lieutenant-Governor; Colonel Lawrence was Lieutenant-Governor and chief ruler of the Punjab at the time of the Delhi rising, and by his great labour the administration of the Punjab held together and he kept raising a large army and sending it to Delhi, for which he was made Governor General and Viceroy of India; and for the two halls named after them the amirs and officials of the Punjab subscribed most willingly (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 399, folio 406). After the uprising of 1857, in Scott Kugle's brief mention, Imdadullah "fled through the Punjab and Sindh, visiting Sufi shrines and eventually sailing from Karachi into exile in Mecca" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 280, folio 259).

The province's institutions gathered in its capital. The Central Jail at Lahore was built as a general jail for the prisoners of the whole region of the Punjab: every district has its district jail for short sentences, but prisoners with long terms are sent here from every district (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 410, folio 417). Women prisoners from all the districts of the Punjab come to the women's prison, built in 1868, the reading of the year uncertain, under the author's own supervision (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 409, folio 416). The Lahore museum building was put up in 1864, when the government first held an exhibition of the wonders of the region of the Punjab, and the great states of the Punjab and its sardars and *jagirdars* sent jewels, textiles, arms and manufactures to it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 402, folio 409). In January 1882, the reading of the year uncertain, an exhibition of the industrial arts of the Punjab was held in the School of Art building under the principal of the School of Art, whose name is uncertainly printed (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 387, folio 394).

The colonial state remade the land. In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, Khalid writes, "the British connected the five rivers of Punjab with each other through canals, turning several acres of jungle land into agricultural land"; "thousands of new villages, referred to as chaks were established, and farming families from all over Punjab were invited to settle"; the former inhabitants, who were mostly nomads, could not prove their ownership of the land, and "Their land was snatched away and allotted to these migrants" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated). Jungli, "which literally means 'people of the jungle', is a popular term used to refer to the indigenous Punjabis, as opposed to those who settled here after the construction of the canal colonies by the British during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated). Boivin sets the Punjab inside a larger colonial economy: "During the Pax Britannica imposed by the colonial rule, the exchange of goods and products was reinforced between the different regions of the north-western Indian subcontinent, including Punjab, Rajasthan, Gujarat and Sindh", and beyond the merchants there were "networks of circulating knowledge, and especially religious knowledge" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 196, folio not stated).

In the twentieth century, among the new middle class of the colonial period, the formulation of nationalist understandings in the Punjab was no simple process, Shackle writes, "given the complexities created by the simultaneous currency of three closely related languages, Urdu, Hindi, and Panjabi, whose differences were exploited to sharpen the rival cultural identities of Muslims, Hindus, and Sikhs, respectively" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 12, folio xi). The province's history in these books ends with its division. Khalid speaks of "the wave of the second migration that took place after the division of Punjab between India and Pakistan" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated). In the south, he writes, "violence here during the partition never scaled the heights it did in the other regions, therefore the resentments between communities that seeped in after 1947 were not that serious" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 31, folio not stated).

## Saints and Pilgrimage

For Lahori the province is first of all a land of saints' tombs. He includes a saint when his tomb lies in the soil of Punjab: having given the Naqshbandi chain down to Khwaja Baqi Billah of Delhi, he explains that since none of the elders before Khwaja Baqi Billah had a tomb in the land of Punjab, he has left out their detailed accounts and begun with Khwaja Baqi Billah (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 127, folio 115). The other books follow the saints into that land, their orders across it and the people who came to their shrines.

Rizvi makes the Ghaznavid conquest the beginning of the Sufi settlement of the province: "the annexation of the Panjab by Mahmud of Ghazna and its incorporation into his empire prompted many sufis to settle in the area" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112). Khattali, he writes, ordered his disciple Shaikh Husain Zinjani to move to the Punjab, and later sent Hujwiri after him; Hujwiri reached Lahore in about 1035, while Sultan Masud I reigned in Ghazna (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112). Lahori also records how Islam spread in Punjab, from a source his editor distrusts. In the time of Sultan Masud of Ghazni, he writes, citing *Tuhfat al-Wasilin*, Shaikh Muhammad Ismail Muhaddith Lahori, a Bukhari sayyid, settled in Lahore and taught *hadith* and *tafsir*; he was the first of the callers to Islam to come to Lahore, thousands became Muslims at his hands, and through him the religion of Islam spread in Punjab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 191, folio 179).

Two books tell traditions of the Bibis of Bibi Pak Daman. Shemeem Burney Abbas reports, as tradition and with her own hedges, that the Bibi Pak Daman, whose shrine is in Lahore, "involved the men in disseminating Islam among the lay non-Muslim populations in the Panjab", the women speaking to the male scholars and their audiences from behind the veil, and that "seven hundred male scholars came with the Bibi Pak Daman to Hind, as the land was known then" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 161, folio not stated). Kanhaiya Lal continues an account which, he says, the author of the *Hadiqat al-Awliya* gives on the authority of a *Tazkira* whose title is uncertainly printed, of the time when in the year 615 AH the infidel Mongols invaded the Punjab in pursuit of Sultan Jalal al-Din Khwarazmi, and the people of Lahore were put to death for resisting them (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 305, folio 308). That invasion is the setting of the tradition that the Bibis of Bibi Pak Daman prayed to be taken into the earth (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 305, folio 308).

The family of Baba Farid "came from Kabul, but migrated to the Panjab in the middle of the twelfth century" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 156, folio 138), and it was the Ghaznavid Sultan of the Punjab who made Farid's father Qazi of Kahtwal (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 157, folio 139). For Eaton, Baba Farid's settling at Ajudhan was "the religious dimension of a very slow transformation of the Punjab's cities from a Hindu to a Turkish-Islamic orientation" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 217, folio 205). Schimmel writes that in Nizamuddin's time "Sufism became a mass movement in northwestern India", and that "A number of Punjabi tribes still claim to have been converted by Fariduddin Ganj-i Shakar" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 381, folio 350). Of Baba Farid, Waris Shah writes in his invocation: "When Shakarganj made his abode at Pakpattan, the Punjab was delivered of all its troubles" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 2, folio 1). His annotator objects: "Much as one would like to agree with Waris Shah this is not historically correct. The Punjab has had plenty of troubles even after Baba Farid honoured it by making it his home and his place of burial!" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 2, folio 1).

Eaton follows the great shrines into the life of the countryside. From the fourteenth century through the Mughal period, he argues, "in Punjab and Sind, shrines of great Sufis attracted and integrated pre-agrarian and non-Muslim pastoral clans into their ritual, socio-economic, and political orbits"; the descendants of the Sufis married into the leading families of the clans, and the clans were "passing from a life of pastoral nomadism to one of settled wheat agriculture" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 48–49, folios 36–37). The practice of writing amulets for the people, he finds, "has not changed from the thirteenth century to the present; even now the successors of Baba Farid and his assistants, in common with those of other shrines in the Punjab, continue to write ta'widh for the masses" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 218, folio 206). He quotes Major Aubrey O'Brien's essay of 1911 on the Muslim saints of the western Punjab, which he holds to be equally true of medieval shrines: "The general idea of our riverain folk seems to be that the Deity is a busy person, and that his hall of audience is of limited capacity", and "Thus, all human beings require an intervener between them and God" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 216, folio 204). One of his chapters asks how the shrine "served to promote a conceptual conflation of Heaven and Earth among Muslims of rural Punjab" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 17, folio 5).

Rizvi follows the orders, and the wanderers who troubled them, through the province and beyond it. In the age of Mongol domination of Central Asia and Iran, wandering Qalandariyyas and Jawaliqis passed through Multan, where they "often came into conflict with Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya"; "From the Punjab, the displeased qalandars moved to Delhi creating scenes wherever they saw a khanqah. Some went as far east as Bengal" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 325, folio 303). In the time of Makhdum-i Jahaniyan, he writes, the Suhrawardiyya "spread from Uch to Gujarat, the Panjab and Kashmir", reaching even the Delhi of Sultan Firuz Shah Tughluq, whose name the printed text garbles (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 299, folio 277).

The Chishti order spread in Hind and Punjab from Khwaja Muin al-Din Hasan Sijzi of Ajmer, Lahori writes (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 85, folio 73). Boivin writes that the Chishtiyya "is dominant in Punjab and North India" while it was not widespread in Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 217, folio 204), and that it "is usually considered to be one of the most powerful Sufi orders in Northern India and Punjab" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 227, folio 214). Carl W. Ernst and Bruce B. Lawrence name the Punjab among five regions, with Gujarat, Bengal, Uttar Pradesh and the Deccan, where the early saints, and the Chishti masters above all, mark "a particular kind of South Asian spiritual authority that is not only political and religious but also cultural", and where they describe "a spectrum of spiritually charged spaces or sites" that are "devotional loci for Indian Muslims from several classes, and also for Hindus"; they say nothing there that is specific to the Punjab (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 110, folio 97).

Lahori traces how the Qadiri line reached the province. Of that line, whose ancestors had left Baghdad in the time of Hulagu Khan, the first to come to Punjab was Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus Uchchi Jili Gilani, who came by way of Khurasan to Multan and settled at Uch (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 40, folio 28). His modern editor adds, on the authority of Ali Asghar Gilani, that he entered Uch in 887 AH; the date is the editor's, not the author's (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 40, folio 28). By Guru Nanak's day, Rizvi holds, the Qadiris "had made the Panjab the main centre of their activities" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 420, folio 396). The Naushahi order flourished in Punjab through Shah Maruf Chishti and Qadiri, or through Sayyid Mubarak Haqqani, named just before him, for the text does not settle which of the two is meant (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 50, folio 38). The Naushahi way, whose faqirs in Punjab number in the thousands, began with Shaikh Haji Muhammad Qadiri, known as Hazrat Naushah Ganj Bakhsh (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 70, folio 58). The Punjab also drew learned men from afar: in Shah Jahan's reign Abul Khair came from Baghdad touring the countries, and because the climate of the Punjab pleased him he settled in Lahore, where his madrasa stood at Garhi Shahu (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 362–363, folios 369–370).

The Chishtis rose again in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. "The Nizami Chishtis came to prominence in the eighteenth century, principally in Delhi, the Deccan, and Punjab, through masters who were related to but in tension with local political leaders" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 119, folio 106). Shaikh Sulayman Taunsawi, who died in 1850, "consolidated the order by stressing learning through establishment of institutions", among them the academy at Taunsa on the upper Indus and "other religious schools throughout the Punjab"; Muhammad 'Aqil and Gul Muhammad Ahmadpuri, "both of whom founded religious schools in the southern Punjab that provided free instruction and lodging to hundreds of students", did the same (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, pp. 121–122, folios 108–109). In the footnote of Lahori's editor the Chishti Nizami Fakhri line spread most widely in Punjab through Khwaja Sulayman Taunsawi, born in 1184 AH and died in 1267 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 120, folio 108). The line of Shah Mirra, Ernst and Lawrence write, "resurfaces in the nineteenth century in the figure of Hafiz Sayyid Muhammad 'Ali Khayrabadi, marking the expansion of the Nizami branch of Chishti Sufism through disciples of Shaykh Sulayman Taunsawi in the upper Punjab" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 71, folio 57).

Smaller orders had small followings. In Chishti's day the Rasul Shahi order was not numerous: he writes that in all Punjab it had no house but this one, and that only four faqirs of the line were living at the time of writing, with no other house, *takia* or *khanqah*, though he then names one more house near Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 145). He dismisses the claim that the Jalali faqirs are Chihil Tani as belonging to the talk of the ignorant of Punjab (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 623). Of one boy, a *sajjada nashin* foretold that he would be the *subahdar* of *faqr* in Punjab (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 84).

The Punjab is also the home ground of Guru Nanak's journeys as Dhillon retells them. At Haridwar, in an episode dated 1504, Guru Nanak faces "west, in the direction of Punjab" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 79, folio 87), and in the same episode "Punjab is very far away" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 80, folio 88); at Prayag he is called "Nanak, the great Guru from Punjab" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 102, folio 113). By his return to Talwandi in 1506, a year imperfectly printed, "there were very few in the Punjab who did not know of him and his teachings" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 123, folio 137).

On his second journey Guru Nanak "crossed through Rajpurana and the south-west Punjab till he came to Multan" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 164, folio 180); "Nanak was by now a well-known figure in Punjab" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 167, folio 183), and the Sufis of Multan, in Dhillon's telling, had shown him a "patronizing tolerance" as long as he preached elsewhere in the Punjab (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 168, folio 184). In the more modern accounts his third journey, of 1514 to 1516, went as far as Tashkent "and then back to Punjab via the Kashmir Valley" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 55, folio 61). The fourth journey closed, around the time of Babur's third incursion into India, with a south-eastern route back to the Punjab (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 224, folio 246). In the Puratan Janamsakhi the fifth, of 1523 to 1524, "lasted about one year and covered the towns and regions within Punjab" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 55, folio 61).

The death of a saint could move the whole province. At Hazrat Husain's death, Chishti writes, there was hardly anyone in Punjab who did not grieve, and in the mourning for Husain a clamour like that of Karbala rose in Punjab; some words of the passage cannot be read (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 70). Shah Hussein, writes his translator Naveed Alam, "continues to command great reverence in Punjab" more than four hundred years after his death (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 6, folio 6).

The annotator of Waris Shah records the beliefs and devotions of the countryside. "According to popular superstition in the Punjab countryside" *pirs*, *fakirs* and *jogis* can, by the force of their piety, make the Moving Finger of Destiny cancel what it has written (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 31, folio 30). Lighting lamps in a mosque, even with ordinary oil, "is still a well-known way of expression of gratitude to God for His favours", and "Punjabi mothers still promise lamp-lighting in mosques when they pray for their children" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 41, folio 40). Bhang "is a favourite with the mystically inclined, particularly among Muslims and Sikhs", and is still taken "in the old Punjab area by wandering mendicants and many of those seeking spiritual exaltation or sexual pleasure" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 12, folio 11). The verse's dawn at the mosque, he writes, "is how a morning begins even now in a Punjab village" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 5, folio 4).

Not every holy place in the Punjab belongs to a named saint. Pnina Werbner and Helene Basu, introducing Lukas Werth's chapter, point to "the countless shrines to unknown saints found on the outskirts of Punjabi villages", whose anonymity is, in Werth's suggestion, a key feature of "their placement as mediators between the dangerous forces of nature and cultural continuity" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 23, folio 12). Werth himself notes that Khidr "is often called Zinda pir in Punjab, because he also is thought to have disappeared alive" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 101, folio 90). Abbas defines the *dhammal* as "The vigorous ritual dance performed at Sufi shrines in Panjab and Sind" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 180, folio not stated).

Robert Rozehnal shows how a Sufi master's domain could be drawn on the map of the province in the twentieth century. Shahidullah Faridi's last will and testament says that Wahid Bakhsh, whose *khilafat* was publicly confirmed in 1975, "has initiated halqa zikr and guidance in Bahawalpur" and "should spread the silsila in this place only"; Rozehnal reads this: "Here Shahidullah effectively delineates the southern Punjab as Wahid Bakhsh's personal spiritual domain (wilayat)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 90, folio 80). The will itself names only Bahawalpur; the southern Punjab is Rozehnal's reading of it (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 90, folio 80).

In the present the books place the heart of shrine devotion in the south of the province. Khalid "travelled to southern Punjab, believed to be the hub of shrine culture in the country" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated), and writes that "Saeen is a title used to refer to a feudal lord or a spiritual leader from lower Punjab and Sindh, where the shrine culture thrives" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated). "The southern region of Punjab, where Uch Sharif lies, has more traces of religious and cultural syncretism compared to the northern and central region, where there is a greater prevalence of education and hence religious intolerance" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 277, folio 251).

## Communities

The books weigh the meeting of the province's faiths in different ways. Dhillon describes the medieval province as divided: "For over three hundred years, Islam and Hinduism existed side by side in the Punjab region in a state of constant conflict" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 16, folio 18). Eaton observes that a map of South Asia's religious demography shows that "Islam took root much more readily in Bengal or the Punjab than in upper India, the area with the deepest traditions of Indo-Muslim rule and patronage" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 14, folio 2). In both Bengal and the western wing of Punjab and Sindh, he argues, "the growth of Muslim societies correlated with the adoption of sedentary agriculture", and "both regions were still frontier societies where Hindu religious values and the hierarchic social ideals of Brahmin priests had not yet deeply penetrated" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 48–49, folios 36–37). So "by the eighteenth century large communities of Muslim peasants had appeared in both Punjab and Bengal", whereas in India's heartland conversion was far less significant, and "Unlike Punjab or Bengal, Malabar lay beyond the orbit of Mughal imperial influence and the Persianized culture associated with it" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 49, folio 37).

The Jats are at the centre of Eaton's account. He asks "why and how it was that Jat tribesmen, as they abandoned pastoral nomadism for settled agriculture between the thirteenth and nineteenth centuries, gradually took on an Islamic identity" in the western Punjab (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 16, folio 4). Quoting a dissertation then in progress by Emily Hodges, he describes the towns of the western Punjab as living by intensive cultivation along the river flood plains, and beyond them "a vast tract of sparsely populated land, the barr country between the five rivers", "but little cultivated owing to the very scanty rainfall", which supported "pastoral nomadism based primarily on the herding of goats and camels"; the herders moved down to the rivers in the hot dry months and back to the barr after the rains, "never leaving the Punjab plains and covering at the most a distance of less than one hundred miles", in "a symbiotic relationship with the settled peoples of the riverine area" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 223, folio 211). The Jat pastoralists, "before their entry into the Punjab, had not yet been integrated into Hindu society"; "By the time of Baba Farid, however, in the 1260s, we find the first mention of Jats occupying the Punjab proper", Babur "described Jat pastoralist groups in the Sind Sagar doab and Sialkot regions", and "The northward movement of Jat clans and their settlement in the grazing tracts of the Punjab is thus well supported by contemporary evidence" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 224–225, folios 212–213). In Akbar's statistics, Eaton notes, of the 186 Punjabi parganas whose dominant zamindars are known, 82, nearly half, were held by Jat groups; "Only in the extreme western Punjab, in the Sind Sagar doab, where the Janjua Rajputs and Ghakkars dominated, were the Jats conspicuously absent as zamindars" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 225, folio 213). The Jats whom al-Biruni had called low Sudras in the eleventh century had become "the dominant agrarian caste in nearly half of the Punjab", and "by the 1650s, as today, the very word 'Jat' had become virtually synonymous with peasant agriculturalist"; Eaton gives Irfan Habib's explanation, "the introduction of the Persian wheel in the Punjab and its extensive use by Babur's time", which made "much of the arid western Punjab's naturally rich soil capable of supporting the cultivation of food crops" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 225, folio 213).

Conversion followed migration. The Jats' movement north from Sindh to Punjab, their rise from low Sudras to zamindars and their change from herders to farmers were accompanied, in Eaton's argument, by their conversion to Islam, and "of those Jat and Rajput groups that became Muslim in the medieval period, the vast majority of them claim to have been converted either by Baba Farid or by his contemporary, Baha' al-Haqq Zakariya (d. 1263), whose tomb is in Multan city" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 227, folio 215). The Punjabi Muslim clans that claim Baba Farid include the Bhatti, Chhina, Dhudhi, Dogar, Gondal, Hans, Jo'iya, Khokhar, Siyal, Tiwana, Watt, Kharral and Ara'in (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 227, folio 215). In court depositions of 1935 to 1938 before the Lahore High Court, "many of the major endogamous clans that are spread throughout the southwestern Punjab, mainly as peasant agriculturalists" claimed conversion by Baba Farid, among them the Wattu, the Siyal, the Dogar, the Kathia, the Bhatti and the Tiwana (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 241, folio 229). From the genealogies of the Siyals of Jhang District, which run from the late fourteenth to the early nineteenth century, Eaton concludes that "religious conversion among Punjabi clans was very slow indeed", "not only slow, but probably unconscious as well", in "a period long before either British census officials or zealous reformers began urging Indians to place themselves into sharply differentiated religious categories" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 234–235, folios 222–223). Kugle adds the weavers in an endnote: "New Muslims (naw-musalman) from weaver castes converted to join the Islamic community in Punjab slightly later than in Hindustan, where the mystic poet Kabir displays the same kind of Sufi-bhakti synthesis a century earlier" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 329, folio 308).

The province's peoples moved and settled across it. The Mongols who made peace with Jalal al-Din Khalji became Muslims and were settled at the place that came to be called Mughalpura (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 370). Under the Langah sultans, Bhandari writes, Malik Sohrab Dodai, father of Ismail Khan and Fath Khan, came with his tribe from the country of Kech and Makran, and Sultan Husain gave him a *jagir* from the fort of Kot Kardaz to Dhankot, the names uncertainly printed (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 295). Many more Baluch followed, the rest of the country along the Indus was assigned to the Baluch, and little by little the land from Sitpur to Dhankot came to them, so that from those days it has been in the hands of Malik Sohrab's descendants; Bhandari does not say where these places lie (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 295). In another passage he gives the Baluch who had come from Kech Makran a *jagir* from Karor Kot to Mankot (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525).

Famine moved people too. A few years before Kanhaiya Lal wrote, famine fell on Kashmir and thousands of famine stricken Kashmiris came into the Punjab, hundreds of them reaching Lahore itself (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 75, folio 78). The famine of the land of Punjab is remembered in Chishti's life of Mai Bhagi. It came shortly after her marriage at the age of seven, a famine known as the two and a half ser famine, in which the whole country was thrown into confusion and a mother would not look after her son, and because of it Mai Bhagi and her mother left their town, whose printed name is uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 789).

Other communities moved out of the province. Jürgen Schaflechner reports, after Cook, that "The Bhatia and the Khatris, for example, arrived in the eighteenth century from cities in the Punjab", Multan among them, "and were ultimately absorbed as Lohanas", and, after Thakur, that migrants from Rajputana and the Punjab who followed Lohana occupations acquired membership of the Lohana caste (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 80–81, folio not stated). Sorley, for his part, explains the Punjab tale of Suhini and Mehar in Sindh by a migration: "it must have come into Sind through the immigration from the Punjab of those Hindu races which form an important element in the population", as the Census of India volume for 1931 relating to the Bombay Presidency makes clear (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 270, folio 261).

Kanhaiya Lal's Punjab is a country of Hindus and Muslims serving the same state. Under the Mughal *suba* a Hindu amir served as *diwan* of the *suba* of the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 336, folio 343), and in the time of Nawab Zakariya Khan Bahadur, *suba* of Lahore, the brothers Diwan Lakhpat Rai and Jaspat Rai were *diwans* of the government of the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 337, folio 344). Of their wealth he tells that at his son's wedding Lakhpat Rai gave a gift to every Hindu and every Muslim in Lahore, a gift whose printed description, which seems to include a weight of sugar measured in seers, cannot be read with certainty (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 337, folio 344). Khalid writes that Meer Manu "cast the battle between the state and insurgents as a war between Muslims and Sikhs, even though there were several Muslims in the Sikh groups and many non-Muslim soldiers in the army of Meer Manu", and that "The Sikh groups did not control much land but because of their anti-status quo politics the lower rungs of the society supported and protected them" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).

The Muslims of the Punjab under Sikh rule appear through the plea of the Fakir sahibs. Pleading with Ranjit Singh for the mosque by the Baoli, they told the Maharaja that nowhere in the mosques of the whole Punjab did the mullas give the call to prayer in a loud voice, and they offered a bond that the mulla would not give the call (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 148, folio 151). Ernst and Lawrence see Chishti biography take sides in Shaikh Sulayman Taunsawi's time, as British colonialism began to take hold of India: Taunsawi "urged his followers to participate in resistance against Sikh aggression in the Punjab", so that "apparently for the first time in the annals of Chishti history, we have spiritual biographies drawn into partisanship on behalf of one political group"; yet "Notable, however, is the effort of Shah Sulayman to maintain cordial relations with Punjabi Hindus even while he opposed their Sikh neighbors" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 121, folio 108). The province's old ruling class kept its habits into English rule: silk drawstrings went out of use and everyone wears cotton, but the amirs of the Punjab are still accustomed to silk ones (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 47, folio 50).

Other books describe a Punjab in which the boundaries between communities were porous. Schimmel calls it a strange fact, "more visible in Sind than in the Punjab", that Hindus shared in the Muslim orders: "Some saints were claimed by both communities" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 418, folio 387). Shah Abdul Latif of Sindh, she writes, "as did his Panjabi contemporaries and his successors in Sind, accepted the Hindus who were striving on the way to perfection as true pilgrims in the eternal Light" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 267, folio 267). The annotator of Waris Shah describes a village life shared across faiths. Judging by their names some of Hir's friends are Hindus, and "This symbolises the atmosphere of the village in the old Punjab, where the village community as a whole was regarded as one social unit irrespective of caste or creed"; even a sweeper woman would be addressed as daughter, sister or mother (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 20, folio 19). "It may appear strange that a Muslim family should depend on Hindu Brahmans for fixing a marriage but this was by no means unusual in the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 26, folio 25).

Boivin follows the Sikh tradition outward from the Punjab and back. "The Nirguni tradition was more influential in Northern India, especially in Punjab, where the Sikh tradition historically derived from the Nirguni tradition of the Bhakti" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 89, folio 76). He suggests, as a possibility only, that "the Aroras imported this cult from Punjab during their migrations in Sindh in the eighteenth century", the cult being the Nanak Panth, "a form of Sikhism that does not recognize the authority of the Khalsa" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 43, folio 30). In the second half of the nineteenth century, he writes, came the first attempt to rationalise and make uniform the Sikh tradition, and "This process of normalizing the Sikh tradition was centred on Punjab, and it did not concern the Sindhi Nanakpanthis who were heterodox Sikhs" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 53, folio 40).

The land's people appear in the books by caste and clan, and the annotator of Waris Shah sets out the castes and tribes of "the old Punjab". "Ranjha is still a well-known caste of the Jats in the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 3, folio 2). "Khojas are a well-known tribe of Hindu converts to Islam in the old Punjab who are mainly engaged in trade" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 7, folio 6). "The Gujars are a famous tribe of the old Punjab who keep animals and sell their milk", and towns such as Gujranwala, Gujrat and Gujar Khan are named after them (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 19, folio 18). Shackle sets the legend of Hir and Ranjha "in the world of the Jat pastoral tribes of western Panjab" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 20, folio xix).

The annotator and Abbas say almost the same thing of the Siyal. The Siyal tribe, the annotator writes, "is one of the few tribes in the Punjab which allows women to inherit property under customary law" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 20, folio 19). Abbas writes that "The Jats of the Panjab are zamindars (landowners), known for practicing widow-marriage", and that the Siyal tribe "is one of the few tribes in the Panjab that allow women to inherit property under customary law" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 121, folio not stated). Her glossary calls the Siyal, Hir's family, "powerful landowners of the Panjab" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 188, folio not stated), names the Kheras as "A powerful clan of landowners in the Panjab mentioned in the Hir-Ranjha myth" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 183–184, folios not stated), and adds that "Some sections of Indian society, such as the Siyal Jats of the Panjab, permitted a widow to remarry, and she was not forced into satti" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 189, folio not stated). Of the Ara'in, she writes in her text that "The arāĩ in the Panjab were gardeners or small cultivators who worked on their own land" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 108, folio not stated), while her glossary calls them "A caste of landowners in the Panjab" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 178, folio not stated).

Marriage customs fill many of the annotator's notes. "Brides in the old Punjab wore coloured (and colourful) garments", while white clothes were by tradition worn by widows, particularly among the Hindus, and childless Muslim widows wore black or white; "The social pattern is now changed" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 28, folio 27). "A traditional Punjab marriage still retains the symbolism of ‘marriage by capture’", the bridegroom made to look like a general, and the bride's side now resisting only with abusive language (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 28, folio 27). Betrothed boys and girls of "the rural Punjab" do not see each other, and "After the ‘mangni’ or betrothal, a girl does not see her fiancé until marriage" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 48, folio 47). The Arsi "was one of the favourite ornaments of a bride in the old Punjab", and the newly married girl was not expected to do household work for a couple of months, as is "still the case in the less sophisticated parts of the Punjab countryside" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 26, folio 25). The ‘Mehndhi’, a fringe of intertwined strands of hair round the forehead, marks a virgin girl in the old Punjab and disappears at the wedding (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 43, folio 42).

Chishti records marriage customs of his own day. Following the custom of the Muslims of Punjab, the bride's family in the story of the marriage of Hazrat Naushah's daughter demanded a hundred rupees in customary dues (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 484). He writes that the killing of daughters was an old custom among the descendants of Guru Nanak through Lakhmi Chand, who counted themselves the noblest of creatures in Punjab and would give a daughter to no one, because, he explains, the custom of that country is that the girl's father becomes subordinate to the boy's family (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 685).

Of the household and the village the annotator gives many details. "Old houses in the Punjab countryside had a big courtyard with a number of living-rooms and bedrooms round it", and a joint family of brothers living with their parents was quite common (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 16, folio 15). "A spinning-wheel (charkha) used to be the inseparable companion of every woman, old or young, in the rural Punjab", and "The spinning-wheel was an important link in rural society. Women spun cotton yarn, and the village weaver made it into cloth" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 17, folio 16). Spinning parties "were a famous institution of the Punjab village", where young girls, generally unmarried, competed in spinning skill and scandalous conversation (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 4, folio 3, note 14). Printed red scarfs with white flowers were thrown at each other by young girls at play, "A favorite game with young girls in the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 21, folio 20).

Food and offerings follow the same pattern. "Lassi is a favourite drink in the Punjab countryside", and "Washing the hair with curds is still well known in the Punjab countryside" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 4, folio 3). Gur, raw sugar, "is well known as a ceremonial offering in the Punjab countryside", and an invitation accompanied by it cannot be trifled with (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 33, folio 32). Rice cooked in ghee and sprinkled with sugar is the common ceremonial dish, and "The institution is alive in the countryside of the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 23, folio 22). Millet "is even now considered inferior food in the Punjab countryside" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 44, folio 43). Basmati rice "is grown In the central districts of the old Panjab", the best in the subcontinent and now a valuable export from Pakistan (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 26, folio 25). "Sawan and Bhadon are the two rainy months of the year in the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 26, folio 25).

Other customs complete the annotator's picture. "Mauj Din is now a common name In the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 4, folio 3), and "Jugglers and their bears are still a familiar sight in the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 40, folio 39). "Fingers in their mouths is a typical female gesture common in the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 14, folio 13), and "A snub nose is not regarded as a point of beauty either in men or women in the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 39, folio 38). A mother's exaggerated threat "is by no means unfamiliar in the Punjab villages even now" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 14, folio 13). "Dandasa" bark served as the lipstick of "the rural Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 48, folio 47); shaving with soap "is a much later invention in the Punjab", and village barbers still commonly shave with water alone (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 55, folio 54); and the ‘pearha’ is "an article of furniture common in Punjabi homes" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 56, folio 55). The tufted turban "beloved of the rural aristocracy of the Punjab" is a recent development, said to have been invented by the *mirasis*, "or hereditary bards of the Punjab in the late nineteenth century" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 11, folio 10).

The introduction to Rafat's selection reads the province's history into its people. The period of duress after the Mughals "formed the character of the" inhabitants "of the region", and the crisis of character in the Punjabi of today "is traceable to this period of duress in the history of his land" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, pp. 26–27, folios 6–7). To illustrate this lapse it quotes a satirical questionnaire by the columnist Janus, "Are you a Punjabi?", in which the Punjabi response to learning that one's ancestors came from Central Asia is "What the hell! I am a Bokhari and superior to all the low stock inhabiting the Punjab" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 236, folio 216; Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, pp. 237–238, folios 217–218).

Reform movements have pressed on the shrine culture of the countryside. In the early twentieth century Muhammad Ilyas, founder of the Tableeghi Jamaat, "worked in undivided rural Punjab, exhorting Muslims to 'purify' their religious practices which he argued were corrupted due to the influence of the dominant Hindu culture", and Muslim reformists like him "founded reformist movements of their own, aggravating the communal tension in Punjab" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 10, ch. 4, folio not stated). Tableeghi preachers, Khalid writes, "focus on southern Punjab and rural Sindh where syncretistic religion held sway", and "The Tableeghi Jamaat has managed to permeate the social fabric of the major cities of Punjab", so that "shrines similar to the ones at Kamalia and Apal Muri are likely to face a major onslaught due to their 'impure' religious practices, in coming years" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 10, ch. 4, folio not stated). He cites Tahir Kamran: "In recent years, the focus of the Deoband has been on South Punjab and Sindh where there is an ubiquitous saint and shrine culture", and "the people of the South are attracted towards the Deoband school of thought, and feel victimized by the feudal nature of society intertwined with the saint and the shrine culture" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated). The Deobandi school, he adds, "gained popularity in the lower and middle echelons of the urban Punjabi Muslim populace, a feature that they have retained in contemporary Pakistan" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated).

In *Walking with Nanak* Khalid reports the view of Ayesha Siddiqa, whose name is imperfectly printed, that organisations of the Ahl-e-Hadith school, whose names are also uncertainly printed, "have seeped into the social fabric of southern Punjab, a region that has historically rejected such strands of Islam and adopted a syncretistic version of religion. Such Sufi shrines are a symbol of that strand of Islam", and that this is changing as they open seminaries in the rural areas and draw in unemployed young men (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 284, folio 258). A recent paper by Tahir Kamran on Deobandi Islam in the Punjab, he adds, finds such organisations popular with the young because "they offer an explanation for their current oppression", and their first step is "to target such shrines and their guardians, who have become immensely rich feeding off the oppressed classes" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 284, folio 258).

Partition changed who lived beside whom. Locals, Khalid finds, "generally tend to be more embracive of practices 'non-Muslims'" than the migrants who came after the division of the Punjab, and he has "seen several gurdwaras and temples being taken over and destroyed by the migrants", while where the population is largely indigenous "such 'non-Muslim' religious spaces are better looked after", with exceptions (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated). "Compared to Central Punjab, Southern Punjab has been historically tolerant towards other non-Muslim faiths, which is why a significant Hindu population continues to live here" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 31, folio not stated).

Khalid's account of the province's religious minorities today is drawn from his own travels. Of Pakistan's non-Muslims other than the Ahmadiyya, "the majority are Hindus living in the 'faraway' 'peripheral' regions of Southern Punjab, inner Sindh and Baluchistan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 12, folio not stated). In Khalid's words, "unlike the Hindus, the Christian community has a formidable presence in the Punjab", and Hindus "are a smaller community in the Punjab and can therefore be ignored" by political parties; most Hindus are scattered in other provinces, "with only a few in Punjab. The Christians on the other hand have an overwhelming majority in the Punjab (among the minorities)" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 87, folio not stated). "The religious distinctions between Hindus and Christians have become blurred in urban Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 28, folio not stated). "A lot of Hindus in Punjab do this, passing off as Muslims or Christians by taking up non-Hindu names. This is a survival technique in a hostile environment" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 39, folio not stated), and where minorities in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa are visible in society, that is "hardly the case in Punjab, which is much more monolithic" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated). After the demolition of the Babri Mosque on 6 December 1992, "Well-known Hindus living in the cities of Punjab had to hide for several months after the incident" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 22, folio not stated).

In most villages of the province, Khalid finds, Christians are confined to menial work. At Shanti Nagar, where Christians own the land, he finds "a unique case study in the social fabric of the Punjab. Whereas in most of the villages across the province, Muslims are the dominant landowners, while Christians are confined to menial jobs associated with the untouchable caste" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 123, folio not stated); in an ordinary village, he writes, they would be treated as untouchables (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 124, folio not stated). He lists the attacks at Sangla Hill in 2005 and at Gojra and Bahmniwala, Kasur, in 2009, "all of which happen to be located in Punjab", and adds that "The story of a few burnt pages from the Quran found in local mosques seems to happen often" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 125, folio not stated). An informant he calls Daria Boatwala, who moved to Lahore in the 1980s, tells him: "here in the Punjab, the situation is much different. There are hardly any Zoroastrians here and I have never come across any Hindu. The only Christians that you see are those who are cleaners and sweepers" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 142, folio not stated).

Khalid also follows the province's Sikhs and Nanak Panthis. Nanak Panthi Sikhs, in Khalid's account, "belong to the areas of South Punjab and Sindh", with temples "in Sindh and Southern Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 197, folio not stated), and the langar workers at Dera Sahib are "low-caste Nanak Panthis from Southern Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 201, folio not stated). Of the devotees of Guru Nanak who did not join Guru Gobind Singh's Khalsa, he writes: "There are thousands of such Sikhs living in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Punjab and Sindh in Pakistan, and they are referred to as Nanak Panthi Hindus. In their temples, along with pictures of Hindu deities, one also finds a picture of Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 98, folio 80). At the November 2011 festival at Nankana the kirtan singers "have covered their heads with a loose turban, a popular style in the villages of West Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 230, folio not stated).

Many of the Sikhs Khalid meets in the province are Pathans who came from the tribal areas: "Moving from tribal areas to the heart of Punjab, an overwhelming majority of them could not even speak the language. They remained confined to their little enclaves" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 203, folio not stated). The Sikhs of Nankana "remember this internal migration from KPK to Punjab", which gives "a good picture of the different socio-economic conditions in both of these provinces" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 235, folio not stated). Boota Singh of Nena Kot, who told him that his people were not from the Tribal Areas but Punjabis, was "the first Pakistani Punjabi Sikh I had interacted with. Earlier, I had only encountered Pathan Sikhs who had moved to Punjab in the past few years" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 83, folio 65).

The modern books look at the hardships of the countryside. Omar Kasmani writes that "Being a child in a poor rural family in central Punjab, one who is regarded as intersex means being given to particular bodily horizons, oriented to certain futures more than others", and that the most likely path for such a child would have led to a life among the *khwaja-sara* (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 73, folio 56). He cites Lubna Chaudhry's study of women and poverty in parts of Sindh and Punjab, which finds that "unmarried girls in their teens and early twenties are most vulnerable", pointing to "discriminatory food portions within the family and limited access to health care" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 191, folio 174).

The province's shared festivals belong to its farming year. "Punjab, still predominantly an agricultural society, upholds agrarian culture and traditions, of which the harvest season is the ultimate source of happiness", Khalid writes, but in the days after the creation of Pakistan, among the country's elite, "several cultural festivals like Vaisakhi, Lohri and Basant were given up" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). "Lohri like Vaisakhi or Basant, is an ancient festival of the Punjab that commemorates the change of season", kept "on the 13th of January, which is generally the last night of the month of Poh", with a village bonfire, dry fruit, folk songs, children collecting wood and kheer left to cool overnight (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 192–193, folio not stated). After the creation of Pakistan "this indigenous festival of the Punjab died down", and "the Sikh and the Hindu minorities of central Punjab also stopped this celebration" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 193, folio not stated). It was "earlier celebrated on a large scale throughout the province. It is ironical though that this Punjabi tradition shunned by Punjab is now being revived by a group of Pathan men residing in Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 194, folio not stated).

## Language, Poetry and Music

The Punjab of these books is above all a land of mystical poetry in its own tongue. Schimmel writes that "Reliable tradition traces the use of the native language for mystical poetry both in Sind and in the Punjab back to the fifteenth century", and that Madho Lal Hussein's allusions to the stories of Hir and of Sohni and Mahiwal seem to inaugurate, or to continue from unknown beginnings, "a tradition typical of the religious poetry of the two provinces: the insertion of folk tales into the context of mystical thought" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384). In another book she suggests that "In the Panjab, popular mystical poetry may have emerged as early as in the days of Fariduddin Shakarganj", the Chishti saint of Pakpattan who died in 1265, and that "Numerous folk songs and ballads existed but were never committed to writing" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 16, folio 12). In a third she places the development of folk tales as media for mystical instruction by the poets of Sindh and the Punjab only in the centuries after the death of the historian Badaʾuni (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 154, folio 136). Persian poetry, meanwhile, was common ground in Guru Nanak's time, in Rizvi's argument: "all literate Panjabis, both Hindus and Muslims, at the time of Guru Nanak were conversant with" 'Attar, Rumi and Sa'di (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 420, folio 396).

The *kafi* carried the mystics' teaching. Naveed Alam writes that "Kafis, the stanzaic poems couched in folkloric themes and techniques, were easy to understand and memorize, and thus served as the ideal means to disseminate the mystical teachings among the illiterate masses dependent on the oral transmission of knowledge", and that "Even to this day, most Punjabis and Sindhis are more likely to have heard a kafi than read one" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 15, folio 15). He sets Shah Hussein in the Punjab of Akbar, recalling a rebellion in Punjab led by Abdullah Bhatti, known as Dullah Bhatti, "who was captured and hanged in public on Akbar’s orders" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 14, folio 14). He notes that Najm Hosain Syed's play *Takht L’hore (Throne of Lahore)* "extrapolates the socio-economic conditions of Punjab" in Shah Hussein's time (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 14, folio 14).

"The golden age of Muslim Panjabi poetry", in Schimmel's account, "is the late 17th and the 18th century, e.g., the time when the province was invaded one time after the other by Mahrattas, Sikhs, and, in between, by the liberating Muslim armies" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 17, folio 13). Comparing Shah Abdul Latif of Sindh, she finds the similarities with "Panjabi mystical folk poetry, which reached its apex with Bullhe Shah during exactly the same period" conspicuous, and notes that Siraiki "forms the transition to Panjabi", so that the imagery of all three linguistic areas is closely related, as one would expect among the people of mainly rural provinces (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 154, folio 154). Shah Abdul Latif, she writes, represents the rural mystical tradition "in which his contemporaries in the Panjab and, to a certain extent, in the Pashto speaking areas were rooted" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 267, folio 267).

Schimmel draws the Punjab and Sindh together as one literary landscape. "The Punjab and the Indus valley have a common tradition of mystical folk literature" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 414, folio 383), and "The Punjab and Sind show close similarities in the types of mystical literature that nourished there"; Punjabi and Sindhi "were excellent media to express mystical feelings, though not mystical theories", their themes "sung time and again in forms inherited from the Indian past", not in the Persian *ghazal*, forms "closely connected with indigenous musical modes" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 416, folio 385). The mystics of the north west of the Subcontinent took up the attack on the mulla, and such verses "appealed particularly to the peasants and the illiterate" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 416, folio 385). "In both provinces, a preference for the motif of spinning and weaving can be observed, a natural propensity in a cotton-growing country"; both also produced hymns praising the great masters of the orders, above all ʿAbd al-Qadir Gilani, in imagery that "could easily be understood by everybody in the village" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 417, folio 386). In *Pain and Grace* she adds that "A topic which is not rarely found in the poetry of Sind and the Panjab is the spinning of cotton, one of the most important occupations of women", sung to traditional folk melodies, whose printed name the text renders uncertainly as Kapa'iti, that the womenfolk sang while spinning (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 160, folio 160).

The same poetry turned to the Prophet and to the soul. While the urban poets of India praised the Prophet in grandiose hymns, "their contemporaries in the plains of Sind and the Punjab wrote tender little poems or instructive Golden Alphabets to instil love for him into the souls of the villagers" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 222, folio 204). The tradition of *Ahmad bila mim* "has been repeated in high literature as much as in popular mystical poetry, be it among the Turkish Bektashis or among the mystical bards of Sind and the Punjab" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 451, folio 420), and the story of Muhammad ibn al-Hanafiyya, "a son of 'Ali by another wife than Fatima", "is found in the Panjab, in Bengal, South India, and Malaysia" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 284, folio 266). Schimmel also finds in Sindh and the Punjab "a special aspect of mystical appreciation of women" rarely met elsewhere in the Muslim world, the longing soul represented as a woman, so that the tragic heroines of the Sindhi and Punjabi folk tales, Hir, Sassi, Sohni and many others, "represent the human soul in search of the beloved" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 465, folio 434).

In the south the province's language shades into its neighbour. Boivin describes Siraiki as "a language spoken in Northern Sindh and Southern Punjab" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 220, folio 207), "mainly spoken in Southern Punjab, with Multan as capital" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 216, folio 203). Khalid finds the same blur on the road. "The boundary between Sindh and Punjab becomes a blur as we drive through Bahawalpur"; "This is where the cultures of Punjab and Sindh merge. Some of the roadside hotels are called Punjab Hotel, while others are Sindh Hotel"; Siraiki is "an amalgamation of the two languages", and his guide, the historian Iqbal Qaiser, tells him that "The Siraiki spoken in Multan is closer to Punjabi, while here it is more like Sindhi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 276, folio 250).

The love of Hir and Ranjha is the province's own story. For Kanhaiya Lal the Punjab had a story and a language of its own: in his preface he writes that the tale of Hir and Ranjha is on the lips of good and bad, young and old, rich and fakir in the country of the Punjab; many books on it exist in the Punjabi language, but no one other than the Punjabis could profit from them, which is why he wrote his own version in Persian (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 10, folio 5). The people of Punjab, Bhandari writes, compose wonderful verses and designs on the love of Hir and Ranjha and sing them (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 512–511). Kugle sets the legend against the devotional world further east: "Punjabi poets and their eager audiences poured into Hir and Ranjha the same erotic emotion that was invested in Radha and Krishna in regions closer to the Ganges. The story of Hir and Ranjha became a favorite Sufi device for conveying the value of erotic passion in spiritual life", and Shah Hussein's poems explore divine love "in terms Islamic, Hindu, and indigenous Punjabi" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 228, folio 207). Schimmel writes that the romance was elaborated in more than a hundred versions in Punjabi, Urdu, Sindhi and Persian and given its final form in the late eighteenth century by Waris Shah: "It is his verse which is sung, time and again, wherever Panjabis meet" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 171, folio 153). "The visitor who stays in a Panjabi village will certainly find somebody to sing for him the old romance of Hir Ranjha in the classical form given it by Warith Shah" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 414, folio 383), and "even today a Panjabi will be deeply touched when this moving story is sung" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 17, folio 13).

The romance lives on in performance and in custom. Abbas writes that Waris Shah "is said to have recited it with great emotion among his speech communities in the Panjab", and that "Many renowned folk artists in the Panjab, such as Reshma, Zahida Parvin, Nazir Begum, Sabiha Khanum, Tufail Niazi, and Iqbal Bahu, have sung it" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 123, folio not stated). Hir's lament as she is carried off in the palanquin "is traditionally sung at almost every Panjabi wedding at the time of the bride's departure to her husband's home" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 124, folio not stated), and the palanquin itself carries a strong emotional charge in Punjabi ritual (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 206, folio not stated). In conversation Khalid asks: "Isn't Heer Ranjha part of Punjabi culture? It is the most celebrated folk story here. For centuries it has been sung and dramatized"; Mirza Sahiban is "another famous folk love legend of Punjab", yet "the story of Mirza Sahiban is such a blemish on the legacy of spiritual love stories of Punjab, that it was said that these two brought an end to the era of love legends" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).

Other tales travel across the Punjab's borders. Waris Shah's annotator writes that Sohni and Mahiwal "has its setting In The old Punjab", Sohni belonging to Gujrat on the Chenab, and that this tale and Sassi and Punnoo have travelled beyond their original setting to be popular in both the old Punjab and Sindh (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 12, folio 11). "The Chenab, in the folk culture of Punjab, is known as the river of love", the river of Sohni and Mahiwal and of Ranjha's Takht Hazara (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 9, ch. 3, folio not stated). "Sohni is one of the favorite folk tales in both Sind and the Panjab" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 156, folio 156); Abbas hears the Sohni myth sung in Sufi poetry in all the regions of Sindh and in the Siraiki speaking area of the Punjab (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 126–127, folios not stated), and calls Mahiwal "The hero in the Sohni-Mahival myth of the Panjab" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 184, folio not stated). Sorley calls "The story of Suhini and Mehar" "a Punjab tale which has found a home in Sind", one of the stories of Shah Abdul Latif's Risalo that came from the Punjab (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 270, folio 261; Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 271, folio 262).

The poets made Punjab itself a figure in their verse. Usborne's Waris Shah uses the invasions of the province as similes: "as if armies from Kandahar had swept over the Punjab", "looted him as armies have looted the Punjab", and "today the Punjab has fallen into the hands of Kandaharis", which the annotator takes to refer to Ahmad Shah Abdali (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 7, folio 6; Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 44, folio 43; Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 47, folio 46; Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 48, folio 47). When the poet writes "as if the armies of the Punjab had fallen upon Hindustan", the annotator calls it "an imaginary situation", since the armies of the Punjab are known to have defended Hindustan rather than fallen upon it; even the later Sikh power was contained on the Sutlej by the Anglo Sikh treaty of 1809, and "The poet’s flight of fancy may have been inspired by a mistaken sense of local patriotism" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 7, folio 6; Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 8, folio 7).

Bulleh Shah's lament for the province is the best known of these lines. Rafat's translator's note says that "The full bitterness of Bulleh Shah’s ‘Punjab has gone to the dogs’ will only come through against this backdrop" of upheaval (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 16, folio xvi). In Rafat's translation of the poem Come See Me Once in a While the lines run as follows.

*Now hell has opened its bogs. / Punjab has gone to the dogs. / This hell is the nethermost hell.* (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 149, folio 129)

The province's saints and rulers are named in verse as well. Lahori's book closes with a chronogram for the year 1292 AH by his son, Mufti Ghulam Safdar Lahori, which speaks of the province's saints.

جو خدا کے دوست تھے پنجاب میں
حال ان کا اس سے ہوتا ہے عیان

*A chronogram for the book, on the friends of God in Punjab whose lives it makes known.* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 282, folio 270)

Chishti ends his account of the annexation with a *qita* on Dalip Singh's departure from Punjab, of which he gives two hemistichs likening Dalip Singh to the candle of Punjab and to a new flower from the garden of Punjab; one word of the first hemistich is uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 592). The *suba* of the Punjab is named in verse on the buildings of its governors too. On the northern *mihrab* of the mosque of Nawab Zakariya Khan, west of the mazar of Madho Lal Hussein, a tile inscription that Kanhaiya Lal copied carries these lines (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 262–263, folios 265–266).

زبدۂ بارگاہ او نواب
زکریا خان صوبہ پنجاب

The verse names the mosque's builder, Nawab Zakariya Khan of the *suba* of the Punjab, and the inscription places the building in the reign of Muhammad Shah (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 262–263, folios 265–266).

The province's minstrels carried the songs of every faith. In Guru Nanak and Mardana's time, Khalid writes, the Mirasis went from city to city singing of local chieftains, and landlords showered them with gifts; Mardana "knew by heart several folk stories of Hindu deities and Muslim saints that he would sing during different festivals of Punjab" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 121, folio 103). As a younger man "he would roam the cities and villages of Punjab, singing folk songs and legends", the most popular of them Puran Bhagat's (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 184, folio 158). Two musical instruments, one of them the *nad*, the other's name uncertainly printed, were "popular in the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 39, folio 38). The poetry of the saints lived on in song. In Khalid's reconstruction of the time of Shaikh Ibrahim, Baba Farid's twelfth successor, Farid's poetry "was still sung by qawwals at the shrine and by fakirs in the cities of Punjab" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 142, folio 124). Ernst and Lawrence write that "The name Qalandar continues to be intoned in popular qawwali lyrics of the Punjab", without saying on which side of the border (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 36, folio 22).

Regula Burckhardt Qureshi treats the Punjab as one of "the two principal regions of the Qawwali tradition", set beside Uttar Pradesh, whose tunes can be told apart mainly by their rhythmic setting: "A predominantly quantitative emphasis characterizes tunes from Uttar Pradesh, while Panjabi tunes tend to be rhythmically organized according to 'quality' or stress", the drum articulating the metre more strongly in the Punjab, in keeping with each region's drumming tradition (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 73, folio 53). Her musical example of the Punjab style is a song sung by "a Panjabi Qawwal, Rahmat Khan" (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 57, folio 37), which she takes to exemplify "the 'Panjabi style'" (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 58, folio 38). She does not say whether her Punjab is the Indian or the Pakistani side, or both (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 73, folio 53).

Books reached the province late. In Sikh times, Kanhaiya Lal writes, no printed book came from Hindustan into the Punjab, and scribes wrote out manuscript books and sold them (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 42, folio 45). Shackle notes that the lyrics of Bulleh Shah and the other Sufi poets, "who lived at different times and in different areas of Panjab", were never systematically preserved by an organised faith community, and were for the most part recorded and gathered into printed collections only in the late nineteenth century (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, pp. 9–10, folios viii–ix), when publishers in Lahore brought the older Sufi poetry into the new print culture (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, pp. 11–12, folios x–xi). In the Punjab, he writes in his translation of Shah Abdul Latif, Urdu was installed as the immediate successor of Persian, which did not happen in Sindh, administered from Bombay rather than Calcutta (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 25, folio not stated). Schimmel writes that the literary heritage of Punjabi "was rarely continued by Muslims after 1800", though in the twentieth century a number of mystical treatises were written and published in Punjabi, among them a partial translation of Rumi's *Mathnawi*; the language "was used almost exclusively by the Sikhs as their literary medium, whereas the Panjabi Muslims resorted to Urdu", "though they enjoyed and still enjoy listening to the simple mystical verses and ballads in their spoken mother tongue" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396).

Of the language itself the modern books are gloomy. The introduction to Rafat's selection writes that "Punjabi literature in Pakistan is in a state of neglect. The language has become a purely spoken medium", and that the Punjabis can no longer read it in written form (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 50, folio 30). "In terms of written sources, the Punjab and indeed the whole of the Indus basin, has always" seemed "a wasteland to scholars" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, pp. 46–47, folios 26–27). Writing of how Baba Farid's and Shah Hussein's poetry survived through Sikh collectors, it blames a lack of intellectual tradition among the Muslims of the Punjab for indifference to the classics, adding that "Bulleh Shah was treated no differently" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 47, folio 27). "After this the Punjab awaits a proper" scholarly "assessment of its greatest poet, a tryst that may never take place given the regional antecedents in the realm of letters" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 49, folio 29). The Janus questionnaire it quotes observes that "All regional personalities are moulded by a deep love of their idiom", and that while a Sindhi can name the greatest living Sindhi poet, and so can the Pathan and the Baluch, the Punjabi cannot; Punjabi alone, it says, is not taught in schools and has no dictionary (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 236, folio 216; Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, pp. 237–238, folios 217–218).

The province's language has a low standing among its own educated people, in Khalid's experience. In Pakistani Punjab, he writes, Punjabi "is looked down upon. It is considered to be a language of the boorish and the uneducated". His elite private school fined any student who spoke Punjabi, even in the playground, and "the educated elite of Punjab ends up disowning, in fact, ridiculing anything Punjabi", the poetry of Baba Farid, Guru Nanak, Shah Hussein, Waris Shah and Bulleh Shah among it (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 128, folio 110).

## Shrines and the State

Rulers tied themselves to the province's shrines and its land from early on. Eaton writes that "the Delhi sultans and Mughal emperors granted huge tracts of rich land" for the ritual ceremonies of the great Sufi shrines of Punjab and Sindh, while irrigation "permitted a rapid growth in wheat production" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 48–49, folios 36–37). For Ernst and Lawrence, Akbar used the Chishti saints, Shaikh Salim and "Shaykh Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar in the Punjab", to rule his provinces: "Through a twofold, redoubled Chishti loyalty, Akbar could anchor his imperial legitimacy in two provinces adjacent to Uttar Pradesh, the Punjab and Rajasthan, both of which also happened to be crucial to the political-military ambitions of his reign" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, pp. 112–113, folios 99–100). The book does not state the extent of that Mughal province. Rulers in the books deal with the province's holy men and with its revenue alike. Bhandari writes that while the imperial armies were quartered in Punjab grain was dear and so the assessed revenue of the *parganas* was raised; when the armies left Lahore for the Deccan, grain became cheap, the peasants could not pay, and a reduction was ordered at a rate uncertainly printed; Bhandari gives this as the petition of Guru Arjan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 164).

The Mughal state built across the province and paid for its builders' tombs. Ali Mardan Khan, in Shah Jahan's time, built great and splendid buildings in Hind and Punjab, and his gardens remain his memorial in many places, Chishti writes, following *Mirat al-Hind* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 415). In Shah Jahan's reign the builder of the garden Chishti calls Sanhla Bagh was appointed to an office over the buildings of Punjab, its title damaged in the printed text, and on his death money was granted from the treasury for his tomb (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 727). Every ruler of the Punjab left a mark on its religious buildings, in Kanhaiya Lal's account. In Shah Jahan's reign Munshi Muhammad Salih, *diwan* of the *suba* of the Punjab, built a mosque that was completed in the year 1058, the reading of the year uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 152, folio 155), and Dara Shikoh built a great deal while he governed the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 139, folio 142).

The Sikh years, in Lahori's editor's estimate, were hard on the shrines. Among the merits of the book he counts that the Mufti recorded, as an eyewitness, the disgrace of the notables of Punjab at the hands of the Sikhs and the destruction of the tombs of the shaikhs, his own family having been a target of the Sikh disorders (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 33, folio 21). Ranjit Singh's faith in the *majzub* Mastan Shah, and Mastan Shah's refusal of his thousands of rupees, are recorded by Lahori (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 246, folio 234). Under Ranjit Singh the Moti Masjid in the fort was renamed the Moti Mandir and held the treasury of the state, and the English government too keeps the treasury of the *suba* of the Punjab in it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 339, folio 346). Under the same Maharaja the Fakir sahibs bargained for a mosque by the Baoli by promising that its mulla would not give the call aloud (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 148, folio 151).

The Sikh state endowed the gurdwaras. "When Maharaja Ranjit Singh became the sovereign of Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century he spent large amounts of money on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras", and "attached vast tracts of land to these gurdwaras, so that the revenues from the lands could facilitate their functioning" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16); he renovated "many Gurdwaras all over Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 208, folio not stated). Under the British their custody changed hands: "As a result of sectarian divisions between the Sikh communities and the takeover of Punjab by the British, a large number of important Sikh Gurdwaras were being administered by Hindu mahants" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 189, folio not stated).

The new rulers lived in the old buildings. The troops left the Badshahi Mosque when English rule over the Punjab was complete (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 141, folio 144). When Sikh rule passed from the Punjab and the country of the Punjab came under the shadow of the English government, an octagonal *kothi* built over a tomb housed in turn Mr Bowring, Deputy Commissioner, Major Macgregor, Deputy Commissioner, both names uncertainly printed, and Montgomery, Commissioner, who in the end became Lieutenant-Governor of the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 395, folio 402). Under British rule Chishti argues for the khanqahs before the new government: attention from the government to the khanqah, he writes, would win the goodwill of the subjects of Punjab (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 95). When British rule and its legal settlement were established in Punjab, Jhuggi Shah returned to Dori Shah's site and set about restoring it (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 777).

The books see the Punjab's shrines bound up with land and power under the colonial state and after it. Rozehnal, quoting Katherine Ewing, describes how the British dealt with the hereditary saints, who "were seen primarily in terms of their economic power": "British policy, especially in the Punjab and Sind, was to treat them in essentially the same way that landlords and tribal leaders were treated", and "They tried to maintain the traditional social structure intact, securing the loyalty of the pirs, landlords, and chiefs by reinforcing their economic positions and educating them in the British tradition" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 33, folio 23). Ernst and Lawrence observe that "If we were to follow only the dictates of political history and the criteria of social science, the test case for the Chishti Nizami Sulaymaniyya lineage in the colonial period might seem to be the battles over shrines in the Punjab", with Taunsa as the example, and that "Political power struggles internal to the Sulaymaniyya can be traced to British manipulation and also the rivalry of local Sikhs, Hindus, and Muslim reformers"; they then set this reading aside, since "political contestation is only part of the narrative" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 124, folio printed "Ill" [text-layer?] [111]). Jamal Malik, writing in *Embodying Charisma*, finds the cult of the holy man and its folk piety "particularly evident in the countryside, where major landlords and large-scale land concentrations are crucial features, especially in Punjab and Sindh", and adds that "In many cases, the local pîr represents the local landlord or is at least closely related to him" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 199, folio 188). Kasmani, drawing on Umber bin Ibad's work on central Punjab, sets the colonial state, which in Ibad's words "did not try to control shrines directly through its bureaucracy", against the postcolonial state, which has gone further, driven by a desire "to harness the pluralistic traditions of shrines" in favour of "representing a Singular Islam" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 69, folio 52).

After Partition many non-Muslim holy places lost their purpose. Without the family that cares for it, Khalid writes, the shrine of Ram Thamman "would have also been taken over by the refugees and converted into a household, like the hundreds of temples and Gurdwaras all over Pakistani Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 58, folio not stated). At Makhdoom Pur Pahuran he finds the gurdwara inside a school, its gate locked, with a banner over it describing the Punjab government's new policy for improving education in public schools (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 128, folio 110).

The provincial state appears in the books in many guises. Schaflechner notes that "the Punjab has 8 reserved minority seats out of 371" in its assembly, against Sindh's 9 of 168 (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 28, folio not stated, endnote 5 to p. 15). He also cites a class V Urdu textbook of the Punjab Textbooks Board, published in Lahore in 2002, which teaches that the Hindu has always been an enemy of Islam (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 28, folio not stated, endnote 7), and draws from it, after Nayyar and Salim, that in Pakistani schoolbooks "the Hindu" is not only a danger to national security but "has always been an enemy of Islam" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 20, folio not stated). The road through Changa Manga, Khalid writes, "is much safer now, since the Punjab government introduced the motorway police" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated). He reports that "human rights activists, opposition party leaders and segments of the media have alleged that the Punjab government, dominated by the right-winged Pakistan Muslim League Noon, has been supporting these radical outfits who are based in Jhang", and that "The Muslim League Noon, on the other hand, rubbishes the claims" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).

The killing of the province's governor runs through Khalid's books. "On 4 January 2011 the Governor of Punjab Salman Taseer was shot down by one of his bodyguards, Mumtaz Qadri, for supporting the cause of a Christian woman, Aasia Bibi, accused of blasphemy"; Qadri "became an instant hero, and was hailed by the lawyers' community", and a retired chief justice of the Lahore High Court, Khwaja Sharif, offered to fight his case free of charge, calling him a hero of Islam (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 19, Notes, note 44, folio not stated). In *A White Trail* the governor "was gunned down" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 48, folio not stated), and the Aasia Bibi case "received international coverage after the former Governor of Punjab, Salman Taseer came out in support of this woman and was later killed for his pains" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 90, folio not stated).

## The Province in the Books

Each book draws the Punjab as its own unit, and the earliest of them draws it as a Mughal *suba*. Bhandari, writing about 1107 AH, describes the Mughal *suba* of Lahore as a country of five *doabs*, setting out the famous places of the five *doabs* and then the six rivers of the *suba* that divide them (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 513). He names the *doabs* as Bist Jalandhar between the Sutlej and the Beas, Bari Manjha between the Beas and the Ravi, the *doab* between the Ravi and the Chenab, which he calls Rachna where he places Sialkot in it, the *doab* between the Chenab and the Bihat, which he calls Chunhat where he places Gujrat in it, and Sind Sagar between the Bihat and the Indus, some of the names uncertainly printed (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 511; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 512; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 513; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 515; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). The five rivers of Punjab join the Indus beyond Multan in the Baloch country, and from there the whole stream is called the Sind and becomes a vast sea (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 510). Turning from the capital to the rest of the *suba* of Lahore, he sets out to write of some of its towns (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 523).

To Bhandari the *suba* of Lahore is a country of good water and air. Its summers are very hot and its winters cold; its melons and grapes are like those of Iran and Turan and its mangoes like Hindustan's; its rice is better than Bengal's and its sugarcane better than the Deccan's (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 510). Most of its irrigation depends on wells, worked by a wheel of three hundred and sixty pieces of wood and more than a hundred small pots, turned by a pair of oxen, each turn lifting several hundred maunds of water, while the autumn and spring harvests and cheap grain depend on the rain (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 510). Gold is washed from the sand, especially of the Beas and the Bihat, and the northern hills hold mines of silver, copper and other metals whose names are uncertainly printed; the miners profit and pay the duty to the imperial treasury (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 510). The *suba* measures one hundred and eighty *kos* from the Sutlej to the Indus, and eighty seven *kos* across from Bhera to a place whose name is uncertainly printed; it is bounded by Sirhind to the east, Multan to the west, Kashmir to the north and, to the south, a place whose name is also uncertain (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 510). The five *doabs* comprise three hundred and sixteen *mahals*, yielding eighty nine crore, thirty three lakh and seventy thousand *dams* (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 509).

Beside it Bhandari sets the *suba* of Multan, which he calls the abode of safety. He writes that it runs four hundred *kos* from Firozpur to Siwistan and one hundred and twenty five *kos* across from Khatpur to Jaisalmer, and six hundred and sixty *kos* in length from Sadgahra to Kech Makran; it adjoins the *sarkar* of Sirhind to the east, Kech Makran to the west, Shorkot to the north and the *suba* of Ajmer to the south (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525). Its three *sarkars*, Multan, Dipalpur and Bhakkar, comprise ninety six *mahals* and yield twenty four crore, forty six lakh and fifty five thousand *dams* (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525). The *sarkar* of Dipalpur is a dependency of this *suba* (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 526), and Bhandari bounds the *suba* of Ajmer on the west by Dipalpur, dependent on Multan, without saying whether he means the *suba*, the *sarkar* or the city (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 534). The *suba* of Shahjahanabad has the *suba* of Lahore on its west, but he draws no line between them (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 550).

Bhandari's Punjab is a frontier made safe by union with Kabul. In former times, when Kabul lay outside the rule of the sovereign of Hind, the Turanians raided its borders and the *wilayat* of Punjab lay ruined and waste; since Hind and Kabul came under one ruler, this has been the cause of the prosperity of Punjab and the security of all Hind (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 501). Among his sources he lists a history of the sultans of the *wilayat* of Multan, together with those of Malwa, Daulatabad, the Deccan, Jaunpur, Bengal and Orissa, lands that at various times passed out of the control of Delhi and each had a separate ruler (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 581).

For Kanhaiya Lal the Punjab is a unit with many names, and he uses them all: the *ilaqa* or territory of the Punjab given in *jagir* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 14, folio 9; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 76, folio 79), the Mughal *suba* of the Punjab with its *diwan* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 152, folio 155), the *khitta* or region of the Punjab that Ranjit Singh ruled (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 9, folio 4), the kingdom of the Punjab that passed to Rani Chand Kaur (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 443, folio 450), the country of the Punjab that the English annexed (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 68, folio 71), and the *suba* of the Punjab whose capital in his own day is Lahore (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 60, folio 63). His account of its past leans on earlier writers, whom he names: the *Tuhfat al-Wasilin* of Shaikh Ahmad Zanjani, set against the author of the *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 14, folio 9), and the *Hadiqat al-Awliya* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 305, folio 308).

He draws its edges by what crosses them. Jalal al-Din entered the Punjab by crossing the river he calls the river of Sind (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 165, folio 168); the Tatars, as he names them, came into it from the west (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 27, folio 30); Sikha Gakkhar entered it from the country belonging to Hazara, and Timur passed through its foothills on his way to Delhi (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 28, folio 31); the Sutlej is the river Yalduz crossed on his way from the Punjab toward Delhi (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 25, folio 20). Kashmir and the Derajat were Ranjit Singh's possessions besides the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220), the Kashmiris came into the Punjab in their famine (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 75, folio 78), and printed books came from Hindustan into it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 42, folio 45). He was also a servant of the province he described. The women's prison of the Punjab was built under his supervision (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 409, folio 416), and he describes the province's governors, courts and jails as they stood in his own day (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 60, folio 63).

Lahori, writing in 1875, defines his own province. Friends asked him, he says, for another short collection in the Urdu language holding the lives of the saints of the land of Punjab, that is, of all the territory from Delhi to Peshawar then attached to Punjab, where the tombs of famous saints lie (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 37, folio 25). His fifth chaman holds the elders who were learned and renowned scholars in Punjab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 191, folio 179), his seventh the pious women who lived in Punjab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 247, folio 235), and a later section the elders of Punjab whose dates of death could not be established (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 261, folio 249). In his closing he explains that his Persian *Khazinat al-Asfiya* was so large that those interested in the land of Punjab could not learn from it how many masters of the path had lived in the land of Punjab, a difficulty this short Urdu book removes (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 280, folio 268).

Lahori's modern editor sees the book as filling a gap. Punjab, he writes, is in this respect the most unfortunate of regions, for no regular and researched history of it has yet been written, and those who wrote on its history gave only formal chapters without touching the Sufi tazkiras; the need is to fill the gaps in the political, cultural and spiritual history of Punjab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 23, folio 11). He has compiled a list of the tazkiras, *malfuzat* and letters of the Sufis of Punjab, and no comprehensive tazkira of them has come to his notice; without the Mufti's general tazkiras, he writes, we would see no scholar or gnostic in Punjab today (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 24, folio 12). The book holds the lives of 244 Sufis of Punjab, the numeral uncertainly printed, in seven chamans, from Sultan Mahmud of Ghazni to 1292 AH, 1875 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 31, folio 19), and also takes in some well known Sufis who had no connection with Punjab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 32, folio 20). Some Punjab shaikhs are found only in this book (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 32, folio 20), and by preserving his contemporaries the Mufti did Punjab a great service; yet, the editor adds, he wrote up whichever elders of Punjab came to mind at a single sitting (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 33, folio 21). The modern edition's subtitle calls it an authoritative tazkira of the great Sufis of Punjab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 9, folio not stated).

The editor also weighs the other sources for the province's Sufis. He points to Maulana Muhammad Aqil Lahori's *Tuhfat al-Muslimin*, which records the sayings of Shaikh Hamid Qari and the lives of the great scholars of Punjab from Shaikh Hamid to Shaikh Bahauddin Zakariya Multani, and whose rare manuscript is in his own library (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 278, folio 266). Of another modern work he writes that the author of *Nuzhat al-Khawatir* took *Khazinat al-Asfiya* as his largest source for Punjab and generally included its reports without any research (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 191–192, folios 179–180).

The translators of the province's poets draw it in their own ways. For the annotator of Usborne's Waris Shah the poem's world is "the old Punjab", with its *bars* such as the Nili Bar "in the Montgomery area (now renamed Sahiwal)", a *bar* being a kind of jungle (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 10, folio 9). Naveed Alam reads Shah Hussein's Punjab against W. H. Moreland's account of the Mughal Golden Age, though that account speaks of India taken as a unit rather than of Punjab (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 14, folio 14). Shackle writes of Panjab without the article, and narrows it to "western Panjab" for the world of Hir and Ranjha (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 20, folio xix). Schimmel most often speaks of Sindh and the Punjab together, as "the two provinces" of one tradition of religious poetry (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384), and of a "cotton-growing country" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 417, folio 386); in *Pain and Grace* "the province" is the land invaded by Marathas, Sikhs and Muslim armies in the late seventeenth and eighteenth centuries (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 17, folio 13). For Sorley, writing of eighteenth century Sindh, the Punjab is the theatre of the armies of Afghans, Sikhs and Marathas (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 46, folio 37) and a source of migrants and tales (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 270, folio 261).

For the historians the Punjab is defined by comparison. Eaton sets it against Bengal, where the Mughal officials who arrived around 1620 found that "The Bengali diet of fish and rice, for example, contrasted sharply with the wheat and meat diet of the Punjab and seems to have posed a special stumbling block for immigrants" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 263, folio 251). Rizvi writes against W. H. McLeod's picture of the Punjab of Guru Nanak's time: McLeod, he says, "seems to be unaware of Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Quddus Gangohi who lived in Shahabad, his disciple, Shaikh Jalal of Thaneswar, and the Qadiris who had made the Panjab the main centre of their activities" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 420, folio 396).

Each modern author's Punjab is drawn to the scale of his travels. Khalid limited the research for *In Search of Shiva* to the country around Lahore: "Since this was mostly a self-funded project I could only limit my research around my hometown of Lahore. Therefore only parts of central Punjab are included and southern Punjab and Sindh are excluded, where there are many such shrines" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 6, Introduction, folio not stated). In the afterword to *A White Trail* he explains that he worked in "Central Punjab where I conducted my research", where Hindus live in ghettos, adding: "My perception is that the conditions under which members of the Hindu community live in Central Punjab are the harshest compared to the other regions" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 233, folio not stated). "The majority of the Hindu community is based in Sindh and Southern Punjab, where they have a proportionate representation from the lower and the upper castes" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 233, folio not stated), whereas almost all his interviews were with low caste Hindus "because there are hardly any high-caste Hindus in this region", so that his observations from Central Punjab should not be taken to reflect Hindu communities where the population is large (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 233–234, folio not stated). On the other hand, "The majority of the Christian minority is based in Punjab", so that his Christian material can stand for the community in the country as a whole (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 234, folio not stated).

Khalid's Punjab is also personal. He was "born and brought up in Pakistani Punjab" and knew of Guru Nanak as the founder of Sikhism, a poet and a spiritual leader, "Yet given the Muslim-dominant culture of Pakistani Punjab, there were no avenues available for me within the culture to explore his personality" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 15, folio not stated). On the road he asks whether "is it the bubble that we occupy in the urban centres that is out of context in the culture of Punjab", and remarks that "One rarely finds such a lonely road in the heartland of Punjab, the most prosperous province of Pakistan" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated). At the banyan of Apal Muri, where visitors had left their mobile numbers, he guesses that they were "probably hoping to initiate a secret love affair in this highly monitored social life of rural Punjab" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 9, ch. 3, folio not stated). He notes that "yellow was commonly used for buildings in Punjab during the older days" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 197, folio not stated), and on an abandoned mound he finds karir, "another ancient Punjabi species of plant that is no longer found anywhere except in abandoned places" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 92, folio 74).

In the novelised chapter of *Walking with Nanak* on the Sikh capture of Hassan Abdal under Hari Singh, Khalid imagines the province from its western frontier. The Sikh soldiers "had arrived from the Punjab"; invaders had for almost a thousand years "crossed into the Punjab from the West", and so, in the Punjabi imagination as the damaged sentence can be read, "the West was a fearful place where giants resided" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 258, folio 232). Hari Singh wanted the tribesmen to know "that now it was the time of Punjab to create havoc in the Khyber" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 262, folio 236).

The modern ethnographers work with parts of the province. Kasmani writes of central Punjab (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 73, folio 56), Rozehnal of the southern Punjab as a Sufi master's domain (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 90, folio 80), and Boivin of a southern Punjab that was once part of Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 216, folio 203). Boivin also criticises scholars of Sindhi studies who treat Sindh's religious encounters "as if comparable dynamics were not implemented in other regions, be they neighbouring Sindh, such as Punjab, Rajasthan or Gujarat, or elsewhere" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 196, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The seat of Raja Jaipal.** Bhandari's text names the fort that was Jaipal's seat in one form, uncertainly printed, and the editor's footnote gives a different reading (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 427).
- **Who first brought Islam to Lahore.** Lahori, citing *Tuhfat al-Wasilin*, calls Shaikh Muhammad Ismail Muhaddith Lahori, who settled in Lahore in the time of Sultan Masud of Ghazni, the first of the callers to Islam to come to Lahore (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 191, folio 179). Rizvi writes that Khattali ordered his disciple Shaikh Husain Zinjani to move to the Punjab, and later sent Hujwiri after him (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112).
- **When Shaikh Ismail Muhaddith Lahori died.** Lahori, following *Tuhfat al-Wasilin*, places him in the time of Sultan Masud of Ghazni and gives his death as 448 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 191, folio 179). His modern editor calls that source a fabricated tazkira and gives the correct year of his death as 980 AH, not 448 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 191–192, folios 179–180).
- **The Ghaznavid account of *Tuhfat al-Wasilin*.** Lahori retells from it the Ghaznavid rule of Punjab, its weakening under Bahram Shah and Raja Anangpal's taking of Lahore (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 264, folio 252); his editor calls *Tuhfat al-Wasilin* a fabricated tazkira (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 192, folio 180).
- **The end of Ghaznavid rule in the Punjab.** Kanhaiya Lal ends Khusrau Malik's rule over the Punjab with his surrender to Sultan Shihab al-Din Ghori, when the Ghurid sultanate in Hind began (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 24, folio 19). Bhandari ends Khusrau Malik's government of the *wilayat* when he went to Ghazni in 582 AH (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 405). Dhillon writes that "Shahabuddin Muhammad Ghori captured Punjab in 1192" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 136, folio 151).
- **Chingiz Khan in Punjab.** Lahori, from *Tazkira-e Hamidiya*, has Chingiz Khan's army plunder the whole land of Punjab and the people of Lahore fight it for two months, while himself doubting whether anyone will believe it (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 248, folio 236). His editor's addenda note that Shihab al-Din Nasawi does not mention an attack by Chingiz Khan on Lahore at all (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 352, folio 340).
- **The year the Mongols came to Punjab under Jalal al-Din Khalji.** Bhandari's text gives 693, uncertainly printed; the editor's footnote gives 691 from Firishta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 370).
- **The sultan of the fourth blow.** Kanhaiya Lal sets it in the time of Sultan Jalal al-Din Firuz Shah Khalji, but names the sultan who was away on the Gujarat campaign, in an uncertain reading, as Shams al-Din Iltutmish (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 26, folio 29).
- **Jasrath's tribe.** Kanhaiya Lal makes Jasrath the brother of Sikha Gakkhar (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 29, folio 32); Bhandari calls him Jasrath Khokhar, the name uncertainly printed (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 191).
- **Which Lodi sultan made Tatar Khan governor.** Bhandari has Tatar Khan sent to govern Punjab by Sultan Bahlul Lodi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 190; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 296), and places his governorship of Lahore in Bahlul Lodi's time, where the editor's footnote gives the variant Sikandar Lodi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 522–523).
- **The bounds of the Baluch *jagir*.** In one passage Bhandari has Sultan Husain Langah give Malik Sohrab a *jagir* from the fort of Kot Kardaz to Dhankot, the Baluch later holding the land from Sitpur to Dhankot (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 295); in his description of the *suba* of Multan the grant to Malik Sohrab and the other Baluch runs from Karor Kot to Mankot (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525).
- **The title of Zakariya Khan's office.** The tile inscription on his mosque calls him Zakariya Khan of the *suba* of the Punjab, while the book's own heading to the same entry calls him Nawab Zakariya Khan, known as Khan Bahadur, of the *suba* of Lahore (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 262–263, folios 265–266); elsewhere the book calls him Nawab Zakariya Khan Bahadur, *suba* of Lahore (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 337, folio 344) and counts him among the rulers of the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 90, folio 93).
- **Ahmad Shah Abdali's dates.** Lahori gives a year read as 1120 AH, uncertainly printed, for Ahmad Shah Durrani's *farman* to Mufti Muhammad Mukarram; his editor calls the year wrong, writing that Abdali began his attacks on Punjab in 1161 AH, 1748, and died in 1187 AH, 1773 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 277, folio 265).
- **Abdali's invasion of 1748.** Rafat's introduction calls the invasion of 1748 Ahmad Shah Abdali's second invasion of the Punjab, followed by a third in 1752 (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 27, folio 7). The annotator of Waris Shah writes that Abdali "in 1748, began a series of invasions of India through the Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 8, folio 7), and Lahori's editor dates Abdali's first attack on Lahore to 1161 AH, 1748 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 214, folio 202).
- **The year of Shaikh Karam Shah's death.** Lahori gives it in words as one thousand two hundred, and in the line of his chronogram as 1201 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 190, folio 178).
- **When Ranjit Singh became ruler of the Punjab.** *Walking with Nanak* has him become "the sovereign of Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16), and elsewhere calls him "the Sikh ruler of Punjab, Maharaja Ranjit Singh in the nineteenth century" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 87, folio 69); *A White Trail* has him "crowned as the Ruler of Punjab" in 1801 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated).
- **How long Ranjit Singh ruled the Punjab.** Kanhaiya Lal writes that he ruled the Punjab for forty years (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220), and Khalid that he brought stability "in Punjab which lasted a good forty years" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 208, folio not stated). Khalid's own dates have him "crowned as the Ruler of Punjab" in 1801 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated) and give his life as "Maharaja Ranjit Singh (1780-1839)" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 42, folio not stated).
- **Baba Farid and the troubles of Punjab.** Waris Shah writes that when Shakarganj settled at Pakpattan "the Punjab was delivered of all its troubles"; his annotator replies that this "is not historically correct" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 2, folio 1).
- **Whether Baba Farid converted the Punjab's clans.** Most Jat and Rajput groups that became Muslim in the medieval period claim conversion by Baba Farid or by Baha' al-Haqq Zakariya (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 227, folio 215), and the clans of the southwestern Punjab said so in court in the 1930s (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 241, folio 229); but the earliest sources on Baba Farid "make no mention of his having converted anyone", and many of these clans, "in the course of their northward migrations up the rivers of the Punjab, had not yet reached the Ajudhan area during Baba Farid's lifetime" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 227, folio 215).
- **The Sufism of the Punjab in Guru Nanak's time.** W. H. McLeod, as Rizvi quotes him, holds that "the Panjabi sufism of Guru Nanak's period had evidently departed radically from the classical pattern of Arab and Persian Sufism", and that most Muslims Nanak met represented "the blend of modified orthodoxy and debased Sufism" then dominant in the Muslim community of the Panjab (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 419–420, folios 395–396); Rizvi answers that "all literate Panjabis, both Hindus and Muslims, at the time of Guru Nanak were conversant with" 'Attar, Rumi and Sa'di, and that the Qadiris had made the Panjab the main centre of their activities (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 420, folio 396).
- **The Rasul Shahi houses.** Chishti writes that the order had no house in all Punjab but this one, and no other house, *takia* or *khanqah*, and then names one more house near Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 145).
- **What the Ara'in of the Punjab were.** In her text Abbas calls them "gardeners or small cultivators who worked on their own land" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 108, folio not stated); her glossary calls them "A caste of landowners in the Panjab" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 178, folio not stated).
- **How many Hindus live in the Punjab.** In one passage of *A White Trail* most Hindus are scattered in other provinces, "with only a few in Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 87, folio not stated); in others a significant Hindu population continues to live in Southern Punjab (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 31, folio not stated), and "The majority of the Hindu community is based in Sindh and Southern Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 233, folio not stated).
- **When mystical poetry in the Punjab's own language began.** Schimmel writes in one book that "Reliable tradition traces the use of the native language for mystical poetry both in Sind and in the Punjab back to the fifteenth century" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384); in another, that popular mystical poetry in the Punjab "may have emerged as early as in the days of Fariduddin Shakarganj", who died in 1265 (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 16, folio 12).
- **When the poets began to use folk tales as mystical teaching.** In *As Through a Veil* Schimmel places the development of folk tales as media for mystical instruction by the poets of Sindh and the Punjab only in the centuries after the death of the historian Badaʾuni (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 154, folio 136); in *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* Madho Lal Hussein's allusions to the stories of Hir and of Sohni and Mahiwal seem to inaugurate, or to continue from unknown beginnings, the insertion of folk tales into the context of mystical thought (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384).
- **Whether the Siraiki of Sindh and of the Punjab is one language.** Boivin's glossary calls Siraiki "a language spoken in Northern Sindh and Southern Punjab" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 220, folio 207); in his text he reports that for some specialists the Siraiki of Northern Sindh is "a distinct language from the Siraiki of South Punjab" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 171, folio 158).
- **The armies of the Punjab.** Waris Shah's verse speaks of the armies of the Punjab falling upon Hindustan; his annotator answers that the armies of the Punjab are known to have defended Hindustan, never to have fallen upon it (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 7, folio 6; Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 8, folio 7).
