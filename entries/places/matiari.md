## Overview

Three books speak of the town of Hala, in Sindh as an account quoted in one of them puts it (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 71, folio not stated; Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 91, folio 78; Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 46, folio not stated). Michel Boivin gives Hala as the birthplace of Shah Abdul Latif, born there in a Sayyid family (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 91, folio 78). Shemeem Burney Abbas calls Hala "the heart of a fertile agricultural belt", where Kolhi and Bhil women who go to Shah Abdul Latif's shrine can make a living on the large feudal farms (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 46, folio not stated). An account of the origins of the Goddess Hinglaj, quoted by Jürgen Schaflechner, holds that Hala was once called Kohala (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 71, folio not stated). Abbas elsewhere places the same shrine at Bhit Shah without naming Hala (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 50, folio not stated).

## History and Legend

The oldest name the books give the town comes from a legend. In an account of the origins of the Goddess Hinglaj that Schaflechner quotes, "Hala in Sindh was called Kohala in the former times, and the Goddess Hinglaj was once the queen of the Kohala or Kohana area." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 71, folio not stated). The former name and the Goddess's rule over the area are that account's claims, not Schaflechner's own, and this is the only place the book names Hala (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 71, folio not stated).

Boivin records the town as the birthplace of a saint. Of Shah Abdul Latif the book says: "He was born in Hala in a Sayyid family." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 91, folio 78).

## Communities and Livelihood

Abbas writes of Kolhi and Bhil women who go to Shah Abdul Latif's shrine, and gives their reason as the place itself: they go "primarily because the shrine is located in Hala, the heart of a fertile agricultural belt, where they can make a living working on the large feudal farms" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 46, folio not stated). Abbas's Hala is thus farming country, a fertile belt of large feudal farms that offers these women work (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 46, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- Where Shah Abdul Latif's shrine stands: in one passage Abbas writes that the women go to it "primarily because the shrine is located in Hala" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 46, folio not stated); in another the same book places the same shrine "in Bhit Shah, Sind", without mentioning Hala (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 50, folio not stated).
