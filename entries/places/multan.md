## Overview

Sujan Rai Bhandari, describing the suba of Multan, calls the city one of the ancient cities, with a fort of brick (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528), where people of every kind live and the goods of every land are bought and sold (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). Harish Dhillon calls it "one of the oldest cities" of what he calls South-East Asia, where three rivers merge before the Indus, and "a commercial hub for national and international trade, including with Persia, Central Asia and the Arab world" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 165, folio 181). For Carl W. Ernst and Bruce B. Lawrence it is "a strategic commercial center in the Punjab" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 80, folio 66), and for Haroon Khalid "the historic city of Multan" and "this holy city in the southern region of Punjab" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 122, folio 104).

Its history as the books tell it begins in legend and runs through conquest. Dhillon makes it the capital of the Tuganta kingdom in the time of the Mahabharata and records that for a thousand years it was ruled by Hindu and Buddhist kings (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 165, folio 181), while Khalid finds the Hindus of Multan keeping the memory of Hiranyakashipu, "the tyrant who is believed to have ruled the city of Multan thousands of years ago" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 19, folio not stated). In 711 the Arabs conquered Sind and the provinces northward up to Multan (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 375, folio 344). Rizvi writes that Multan had become a dependency of the Fatimids of Egypt before Sultan Mahmud broke Ismaʿili power there, and that in 1175 Muʿizzu'd-Din Muhammad bin Sam seized it (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 128, folio 110). After the Arghun conquest, Bhandari writes, Multan was so ruined that no one thought it would ever be settled again (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 294). Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti records that under Alamgir II the Afghans took Multan and Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 851), that when Ranjit Singh conquered Multan Nawab Muzaffar Khan was killed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 130), and that in a year printed as 1848, the reading uncertain, Diwan Mulraj, nazim of Multan, raised a disturbance (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 592).

Many of the books know it as a city of Sufis. Dhillon writes that during the twelfth century many Sufi scholars made Multan their base (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 166–167, folios 182–183), and that "By the time Nanak came to Multan, the city was famous as a major centre of Sufi learning and philosophy" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 167, folio 183). Annemarie Schimmel calls it the resting place of the great Suhrawardi saint Baha'uddin Zakariya, who died in 1267 (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 286, folio 268), and Bhandari writes that besides the saints he names, many saints lie at rest in the city (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). Schimmel records the Indian tradition that Shams-i Tabriz was flayed alive by the mollas in Multan, where his shrine is still venerated (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 175, folio 157), and for the Sindhi and Panjabi poets Multan stands beside Mecca and Medina as a holy city of the heart (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 178, folio 160). One of its saints protected the city: when the sultan meant to put the people of Multan to the sword after Malik Bahram's revolt, Shaikh Rukn al-Din turned him from it (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 347).

The books also show a city of more than one faith. In Khalid's account of Guru Nanak's time Multan "was known for the religious shrines of Muslim saints, and Jain and Hindu temples" and "was a sacred space for people of all religious denominations" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 123, folio 105). Dhillon describes its khanqah as a meeting place of the Sufis open to both Muslims and Hindus (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 167, folio 183; Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 168, folio 184). In Khalid's present the Hindus of the city number little more than a thousand (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 23, folio not stated), its pre-partition temples are ruins (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 24, folio not stated), and Holi, not celebrated in the city as recently as 2010, is now kept every year on a small scale (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 24–25, folio not stated; Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 25, folio not stated).

The books give Multan its own speech, verse and song. Bhandari writes that its people speak a tongue in which the speech of Lahore and of Sindh are mixed (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528), and Khalid that the Siraiki spoken in Multan is closer to Punjabi (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 276, folio 250). Amir Khusrau and Amir Hasan served Balban's son Prince Muhammad at Multan as his boon companions (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 386), and the love poems of Fakhruddin ʿIraqi for his master are, Schimmel writes, still sung by Pakistani musicians at the door of the master's tomb in Multan (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 384, folio 353). Shemeem Burney Abbas finds musicians such as Surraiya Multanikar supported by the landowning Syeds of Multan and Bahawalpur and performing at their family shrines (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 168–169, folios not stated).

## History: Legend, the Arabs and the Ghaznavids

Dhillon gives the city a long history before Islam. During the time of the Mahabharata, he writes, it was the capital of the Tuganta kingdom and was ruled by the Katoch clan of the Kshatriya Rajputs; legend has it that Alexander was hit by an arrow there; for the next thousand years it was ruled by Hindu and Buddhist kings; the Chinese traveller Heun Tsang, as Dhillon names him, visited Multan in A.D. 641; and it was the 'city of gold', whose Suraj temple was "Claimed to be by far the biggest temple in the subcontinent" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 165, folio 181).

Khalid, writing of the Hindus of the present city, records the legend of Hiranyakashipu, "the tyrant who is believed to have ruled the city of Multan thousands of years ago" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 19, folio not stated). The city, he writes, "continues to move forward unaware of its ancient past and the mythical association that it has with the story of Hiranyakashipu and Prahlad Bhagat" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 20–21, folio not stated).

Dhillon writes that during the second half of the seventh century Multan had its first experience of Muslim armies (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 165, folio 181), and that Muhammad bin Qasim brought it for the first time under Muslim rule (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 166, folio 182). Schimmel dates the conquest: the western provinces of the Subcontinent had become part of the Muslim Empire in 711, the year in which the Arabs conquered Sind and the adjacent provinces northward up to Multan (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 375, folio 344). Khalid adds that when Muhammad bin Qasim took over Multan, its subsidiary Chunian also fell under his control (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).

Schimmel writes of the Carmathians at Multan. She writes that the Carmathians ruled not only Bahrain but also northern Sind and Multan, places Hallaj had just visited, and that this supposed connection was a cause of suspicion against him (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 98, folio 67). Elsewhere she writes that the Carmathians, an extreme Shia group whose capital was in Bahrain, had conquered central Arabia in 930, that their branches extended to the Indus valley, and that there, at Multan, Hallaj had been in touch with them, according to the tradition (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 113, folio 82). Rizvi writes that Multan had become a dependency of the Fatimids of Egypt, that Sultan Mahmud dealt a blow to Ismaʿili power there, and that by the middle of the eleventh century Sufism had penetrated into the areas surrounding Multan (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 128, folio 110); he speaks too of the news of Sultan Mahmud's conquest of Multan (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 129, folio 111).

Chishti writes that Sabuktigin brought Lahore and Multan into his possession (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 804). Bhandari tells Mahmud's Multan campaigns at length. On his second campaign, for which no year is printed, the Sultan went by way of Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 421). Because Daud bin Nasir held a separate rule in Multan, Bhandari writes, zeal for the faith moved the Sultan to march by way of Multan and take that country from him (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 420). He left the direct road and went by a roundabout one, so that the ruler there would have no warning and he might fall on him suddenly; on the way Raja Anandpal son of Raja Jaipal opposed him, was defeated and withdrew into the hills of Kashmir (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 420). Reaching Multan, the Sultan made its ruler submit, fixed on him a yearly tribute of what is printed as eight thousand dams, the number uncertain, and put the commands of the shariʿa into force (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 420). Bhandari closes the campaign with the city's climate: after a set piece on the summer heat, he writes that the Sultan's soldiers could not bear the hot air of Multan and begged to return, so the Sultan went back to Ghazni (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 420–419).

On the fourth campaign, in a year whose printed digits cannot be read with certainty, Mahmud set out for Multan and brought the whole of that country under his control (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 417). Bhandari writes that the Qaramita, heretics who were in that country, were seized: some were bound and thrown beneath the feet of elephants as large as mountains, those who rejected the commands of the shariʿa lost hands and feet, those who had turned from the shariʿa were beheaded, and those who out of self conceit did not heed the Sultan's orders lost nose and ear; the sentence on a last group cannot be made out (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 417). Daud bin Nasir, the ruler there, was taken captive and imprisoned in a fort whose printed name, perhaps Ghurak, is uncertain, and there he died a natural death (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 417). Dhillon writes that during the eleventh century Mahmud Ghazni damaged the Suraj temple and destroyed its giant idol (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 166, folio 182). Bhandari also records the great army Mahmud sent toward Multan and Sindh with its four thousand boats (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 411), and his return from Somnath, when he went toward Multan by way of the desert and reached Ghazni only with great hardship, many of his men dying of thirst and hunger (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 412).

Chishti writes that Masʿud, returning from Hansi, appointed his own son governor of Multan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 806). Shihab al-Din, on his first campaign, took the country of Multan out of the hands of the Qaramita and left his deputy there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 405), and Rizvi dates the seizure of Multan by Muʿizzu'd-Din Muhammad bin Sam to 1175 (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 128, folio 110). On his second campaign, in a year printed as 574, the reading uncertain, he came again to Multan and Uch on his way to Gujarat (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 405). Under Ghori, Dhillon writes, Multan joined his Delhi kingdom, and the town became studded with mosques as it had once been studded with Hindu temples (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 166, folio 182).

## History: The Delhi Sultans, Timur and the Langahs

In the reign of Rukn al-Din Firuz Shah, Bhandari writes, the nobles called in the governor of Multan, whose printed name reads Malik Izz al-Din Ayaz, the first part uncertain, and he gathered a heavy army from Multan and set out for Delhi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 398). Rizvi's account of Qutbu'd-Din Kashani and his madrasa brings in the Governor of Multan, the Qazi of Multan, the Multan fort and the townsfolk (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 209–210, folios 191–192); he writes too of a governor of Multan and of Maulana Husamu'd-Din of Multan (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 187, 197; folios 169, 179), of a Malik of Uch and Multan (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 172, folio 154), and of the ʿulamaʾ of Multan refusing a fatwa (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 158–159, folios 140–141).

Under Balban, Prince Muhammad Sultan, heir to the throne, was given the country of Sindh in jagir and was appointed, fully equipped, to order the affairs of Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 386–387). Chishti writes that the Mughals, as he calls them, had seized most of Multan, the reading uncertain, and that Balban's son Muhammad Shah drove them out (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 811). Bhandari tells the prince's end: when a Mongol army began to harm the people in the country around Lahore and Multan, the prince came out of Multan of necessity and after a short fight met a martyr's death (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 385). Abbas names this the Battle of Multan, in which Prince Muhammad, Sultan Ghiasuddin Balban's son, was killed (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 39, folio not stated). Balban then appointed Kaikhusrau, the dead prince's son, at Multan and granted him the parasol and the *durbash*, marks of royalty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 384). By Balban's will Kaikhusrau, who was living at Multan and had come to court, was to take the throne, but Fakhr al-Din by a ruse sent him back to Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 384). Kaiqubad, Bhandari writes, summoned Kaikhusrau, whom the text calls his uncle, from Multan and had the innocent man put to death at Rohtak, though the wording leaves some doubt over who is meant (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 380).

Bhandari also tells of men sent to Multan or fleeing to it. After Malik Chhajju's revolt, the name uncertainly printed, the sultan had him seated in a litter and sent to Multan, ordering that he be kept with honour in a house and given every means of pleasure and ease (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 373). The sons of Jalal al-Din, Rukn al-Din and Ibrahim, fled from Delhi to Multan, and the sultan sent forty thousand horse against them under Alf Khan and Zafar Khan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 368). The commanders besieged Multan, and the princes, unable to hold out, submitted through Shaikh Rukn al-Din (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 368–367). Malik Ala al-Mulk counselled that Samana, Dipalpur and Multan, which stand in the path of the Mongols, the reading of that phrase tentative, must be made strong (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 366).

The books tie the rise of Ghazi Tughluq to Multan. Chishti writes that Ghazi Beg Tughluq, governor of the suba of Multan, raised an army in the Punjab, the name uncertainly printed, killed Khusrau Malik and took the throne as Ghiyas-ud-Din (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 814). In Bhandari, Ghazi al-Malik marched on Delhi in league with Bahram, governor of Multan, who had also girded himself for revenge (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 354). Bhandari tells of Malik Bahram's revolt in Multan, his defeat and death, and of the general massacre the sultan intended for the people of Multan, which Shaikh Rukn al-Din averted (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 347). In the contest after Firuz Shah, Prince Muhammad Shah wrote orders to the people of Multan, Lahore and other cities that wherever they found a slave of Firuz Shah they should kill him, and in most places there was general slaughter and plunder (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 336).

Timur's armies came next. Mirza Pir Muhammad, Timur's grandson, crossed the Indus, took the forts of Uch and Multan and stayed some days in Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 333); Chishti writes that he fought a hard battle near Multan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 815), and Michel Boivin that in 1397 Pir Muhammad was in Multan (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 189, folio 176). Timur himself came from Kabul in a year printed as 801, the reading uncertain, halted at Multan, put to death those who had been taken captive by Mirza Pir Muhammad, and left Multan by way of the desert for Bhatnir (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 333).

The books give Khizr Khan the government of Multan. Chishti writes that Timur made him governor of Multan and Dipalpur, the second name uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 816), and Bhandari that Timur granted him the government of Lahore, Multan and Dipalpur (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 331). Afterwards Khizr Khan held Lahore, Multan and Dipalpur as far as the borders of Sindh, and Iqbal Khan marched toward Dipalpur and Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 330). Khizr Khan came from Multan with a great army as far as Samana and Sirhind, and three times gathered an army in Multan and marched on Delhi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 329); his seat of government was Fatehpur, a dependency of Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 328). In another passage Bhandari counts Khizr Khan, at Multan and Dipalpur, as one of four men to whom Sultan Muhammad Shah bin Sultan Firuz Shah gave four provinces, the others being Zafar Khan at Gujarat, a Khwaja whose name is uncertainly printed at Jaunpur, and Dilawar Khan at Malwa (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 241). Shaikh Ali of Kabul set out for Multan and was beaten in a battle near the city, then returned to raid the country around Lahore and Multan, and Sultan Mubarak Shah set out toward Lahore and Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 326).

In Bhandari's account the Lodis had Multani beginnings. Malik Bahram, Sultan Bahlul's grandfather, was a trustworthy man who in Firuz Shah's reign, after a quarrel with his brother, came to Multan and entered the service of its governor, printed as Malik Marwan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 322). His five sons, Malik Shah, the first name uncertain, Malik Kala, Malik Firuz, Malik Muhammad and Malik Khwaja, all lived in Multan after their father's death (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 322). In a year printed as 835, the reading uncertain, the sultan reached Multan, made the round of the tombs of the shaikhs, sent a force against Jasrath Khokhar, that name also uncertain, and returned to Delhi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 324). In those days, Bhandari writes, the Langah people rose in revolt in Multan and raised the banner of sovereignty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 324). When Sultan Mahmud of Jaunpur besieged Delhi the sultan had set out toward Multan, and he turned back from Dipalpur on hearing the news (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 318).

Bhandari then tells how Multan became a kingdom. Because of the Mongol attacks that came every year from the direction of Kabul to raid and plunder, the country of Multan was left without a ruler (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 297). The notables and pillars of the country and the people of the city met in council, agreed that a ruler was needed to protect the people and keep the affairs of the realm in order, and with one voice raised Shaikh Yusuf Qureshi to the sultanate of the country of Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 297). Rai Sanhra, the name uncertainly printed, the chief of the Langahs, to whom the qasba of Sivi belonged, warned the Shaikh that Sultan Bahlul Lodi, having taken Delhi, might march on Multan, and offered him the Langahs' service; he gave the Shaikh his daughter in marriage, always sent her fitting gifts and sometimes visited her (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 297).

The visits were a stratagem, and Bhandari tells the coup in the fort in detail (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 296–297). Rai Sanhra brought a body of his own men into Multan to be reviewed, and the guileless Shaikh, unaware of the deceit, showed him favour; Rai Sanhra drank a cup of the warm blood of a kid and cried out that a pain had seized his stomach (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 297). Near midnight he called for Shaikh Yusuf's attendants as though to make his last bequests and vomited blood before them, and by this trick his kinsmen were let in from outside the city into the fort (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 296). He then set men to guard all four gates, let no servant of the Shaikh pass from the fort into the city, posted men through the Shaikh's private apartments, seized and imprisoned him, took the title of Sultan Qutb al-Din, and struck coin and had the *khutba* read in his own name (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 296).

Bhandari heads and dates the Langah rulers. Sultan Qutb al-Din, known as Rai Sanhra Langah, struck coin and had the *khutba* read in his name in a year printed as 859, ruled the country of Multan with full independence, died a natural death and reigned sixteen years (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 296). His son Sultan Husain succeeded him in a year printed as 876, a man of enterprise and courage, and reigned twenty two years, the number uncertain (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 295–296). Sultan Mahmud, son of Firuz Shah son of Sultan Husain, succeeded his grandfather in a year printed as 898 (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 295) and reigned twenty seven years (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 294). Sultan Husain son of Sultan Mahmud was three years old when he was seated, in a year printed as 945, and reigned for seven years a reign that was in name only (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 294). Every one of these years is uncertainly printed.

Bhandari writes of a battle near Multan in which a commander whose name is printed as Barbak Shah, with Tatar Khan, governor of the Punjab, drew up against Sultan Husain, who met them with ten thousand horse and foot (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 296); each of his men loosed three bundles of arrows at once, the word uncertain, so that thirty thousand arrows fell on the army of the commander now printed as Baba Shah, which broke and did not draw rein before Chiniot (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 295).

Bhandari then tells the end of the Langahs. On reaching the Punjab, Babur issued a decree to Mirza Shah Husain Arghun, ruler of Thatta, granting him Multan in jagir, and Arghun fought Sultan Mahmud, who died during the fighting (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 295). Under the child Sultan Husain, Qawam Khan and Lashkar Khan, whose further name is uncertainly printed, chiefs of their people with a following of their own, seized most of the districts of Multan, joined Arghun, fought Husain and took Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 294). Elsewhere Bhandari writes simply that Mirza Shah Husain, son of Mirza Shah Beg, took Multan from Sultan Husain son of Sultan Mahmud Langah (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 171). The conquerors plundered the city, and its people from seven years old to seventy were taken captive; Sultan Husain was imprisoned and died; and Multan was so ruined that no one thought it would ever be settled again (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 294). Under the heading of Mirza Shah Husain Arghun, Bhandari dates the conquest to a year printed as 932 and writes that Arghun set a slave of his named Shams al-Din to guard Multan together with Lashkar Khan, and that Lashkar Khan took over the revenue and the government, did away with Shams al-Din and ruled independently (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 294).

## History: Mughal, Afghan and Sikh Multan and After

Chishti writes that in Ibrahim Lodi's time Daulat Khan, governor of Multan, called in Babur, the king of the Mughals, from Kabul (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 817). Under the Mughals Lahore and Multan were assigned by the emperor to Kamran Mirza in jagir; Kamran summoned Lashkar Khan from Multan, gave him another jagir and put his own men in charge of the city, Arghun rule there having lasted four years (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 294). Bhandari records the grant of Lahore and Multan to Kamran in a second place as well (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 298).

In the wars with Sher Khan, the emperor's road of flight ran by Multan. From Lahore the emperor went toward Multan and Thatta, and Khawas Khan pursued him as far as Multan and turned back (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 269). With Hindal and Yadgar Nasir Mirza the emperor went by way of Multan and reached Bhakkar, while Khawas Khan, Sher Khan's slave, followed him with a large army as far as Multan and Uch before turning back; Bhandari calls Uch subordinate to Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 285).

Bhandari's later chapters show the governors of Multan at work. When Khan Jahan, governor of the Punjab, went after Ibrahim Husain Mirza and Masud Husain Mirza and fought them near a place whose printed name is uncertain, described as subordinate to Multan, Masud Husain Mirza was taken and Ibrahim Husain Mirza fled toward Multan and hid in the house of a Baluch; the Baluch seized him and handed him to Said Khan, governor of Multan, and he died of the wound he had received in the battle (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 228). The Khankhanan, sent against Qandahar, was ordered on reaching the neighbourhood of Multan to take Thatta first, and halted between Multan and Bhakkar, which was his jagir (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 175). During his siege of Jani Beg, boats of grain from Lahore and Multan reached his army (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 174). Rustam Mirza was given a rank of five thousand and, in jagir, the country of Multan and Baluchistan, whose revenue was greater than that of Qandahar (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 175).

Dara Shikoh's flight brought the war of succession to the city. From Lahore Dara sent robes of honour to the zamindars and faujdars of Multan, Bhakkar and Thatta, all of which belonged to his assignment (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 77). He meant to go toward Multan and Qandahar (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 76), and on the twenty second of Dhu'l-Qaʿda he left Lahore for Multan with fourteen thousand horse (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 68). At a place whose name is printed as Haibatpur Patti the imperial court heard that Dara had reached Multan with fourteen thousand horse, much artillery and the gear of war (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 66). Khalid writes that Dara had planned to flee to Multan from Lahore and from there to head further west to Kandahar (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 211, folio 185). But Dara did not stand at Multan: on the twenty fifth of Dhu'l-Hijja, after eight halts, the number uncertain, he took twenty two lakh rupees, the sum uncertainly printed, from the treasury of Multan and fled toward Bhakkar, and most of his servants would not go with him (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 66). Haji Khan Baluch with a band of men came upon the treasure boats and tried to bar their way, but after a small skirmish could not stop them (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 66). Bhandari later calls him one of the chief zamindars of Multan; he waited on the emperor near Multan, received a robe of honour, an elephant and a horse, and was sent home (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 65).

The emperor followed. Khalilullah Khan was ordered to wait at Multan until the imperial train arrived, and the imperial camp, on the seventh of Muharram of a year printed as 1069, stood on the bank where the Ravi and the Chenab flow together, three kos from Multan, the distance uncertain (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 66). Shaikh Musa Gilani, the name uncertainly printed, who held the government of Multan on Dara Shikoh's behalf, submitted, and with him Sayyid Izzat Khan, who received a robe of honour and a rank of three thousand *zat* and five hundred horse (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 66). The emperor visited Bahauddin's tomb, and Lashkar Khan was moved from the suba of Kashmir to the governorship of Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 65). The emperor stayed five days near Multan, and having sent forces toward Bhakkar he marched on the twelfth of Muharram of a year printed as 1068, from outside the city of Multan, toward Shahjahanabad (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 65). The chronicle's table of contents gives three headings to these events: Dara Shikoh's hurried flight from Lahore toward Multan, the imperial march toward Multan in pursuit of him, and the return from Multan toward Shahjahanabad to put down Muhammad Shuja's rising (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 589). News reached the court that Dara had come from Multan toward Gujarat (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 52).

Chishti's history continues the story. At Aurangzeb's death Muhammad Muʿizz al-Din Jahandar Shah was subadar of Multan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 844), and as governor of Multan he joined his father at Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 845). Abdus Samad Khan was appointed nazim of Multan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 716); he later died in Multan, and his body was brought to Lahore and buried beside the grave of Nawab Khan Bahadur (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 717). The suba of Multan was joined to Lahore in a year printed as 1155, the reading uncertain, and in a year printed as 1158, also uncertain, there was some disorder in Multan, in which Nawab Shahnawaz Khan was killed by a horseman of Diwan Kaura Mal, the name uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 717). Under Alamgir II the Afghans took Multan and Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 851), and Multan is among the cities the Marathas, with Sikh help, are said to have conquered (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 852). Of Ahmad Shah Abdali Chishti adds that some historians write his birthplace as the region of Multan, the word for region uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 853).

The books tell of Sikh attacks on Multan. Khalid writes that uprisings led by Muslim leaders began in Kasur and Multan, that the rebellion was given a religious colour against the infidel Ranjit Singh, and that Ranjit Singh employed the Nihangs to give the battles a religious tone in turn (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 210, folio not stated). Ernst and Lawrence write that Hafiz Muhammad Jamal, who died in 1811, a successor of Nur Muhammad and a contemporary of Sulayman Taunsawi, "is also said to have taken part in resistance to Sikh attacks on Multan" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 121, folio 108). Chishti writes that in the year written out in words as eighteen eighteen an army under Kharak Singh was sent to conquer Multan, and that Multan was conquered (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 580). In another place he writes of the Nawabs, rulers of Multan: when Ranjit Singh conquered Multan, Nawab Muzaffar Khan was killed, and Sarfraz Khan, part of whose name cannot be read, and others were captured with their women and children, came to Lahore, and now live in the place Chishti is describing (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 130). He records too that when Multan was conquered, by a conqueror whose name cannot be read or is not given and in a year he does not give, a learned man was needed to write the letter announcing the conquest to London, the name uncertainly printed, and the author's own father wrote it (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 22).

Chishti also records a rising in the city. In a year printed as 1848, the reading uncertain, Diwan Mulraj, son of Sawan Mal and nazim of Multan, raised a disturbance and had Agnew Sahib Bahadur, that name uncertain, killed with another sahib and with Kahn Singh, the new nazim of Multan, that name also uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 592). The Hindus Khalid met in the city, he writes, lived there before Multan became a part of Pakistan (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 21, folio not stated), and Dhillon writes of Multan as a city that is now in Pakistan (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 164, folio 180).

## A City of Saints

Several of the books describe Multan as a city of saints. Dhillon writes that during the twelfth century many Sufi scholars made Multan their base and that conversion gained momentum in the early thirteenth century (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 166–167, folios 182–183). "By the time Nanak came to Multan, the city was famous as a major centre of Sufi learning and philosophy", he writes, its schools fallen into a sense of rivalry (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 167, folio 183). He writes of the deras and dargahs in Multan and of its khanqah, the meeting place and residence of the Sufis, open to both Muslims and Hindus (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 167, folio 183; Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 168, folio 184). Bhandari, after naming the saints of the city, writes that many other saints lie at rest there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). In Rozehnal's account of a present day order, one speaker puts Multan beside Lahore and Karachi in the order's network: it is "the same data available on a computer in Karachi or Multan", and "It is like the Internet" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 155, folio 147).

Several books tie Multan to the Suhrawardi order. Schimmel writes that "Multan is the resting place of the great Suhrawardi saint Baha'uddin Zakariya (d. 1267)" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 286, folio 268), and names him Baha'uddin Zakariya of Multan, with the same year of death (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 104, folio 94). Omar Kasmani gives the same year for Bahaʾuddin Zakaria of Multan and adds that the shrines of these saints are spread across southern Punjab (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 17). Robert Rozehnal calls Makhdum-i Jahaniyan "the famous fourteenth-century Suhrawardi master of Multan" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 57, folio 47). In Multan, Schimmel writes, the Persian poet Fakhruddin ʿIraqi attached himself to Baha'uddin, to whom he dedicated some impressive *qasidas* (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 383, folio 352), and soon after Baha'uddin Zakariya's death ʿIraqi left Multan for Konya (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354). Bhandari records that Qutbuddin Bakhtiyar Kaki, coming from Baghdad, met Shaikh Baha al-Din Zakariya at Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 556).

Saints of other orders passed through the city. Ernst and Lawrence write that "The visit of Qutb ad-Din to Multan (a strategic commercial center in the Punjab) became the occasion for Farid ad- Din's pledge of allegiance to the Chishti master" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 80, folio 66). For Lal, the saint of Kasmani's study, Multan was the first destination: a few sacred sites in Balochistan and Sindh are the only traces of the route his journey might have taken to Multan, where he was first headed (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 62, folio 45), and "he is likely to have first arrived in Multan in 1263" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 14). Kasmani writes that sources mention a rare scene in Multan in which Lal is found in an ecstatic performance in the company of other Sufi figures, who are not named (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, pp. 62–63, folios 45–46), and that according to the Suhrawardi tradition the saint was initiated in Multan by Bahaʾuddin Zakaria (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 184, folio 167, intro n. 34). Rozehnal writes that Shaykh Muhammad al-Husseini al-Jilani, a native of Turkey, travelled to Khurasan and then Multan before settling with his family in Uch (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 259, folio 252). In a miracle that Zauqi Shah narrated, he and Muʿin ad-Din Chishti travelled instantaneously from Ajmer to Multan to help a sick friend (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, pp. 61–62, folios 51–52). Khalid writes that the body of Moosa Pak Shaheed was later shifted to Multan (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 278, folio 252).

Shams-i Tabriz has a shrine in Multan, and the books differ on whose it is. Schimmel, reading a poem of Sachal, writes of Shams-i Tabriz, "Maulana Rumi's spiritual beloved who, according to the Indian tradition, was flayed alive by the mollas in Multan where his shrine is still venerated" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 175, folio 157). She refers the reader, for "the mysterious figure of Shams", to Vladimir Ivanow's study of Shams Tabriz of Multan, without identifying him further (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 285, folio 267). Abbas, taken by one of her students to the shrine of one Hazrat Shams Tabrizi in Multan, writes that it may be the renowned Hazrat Shams Tabriz, the spiritual mentor of Maulana Rum, or another local Sufi by the same name, adding that "Multiple shrines to the same person are not unusual" and that sometimes there is an actual relic and at other times simply a connection through a *silsila* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 193, folio not stated).

Not all the city's holy places are the great ones. Schimmel writes that in all the provinces of Muslim India and Pakistan one may see shrines of women saints to which men are not admitted, and recalls one in Multan: "I vividly remember my visit to one such small shrine in Multan, decorated with lovely blue and white tiles; the cheerful women in charge of the tomb were no less greedy than the guardians in many other sacred places." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 464, folio 433). She names neither the shrine nor its saint (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 464, folio 433). Chishti, telling the legend of Sakhi Sarwar, refers in a damaged passage to an unnamed perfect saint who lived in Multan and lies, it seems, near the fort (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 260). Bhandari makes Multan the starting point of the pilgrim road to Sultan Sarwar's tomb (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527).

Dhillon and Khalid write of the city's Hindu holy places. Dhillon writes of the Suraj temple, once claimed to be the biggest in the subcontinent (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 165, folio 181), and of its giant idol destroyed by Mahmud (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 166, folio 182). Khalid's informant Parvati Devi told him that "There were several historical temples in Multan from the pre-partition days. All of them have become ruins now", and that the community has turned other spaces into makeshift temples (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 24, folio not stated). The thirty odd women who burned the Holika at Holi, he writes, "are now the only link that the city has with that story", the story of Prahlad (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 21, folio not stated).

## Communities

The books describe a city that held many peoples. Bhandari writes that people of every kind live in Multan and that the goods of every land are bought and sold there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). In Guru Nanak's time, Khalid writes, Multan "was one of the biggest cities of Punjab and was known for the religious shrines of Muslim saints, and Jain and Hindu temples. It was a sacred space for people of all religious denominations" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 123, folio 105). Of Nanak's later visit Khalid writes that "The Hindus were assured of their superiority, while the Muslims were assured of theirs" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 123, folio 105). Abbas writes of the Muftis and Gardezis who belonged to Lahore and Multan (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 26, folio not stated), and Boivin that Bedil was the scion of a Qurayshi family that had migrated from Multan two generations back (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 98, folio 85, endnote 11).

The Baluch appear often in Bhandari's Multan. He writes that the agents of the Baluch chiefs attend on the governor at Multan, the seat of government (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 526), that Ibrahim Husain Mirza, fleeing, was handed by the Baluch to the governor of Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 228), and that Haji Khan Baluch was one of the chief zamindars of Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 65). The Afghan tribes, he writes, live from the borders of Qandahar and Multan to Swat, over a stretch of more than three hundred kos (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 502). The Langahs, who became the city's sultans, were a people whose chief held the qasba of Sivi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 297).

Three books write of the city's speech. Bhandari writes that its people speak a tongue in which the speech of Lahore and of Sindh are mixed (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). Khalid writes that "The Siraiki spoken in Multan is closer to Punjabi", while at Uch it is more like Sindhi (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 276, folio 250), and Abbas places Multan in "the Siraiki belt around the Multan, Bahawalpur, Mianwali, and northern Sind areas" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 148, folio not stated).

The books record holy places open to more than one community. Dhillon describes the Multani khanqah as a meeting place and residence of the Sufis open to both Muslims and Hindus (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 167, folio 183; Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 168, folio 184). A Tableeghi Jamaat devotee Khalid calls Mussadiq told him: "There are several shrines in the Multan region and in Sindh where people still practise Hinduism and give it the name of Islam", where people light lamps, present offerings and even bow down to the grave, which he called *shirk* (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 10, ch. 4, folio not stated).

Khalid writes of the Hindus of modern Multan. He writes that the Holi locality, next to the railway station, is one of several areas where the city's Hindu community lives; that their population is little more than a thousand; and that of about a hundred houses in that settlement only about ten to twelve belong to Hindu families (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 23, folio not stated). An informant he calls Parvati Devi, a classically trained professional singer and social activist from Multan, recalled for him a mob after the demolition of the Babri Mosque that destroyed the Valmiki temple near her house and wanted to burn her alive (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 17, 21, folio not stated); she then took a Muslim name, Shaila Bibi, and stopped appearing on radio and television (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 21, folio not stated). Usman, as Khalid calls the organiser of the city's token Holi of 2010, has been alleged to be an Indian agent, and more than once he and his friends have been physically attacked (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 24–25, folio not stated; Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 25, folio not stated). Jürgen Schaflechner names Multan among the places where Pakistani Hindu communities live (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 80, folio not stated).

The books record harsh times between the city's communities in the past as well. Bhandari tells of the punishment of the Qaramita under Mahmud (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 417), and of a Prince's order that every slave of Firuz Shah be killed in Multan, Lahore and other cities (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 336). Khalid writes that the Muslim uprisings against Ranjit Singh in Kasur and Multan were given a religious colour (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 210, folio not stated).

## Shrines, Endowments and the State

Bhandari records rulers as patrons of the holy in Multan. He writes that Balban's son Prince Muhammad twice sent his people from Multan to Shiraz to fetch the poet Saʿdi, wishing to build a khanqah for the Shaikh in Multan and to endow villages for it, but that Saʿdi could not come because of his age (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 386). The book does not say whether the khanqah was built (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 386). Rulers came to the tombs: in a year printed as 835 the sultan made the round of the tombs of the shaikhs at Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 324), and the emperor, pursuing Dara Shikoh, visited Bahauddin's tomb (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 65).

In Bhandari the saints also stood between the state and the city. When the princes Rukn al-Din and Ibrahim were besieged in Multan they submitted through Shaikh Rukn al-Din (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 368–367), and after Malik Bahram's revolt it was Shaikh Rukn al-Din who turned the sultan from a general massacre of the people of Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 347). In the Langah story the Shaikh raised to the throne was Shaikh Yusuf Qureshi, chosen in council by the notables and the people of the city (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 297).

Abbas writes of the great families who keep up the shrines in the modern city. She writes that musicians such as Surraiya Multanikar are associated with the powerful landowning Syeds of Multan and Bahawalpur who claim descent from the Prophet's family: "Their training is supported by the Syed households, and these musicians perform in the family shrines of the Syeds", partly because the affluent Syeds, with their large landholdings, support the shrines of their ancestors (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 168–169, folios not stated). The families, she writes, "arrange annual rituals such as the Eid and the Muharram ceremonies marking the tragedy of Kerbala" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 168–169, folios not stated). She writes too of her association with the landowning Syeds of Burewala, Multan, a family of politicians in the Pakistan Muslim League (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 202, folio not stated). Neither passage names a shrine or a family (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 168–169, folios not stated).

The books touch on custodians and offerings. Schimmel found the women in charge of the small tiled shrine she visited in Multan "no less greedy than the guardians in many other sacred places" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 464, folio 433). Mussadiq, Khalid's informant, condemned the offerings presented at the shrines of the Multan region (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 10, ch. 4, folio not stated).

Bhandari records the city's wealth in the state's hands. Dara Shikoh, fleeing, took twenty two lakh rupees from the treasury of Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 66), and Rustam Mirza's jagir of Multan and Baluchistan yielded more than the revenue of Qandahar (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 175). The river carried its trade: merchants from Multan and Bhakkar, Bhandari writes, carry their goods and wares by boat down the river to Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529).

## Festivals and Practice

The books record music at the tombs and hospices of Multan. Ernst and Lawrence tell of a *qawwal* named Abu Bakr who came from Multan to visit Nizam ad-Din's teacher and reported the practice of *samaʿ* in the hospice of the Multani shaykh (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 171, folio 159). Schimmel writes that "The tender and intoxicating love poems that the Persian poet composed are still being sung by Pakistani musicians at the door of the master's tomb in Multan." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 384, folio 353). Abbas writes of performers who also "sing around shrines in Multan and Sahiwal to earn a living" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 199, folio not stated).

Abbas and Khalid write of devotion at the shrines. The Syed families of Multan and Bahawalpur, Abbas writes, arrange the Eid and the Muharram ceremonies marking the tragedy of Kerbala at the shrines of their ancestors (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 168–169, folios not stated). Devotion at the shrines of the Multan region, as Khalid's Tableeghi informant described it with disapproval, means lighting lamps, presenting offerings and bowing down to the grave (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 10, ch. 4, folio not stated). Pilgrimage ran outward too, along the road from Multan to Sultan Sarwar's tomb (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527).

Khalid follows the return of Holi to Multan. He writes that as recently as 2010 festivals including Holi were not celebrated in the city, and that in that year Usman organised "a token celebration of Holi in front of the Multan Press Club" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 24–25, folio not stated). Holi, "even though on a small scale is now celebrated every year in Multan", and is regularly featured in the local media (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 25, folio not stated). There is no centralised celebration, he writes (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 23, folio not stated), and the thirty odd women who burned the Holika are now the only link the city has with the story of Hiranyakashipu and Prahlad (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 21, folio not stated).

Dhillon retells the Janamsakhi of the bowl of milk and the jasmine petal: the oldest and most senior of the Sufi clerics called a conclave of all the heads of the *silsilas*, which lasted four days (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 168–169, folios 184–185); at the gates they set an earthen bowl filled to the brim with milk, and Nanak laid a jasmine petal on it (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 169–170, folios 185–186). Nanak often came to them to learn, Dhillon writes, and when he did finally leave Multan they were sorry to see him go (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 171, folio 187). On his fourth journey, Dhillon writes, Nanak's party first travelled north to Multan (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 194, folio 214).

## Poetry, Music and Language

Multan is a holy city in the vernacular poetry of Pakistan. Schimmel writes that for these poets "the Great Feast" is to meet the Beloved, and that "Mecca and Medina (and for the Sindhi and Panjabi, also Multan) are inside the heart" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 178, folio 160). The Indian tradition of the flaying of Shams-i Tabriz at Multan enters Sachal's poem (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 175, folio 157).

Bhandari places poets at the prince's court in Multan. He writes that Amir Khusrau of Delhi and Amir Hasan were at Multan in the service of Balban's son, among his boon companions, receiving salaries and gifts (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 386). Abbas writes that "The poet spent considerable time in Multan with Prince Muhammad" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 212, folio not stated). The prince's wish to bring Saʿdi from Shiraz to Multan failed only because of the poet's age (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 386). Schimmel writes of "Fakhruddin 'Iraqi's poems for his master Baha'uddin Zakariya Multani" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 287, folio 269).

Multani verse has its own genres. Schimmel names the tales of Tamim ad-Dari in Multani verse among the epic elaborations of early Islamic topics (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 284, folio 266), and writes that Christopher Shackle's "The Multani marsiya" offers an excellent analysis of popular religious poetry (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 285, folio 267; Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 339, folio 321). Khalid writes that the songs about Puran Bhagat spread as far as Amritsar and Multan, where local dialects gave the story unique flavours (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 185, folio 159).

In the verse of Madho Lal Hussein, as Naveed Alam translates it, Multan is a place of fine things: a bride's trousseau gathers "The dress / someone carried across the snows / all the way from Kashmir. The dress / someone brought from Gujarat… The dress bought in Multan" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 51, folio 51, kafi 29). The translator's apparatus points to the red wedding dress and the references to Kashmir, Gujarat and Multan before the image of Krishna with his flute, all of which "add to the mystery" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 182, folio 182).

Abbas names musicians of Multan. She lists Ehsan Wagha, music director, Multan (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 214–218, folios not stated), and Surraiya Multanikar among the musicians the Syeds support (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 168–169, folios not stated). Parvati Devi, the Hindu singer Khalid met, was a classically trained professional singer who stopped appearing on radio and television after the attack that followed the demolition of the Babri Mosque (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 17, 21, folio not stated; Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 21, folio not stated).

## The Place in the Books

Bhandari, in his account of the suba of Multan, describes the city itself. Under the heading of the suba of Dar al-Aman Multan he calls it one of the ancient cities, with a fort of brick (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). Merchants, he writes, bring horses of Arab breed from Iraq by way of Qandahar and sell them in that city (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528), and its people make fine carpets, flowered *shatranji* rugs and chintz in imitation of those of the port (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). The winter is mild, the summer excessively hot and the rain little (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528), and the heat of Multan had once sent Mahmud's soldiers home (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 420–419). He places the city on its rivers and borders: the Indus passes the borders of the Baluch and of Multan, and beyond Multan the five rivers of the Punjab join it (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 510); the suba of Lahore has Multan on its west (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 510); the Ravi joins the combined Chenab and Bihat near a sarai whose printed name, perhaps Sidhu, is uncertain, twenty kos from Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 512); and he names a stretch from the borders of Multan and Uch to Thatta and Kech Makran (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529). Earlier, he writes, Sultan Husain Langah had been the ruler of Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525). The printed edition's index gives Multan a headword with many locators (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 20).

In Chishti's book Multan is often seen from Lahore. A governor of Multan joins his father at Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 845), a governor who died at Multan is buried there (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 717), and the defeated Nawabs of Multan live there (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 130). In his telling of the Sakhi Sarwar legend Multan has a ruler, called both *hakim* and king, with a fort, a jail and a gate of Multan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 259–263).

For Dhillon, retelling the Janamsakhis, Multan is the meeting ground of Nanak and the Sufis, and he locates Tulamba, modern Makhdumpur, in Multan, in the Puratan account of Nanak's first journey of 1499 to 1506 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 54, folio 60). For Khalid it is a city of routes: Tulamba lies about midway between Lahore and Multan, by the old Multan road (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 123, folio 105; Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 129, folio 111), and Deepalpur stood on an important route connecting the cities of Delhi and Multan (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 205, folio 179). It is also, for him, a city that moves forward unaware of its ancient past (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 20–21, folio not stated). Schaflechner quotes Charles King, writing in 1865, on "the desert between Cutch and Multan" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 258, folio not stated).

For the scholars of Sufism Multan is a fixed point on the map. Schimmel locates Uch in relation to it (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354), as does Rozehnal, who glosses Uch Sharif as "an ancient city located seventy miles southwest of Multan in Pakistan" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 210, folio 202), and Rizvi, who writes of Uch, near Multan (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 258, folio 238).

Several books cite works published in Multan. Schimmel and Ernst and Lawrence cite works published there: Shackle's study of the Siraiki mystical poetry of Sind, published in Multan about 1976 (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 340, folio 322; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 503, folio 472), and books on Khwaja Farid printed at Multan in 1978 and 1983 (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 225, folio 214; Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 230, folio 219). Abbas cites the Multan publishers Bazm-e Saqafat and Qadimi Kutab Khana (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 207, 209, 222, 232, 235, folios not stated) and a book of the city's own saints, Bashir H. Nazim's *Auliya-e Multan* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 229, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **Where Uch lies.** Schimmel writes of settling "in Ucch, northeast of Multan" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354). Rozehnal places Uch Sharif seventy miles southwest of Multan (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 210, folio 202).
- **Who gave Khizr Khan Multan.** Chishti writes that Timur made him governor of Multan and Dipalpur (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 816). Bhandari writes in one place that Timur granted him Lahore, Multan and Dipalpur (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 331), and in another that Sultan Muhammad Shah bin Sultan Firuz Shah gave him Multan and Dipalpur as one of four provinces (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 241).
- **Who governed Multan when Ghazi took the throne.** Chishti makes Ghazi Beg Tughluq himself the governor of the suba of Multan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 814). Bhandari has Ghazi al-Malik march on Delhi in league with Bahram, governor of Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 354).
- **Balban's son and the Mongols.** Chishti writes that Balban's son Muhammad Shah drove the invaders out of Multan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 811). Bhandari writes that the prince came out of Multan against them and met a martyr's death after a short fight (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 385), and Abbas that he was killed in the Battle of Multan (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 39, folio not stated).
- **The last Langah years.** Bhandari dates the accession of the child Sultan Husain to a year printed as 945 (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 294), and dates Mirza Shah Husain Arghun's conquest of Multan, which he describes as taken from that same child, to a year printed as 932 (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 294). Both years are uncertainly printed.
- **The commander defeated by Sultan Husain Langah.** Bhandari names him Barbak Shah (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 296) and, a page later, Baba Shah (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 295); both readings are uncertain.
- **The year of Aurangzeb's stay at Multan.** Bhandari dates the imperial camp near Multan to the seventh of Muharram of a year printed as 1069 (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 66), and the march from Multan to the twelfth of Muharram of a year printed as 1068 (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 65); both readings are uncertain.
- **Whose is the Shams Tabriz shrine at Multan.** Schimmel records the Indian tradition that Rumi's beloved Shams-i Tabriz was flayed alive in Multan, where his shrine is still venerated (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 175, folio 157). Abbas, writing of a shrine of one Hazrat Shams Tabrizi in Multan, leaves open whether it is the renowned Shams or another local Sufi of the same name (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 193, folio not stated).
- **The title of Ivanow's study.** Schimmel's text gives it as "Shams Tabriz of Multan" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 285, folio 267); her bibliography prints "Shums Tabrez of Multan" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 326, folio 308).
