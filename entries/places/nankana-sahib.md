## Overview

The books know Nankana Sahib as the town where Guru Nanak was born, and they know it by an older name. Harish Dhillon writes of "Nanakana Sahib (earlier called Talwandi)" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 175, folio "l92" [text-layer?] [192]). Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi places "the village of Talwandi later known as Nankana Sahib" about forty miles to the south west of Lahore, now part of Pakistan (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 407, folio 383). Haroon Khalid writes that the city was originally called Rai Bhoi Di Talwandi and was "rechristened Nankana Sahib in honour of Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* he adds that the name "has been changed to Nankana Sahib in honour of the first Sikh Guru who was born there" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 31, folio 13). The Persian chronicle of Sujan Rai Bhandari records that Baba Nanak was born at Talwandi Rai Bhoi and lived there in his grandfather's house (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

In Dhillon's retelling of the *janamsakhis* the village lay between the Ravi and the Chenab, in the path of the armies bound for Delhi, and rose again each time it was destroyed (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 15–16, folios 17–18); by the time Nanak came back from Pak Pattan it "was now no longer an obscure little village but had become a place of pilgrimage" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 172, folio 188). Khalid calls it a city "considered to be one of the holiest cities in the world for the Sikhs, if not the holiest" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 221–222, folio not stated), where several gurdwaras mark events from Nanak's childhood (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 43, folio 25).

The Nankana Sahib of Khalid's reportage is a town of a Muslim majority (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 176, folio not stated) and a small Sikh community, most of it families that came recently from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 235, folio not stated), about two hundred households in his count in *Walking with Nanak* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 87, folio 69). Its gurdwaras stand locked for most of the year and open for the festivals (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 227, folio not stated). At Guru Nanak's birthday pilgrims come from all over the world (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 182, folio not stated), and "The entire city looks like a mini-India during these few days" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 226, folio not stated).

## History

Dhillon, retelling the *janamsakhis*, sets the village on the road of invasion. Talwandi, he writes, lay "between the rivers Ravi and Chenab, directly in the path of the armies that had set their eyes on Delhi"; thirteen times it was destroyed, and thirteen times, like the fabled phoenix, it rose again from its ashes; and its last rebirth took place under the supervision of Rai Bhoe, a Bhatti Rajput who had converted to Islam, the clan word being damaged in the printed text and read as Rajput (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 15–16, folios 17–18). It also lay, he writes, "in the path of numerous mendicants, sadhus and holy men", who camped "in a grove of trees on the outskirts of the village" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 24, folio 28).

Iqbal Qaiser gave Khalid a version of the same story on the ground. Of the mound behind Gurdwara Janamsthan he said that it "is an archaeological mound. The settlement there was destroyed after which Rai Bhoi di Talwindi was constructed within a boundary wall"; asked "So Nanak was born in the new Nankana?", he answered "Yes" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73).

The books name the families of the village. Dhillon gives Rai Bhoe and his son Rai Bular, of the Bhatti, and Shiv Ram, head of a Bedi family and accountant to Rai Bhoe, who was succeeded by his son Kalyan Chand Das Bedi (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 17, folio 19). In Khalid's reconstruction Mehta Kalu keeps "the accounts of the ruler of Talwindi, Rai Bular Bhatti", who is his friend (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 22, folio 4), and Mehta Kalu is a Hindu of the kshatriya, the warrior caste, the word being damaged in the printed text (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 29, folio 11).

Nanak's birth there is dated differently in different books. Dhillon gives it as "On 14 April 1469" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20); the chronology in *Walking with Nanak* has Guru Nanak "Born Talwandi (now known as Nankana Sahib in Pakistan) on October 20, 1469" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 314, folio 288). Khalid notes that Meherban "claims that Guru Nanak was born in Dera Chahal and not Rai Bhoi di Talwindi", while other *janamsakhi* writers and Sikh scholars "are unanimous that Nanak was born in Rai Bhoi di Talwindi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 43, folio 25). Bhandari's chronicle, in Persian, says that Baba Nanak was born at Talwandi Rai Bhoi and lived there in his grandfather's house; the reading of the place name is uncertain in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

The landscape around the village enters the story of the birth. Citing Harish Dhillon's *The First Sikh Spiritual Master*, Khalid writes that "Mata Triptaji, the mother of Guru Nanak, did not have a child for a long time after her marriage and she would regularly visit an ancient Hindu temple situated on top of a mound not very far from Rai Bhoi di Talwindi to pray for a child" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 30–31, folio 13). In his own reconstruction Tripta presents herself to sadhus and fakirs in "the forest around Rai Bhoi di Talwindi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 21, folio 3).

The *janamsakhis*, as Dhillon retells them, show the boy at school in Talwandi with "a brilliance that had never before been seen in any child in Talwandi" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 18, folio 20). Khalid places the furore over the sacred thread in the traditional society of this small town, in a passage damaged in the printed text (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 62, folio 44). He has Nanak meet ascetics and Sufis on the outskirts of Talwandi (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 67, folio 49) and spend time at the abandoned mound on its outskirts (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 66, folio 48). Khalid writes that a young Nanak "used to spend a lot of time in conversation with yogis, sadhus and dervish who would pass through Talwindi" on the mound, and that "It is believed that in the years between the incident of Sacha Sauda and his migration to Sultanpur, Nanak started to frequent this place", "That period of six to seven years" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73; Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 92, folio 74). Bebe Nanaki and Jai Ram, in Khalid's telling, were visiting Talwandi from Sultanpur (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 67, folio 49), and Mardana was Nanak's best friend from Talwandi (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 106, folio 88). When Nanak said that there is no Hindu or Muslim, the statement was questioned by friends and family at Rai Bhoi Di Talwandi (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 144, folio 126). As a youngster at Talwandi, Khalid writes, Nanak had planned to visit Katas Raj, which was about fifty *kos* from Talwandi, a reading the printed text leaves uncertain (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 237, folio 211).

The story of Sacha Sauda begins and ends in the village. In Dhillon's retelling Nanak was given goods to be sold in Talwandi (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 25, folio 29), and the story ends in the forest and in Talwandi (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 38, folio 42). In the version a storyteller at Sachkand gave Khalid, Nanak's father in Nankana Sahib became worried, the mules were loaded for Nankana Sahib, and at Nankana the father asked what trade his son had done (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 74, folio 56; Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 75, folio 57). Khalid traces Nanak's route back from Chuhrkhana "to Nankana Sahib, which at that point in time was still known as Rai Bhoi di Talwindi", across "a vast jungle that existed then between Nankana and Chuhrkhana" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 86, folio 68).

Talwandi is the place the travels leave from and return to. Dhillon puts Sultanpur "around 60 km from Talwandi" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 44, folio 49), and Khalid writes that for the Sultanpur journey Nanak "travelled from Nankana", and that he set out from Rai Bhoi di Talwandi with Mardana and was back in Talwandi between journeys, the name damaged in the printed text in both places (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36). In Dhillon the first *udasi* passes from Sultanpur to Talwandi (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 56, folio 62). In Khalid's reconstruction Nanak returns to Talwandi and Mehta Kalu, disappointed, says nothing when he leaves again (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 107, folio 89); Sulakhni and her two children, Mehta Kalu and Mata Tripta take leave of him (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 108, folio 90); years after they had left Talwandi, "both of them decided that it was time to go back home" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 163, folio 145); and in another passage Nanak and Mardana leave the familiar streets of Talwandi while friends and families gather at the edge of the town (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 239, folio 213).

Dhillon writes that "Nanak and Mardana finally returned to Talwandi" in 1506, the year being damaged in the printed text and read as 1506; "Mata Tripta felt great joy", and Nanak spent time each day with Rai Bhoe, who looked upon him as his Guru (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 123, folio 137). Mehta Kalu, he writes, was "a little in awe of his son" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 124, folio 138). After Pak Pattan, "Nanak travelled back to Talwandi", which "was now no longer an obscure little village but had become a place of pilgrimage", and he sat by Rai Bular, who was sick and failing fast (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 172, folio 188). Dhillon puts Kartarpur at 180 km from Nankana Sahib by way of Lahore (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 175, folio "l92" [text-layer?] [192]), and Khalid writes that Nanak sent for his family from Talwandi to Kartarpur (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 296, folio 270). Kartarpur in turn became a town of pilgrims, Dhillon writes, like Talwandi and Sultanpur (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 176, folio 193).

Partition emptied the town of its Sikhs. An informant Khalid calls Kartar told him: "There was no Sikh left here after partition. Our Gurdwaras became barren and were in a bad state" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 184, folio not stated). Iqbal Qaiser told him that "The majority of the Muslims in Nankana Sahib are" those who migrated from the other side of the border following Partition, their attitudes "shaped by those experiences" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 76, folio 58). From a rooftop Khalid saw the "remains of a few Hindu temples; cone like structures rising from houses abandoned at the time of partition" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 225, folio not stated), and he writes that the traditional war games of Guru Gobind Singh's birthday "ended here at the time of partition" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 176, folio not stated).

The years of the Khalistan Movement were, in the words of an informant Khalid calls Rajbir, "one of the most tumultuous times in the city's history" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 213, folio not stated). Of the recent past Khalid writes that during the Musharraf era, from 2001 to 2008, "newly constructed roads and other government facilities made their way into the city", and that "In 2005, Nankana Sahib was separated from Sheikhupura and made into an independent district, with this city becoming the district headquarters" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 226, folio not stated). He gives it a population of over a million (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 176, folio not stated).

## Holy Places

The gurdwaras are what the books see first. "There are seven Gurdwaras in Nankana Sahib, most of which are associated with incidents from the life of Baba Guru Nanak", Khalid writes in *A White Trail* (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 192, folio not stated); in *Walking with Nanak* he counts "a total of six gurdwaras associated with Guru Nanak at Nankana Sahib" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72). Because Nanak spent his early years here there are several gurdwaras in the city, and pilgrims visit all of them (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated). "All year round most of them remain locked, only to be opened at the time of the festival. There are security guards employed at the entrance to all of them." (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 227, folio not stated)

From a roof Khalid looked over the town: the minarets of mosques and the "small white domes of the Gurdwaras", the remains of a few Hindu temples, cone like structures rising from houses abandoned at Partition, and the railway station (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 225, folio not stated). "There is one main road that runs through the entire city", he writes; "The entire city needs a major uplift. The buildings are old and the road has pot holes"; and "Most of the buildings are from the late British or the early Pakistan era" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 186, folio not stated).

Behind Gurdwara Janamsthan, "away from the hub of the new settlement", rests the mound of the older village (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73). Khalid describes a steep climb "with no proper path", to a height of "at least twenty metres from the ground", over a mound "covered with small shrubs of karir (capparis deciduas)", with "shards of broken pottery and other mud utensils" underfoot (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 92, folio 74). It was there, Khalid writes, that the young Nanak talked with the yogis, sadhus and dervishes who passed through (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73). Khalid also writes of a mound near Shahkot, "only a few kilometres from the holy city of Nankana Sahib" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 31, folio 13), and, citing Dhillon's other book, of the ancient Hindu temple on a mound not far from the village to which Mata Tripta went to pray for a child (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 30–31, folio 13).

A Muslim saint keeps the Guru company there. In *In Search of Shiva* Khalid writes: "There is a picture of Baba Farid accompanying the picture of Guru Nanak at the shrine of Guru Nanak at Nankana Sahib"; the book does not say which gurdwara it means (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).

## Communities

Khalid writes of the Sikhs of Nankana Sahib: "My research on the Sikh community was limited to the Sikhs based in Nankana Sahib", and that "these are not indigenous people from the city", but recent migrants from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, where there is a considerable Sikh population (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 235, folio not stated). He writes that "In the past ten years, the population has increased almost sevenfold. There were only about thirty to forty Sikh houses in Nankana ten years ago", with families arriving after the alienation that followed the wars of 1965 and 1971 and the demolition of the Babri Mosque in 1992 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 174–175, folio not stated). Refugee families, he writes, moved to Peshawar, Hassan Abdal and Nankana Sahib "and started living at Gurdwaras", and "In about ten years, the population in Nankana Sahib increased to about two hundred houses" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 175, folio not stated). Kartar put it this way: "Just a few years ago, there were only about fifty Sikh houses, but now there are almost two hundred to two hundred and fifty Sikh homes" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 193, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* the community amounts to about two hundred households, and Jatha Singh has for several years played the role of its leader (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 87, folio 69).

"Most of the newcomers were incorporated within the Gurdwaras, in rooms which were generally reserved for pilgrims", Khalid writes; the local community helped them with small businesses or with rent, and the influx gave "a confidence boost to the Sikhs who were until now dominated by the Muslim majority" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 176, folio not stated). Kartar told him: "Ours was one of the first families to move to Nankana Sahib" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 184, folio not stated). Rajbir said that from Parachinar, "Within two months, my cousin and I moved to Nankana", at a time when "There were hardly any Sikhs here" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 213, folio not stated). Iqbal Qaiser asked one Boota Singh when his family had moved to Nankana Sahib from the Tribal Areas (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 83, folio 65), and Inderjit's family moved to Nankana in 1965 (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 306, folio 280). Madan Singh's family "has been living at Nankana since the late seventies", and he is "one of the richest Sikhs from the city" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 215, folio not stated). Baljeet's is "one of the latest families to have moved to Nankana" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 175, folio not stated).

The newcomers brought their languages. Surjeet and Udam, as Khalid names them, speak Urdu with a Pashtu accent, their families having moved to Nankana a few years before; "not all Sikhs here can speak Urdu or Punjabi", and the commentator at a match shifts between Urdu and Pashtu (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 174, folio not stated). Kartar recalled: "I was in class at Nankana Sahib during my undergraduate studies when a professor of mine commented that Punjabi is a language of the unrefined" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 202, folio not stated).

Khalid records how the Sikhs of the town earn a living. "A number of Sikhs living here are engaged in this profession", that of the *hakeem*, "following a practice that goes back several generations" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 174, folio not stated); "A lot of the Sikh families at Nankana Sahib are involved in hikmat (traditional practice of medicine)", and Dr. Manoj Singh, of "one of the Pathan families at Nankana Sahib, who settled there after the unrest in the tribal areas", now lives in Lahore but travels to Nankana Sahib regularly and treats old people free of charge (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 202, folio not stated). "There has been a growing migration of Sikhs boys and men from Nankana Sahib to bigger cities like Lahore, Faisalabad, and Gujranawala", where they work in grocery shops, cloth trading and *hikmat*; Surjeet's cousin Manjeet has done an MBA and works in Lahore, and Udham Singh told Khalid that he is originally from Nankana but has his business in Gujranawala (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 174, folio not stated).

The town carries Nanak's name in its trade. "Guru Nanak is celebrated all over Nankana Sahib. There is a Guru Nanak High School, a Guru Nanak grocery store and a Guru Nanak cloth shop, all run by Sikhs. The Muslims generally refrain from eulogizing Guru Nanak in this particular way" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 172, folio not stated). At Guru Nanak Government College "there are only a few Sikh boys studying here; the majority of the students are Muslim" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 172, folio not stated). Madan Singh, "A successful businessman from Nankana Sahib" and a former head of the PSGPC, runs Guru Nanak High School, "the only school in the country where the Granth Sahib is taught to Sikh children" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 203, folio not stated).

Living side by side has its lines. At a juice shop Kartar showed Khalid the separate glasses, and told him that a few shopkeepers in the town keep separate utensils for Muslims and for others (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 186, folio not stated). Aslam Chaudary, who has kept a restaurant on the main artillery road for ten years, turns Sikh customers away with "We do not have your utensils", and complains that "We become prisoners within our own city"; not all traders are resentful, Khalid adds, since the pilgrims boost the local economy (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 226, folio not stated). During a Sikh procession through the town, Muslims in shops and on bikes watch "with burning curiosity" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 192, folio not stated). Of the respect the Muslims around Sachkand show Nanak, Khalid says that one does not find it "in the Muslims of Nankana Sahib", and Iqbal Qaiser answers that most Muslims of the town migrated from across the border at Partition, their attitudes shaped by that experience (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 76, folio 58).

Sikhs from Nankana Sahib also keep other places. Rori Sahib's Pathan Sikh caretakers "have moved here from Nankana Sahib" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 117, folio 99), and Baba Bair was said to be run by "a few Sikhs living in Nankana Sahib" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 194, folio 168).

## Gurdwaras, Custodians and the State

The first care of the gurdwaras after Partition came from India. Kartar told Khalid that "Giani Pratap" came with an Indian pilgrimage party "sometime in the sixties", stayed "to look after the Gurdwara", housed Kartar's family inside it for a short time and made his father "a sewaadar (worker) at the Gurdwara"; Giani Pratap "died recently in Lahore" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 184, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* Khalid writes of him: "After looking at the dilapidated condition of the shrines here he decided to stay and look after these holy buildings. That was the first time someone had taken the initiative to take care of the gurdwaras in Nankana Sahib after Partition" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72). Rajbir said that a speech by Chauhan "invited Pakistani Sikhs to move to Nankana Sahib to take care of the Gurdwaras" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 213, folio not stated).

In 1985, Khalid writes, the PSGPC had not yet been formed, and the SGPC, based in Amritsar, managed the Pakistani gurdwaras through the Indian High Commission in Islamabad, whose staff received the pilgrims at the border and arranged their stay inside the gurdwaras (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 215–216, folio not stated). That November, at Guru Nanak's birthday, "a year after Operation Blue Star", thousands of Sikh pilgrims had come to Nankana from all over the world, and after the Akhand Path and the *bhog* "Sardar Ganga Singh Dhillion, an American Sikh", spoke for a separate Sikh state (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 215, folio not stated). During the speech the gate of the gurdwara was closed and "Counsellor B. Jain and First Secretary K. K. Khanna" were attacked and barely escaped alive. Madan Singh, "only 19 at that time", was among the attackers, and was arrested with twenty five others and held at Sheikhupura Jail for ten days; afterwards banners reading "Khalistan Zindabad" and pictures of "Sant Jarnail Singh Bhindrawale" were all over the gurdwara (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 216, folio not stated). "The religious festivals at Nankana and other cities of Pakistan have gone back to being religious festivals", Khalid concludes (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 216, folio not stated).

The PSGPC's standing in the town is disputed. "Several people whom I interviewed at Nankana Sahib felt that PSGPC was not representative of the Sikh community in Pakistan", Khalid writes (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 36, folio 18). Madan Singh, a former head of the PSGPC, says of its current head: "He never visits the city" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 203, folio not stated).

Guards, police and officials stand around the gurdwaras and the festival. Security guards are employed at the entrance to all the gurdwaras (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 227, folio not stated). At Guru Nanak's birthday there is "a heavy presence of police officials at all the entrances to the city. No car is permitted inside" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 220, folio not stated). Representatives of the ETPB and the PSGPC receive the Indian pilgrims at Wagah, where a *langar* is arranged for them (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 222–223, folio not stated). The name of Kar Seva is on "yellow dustbins and other boards that have been placed in different Gurdwaras of the city" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 223, folio not stated). Sikhs living abroad, Khalid writes, ask for an international hotel and for visa free "holy city" status for Nankana Sahib, and there has been no progress (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 226, folio not stated).

## Festivals and Practice

Khalid's festivals at Nankana Sahib are those of 2011. "The date is 10th of January 2011 and the occasion is the birthday celebration of Guru Gobind Singh": on the ground of Guru Nanak Government College, reserved for the Sikh community that day, a twenty over cricket tournament is played among "Khalsa XI", "Nankana XI" and "Singh XI", with live commentary by two Sikh boys, a *dhol* player, a dismissed batsman dancing *bhangra*, women watching from a tent and a few Muslim students looking on (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 172–173, folio not stated). Surjeet told him: "For the first time, this year we have arranged this cricket tournament" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 176, folio not stated). "The concluding ceremony of the festival is held on the morning of 11th January 2011" at the gurdwara at eight in the morning; people working away from the town have come back to Nankana, trophies are brought from the main shrine, the tallest goes to Khalsa XI, and a communal prayer follows (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 179–180, folio not stated).

These celebrations are new. Kartar told Khalid that Lohri and Guru Gobind Singh's birthday "have only begun in the last couple of years", made possible "by the increase in the Sikh population as a result of the migration from the tribal areas after the war"; "Earlier, there was only a prayer at the Gurdwara." (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 193, folio not stated). "The celebration of Lohri at Nankana Sahib this year is an attempt to revive this cultural tradition of Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 193–194, folio not stated). Events kept alive by foreign pilgrims, Khalid writes, included Guru Nanak's birthday at Nankana Sahib (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 176, folio not stated).

The Sikh New Year of 14 March 2011 was quieter: "unlike the other festivals, this is not publicized as it is not only a religious event but also a political one, with no precedent in Pakistan", and the visit of Sindhi pilgrims coincided with it (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 215, folio not stated). Pakistani Sikhs like Kartar keep Purewal's original calendar: "This explains why March 14th was being celebrated as Sikh New Year here, while Indian Sikhs continue to celebrate New Year on April 13th" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 217, folio not stated). The reading of the Granth in a pavilion ended in prayer, *kirtan* was sung with tablas and harmonium, and a speaker named Kartar Singh declared from a pulpit: "We Sikhs are a separate nation and we will not let the Indian sarkar, which is a Hindu lobby, take our identity away"; the crowd listened indifferently (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 215, folio not stated). Then six *qawwals* in brown shalwar kameez and Muslim caps sang, first to "the Muslim Saint Baba Fareed" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 217–218, folio not stated). Kartar said: "This is the first time since partition that Muslim Qawwalis have been invited to perform at Nankana Sahib. We have called them from Faisalabad" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 218, folio not stated).

The great festival is Guru Nanak's birthday, which Khalid calls "Guru Nanak Jayanti or Gurpurab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated). Kartar told him that "thousands of people come to Nankana Sahib from all over the world" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 182, folio not stated). "According to official estimates, ten to fifteen thousand people come to Nankana Sahib for this festival every year, which is celebrated during the last days of November"; in 2011, "According to media reports, only three thousand people are attending", and "This year it began on the 8th of November and will conclude today, on the 10th of November" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated). Cars are kept out of the city and police stand at its entrances (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 220, folio not stated), and devotees walk barefoot (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 221–222, folio not stated). Mahtab Singh, seventy two years old, "became a celebrity when his interview was broadcast on national television at the 2010 festival", having vowed to go shoeless throughout his pilgrimage (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated).

Trains bring pilgrims from India, from "Delhi, Gujarat and Amritsar", across the border at Wagah, and they wait three days at Nankana (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 222–223, folio not stated). There are three days of Akhand Path, and the Nagar Kirtan follows the *bhog* on the final day; the pilgrims then go on to other cities, while richer pilgrims from Britain, America and Canada come by chartered plane and hire buses, and the posters of travel companies promise all the gurdwaras of Pakistan (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 223, folio not stated). Pilgrims buy souvenirs; women wear the *mangalsutra* and *sindoor*; banners reading "STD" are put up for the Indian pilgrims; and "The entire city looks like a mini-India during these few days" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 226, folio not stated). Tourists on pilgrimage, Khalid writes in *Walking with Nanak*, come to Nankana Sahib and Hassan Abdal (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 79, folio 61). The festival is felt far away: Jeeta Singh of Manora holds a small festival of his own "every November when the festival of Guru Nanak is celebrated at Nankana Sahib" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 228, folio 202).

## The Place in the Books

For Dhillon, retelling the *janamsakhis*, Talwandi is the village of Nanak's birth and youth, and he marks how it changed: when Nanak came back from Pak Pattan it "was now no longer an obscure little village but had become a place of pilgrimage" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 172, folio 188), and Kartarpur in turn became a pilgrim town like Talwandi and Sultanpur (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 176, folio 193). The older histories give the place a line each. Rizvi locates it, as "the village of Talwandi later known as Nankana Sahib", about forty miles south west of Lahore (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 407, folio 383). Bhandari's Persian chronicle names Talwandi Rai Bhoi as the place where Baba Nanak was born and lived in his grandfather's house (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

Khalid's three books give the town the most space. In *A White Trail* it is one of the holiest cities in the world for the Sikhs (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 183, folio not stated), and four of the book's chapters are set there, on Guru Gobind Singh's birthday, Lohri, the Sikh New Year and Guru Nanak's birthday at Nankana Sahib (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 5, folio not stated; Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 6, folio not stated). He places it for the reader as "the historical city of Nankana Sahib, associated with the birth of the founder of the Sikh religion", near Itan wali, the village of Aasia Bibi (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 90, folio not stated), and writes simply that "Guru Nanak was born in a city now called Nankana Sahib, Pakistan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 168, folio not stated). His eye is on small things as well: "Nankana still has Pepsi bottles with the older logos" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 221, folio not stated). He ends the festival with the town emptying: "Things in Nankana Sahib will soon calm down. Atleast till the next year…." (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 232, folio not stated)

In *Walking with Nanak* Nankana Sahib is "the holy city", a few kilometres from the mound near Shahkot (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 31, folio 13), and Talwandi is the small town of Nanak's youth, where he met ascetics and Sufis on the outskirts and where his father watched him leave again in disappointed silence (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 67, folio 49; Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 107, folio 89). For Jeeta Singh of Manora, as Khalid reports him, Nanak never came to Manora: "He was from Punjab, Nankana Sahib", and he had never left Nankana Sahib, the place of his birth (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 228, folio 202; Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 229, folio not stated). In *In Search of Shiva* the picture of Baba Farid beside Guru Nanak's at the town's shrine of Guru Nanak comes in the chapter "Syncretism in the Mainstream" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The date of Guru Nanak's birth.** Dhillon gives it as "On 14 April 1469" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20). The chronology in *Walking with Nanak* gives "October 20, 1469" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 314, folio 288).
- **The place of Guru Nanak's birth.** Meherban, as Khalid reports, claims that Guru Nanak was born in Dera Chahal and not in Rai Bhoi di Talwandi; other *janamsakhi* writers and Sikh scholars are unanimous that he was born in Rai Bhoi di Talwandi (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 43, folio 25). Dhillon and Bhandari place the birth at Talwandi (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).
- **Whether Nanak ever left.** Jeeta Singh of Manora told Khalid that Nanak had never left Nankana Sahib, the place of his birth (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 228, folio 202; Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 229, folio not stated). Dhillon and Khalid's own narrative have him set out from Talwandi and return to it between journeys (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 56, folio 62; Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 123, folio 137; Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36).
- **How many gurdwaras.** *A White Trail* counts seven gurdwaras in Nankana Sahib, most of them associated with incidents from Nanak's life (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 192, folio not stated). *Walking with Nanak* counts six gurdwaras associated with Guru Nanak there (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72).
- **How many Sikh households.** In one passage of *A White Trail* there were only about thirty to forty Sikh houses ten years before and the population had grown almost sevenfold (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 174–175, folio not stated); in another the population rose in about ten years to about two hundred houses (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 175, folio not stated); Kartar spoke of about fifty Sikh houses a few years before and almost two hundred to two hundred and fifty now (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 193, folio not stated). *Walking with Nanak* gives about two hundred households (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 87, folio 69).
- **When and from where the Sikh families came.** *A White Trail* calls them recent migrants from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 235, folio not stated) and dates their coming to the years after the wars of 1965 and 1971 and the demolition of the Babri Mosque in 1992 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 174–175, folio not stated); Kartar's was one of the first families, housed for a time by Giani Pratap, who came in the sixties (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 184, folio not stated); Rajbir came from Parachinar during the Khalistan Movement, when there were hardly any Sikhs in the town (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 213, folio not stated); Madan Singh's family has lived there since the late seventies (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 215, folio not stated); and in *Walking with Nanak* Inderjit's family moved to Nankana in 1965 (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 306, folio 280).
- **How many come for Guru Nanak's birthday.** Official estimates, as Khalid gives them, put the number at ten to fifteen thousand a year, and media reports at only three thousand in 2011 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated); Kartar spoke of thousands of people from all over the world (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 182, folio not stated).
