## Overview

The books that speak of Karachi know it first as a port and a great modern city. Haroon Khalid writes that at the time of Partition it was "a minor port city", which migrants from India made "the largest city of Pakistan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 226, folio 200), and he found himself in "the megapolis of Karachi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 215, folio 189). Its older name survives in legend: Khalid records that the area is known as the area of Mai Kolachi, the old woman who tore apart the fish that had killed her son, and that the city came to be known as Kolachi after her (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 225, folio 199).

Michel Boivin's Karachi is the city of colonial Sindh: the conqueror and first governor of Sindh, Sir Charles Napier, built a Masonic lodge there in 1843 (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 46, folio 33), a printing press was brought to the city in the 1850s (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 48, folio 35), and in 1896 the Theosophical Society was founded there (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 46, folio 33). In 1907 the first meeting of the Muslim League was held in Karachi, and the Chishti master Muhammad Zauqi Shah was present at it (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 96, folio 82; Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 108, folio 99).

For the historian of music Regula Burckhardt Qureshi, Karachi is one of "two important urban centres of Sufism" in the subcontinent, paired with Hyderabad in India, where members of the Qawwal Bachche lineage "actively maintain their special Qawwali heritage" (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 33, folio 13). For Robert Rozehnal it is the home of a living Chishti order, whose teaching shaykh leads weekly circles of *zikr* in the city (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 165, folio 157) near the tomb complex of Shahidullah Faridi (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 201, folio 193).

The books also show a city of many faiths. Khalid sought out Hindu temples at Clifton, where his visit was aborted, at Ram Bagh and inside the Port Grand complex (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 221, folio 195; Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 223, folio 197; Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 225, folio 199), and he writes that "The majority of the Zoroastrian community of Pakistan is based in Karachi" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 234, folio not stated). Shemeem Burney Abbas records a Sidi congregation in Karachi at Manghopir (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 70–71, folios not stated) and, in a passage on the journey of Bava Gor, a memorial shrine in the city (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 73, folio not stated). Jürgen Schaflechner makes Karachi the start of the road route to Hinglaj (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 158, folio not stated) and the home of the Devipujak-Vagri community (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 306–312, 322, folios not stated).

## History

Khalid records the legend of the city's name as a man he calls Humayun told it to him. The area, he writes, is known as the area of Mai Kolachi: an old woman of that name, whose son was killed by a fish, tore the fish apart and retrieved him, and the city came to be known as Kolachi; the printed passage is damaged in places (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 225, folio 199). Khalid writes that fisherfolk lived on this shore before Kolachi became Karachi, and that "these Hindu fisherfolk are now the only connection" the city keeps with the village of Kolachi, "the village that Nanak once visited", in a passage that is again damaged in the printed text (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 225, folio 199; Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 226, folio 200). Of the Ram Bagh temple he concludes that, along with the temple at Clifton, it must have been a historical Hindu temple in Nanak's time, and that Nanak must have visited it as well (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 224, folio 198).

Boivin writes of Karachi in the colonial period. He speaks of "the growth of new emporium in Sindh, especially Karachi" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 44, folio 31), and records that the conqueror and first governor of Sindh, Sir Charles Napier, built a Masonic lodge in Karachi in 1843 (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 46, folio 33). A printing press was brought to Karachi in the 1850s (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 48, folio 35), and the Reverend Bambridge held his debates in the city (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 46, folio 33). Khalid describes the port as "a modest structure, a minor tower", with a clock placed within the tower, a symbol of colonial rule (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 226, folio 200).

In 1896, Boivin writes, the Theosophical Society was founded in Karachi; its three founders were Dhunjishaw, Jamshed Nusserwanji and Dayaram Jethmal (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 46, folio 33). The society first met at the new Dayaram Jethmal Science College, and Jamshed Nusserwanji then decided to build it a new building with an auditorium, a library and a lecture room (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 47, folio 34). The city's colleges appear again in his book: Dadi Dhan "obtained a M.A. in Philosophy from Karachi, which was then under the University of Bombay" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 115, folio 102), and Ram Panjwani began his career in education as a teacher at the D. J. Sindh Government Science College in Karachi (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 156, folio 143). Pandit Brahmashankar Mishra, who lived from 1861 to 1907, spent years in Karachi and Hyderabad, though Boivin adds that "no formal relation between Sindh and the Hindu branch of the Naqshbandiyya has been found" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 26, folio 13). Rochaldas worked as a medical doctor in the sanatorium he had created in Karachi (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 70, folio 57) and later created a dispensary, as Boivin puts it, "as well as in Karachi and Hyderabad" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 78, folio 65), and Hiranand Advani hastily left his work in Karachi and went to Calcutta (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 103, folio 90). Among the charitable institutions Boivin names is the leper asylum in Mangho Pir (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 104, folio 91).

Carl W. Ernst and Bruce B. Lawrence write that the Chishti master Muhammad Zauqi Shah "finally moved to Karachi" and "was therefore present at the first meeting of the Muslim League in Karachi in 1907" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 96, folio 82). Rozehnal likewise records that he attended the first meeting of the Muslim League in Karachi in 1907 (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 108, folio 99). Karachi was one of the four cities of Zauqi Shah's newspaper work (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 139, folio 126), and Rozehnal notes the numerous essays he published in *Dawn* in 1945 and 1946 (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 108, folio 99).

The port was also a place of departure. Scott Kugle writes that Hajji Imdadullah went "from Karachi into exile in Mecca" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 280, folio 259). In Pnina Werbner and Helene Basu's collection, the *murid* Liaqat ʿAli Bhatti was about to sail from Karachi port to Iraq when he saw the face of his *murshid* in the water (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 154, folio 143).

Partition remade the city. Khalid writes: "At the time of Partition, Karachi was a minor port city"; migrants from India made it "the largest city of Pakistan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 226, folio 200). Shahidullah Faridi moved from Bombay to Karachi in February 1947 on Zauqi Shah's advice (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 76, folio 66), and a disciple "later transferred the company to Karachi following Partition" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 83, folio 73). Ernst and Lawrence write: "After the birth of Pakistan, Zauqi soon settled in Karachi" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 140, folio 127).

The city of the present is large and troubled in these books. Khalid stood on a Friday in the parking lot of Park Towers, a mall "of the megapolis of Karachi", while the *khutba* sounded from the loudspeakers of the mosques (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 215, folio 189). He writes: "In a city torn by conflict, security guards and law enforcement authorities are quick to react", and speaks of the street crime rate in Karachi (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 216, folio 190). In a sentence the printed text leaves garbled he speaks of a city of "twenty million people" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 222, folio not stated). Schaflechner names Karachi as a city of violence against Hindus (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 18, folio not stated), and he records that its growth has erased the older pilgrim road: "The ongoing growth of Karachi and the corresponding urbanization of the areas where the early stops occurred caused all but a few of the resting places to completely disappear." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 121, folio not stated)

## Holy Places

The books name only a handful of the city's Sufi shrines. Zaheda, in Omar Kasmani's account, petitioned at the shrine of Abdullah Shah in Karachi two or three times, without result (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 119, folio 102). Qureshi names the shrine of Sheikh Yusuf Shah in Karachi among the shrines, "with their own local networks", into which her work branched out (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 33, folio 13), and among the places she visited in accordance with the Qawwali calendar of saints' anniversaries (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 17, folio xv); she gives no dates for the saint. Rozehnal writes of the tomb complex of Shahidullah Faridi, near which the order's teaching shaykh leads a large *zikr* circle in a private home (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 201, folio 193), and of a shaykh's personal library "preserved in a house near the shrine of Shahidullah Faridi in Karachi" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 60, folio 50).

Manghopir, as Abbas writes the name, appears in several of the books. Annemarie Schimmel mentions a miracle with alligators "as happened in Mango Pir near Karachi" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 238, folio 207). Abbas records the April congregation of the Sidis "in Karachi at Manghopir", and the attention it received in the press (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 70–71, folios not stated). Boivin, who writes Mangho Pir, knows the place for its leper asylum (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 104, folio 91).

Abbas writes of another shrine in the city: "In Karachi there is also a memorial shrine, and everywhere that Bava Gor stopped on his journey a shrine was created and each time one of the Sidis from his army remained in the place" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 73, folio not stated). She does not name the Karachi shrine. Her filmography lists Jamil Dehlavi's film *Immaculate Conception*, "about a hijṛā shrine in Karachi, Pakistan", and that shrine too goes unnamed (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 205, folio not stated). Kasmani writes that after the three day annual fair at Sehwan the fakirs "follow other fakirs and proceed to shrines in Karachi" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 114, folio 97).

Khalid went looking for the city's Hindu temples. A large part of Clifton, he writes, "is constructed on reclaimed land" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 217, folio 191). His visit to the temple at Clifton was aborted, and, dejected, his party went on to other temples (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 221, folio 195). One stood in the area of Ram Bagh, near a road whose printed name, Justice Kayani Road, is uncertain; the area, he writes, was no longer called Ram Bagh but Aram Bagh, and the addition of an 'A' had neutralised its religious connotation (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 222, folio not stated). Under the roof of that temple Krishna was painted "with his maidens"; the main door was locked, with a peacock guarding the entrance; an old man told him the temple was one hundred and fifty years old, and Khalid remarks that "Hundred and fifty seemed to be the standard age of all temples here" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 223, folio 197). Behind it were living quarters, most of them Hindu households, though he saw a Muslim family there as well, and cows "probably used in religious ceremonies"; the temple was "much more elaborately decorated", and the community "much larger", than in Lahore (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 223, folio 197). An officer, shown the author's press card, told him: "I don't know exactly how old this temple is but it is old", and that it existed before Partition (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 223–224, folios 197–198).

A third temple stands within the Port Grand complex, where "a newly constructed mosque for tourists" has been built right on top of it (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 224, folio 198). Khalid describes separate entrances for women and men, people preparing food that was probably an offering from a rich Hindu, stairs leading down into water "blackened by all the industrial waste", the boats of fishermen, pigeons fed with rice, and rooms reserved for different deities; a man sternly told Sadia to stop photographing, and a mural showed a male deity seated on a giant fish, Jhule Lal (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 225, folio 199).

Schaflechner names Karachi as the seat of the Nagnath *akhāṛā* and the Jagnath *akhāṛā* (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 127, 138, 159, 207, 292–294, folios not stated). The Parsis of the city say the morning prayer of Navroz at the Fire Temple (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 142–143, folio not stated), and an informant Khalid calls Farah tells him: "There is a Tower of Silence in Karachi but none in Lahore" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 144, folio not stated).

## Communities

Karachi's Hindus appear in the books as a living community, and as a source of idols for Hindus elsewhere. In Khalid's *A White Trail* boys from Bahawalnagar bought an idol of Durga from Karachi (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 31, folio not stated), and a vendor's idols "have been brought from Karachi, where there are still a reasonable number of Hindus living", or from India through illegal channels (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 33, folio not stated). Khalid writes of the Hindu fisherfolk of the shore, and records that women on a boat spoke "a combination of Sindhi and Makrani" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 226, folio 200), and at Ram Bagh most of the households behind the temple were Hindu (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 223, folio 197). Schaflechner makes Karachi the home of the Devipujak-Vagri community and the engine of change on the pilgrimage route to Hinglaj (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 306–312, 322, folios not stated); the donors of Melari Mata came from Karachi (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 271, folio not stated), and so did one elderly volunteer of the HSM, as the book abbreviates the organisation (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 238, folio not stated).

The Parsis are the other community the books follow. Khalid writes that "The majority of the Zoroastrian community of Pakistan is based in Karachi", and says of his account of the differences between Lahore and Karachi that they "aren't my personal observations" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 234, folio not stated). He gives them in the words of his informants. One he calls Daria Boatwala told him: "Karachi is a much more happening city with a huge population of Zoroastrians and other non-Muslims. One interacts with Hindus, Christians, etc. in several social forums"; "There in Karachi I would wear dresses everywhere"; and "there are thousands of Parsis in Karachi which makes occasions like Navroz a lot of fun there", though she also called the city "really a desert" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 142–143, folio not stated). Another, Farah, said that for a traditional wedding the Parsis of Lahore "either invite the priest up here or just travel down South to Karachi" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 144, folio not stated).

Abbas ties the shrines of the Sidis to the journey of Bava Gor: everywhere he stopped a shrine was created, and each time one of the Sidis from his army remained in the place; in Karachi, she writes, there is also a memorial shrine (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 73, folio not stated).

The Chishti order of Zauqi Shah and Shahidullah Faridi drew disciples to the city from abroad. A shaykh of the order met regularly with a group of foreign students at a large Islamic Center in Karachi, giving lectures in English and Urdu, some of them recorded on tape (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 78, folio 68). One disciple recalled the advice he was given: "You ought to go to Karachi, to the Islamic Center. You should learn Qur'an and Arabic at the madrasa", and added that "Most of the foreign disciples from the time of Hazrat Shahidullah Faridi" used to go there (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 163, folio 155). A Malaysian disciple arrived in Pakistan in 1978 and immediately enrolled at a local madrasa in Karachi (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 157, folio 149). One of Rozehnal's informants put the size of the order in the city in these words: "Just here in Karachi there may be three or four hundred branches" of the Chishti *silsila* (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 154, folio 146).

Kasmani's book shows people moving to and from the city. Zaheda came to know the shrines of Sindh after her family moved to Karachi in the early 2000s, when her husband found work in the army (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 119, folio 102). Shah-Bibi "was forced to flee from Karachi in order to escape a criminal legal case" after a fire destroyed her blanket factory, and lived in hiding for a few years in a village in Sindh (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 157, folio 140); Kasmani speaks of "her former life in Karachi" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 153, folio 136). Among those at the *qadimi ʿalam* Kasmani notes "a middle-aged woman from Karachi" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 124, folio not stated). Abbas writes that the sons of landlords live in large urban centres like Karachi, Lahore and Islamabad (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 81, folio not stated).

## Custodians, Institutions and the State

Qureshi names a custodian and a sheikh of the city. Her list of oral sources gives "Baba Zahin Shah Taji Sajjadanashin (Yusuf Shah)" and "Syed Abdul Hamid Badayuni" (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 278, folio 258), and her preface speaks of "the late Baba Zahin Shah, leading Sheikh of Karachi" (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 17, folio xv).

Rozehnal describes the institutions of the Chishti order in the city. "First and foremost among them is Mahfil-i Zauqiyya. Located in Karachi, it publishes the bulk of the original Urdu works of Muhammad Zauqi Shah and Shahidullah Faridi"; founded by Shahidullah himself, it is administered by a senior male disciple who was expressly ordered by the shaykh to lead the order's publishing (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 130, folio 121). The order keeps separate living compounds for men and women adjacent to the saint's tomb in Allahabad, Karachi and Pakpattan (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 216, folio 208), and a shaykh's library is kept in a house near Shahidullah Faridi's shrine, not inside it (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 60, folio 50).

The books say little of the state and religious property in Karachi. Khalid notes that Ram Bagh had become Aram Bagh, the added 'A' neutralising its religious connotation (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 222, folio not stated); that a newly constructed mosque for tourists had been built on top of the Port Grand temple (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 224, folio 198); and that it was an officer, shown his press card, who answered his questions at the Ram Bagh temple (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 223–224, folios 197–198). Kasmani's fieldwork "involved a brief stint at the Sindh Archives in Karachi" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 49, folio 32), and much of the literature on Lal Shahbaz Qalandar cited in the book was published in Karachi, several works of it by the Department of Culture, Government of Sindh (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, pp. 202–215, folios not stated–198).

## Festivals and Practice

The Chishti order's week in Karachi has a fixed shape. "In Karachi, the order's current teaching shaykh, Siraj 'Ali Muhammad, leads weekly halqa (communal zikr sessions) on Thursday evenings", and he holds an informal public question and answer session, a *dars*, at his home on Sunday mornings (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 165, folio 157). Writing of the order's tomb shrines, Rozehnal also notes that "Disciples generally avoid zikr in Karachi and Pakpattan altogether" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 218, folio 210).

Qureshi visited Karachi's shrines in accordance with the Qawwali calendar of saints' anniversaries (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 17, folio xv). She records that "On 20 February 1983, Sufi Abdul Karim Sumar died during the 'urs assembly of Baba Ahmad Shah in Karachi, during the recital of a verse by Jami", as the newspapers *Dawn* and *Nawae Waqt* reported (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 255, folio 235). She gives no name for Baba Ahmad Shah's shrine and no dates for the saint.

Abbas records the April congregation of the Sidis in Karachi at Manghopir (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 70–71, folios not stated). For the Parsis the great occasion is Navroz: its morning prayer is said at the Fire Temple, and its evening is spent at the Beach Luxury Hotel, with lawn games such as the spoon and lemon race and the three legged race, stalls of "Pakistani as well traditional Parsi food", and dancing (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 142–143, folio not stated).

Karachi is also a city pilgrims set out from. Kasmani places Sehwan "hardly 300 kilometers north of Karachi", "a four-hour drive to the town" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 23, folio 6), and describes a "road journey from Karachi to Sehwan" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 125, folio 108). Khalid gives Hinglaj as "about two hundred and fifty kilometres from the coastal city of Karachi" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 32, folio not stated), and an informant he calls Raza says that Navratri pilgrims now come "from as far as Karachi" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 33, folio not stated). For Schaflechner the city is the start of the road route to Hinglaj, "about 240 kilometers long" from Narayanpur (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 158, folio not stated). It is the staging city for Indian pilgrims and the destination of their flight from Bombay (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 175, 180, 181, 183, folios not stated), the port of departure of the 4x4 Off-Road Club and the city where its members were interviewed (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 100, 101, 113, 115, folios not stated), a source of pilgrims at *ʿīd* and *muḥarram* and a place of public slaughter (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 291, 294, 304, folios not stated), and the city to which a drowned child was taken (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 253, folio not stated).

## Qawwali and Song

Qureshi writes that the verses and settings of Amir Khusrau "are favourite songs in Qawwali assemblies all over the subcontinent, but especially in two important urban centres of Sufism", Karachi and Hyderabad in India, "where members of the same Qawwal Bachche lineage actively maintain their special Qawwali heritage" (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 33, folio 13). Her preface names "Bahauddin Khan and Raziuddin Khan of Karachi" (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 17, folio xv).

Karachi is the one Pakistani city in her list of the Qawwals she consulted: Bahauddin Qawwal, Raziuddin Khan and Manzur Nyazi, the three marked as Qawwal Bachche, with Pyare Khan, Faruq Ahmad Nizami, Iftekhar Ahmad Nizami, Ghulam Farid Sabri and Madan Qawwal (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 277, folio 257). Among the Qawwal parties she recorded in assemblies of *samaʿ* in Karachi are Manzur Niyazi, Bahauddin Qutubuddin and Raziuddin Khan, again marked as Qawwal Bachche, with Faruq Ahmad Nizami, Iftekhar Ahmad Nizami, Anwar, Bakhshi Salamat, Bashir Ahmad, Ghulam Farid Maqbool Ahmad Sabri, Kalian, Madan, Muhammad Ali Afridi and Tuti (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 281, folio 261). Her other recordings include Ahmad Azad, Ismail Azad, Haji Fayyaz and Muhammad Husain Warsi, all of Karachi (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 282, folio 262). She gives no dates for these recordings.

She also consulted devotees and experts in the city: Iftekhar Ahmad Khan Adni, Mohammad Vazir Ahmed Kureshy, Ataul Haq Khan, Anwar Enayatullah, Prof. Abul Khair Kashfi, Jaliluddin Faridi, Mrs Zahidi, Mrs Mariam Khan and Mrs Husan Bano Jamil (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 279, folio 259). Anwar Enayatullah wrote the piece in the *Karachi Morning News* that she cites (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 270, folio 250).

The city recorded the music as well as hearing it. Abbas names two of its recording companies, Rita Recording Co. and Jugun Recording Co., Karachi (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 214–218, folios not stated).

## The Place in the Books

Each author sees a different Karachi. For Qureshi it is one of "two important urban centres of Sufism" in the subcontinent (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 33, folio 13). For Rozehnal it is one end of a Sufi order's reach: "From Karachi to Kuala Lumpur, these twenty-first-century Sufi adepts perpetuate a tradition that remains grounded in Indo-Muslim history" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 16, folio 5); one of his informants likens the order's network to the Internet, where "if you come to Lahore it's the same data available on a computer in Karachi or Multan" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 155, folio 147).

For Khalid, walking in the steps of Guru Nanak, it is a megapolis, a city torn by conflict (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 215, folio 189; Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 216, folio 190), whose temples he thought Nanak must have seen (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 224, folio 198). In his *A White Trail* it is a place where Hindus still live in reasonable numbers and from which idols are brought (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 33, folio not stated), and the city his Parsi informants compare with Lahore, a comparison he takes care to call theirs and not his own observation (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 234, folio not stated).

Kasmani writes from the city: the self description in the book reads "Karachi-raised, academic by way of London, Berliner-by-love" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 17, folio xvi). The argument over a doctored photograph of the Sehwan shrine took place in the family home in Karachi (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 20, folio 3), and the book speaks of a weekend break in the city (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 150, folio 133). For Schaflechner Karachi runs through the whole account of Hinglaj: it is the base of a magazine (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 21, folio not stated), the seat of a housing authority (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 16, folio not stated), a march route (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 23, folio not stated) and the seat of the HSM (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 173, folio not stated).

Boivin's Karachi is the city of colonial and modern institutions, Masonic and Theosophical, medical and educational (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 46, folio 33; Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 70, folio 57; Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 156, folio 143). A note of his records that "the activities of the society stopped in Karachi after the 2007 assassination of the society's president, the late Dara Feroze Mirza", who lived from 1937 to 2007; the note does not name the society (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 209, folio 196).

Schimmel's Karachi is a city of scholarship and publishing. She cites the proceedings of the Seerat Conference held in Karachi in 1975, *Message of the Prophet*, where Muhammad is represented as "the model of tranquillity in a turbulent age" and as the "seal of prophecy", but where topics such as "The convergence of business management thought to the Holy Prophet's teaching" were also discussed (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 292, folio 274); she cites the same volume again for Gerhard Bowering's essay (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 296, folio 278). Her bibliography lists many books published in Karachi on the Sufis and poets of Sindh and beyond: Maulwi Abdul Haqq's *Urdū kī nashw a namā meṉ ṣūfiyā-i kirām kā kām*, from the Anjuman-i taraqqi-yi Urdu in 1953 (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 311, folio not stated); Muhammad Aslah's *Tadhkirat-i shucara-i Kashmir* edited by Sayyid Husamuddin Rashdi, from the Iqbal Academy in 1967 and 1968 (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 313, folio 295); the Sindhi Adabi Board's editions of Bedil Rohriwaro (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 315, folio 297), of Rashid Burhanpuri's book on Sindhi saints, *Burhānpur ke Sindhī auliyā* (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 317, folio 299), of two works of Sachal Sarmast (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 335, folio 317) and of H. J. Sadarangani's *Persian Poets of Sind* (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 336, folio 318); and a second edition of *Sirr-i dilbarān* by Hazrat Shah Sayyid Muhammad Dhauqi, published in Karachi by the Mahfil-i dhauqiyya in 1388h/1968 (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 319, folio 301).

The other books, too, know Karachi as a city of presses and universities. Ernst and Lawrence cite a book from Oxford University Press in Karachi and a Karachi University doctoral dissertation (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 227, folio 216; Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 238, folio 228), and Werbner and Basu's reference lists name the city as a place of publication (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, pp. 216 and 218, folios 205 and 207). Abbas credits a photograph of the Sehwan shrine to the Hakim Center, Karachi (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 33, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The temple at Clifton and Guru Nanak.** Khalid reports that "Later Sikh writers have asserted" that the temple of the sea god which Nanak visited was the temple at Clifton, and objects that it stood "on the mainland" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 229, folio not stated). Elsewhere he writes that, along with the temple at Clifton, the Ram Bagh temple must have been a historical Hindu temple in Nanak's time, and that Nanak must have visited it as well (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 224, folio 198).
- **The age of the Ram Bagh temple.** An old man told Khalid that it was one hundred and fifty years old (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 223, folio 197). An officer said he did not know exactly how old it was, only that it was old and existed before Partition (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 223–224, folios 197–198).
- **When Zauqi Shah came to Karachi.** Ernst and Lawrence write that he "finally moved to Karachi" and was therefore present at the first meeting of the Muslim League there in 1907 (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 96, folio 82), and also that after the birth of Pakistan he soon settled in Karachi (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 140, folio 127). Rozehnal writes that he attended the 1907 meeting (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 108, folio 99).
- **The distance to Hinglaj.** Khalid gives Hinglaj as about two hundred and fifty kilometres from the coastal city of Karachi (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 32, folio not stated). Schaflechner gives the road route from Narayanpur as about 240 kilometres long (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 158, folio not stated).
