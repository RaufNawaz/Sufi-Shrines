## Overview

In Haroon Khalid's account Eminabad is the town of Guru Nanak's day called Saidpur: "When Babur took over the city of Saidpur, he renamed it Eminabad." (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115, folio 97). Khalid reconstructs that Saidpur as an ancient city of great temples and traders, among the most important trading stops between the Chenab and the Ravi (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 103, folio 85; Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 104, folio 86). On his own visit the spires of old Hindu temples still rose among the houses, the temples abandoned in 1947 and taken over by migrants from across the border, and the town had lost its trade to a newer city nearby (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115, folio 97). The Persian chronicle of Sujan Rai Bhandari names a pargana and a halting stage spelt as Eminabad is spelt, though only the spelling links that place to this town (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 176; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 177).

## History

Khalid identifies the Saidpur of Guru Nanak's time with Eminabad, writing that "When Babur took over the city of Saidpur, he renamed it Eminabad." (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115, folio 97). Elsewhere he writes that Babur destroyed "the cities of Eminabad and Lahore" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 15, folio not stated).

Khalid also tells a story of how the town came by its new name. A woman, whose name the printed text gives as Eni though the reading is uncertain, offered Babur roasted grain when he had not eaten for days, shortly before he reached Saidpur, and the city, Khalid relates, was renamed in her honour (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 116, folio 98).

In Khalid's account the town's fortunes followed the road. "Once, it was an important trading centre on the Grand Trunk Road that connected with Lahore and then Delhi", he writes; the highway has since moved south, and a new city not far away, Gujranwala, whose name is damaged in the printed text, has replaced Eminabad as an important trading centre (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115, folio 97). On his visit the dusty roads and worn houses showed him that the city had lost its economic strength, though the page is badly damaged at that point (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115, folio 97).

Sujan Rai Bhandari's chronicle records the setting up of a pargana whose name it spells as Eminabad is spelt, though nothing but the spelling ties that pargana to this town. It says that a few years earlier the pargana was established out of Gujrat, and, in the next sentence, that at this time, in the reign of the emperor it styles the lord of the world, one Muhammad Amin, a *karori*, established by royal order the town and pargana given that name; two words of the second sentence, *karori* among them, are uncertain in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 176).

## Temples and Holy Places

Khalid's picture of Saidpur in Nanak's day is his own reconstruction. He imagines "magnificent houses and havelis" and huge temples with gold tridents, with idols in every shop, Laxmi the most popular and then Ganesh (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 103, folio 85). "Saidpur was an ancient city", he writes, its temples said to have been built "thousands of years ago, long before the birth of Islam", and grown into complexes with rooms for pilgrims, shops let on lease, stables and "an army of servants" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 103, folio 85). In his reconstruction "the Brahmin pandits were the bosses": the offerings were kept, given out or sold, and guards kept out "naked and starving beggars" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 103–104, folios 85–86).

Of the present town Khalid writes as a visitor. "The cone like spires of ancient Hindu temples" rise among the houses, beautiful, he finds, "in contrast to the monolithic Muslim architecture that now dominates the city of Eminabad" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115, folio 97). Of one temple he writes: "Despite being abandoned in 1947, the temple still looked sturdy"; its paint had worn off and a corrosive layer had formed (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115, folio 97). He wondered whether the temples had been attacked "in 1992, after the destruction of the Babri mosque in India", and looked for signs of an attack without finding any (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115, folio 97). He marvelled at how much of the historical architecture had been preserved (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 116, folio 98).

## Communities

In Khalid's reconstruction Saidpur was a city with a Hindu majority and a few Muslim households, its Hindus mostly traders and its Muslims aristocrats (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 104, folio 86). He calls it "one of the most important trading centres between the Chenab and the Ravi", the most important stop for traders after Delhi and Lahore, a city with "some of the tallest temples in the Punjab and also the most beautiful courtesans" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 104, folio 86).

Malik Bhago, "a minister of the Rai of Saidpur", lived in Khalid's reconstruction in a frescoed "four-storey haveli known as the Shahi Qilla at Saidpur", and held a wedding feast for "all Brahmins and members of the high castes" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 109, folio not stated). It was "at the court of Malik Bhago in Eminabad", Khalid writes, that Nanak was questioned (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 144, folio 126).

The town Khalid visited had a different people. Its old temples "had been taken over by migrants from the other side of the border following the creation of Pakistan", and their descendants stared back at him (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115, folio 97).

Khalid also lists Eminabad, though the reading of the name is uncertain in the printed text, among cities whose Sikh communities "Only recently" can "exert themselves in a way that was unthinkable earlier", with enrolment at university and many having "their own cars" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 88, folio 70).

## The Place in the Books

For Khalid, Eminabad is a town seen in two times: the Saidpur he reconstructs for Nanak's day, ancient, rich in temples and in trade (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 103, folio 85; Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 104, folio 86), and the town he walked through, its temples abandoned since 1947 and its trade gone to Gujranwala (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115, folio 97). After their visit to Eminabad, he and Iqbal Qaiser went on to Sialkot (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 191, folio 165).

Bhandari's chronicle, if its place of that spelling is Eminabad, knows it as a pargana, a halting stage on a royal journey and a point from which to measure the road. On a journey the chronicle sets in the forty second year, a man in Kashmir who posed as Umar Shaikh Mirza, son of Sulaiman Mirza, was brought before the emperor at the halting stage of that name and was punished there on the spot (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 177). The bridge of Shah Daula over the Deg, on the highway, lies five *karoh* from it towards Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 515), and the Deg passes the villages of the parganas of Zafarwal, Pasrur, this pargana and a fourth whose name is uncertain in the printed text before it reaches the bridge (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 516).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- How the town came by its name: Khalid writes that Babur, taking Saidpur, renamed it Eminabad (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115, folio 97), and relates that it was renamed in honour of a woman, Eni in the uncertain printed reading, who fed Babur (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 116, folio 98); Bhandari's chronicle says that a town and pargana of that name were established by royal order, in the reign of an emperor it does not name, by Muhammad Amin, a *karori*, though only the spelling links the chronicle's place to Eminabad (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 176).
