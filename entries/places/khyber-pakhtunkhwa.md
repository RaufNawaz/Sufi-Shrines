## Overview

For Haroon Khalid, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa is "the province of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KPK)" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated). In the essays edited by Pnina Werbner and Helene Basu it is the "North-West Frontier Province" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 161, folio 150) and the NWFP (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 242, folio 231), and Robert Rozehnal names the Northwest Frontier Province in his account of the national elections of October 2002 (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 32, folio 22). A disciple Rozehnal quotes, a woman originally from Peshawar, speaks simply of "the frontier" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 219, folio 211).

Khalid writes that since partition the province "has been home to a large proportion of religious minorities who have lived there rather peacefully", chiefly Hindus, who form the largest minority (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated). He explains their staying in two ways: on one page by the influence of the Indian National Congress, which made the riots of partition less intense there than in Punjab (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated), and on another by the influence of Abdul Ghaffar Khan, the Frontier Gandhi (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 162, folio not stated). The Sikhs he met at Baisakhi at Hassan Abdal were, he writes, mostly Pathans from the province and the tribal areas (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 158, folio not stated), and in *Walking with Nanak* he finds followers of Guru Nanak who refused the Khalsa still living there (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 230, folio 204).

Charles Lindholm, a contributor to the Werbner and Basu volume, writes that from his perspective "the NWFP has more in common with the Middle East than with South Asia" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 242, folio 231), and Jürgen Wasim Frembgen sets the ritual meetings of its women beside those of Afghanistan and Iran (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 161, folio 150). Rozehnal records that in the elections of October 2002 a coalition of religious parties, the Muttahida Majlis-i Amal, won a controlling majority of the province's Provincial Assembly (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 32, folio 22). And the disciple from Peshawar remembers the frontier as a place of "so many shrines where you can walk into the saint's shrines" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 219, folio 211).

## The Province in History

Khalid uses the modern name of the province even of the Sikh period: during his reign, he writes, Maharaja Ranjit Singh captured the Pathan regions of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and the tribal areas, and as a result several people converted to Sikhism (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 161–162, folio not stated).

Of partition, Khalid writes on one page that "unlike Punjab the riots were less intense due to the influence of the Indian National Congress there", and that "A lot of Hindus and Sikhs continued living in their ancestral lands even after the creation of Pakistan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated). On another he writes that at the time of partition "this area was spared bloodshed primarily due to the influence of the Sarhadi (Frontier) Gandhi", whom he names as Abdul Ghaffar Khan, and that "several Hindus and Sikhs stayed rooted in Khyber-Pakhtunkhwa because of the comparatively peaceful political situation here" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 162, folio not stated).

In the early days of Sikh migration to Nankana Sahib, which Khalid places after the wars of 1965 and 1971, many Sikh families from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and the Tribal Areas found refuge in Gurdwara Patti Sahib (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72). In his own day, too, the Sikhs of Nankana are "recent migrants from Khyber-Pakhtunkhwa (KPK) where there is a considerable Sikh population", and their migration from the province to Punjab, he argues, shows "the different socio-economic conditions in both of these provinces" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 235, folio not stated).

Lindholm, in a note to his chapter that sets them beside other exceptions of the colonial era, writes that "The numerous religiously led uprisings in the NWFP (North West Frontier Province) might also be included", though he gives them no date (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 242, folio 231). Rozehnal writes that in the national elections of October 2002 the Muttahida Majlis-i Amal (MMA), "a coalition of religious political parties", "made unprecedented gains, capturing 19 percent of the seats in the National Assembly, a controlling majority of the Provincial Assembly of the Northwest Frontier Province, and an upper hand as the largest party in Baluchistan" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 32, folio 22). He describes the coalition as "Pro-Taliban, anti-American, predominantly ethnic Pashtun" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 32, folio 22).

## Shrines, Festivals and Pilgrims

A disciple Rozehnal quotes, originally from Peshawar, contrasts her home with Pakpattan: "I am quite used to the culture of segregation when it comes to the saints and the shrines. In the frontier, we have so many shrines where you can walk into the saint's shrines. Women can go inside, we can touch the graves. At many places there is a separate side for the women. It is not like this at Pakpattan Sharif" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 219, folio 211). Her word is "the frontier", the region she comes from, rather than the province's name (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 219, folio 211).

Frembgen, writing of the informal and positive tone of conversation at Mama Ji's darbar in Rawalpindi, finds it "notably different" from that "at ritual meetings of women in the neighbouring North-West Frontier Province, Afghanistan, and Iran", with their emphasis on *gham pasandi*, 'loving grief', or *dard-e del*, 'telling one's heart's sorrow', "in a lamenting way" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 161, folio 150). The statement covers the province, Afghanistan and Iran together, and rests on Fischer, whom Frembgen cites (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 161, folio 150).

Of the province's Hindus, Khalid writes that "Hindu festivals are celebrated with much pomp in these areas" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated).

At Baisakhi at Hassan Abdal in 2011, Khalid writes, "Most of the Sikhs here are fair skinned and blue eyed Pathans, from the regions of Khyber-Pakthunkhwa and tribal areas" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 158, folio not stated). At Shivratri at Katas in the same year, by the account of an informant Khalid calls Gupta, the four marriages held there were of girls from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and FATA "who suffered during the floods of 2010" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 51, folio not stated).

## Communities

Since partition, Khalid writes, the province "has been home to a large proportion of religious minorities who have lived there rather peacefully. These are primarily Hindus, who form the largest minority" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated). "Thousands of them are part of the main stream society, visible often in the economic and political fabric" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated). In a passage that cites the census of 1998, he names Khyber Pakhtunkhwa among the provinces where most of the Hindus are scattered (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 87, folio not stated).

Of its Sikhs, Khalid writes that after Ranjit Singh captured the Pathan regions several people converted to Sikhism (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 161–162, folio not stated). He describes the Sikhs he met at Hassan Abdal as Pathans from the province and the tribal areas (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 158, folio not stated), and calls Khyber Pakhtunkhwa a place "where there is a considerable Sikh population" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 235, folio not stated).

In *Walking with Nanak* Khalid writes that Nanak Panthi Hindus, "thousands of such Sikhs", live in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Punjab and Sindh (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 98, folio 80), and that the independent "followers of Guru Nanak" who refused the Khalsa "still exist in Sindh and in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 230, folio 204).

A man Khalid calls Kartar wears "a grey baggy shalwar kameez, traditional wear of the residents of the province of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 183, folio not stated). Near the border, of an undercover army man, Khalid notes hazel eyes, which he calls a feature associated with the inhabitants of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa; the printed text garbles the province's name at that point (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 52, folio 34).

## The Province in the Books

For Khalid, travelling among Pakistan's religious minorities, it is a province where Hindus and Sikhs stayed on after partition, thousands of them part of the mainstream of society, and where Hindu festivals are celebrated "with much pomp" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated; Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 162, folio not stated). He reads the movement of its Sikhs into Punjab as a sign of the "different socio-economic conditions" of the two provinces (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 235, folio not stated).

Lindholm writes that the religiously led uprisings of the NWFP might also be included beside the exceptions his note lists, but that from his perspective the province "has more in common with the Middle East than with South Asia" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 242, folio 231). Frembgen, in the same collection, likewise groups the ritual meetings of the province's women with those of Afghanistan and Iran, and contrasts them with the tone at a darbar in Rawalpindi (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 161, folio 150).

In Rozehnal's account of national politics the province is where a coalition of religious parties he calls "Pro-Taliban, anti-American, predominantly ethnic Pashtun" won control of the Provincial Assembly in 2002 (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 32, folio 22). In the words of a disciple he interviewed in Lahore in October 2000, it is a frontier of open shrines, where women walk in and touch the graves (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 219, folio 211).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **Why partition spared the province.** Khalid writes on one page that the riots were less intense than in Punjab "due to the influence of the Indian National Congress there" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated), and on another that the area "was spared bloodshed primarily due to the influence of the Sarhadi (Frontier) Gandhi", Abdul Ghaffar Khan (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 162, folio not stated).
