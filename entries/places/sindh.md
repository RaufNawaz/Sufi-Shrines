## Overview

The books mean different units by Sindh: for Annemarie Schimmel it is the lower Indus valley (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 61, folio 30), and in *Pain and Grace* the southern part of present day Pakistan (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 6, folio 2). Sujan Rai Bhandari, in a work he dates to the year 1107 of the Hijra (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 581), calls it the *wilayat* of Sindh known as Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 581), and in his own day it is a Mughal *suba*, the *suba* of Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530), made up of four *sarkars*, Thatta, Siwistan, Nasirpur and Umarkot (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 528–529). H. T. Sorley has Akbar make the country part of the *suba* of Multan, with *sarkars* at Bhakkar in the north and at Thatta in the Indus delta, and holds that Upper and Lower Sindh were never definitely united until a much later day, their unification not complete even in 1843 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 31–32, folios 22–23). Michel Boivin notes that the Siraiki speaking area of Southern Punjab was a part of Sindh during the Middle Ages (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 216, folio 203), and in Baluch poetry, Sorley writes, the word means not the modern political division but the valley of the Indus (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 256, folio 247). Under the British, Sindh was administered from Bombay rather than Calcutta (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 25, folio not stated), and Jürgen Schaflechner, following Oscar Verkaaik, traces Sindhi nationalism to the struggle for independence from the Bombay Presidency in the 1920s and 1930s (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 88, folio not stated). Today, Boivin writes, Sindh is the province of Pakistan with the most significant Hindu population (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 176–177, folios not stated and 164).

Bhandari begins its history with its ancient capitals, Brahmanabad, then Adpur, then Dewal, with Thatta and Debal the seat of government in his own day (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530). In Schimmel's account the Arabs reached Sindh in 711 and laid the foundation of a Muslim rule (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 61, folio 30), and Sorley writes that it was under Arab governors for nearly three hundred years, though the Arabs left no permanent impress upon the land (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 247, folio 238). Local dynasties followed, the Sumra and then the Samma (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 173), and from 1520 the Turkish Arghun and then the Tarkhan, under whom Sindh fell to the Mughal Empire (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 207, folio 207). Sorley dates its annexation by Akbar to 1592 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 18, folio 9). In the eighteenth century the Kalhora rose from governors to semi-independence, paid tribute to Persia after Nadir Shah's invasion and passed under the suzerainty of the Pathan kingdom at Kabul (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 35, folio 26), and the Talpurs who overthrew them ruled the country until the British conquered it in 1843 (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, pp. 30–31, folio not stated). Beneath these changes Sorley finds a deep continuity, the Sindh of Napier not very different in essentials from the Sindh of Akbar, Jahangir, Shah Jahan and Aurangzeb (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 31, folio 22). When Persian was given up as the official language in the 1850s, Sindhi and English became the two languages of administration (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 44, folio 31). At Partition the departure of the Hindu elite and the arrival of Muslim refugees from India altered the province's demography (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 87, folio not stated).

For Schimmel, Sindh never ceased after the Arab conquest to be a centre of Islamic studies and a seat of Islamic mysticism (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 6, folio 2). Hallaj travelled throughout it in 905 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 376, folio 345), and from the thirteenth century, when Lal Shahbaz Qalandar settled in Sehwan and Baha'uddin Zakariya made Multan a Suhrawardi centre, Sindh produced an immense number of mystics whose shrines are scattered all through the Indus Valley (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 25, folio not stated). Dr Burnes, as Sorley quotes him, found the country priest ridden and said to possess a hundred thousand tombs of saints (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 167, folio 158), and in Sorley's own words its social religion was built round the reverence of the disciple for the master (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 166, folio 157). Boivin names the Qadiriyya, the Qalandariyya and the Sohrawardiyya as the orders dominant among the Hindu Sufis of Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 227–228, folios 214–215), while Sorley divides its fakirs into the two great orders of Jelali and Jemali darwishes (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 299, folio 290). The river had its own saint, Khwajo Khizr, whose cult was linked with the worship of the Indus and supported by Muslim and Hindu alike (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 177, folio 168). Wandering yogis traversed Sindh on pilgrimage to the goddess at Hinglaj in Balochistan (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 20, folio not stated), and Schaflechner finds in the south of Pakistan, especially Sindh, a network of pilgrimage routes visited by different religious communities (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 107, folio not stated). Boivin sorts the ties of the province's Hindus to Sufism into three kinds: a tomb visited by both Muslims and Hindus, such as Bhit Shah; a saint with a double identity, such as Udero Lal; and a Sufi *darbar* managed by Hindus, such as that of Pithoro Pir (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 177–178, folios 164–165).

Sorley's eighteenth century Sindh is a Muslim state whose population was composed for the most part of converted Jats, the majority Sunni but very influential sections Shia (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 172, folio 163). The Baluchis had lived in the country for many generations before the Kalhora called them in during the eighteenth century, the moment from which their political importance dates (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 255, folio 246), and the Hindus advanced money to the zamindars, financed trade, farmed the revenue and owned all the petty shops in the villages (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 171, folio 162). In Boivin's account the Lohanas made up half of the whole Hindu population and were divided into Amils and Bhaibands (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 40–41, folios 27–28), and the Nanakpanth was probably the dominant religious path among the Hindu Sindhis of the nineteenth century (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 90, folio 77). Before the census, he argues, people had identified themselves according to their locality, caste and ethnicity rather than by affiliation to the great religions (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 23, folio 10). The Sidis, first brought as slaves from Africa, now speak Sindhi as their native language (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 67–68, folios not stated). Sindh is home to the majority of Pakistan's Hindus, over 90 per cent of whom, in Schaflechner's figures, belong to underprivileged tribes or subcastes such as the Menghwar, the Bhil and the Kohli (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 13, folio not stated), and Haroon Khalid finds the Nanakpanthi Sikhs in very large numbers in the province (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 197, folio not stated).

In Sindh, Schimmel writes, the Sufis made the spoken language into a literary one (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 161, folio 161), though Persian was the court language of the ruling houses until the British conquest (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 24, folio 15). Siraiki, the dialect of Northern Sindh spoken especially among its Baluchi population, gave many of its Sufi poets a second language (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 171, folio 158). The range of folk tales used for mystical purposes is wider in Sindh than elsewhere, from Qadi Qadan's verse in the first half of the sixteenth century to the *Risalo* of Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit in the eighteenth (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 171, folio 153). For Sorley the *Risalo* is the only classic the language of Sindh has yet produced (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 7, folio not stated), and Christopher Shackle calls it universally acknowledged as the greatest classic of Sindhi literature (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 8, folio not stated); it is understood and loved in Sindh by Hindus as much as by Muslims (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 225, folio 216). Its printing in 1866 was the main event in the relation of print to Sufism in the province (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 47–48, folios 34–35), and Hindu Sindhis played a leading part in publishing Sufi books in the late nineteenth century (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 173, folio 160). Most of the musicians of Sindh, Sorley writes, have been Hindus (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 231, folio 222); Shemeem Burney Abbas finds that there can be no ritual there without the Manganhar, the community musicians (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 77, folio not stated), and that *sufiana kalam*, sung in the vernacular, is a feature of the shrines of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar and of Shah Abdul Latif at Bhit (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 85, folio not stated). The majority of the Naqshbandis in Sindh, Boivin writes, are strongly opposed to music (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 228, folio 215).

Holy places and power were long bound together. Since the time of the Sumiros, Sorley writes, the government had a strong theocratic basis with much virtual power in the hands of the Sayyids (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 23, folio 14), and under the Kalhora the reverence for pirs, Sayyids and holy men became the predominant characteristic of eighteenth century Sindh (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 163, folio 154). Burton, as Sorley quotes him, saw Sufism under the native governments as a formidable political engine (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 299, folio 290). British policy in the Punjab and Sindh treated the hereditary saints much as it treated landlords and tribal leaders (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 33, folio 23), and the *hurr*, the obedient disciples of the Pir Pagaro, joined the struggle against the British and played a minor role even in the war of 1971 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 428, folio 397). Omar Kasmani finds that the state's Sindhi publications hardly mentioned Sehwan until the late 1960s while regularly featuring other saints of Sindh (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 70, folio 53), and Schaflechner saw many temples renovated within the past decade, in step with changes in the political landscape (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 16, folio not stated). Sindhi nationalists, as he reports them, joined the province to Sufism, G. M. Syed finding Sufism in its purest form there (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 88–89, folio not stated); Schaflechner calls the notion that the history of Sindh holds no religious tension between Hindus and Muslims a nationalist utopian ideal (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 90–91, folio not stated), and Boivin argues that, despite the spread of Islamist terrorism in Sindh, the Sufi culture still pervades Sindhi society (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 177, folio 164).

## The Province in History: From the Earliest Capitals to the Mughal Conquest

The books name and bound the province in their own ways. Most of their authors write "Sind", Haroon Khalid, Omar Kasmani and Scott Kugle "Sindh", and Shemeem Burney Abbas often "the Sind"; the unit each of them means is the one described in the passage cited, and none of them draws its boundary as a modern map would. For Annemarie Schimmel the Sindh of the Arab conquest is "the lower Indus valley" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 61, folio 30), and in *Pain and Grace* it is "the southern part of present day Pakistan" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 6, folio 2). An unattributed note in Charles Frederick Usborne's translation of Waris Shah reports a different reckoning: "The Arab historians speak of Hind and Sind separately. Sind, In their description, is bounded by Multan, Jaisalmere and Gujrat and Hind, or Hindustan, begins where Sind ends." The same note gives the Arabic title of the Chach Nameh, which it calls one of the earliest histories of Sindh, as The History of Hind and Sind, though one word of the transliterated title is uncertain in the printed text (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 55, folio 54, note 1). The note seems to gloss Ranjha's blessing on a Raja that he may rule over horses, camels, elephants, Hindustan and Sindh (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 54, folio 53).

H. T. Sorley's province is above all the Sind of Shah Abdul Latif's lifetime, and he frames his history of it as a history of the Mughal power in decline: "Nowhere is the need for an adequate description of the Moghul power in its decline felt more than in the case of Sind", where the growing ineffectiveness of Delhi's suzerainty "produced local events which shaped the destiny of the province" in a manner of its own (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 14, folio 5). He warns that the task is made hard by the scarcity, indeed the almost total absence, of reliable contemporary record for the years 1690 to 1760 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 14, folio 5). The East India Company's records for the poet's lifetime, which he gives as 1689 to 1752, are in his words "totally devoid of any reference to Sind" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 28, folio 19), and he finds no European record for Sindh between 1662, as the damaged printed figure is read, and 1758 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 42, folio 33). From 1000 A.D. onwards, he writes, Sindh receives hardly any mention in Indian history, and Moreland found it impossible "to include Sind in his economic reconstruction of the Moghul Empire" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 23, folio 14).

For Sorley the land itself shaped this history. "Sind is difficult country for the historian", he writes, a terrain "woefully devoid of informative historical monuments" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 19–20, folios 10–11). It is an alluvial tract over which the Indus has wandered through the centuries in a multitude of shifting channels, and its buildings were made mostly of mud, sticks or wattles, "just as they are outside all the large towns today" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 19–20, folios 10–11). For six months of the year, he says, the climate is as hot as anything in India and cursed with dust storms, and the whole is "A shifting alluvial plain liable to inundation, surrounded on every side but that of the sea by forbidding desert or barren hilly country" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 19–20, folios 10–11). "For the most part Sind has remained apart from the really crucial events happening in the rest of neighbouring Asia", he writes, and he calls its history episodic and isolated, showing to a late date the theocratic foundation of Islamic society (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 21, folio 12). In his judgement no event of prime importance graced the annals of Sindh between 1659 and 1758, its history being monotonous and self-contained, cut off by "the nature of the country with its fortresses of desert and barren land on all sides" and by a climate that confined campaigning to a few months a year (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 22, folio 13).

For Sujan Rai Bhandari, writing in a work he dates to the year one thousand one hundred and seven of the Hijra (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 581), the country is the *wilayat* of Sindh (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 173), the realm of Sindh (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 386–387), the lands of Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 175), and, in his own phrase, the *wilayat* of Sindh known as Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 581). In his own day it is a Mughal *suba*, the *suba* of Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530). He begins its history with its capitals of ancient times. Brahmanabad, he writes, was a great city and the seat of the throne, and its fort had fourteen hundred towers, each a rope's length from the next; after it Adpur became the capital, and many traces of its towers and walls remain; after that came Dewal, and now Thatta and Debal are the seat of government (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530).

Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti, writing in Urdu in the nineteenth century, carries the story of the Arabs furthest back. Arab trade had begun in Gujarat and Sindh, and a commander whose name is uncertain in the printed text was sent to attack Sindh (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 802). Under Uthman the Arabs attacked the lands along the river of Sindh, and under Ali they conquered some part of the country of Sindh, which was given up after his death (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 802). Under Walid, between the years 705 and 715 of the Christian era, both numbers uncertain in the printed text, they conquered the country of Sindh and reached the Ganges (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 802–803). Schimmel writes that in 711 the Arabs "also reached Sind, the lower Indus valley, and laid the foundation of a Muslim rule that still continues in present Pakistan" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 61, folio 30), and that 711 was "the year in which the Arabs conquered Sind and the adjacent provinces northward up to Multan" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 375, folio 344). Richard Eaton writes that "Muslim navies sailed to the coasts of western India where in 711 they conquered and occupied the densely-populated" Hindu and Buddhist society of Sindh, beginning the long encounter between Islamic and Indic civilisations (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 30, folio 18). He reports Andrew Watson's view that "the Arab conquest of Sind in 711, which established a direct and regular contact between India and the Fertile Crescent" threw open western Asia, Africa and Europe to the agricultural treasures of India, "effectively incorporating all these regions for the first time into a single agricultural universe" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 39, folio 27).

Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi calls it "a myth" that the conquest began as a punitive expedition against "the ruling Raja Dahir": "In reality, the conquest of Transoxiana and Sind was initiated by Hajjaj, Walid's governor in Iraq, and was one aspect of his expansionist policy"; Muhammad bin Qasim al-Saqafi began his advance through Makran and Baluchistan in 710 and conquered Sindh "between 711 and 712" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 127, folio 109). Shemeem Burney Abbas writes that Muhammad bin Qasim "came to Sind through the land route from Iran" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 50, folio not stated). Sorley writes that Sindh was under Arab governors for nearly three hundred years, and remained under Arab influence for longer still while the early dynasties of Multan and Mansura held Upper and Lower Sindh until nearly the end of the tenth century; yet the Arabs "left no permanent impress upon the land" and "were merely a military garrison living amongst strangers" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 247, folio 238).

Rizvi describes the society the conquest made. "Early Arab conquerors settled their families in large numbers in the various towns of Sind"; Brahmans in high office converted to keep their positions, and Buddhists, hostile to Brahman domination, converted as well; "Hindu and Buddhist scholars from Sind were sent to the 'Abbasid court", and in the eighth century a scholar of hadith, Abu Ma'shar Najih (d. 170/787), and the poet Abu'l-'Ata (died after 158/774), both names imperfectly printed, were leading literary figures from Sindh (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 127, folio 109). Schimmel writes that the pious of these western provinces were in the early centuries "apparently interested mainly in the collection of hadith and in the transmission to the central Muslim countries of scientific information from India" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 375, folio 344). Eaton reports, after Y. Friedmann, that "in the eighth century, when Arabs replaced Brahmans as rulers of Sind, the new rulers merely continued the earlier practice of requiring the Jats to associate themselves with dogs" and in this way to affirm their lowly status (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 224, folio 212). Quoting Ray, Sorley writes that "The internal administration of the country was necessarily left largely in the hands of Hindu landlords paying the land tax (kharaj) and the capitation tax (jizya)" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 248, folio 239). Quoting Masudi, he records that Arab merchants "formed the commercial communication between Sind and the neighbouring countries of India", bringing the produce of China and Ceylon to the seaports of Sindh and carrying it on by way of Multan to Turkistan and Khurasan (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 248, folio 239).

The tenth century is the age of the Isma'ilis. Towards its end, Sorley writes, "Sind was infected by Qarmatian heresies from Egypt" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 248, folio 239), and elsewhere, again after Ray, that Islam in Sindh then appears to have been touched by a wave of Qarmatian heresy (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 174, folio 165). For Schimmel the Carmathians "ruled not only Bahrain but also northern Sind and Multan" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 98, folio 67). Rizvi explains the lack of information about Sufis in Sindh by two factors: the land routes "between Khurasan, Transoxiana and Sind were slow and arduous", and the rise of Sufism coincided with the Isma'ili Fatimid Caliphate, so that "Mansura, the capital of Sind, ruled by the Sumira dynasty had also accepted the over-lordship of the Egyptian Caliphs" and Sufism there was cut off from other areas (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 128, folio 110). When Sindh ceased to receive Arab governors from the Umayyad empire, Sorley writes, independent Sindh dynasties arose, among which the Sumra soon became prominent, "a local dynasty of Rajput origin"; quoting Ray, he adds that southern Sindh was never thoroughly conquered by the house of Ghazni (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 247, folio 238).

The house of Ghazni reached Sindh as a frontier of its campaigns. In Sabuktigin's time, Chishti says on the authority of the *Tarikh-e Firishta*, the Muslims knew no route into India except the one by way of Sindh, a word before it being illegible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 804). In Bhandari's chronicle of the sultans of Delhi, Sultan Mahmud led a great army toward Multan and Sindh and had as many as four thousand boats built, each fitted with three iron spikes, one at the prow and one on either side; his enemies sent their families away to the islands and fought, and most of them were killed or drowned (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 411). On another of Mahmud's campaigns a raja, whose name is uncertain in the printed text, abandoned his army out of faintheartedness and poor judgement and made for Sindh, and the Sultan's troops pursued and caught him (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 421–420). Late in the year 417, a year uncertain in the printed text, Mahmud set out to punish a group who had behaved insolently toward his soldiers on the bank of the river Sindh as he returned from Somnath (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 412).

The Sumra held the country for centuries, though the books measure their rule differently. Sorley dates the Sumra regime from about 1025, as the damaged first figure is read, to about 1360, and notes the confusion "as to the authority wielded over Upper and Lower Sind, which have had very different histories" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 174, folio 165). He sets two older authorities beside each other: the *Ain-i-Akbari* says that a Sumra Rajput line of thirty-six princes reigned for five hundred years, and the *Tarikh-i-Tahiri* that the Hindu tribe of Sumra ruled Sindh from the Hijri year 700, the figure imperfectly printed, until 843; a Sumra king, Chanesar, reigned in Lower Sindh for about eighteen years around A.D. 1228 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 270, folio 261). His glossary calls the Sumra a Sindhi tribe that was at one time ruler of Sindh (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 431, folio 422). Bhandari gives the country's own rulers a section of their own, and names his source: the history of Sultan Bahadur Shah, he writes, sets down the affairs of the sultans of the *wilayat* of Sindh thus (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 173–174). The first rulers were the descendants of Bani Tamim Ansari, the reading uncertain, who ruled for one hundred and thirty nine years; then the Sumra, who stood out among the *zamindars* for their strength and following, took over, in a passage partly illegible; government stayed in the Sumra family for five hundred years, and then passed to the Samma (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 173). For Rizvi, "The Sumira dynasty continued to rule the Lower Sind until the middle of the fourteenth century. After the end of the twelfth century, the Upper Sind came under the domination of Sunni rule" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 128, folio 110).

"Arab rule in eighth century Sind having weakened and died, it was left to Persianized Turks to establish a permanent Muslim presence in India from the thirteenth century", Eaton writes (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 47, folio 35), and the thirteenth century tied the province to the Sultans of Delhi and to the great Suhrawardi centre at Multan. On Shihab al-Din's fourth campaign, in a year of the five hundreds whose last figures cannot be read, the Sultan went against Dewal, which is known as Thatta, brought the whole country along the river Sindh under his control, and returned with the rarities of that land and great wealth (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 404). Kanhaiya Lal calls Taj al-Din Yalduz king of Kech, Makran, Sindh and Multan (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 24, folio 19), and writes that Sultan Shihab al-Din Ghori gave him a wide realm that took in Kech, Makran and Sindh with one further named territory (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 25, folio 20). In Iltutmish's reign the fugitive Jalal al-Din fled toward Sindh and Siwistan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 399), and Rizvi has Iltutmish, seven years after November 1221, rout his rival Nasiru'd-Din Qubacha from Sindh (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 152, folio 134). Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya "openly sided with Sultan Shamsu'd-Din Iltutmish when he wished to add Multan and Sind to the Delhi Sultanate" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 209, folio printed "19]" [OCR?] [191]), and "After the annexation of Multan and Sind by Iltutmish in 1228, relations between the Sultan of Delhi and the Shaikh became more intimate" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 210, folio 192). Under Balban the realm of Sindh, with all its dependencies, was fixed as the *jagir* of Prince Muhammad Sultan, who is posted to Multan in the next sentence (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 386–387).

Michel Boivin describes the province's fourteenth century. Of the century in which Pithoro Pir lived he writes that "the period when he lived was turbulent": regionally it was the time of transition between the Sumra and the Samma, and although the Sammas "officially took power from the first in 1351, the Somras had preserved their control of certain outlying areas of Sindh, including, precisely, the area of Umarkot" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 189, folio 176). In 1351 Muhammad Shah Tughluq, sultan of Delhi, "arrived in Sindh in pursuit of a rebel"; in 1367 Firuz Shah Tughluq "returned to lay siege to Thatha", and "Jam Juna II, who controlled Sindh then, sought alliances with the sultan of Gujarat" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 189, folio 176). An endnote gives the book's own measure of how the unit has changed: the Siraiki speaking area of Southern Punjab "was a part of Sindh during the Middle Ages" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 216, folio 203). In Bhandari, the first Samma ruler was Jam Anzar, and the title Jam, given to their headman and chief, he traces back, through a word that cannot be read, to Jamshid (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 173). In the time of Jam Bani Thatta son of Jam Anzar, the name uncertain in the printed text, Sultan Firuz Shah, ruler of Delhi, marched against the Jam three times, at last took the *wilayat* of Sindh and brought the Jam to Delhi, and then, pleased with his service, gave the country back to him (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 173). In the same century, Rizvi writes, the help of Makhdum-i Jahaniyan "was also sought by the governors of Sind" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 301, folio 279).

Bhandari's list of the Samma Jams runs as follows, with many names and figures uncertain in the printed text. Jam Anzar reigned from the year 735, itself uncertain, for a number of years that cannot be read and six months; his brother Jam Junan, fourteen years; Jam Bani Thatta, son of Anzar, fifteen years; his brother Jam Tamachi, thirteen years; Jam Salahuddin, eleven years and some months; Jam Nizamuddin, son of Salahuddin, two years and some months; Jam Ali Sher, six years and some months; Jam Karan, son of Tamachi, a day and a half; Jam Fath Khan, son of Sikandar, fifteen years, raised up by the notables when the country was left without a ruler; Jam Tughluq, son of Sikandar, eighteen years as the numeral is read with some doubt; Jam Mubarak, three days; Jam Sikandar, son of Fath Khan, a year and six months; Jam Sanjar, eight years and some months; Jam Nizamuddin, who was known as Jam Nanda, sixty two years; and Jam Firuz, nineteen years (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 172–173). Sorley, for his part, measures later rulers against them, writing that the Kalhora were "the first Sindhi dynasty to wield permanent power since the fall of the Samma" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 36, folio 27).

On how Sindh became Muslim, Sorley gives more than one account. In one place he writes that the indigenous population of the lower Indus valley in the eighth century consisted of Hindu races speaking a Sanskritic language, and that "Most of the population was converted to Islam in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 174, folio 165); under Jam Nizamuddin, about A.D. 1461, Islam spread widely over Sindh and the mass of the common people became Muslim (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 175, folio 166). In another he writes that "Sind was converted to Islam not by the Arabs but by the central Asian peoples who took up the tale of Muslim penetration of India from the twelfth century onwards", that this penetration went on through the reigns of the Sumra and Samma dynasties, and that by the time of the Lodi Afghans in the sixteenth century the agricultural population and the landholding class were almost entirely Muslim, the Hindus being confined to the business of writing and to trade and commerce (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 248, folio 239). That state of affairs, he says, persisted down to the British annexation, whose year is lost in the printed text at this point, and Sindh became a predominantly Muslim land at a time when Central Asian and Persian influences were at full strength (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 248, folio 239). He counts the comparatively late conversion of the bulk of the population among the peculiar circumstances of the province (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 245, folio 236).

Other authors see the same change from other angles. Historical sources, Boivin writes, show that before the eighteenth century "the people who identified themselves as Muslims were few", probably because the name was restricted to the *sayyids* and their dependents (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 22–23, folios 9–10). Christopher Shackle, in the introduction to his translation of Shah Abdul Latif's *Risalo*, writes that the Muslim conquests of northern India "extended this Persianate cultural world to Sindh", where Persian remained the dominant literary language of the Muslim elite down to the Mughal period and beyond (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 12, folio not stated), and he speaks of the Mughal capital of Thatta and the other urban centres of Sindh as homes of Persian poetry written by the educated elite (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 25, folio not stated).

Fifteenth and sixteenth century Sindh was ruled from the province itself and then from outside it. Bhandari's account of the reign of Sultan Husain Langah shows the quarrels of the Jams reaching north: Jam Bayazid and Jam Ibrahim, offended with Jam Nanda, ruler of the *wilayat* of Thatta, went over to Sultan Husain and were given a *jagir* whose name is uncertain in the printed text, and Jam Bayazid later helped the Sultan kill his vizier Imad al-Mulk (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 295). Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori, writing in Persian in 1875, records that after Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus settled at Uch, Sultan Husain Mirza, the ruler of Sindh, and Sultan Sikandar Lodhi, king of Hind, became his disciples; the text gives no year, and the editor's note on the page dates the saint's coming to Uch to 887 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 40, folio 28). The Samma line ended, in Bhandari's account, in a quarrel within the family. Jam Salahuddin, a kinsman of Jam Firuz whose sister was married to Sultan Muzaffar of Gujarat, twice raised armies from Gujarat and won; Jam Firuz called in Mirza Shah Beg Arghun from Qandahar, Salahuddin was killed, and the government of the *wilayat* of Sindh passed to Jam Firuz alone (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 172). Then in the year 927, a year uncertain in the printed text, Mirza Shah Beg Arghun led an army from Qandahar and took Thatta; Jam Firuz went to Gujarat, entered the service of Sultan Bahadur Shah, gave him his daughter in marriage and died there, and with that the rule of the Samma line was cut off (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 172).

The Arghuns and Tarkhans followed. Schimmel writes that Turkish influence "was prevalent in Sind from 1520 onward, when the Turkish dynasty of the Arghun, descending from Afghanistan, overcame the last indigenous Sindhi rulers; they themselves were followed by the Turkish family of the Tarkhan under whom Sind fell to the Mughal Empire", and finds a trace of it in a Turkish word, *otaq*, in Shah Abdul Latif's verse, its printed form uncertain (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 207, folio 207). In Bhandari, Mirza Shah Beg took the *wilayat* of Sindh by strength and courage and died in the year 930 after a rule of three years; Mirza Shah Husain fortified Bhakkar anew, took Multan, and died in 962 after thirty two years (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 171). When Babur came, his *farman* went to Mirza Shah Husain Arghun, ruler of Thatta, who came up from Thatta to fight for Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 295). Mirza Isa Tarkhan, one of Mirza Shah Beg's amirs, at Thatta, and Sultan Mahmud at Bhakkar each claimed independence, sometimes at peace with each other and sometimes at war, and Isa Tarkhan died in 965 after thirteen years; Mirza Muhammad Baqi overcame his brother Khan Baba, kept up his father's dealings with Sultan Mahmud of Bhakkar, and ruled eighteen years (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 171). These years, and the eighteen, are all uncertain in the printed text. The Naqshbandiyya, in Boivin's account, "came to Sindh from the sixteenth century onwards, with the coming of the Tarkhans and the Arghuns, two Central Asian dynasties that ruled Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 228, folio 215).

Sindh's independent rule ended when its ruler entered the empire. "Sind was added to the Moghul Empire by Akbar in 1592", Sorley writes (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 18, folio 9), and his chronological table records for that year the defeat of Mirza Jani Beg Tarkhan, the name imperfectly printed, and the annexation of Sindh by Akbar (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 200, folio 191). The order Bhandari records was first to take the *wilayat* of Thatta, and after that to turn to the conquest of Qandahar (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 175). Mirza Jani Beg, son of Painda Muhammad son of Muhammad Baqi, came to court with the Khankhanan in the year 1001 of the Hijra, a year uncertain in the printed text, having ruled for eight years, and the country entered the protected dominions of the empire (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 171). Jani Beg was given the rank of three thousand and the *jagir* of Thatta, and Lahari Bandar, the port on the Thatta side, was made crown land (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 174). Among the deeds of the Khankhanan, Mirza Abd al-Rahim, Bhandari lists the conquest of Gujarat, Thatta and the Deccan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 244), and the chronicle's table of contents also lists a section on the conquest of the lands of Thatta and the coming of Jani Beg (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 591).

Kugle tells how one of Akbar's chief nobles, Abd al-Rahim Khan-i Khanan, "was traveling with an army toward Sindh" and stopped in Lahore to receive the blessing of Shah Hussayn; the Mughal armies then "conquered Thatta in Sindh, and Hussayn's reputation spread" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 241, folio 220). For Schimmel, "Sind was, since 1595, part of the Mughal Empire, and although representatives of the Delhi court came there, the indigenous rulers enjoyed much freedom" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 25, folio not stated). What the empire made of the province is the matter of the century that followed.

## The Province in History: Mughal, Kalhora, Talpur and British Rule, to the Present

The Mughal unit was not Sindh alone. Under Akbar, Sorley writes, the country as a whole was made part of the *suba* of Multan, with sarkars established at Bhakkar in the north and at Thatta in the Indus delta (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 31–32, folios 22–23). He restates it later: Sindh consisted of two sarkars, or divisions, of the *suba*, or province, of Multan, and "The headquarters of the sarkars were Bakhar for Upper Sind and Tatta for Lower Sind" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 150, folio 141). He quotes Major P. Goldney's report that Upper Sindh, at the time of Nadir Shah's invasion of Hindustan and sack of Delhi, formed part of the *suba* of Multan, and that its "Moghulee" portion, Sukkur, Bhakkar and Shikarpur, was then annexed to the Durani kingdom (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 38–39, folios 29–30; Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 164, folio 155). "What is certain is that Upper and Lower Sind were never definitely united till a much later day", he writes, and even as late as 1843 their unification was not complete (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 31–32, folios 22–23). From the time of Shah Jahan until the first decade of the eighteenth century the emperor's authority was wielded by officials sent from other parts of the empire; with the Kalhora, the emperor's representative was a native of Sindh (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 31–32, folios 22–23). Callendar's account of 1775 says that "While the Mogul's government flourished Scindy was a province of the Empire", an honorary appointment bestowed each year on some person of rank whose expenses generally exceeded the revenues (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 61, folio 52).

In Bhandari the *suba* of Thatta appears as an office given to the emperor's men. On Shah Jahan's march from the Deccan to his accession, in a year printed as 1037 and uncertain (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 104–106), during a halt of seven days at Ahmadabad, a Mirza Isa Tarkhan received the rank of four thousand *zat* and two thousand horse and the governorship of the *suba* of Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 105). In the pursuit of Dara Shikoh, Qubad Khan, the name uncertain in the printed text, stayed behind by imperial order to hold the governorship of Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 49); at the halt at Lahore he had been raised to the governorship of the *suba* of Thatta with an increase to the rank of four thousand and three thousand horse, being described as one of the foster kin of Shaikh Mir, the words uncertain in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 64). The table of contents of the Aurangzeb reign lists a section on Dara Shikoh's coming from Thatta toward Gujarat (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 589). Zahur ul-Hasan Sharib writes that in Sarmad's day travellers from Iran came to Hindustan by way of Sindh, and that Sarmad came by the same road and reached Thatta (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 297, folio 293).

Sorley's table of events gives the Mughal century in outline: in 1605 the rise of the Daudpota in Shikarpur and Upper Sindh; in 1648 to 1651 Aurangzeb as governor of Multan and Sindh; in 1658 the civil war between Aurangzeb and Dara Shikoh, ending in Dara Shikoh's defeat and his death after pursuit through Sindh; and the Kalhora beginning to oppose the Mughal troops (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 200, folio 191). He judges that "The internal history of Sind for one hundred and fifty years after its annexation by Akbar shows as clearly as any other part of the Moghul dominions these characteristic deficiencies of Moghul government", and that the history of the Mughal occupation is full of conflicts between local and imperial revenue demands (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 33–34, folios 24–25). Withington's narrative, he writes, shows the disorder of that part of Sindh in the early seventeenth century and how weak Mughal control was outside easy distance of the larger towns (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 78, folio 69; Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 81, folio 72). The Sameja were in rebellion against the Mughal governor in 1640, disturbing the peace of central Sindh (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 51, folio 42). In a Company letter of 1644 Spiller wrote that "the people are so exceedingly opprest and kept so miserably poor that notwithstanding the soil is fertile and propper and would produce large quantities of good indicoes they have nether the will nor means to manure and sow the ground" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 52, folio 43). Trade in Upper Sindh, he notes, was harder to finance than in Lower Sindh (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 52–53, folios 43–44), and in 1647, Sorley writes, there was much unsettlement in Upper Sindh from a conflict of authority (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 53, folio 44).

The disorder continued through the century. The bad government of Upper Sindh and the corruption of its petty officials draw caustic comment in the records of 1652 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 55, folio 46). In 1656 the Sameja were pillaging in Middle Sindh (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 56, folio 47), and a letter of 1656 shows how far the Baluchi population lay outside the effective control of the Mughal officers (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 55, folio 46). "In that year and the next, Sind was ravaged by famine and plague", Sorley writes of 1659, and a Company letter says that "neaver famine raged worse in any place, the living being hardly able to burye the dead" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 56, folio 47). By 1699, in his words, "travelling in Sind was still an undertaking not to be lightly faced", the countryside infested by bands of robbers (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 90, folio 81), and "the later Moghul empire in Sind was no better than the early one in preserving law and order" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 91, folio 82). Hamilton's picture of Mughal Sindh at the end of the seventeenth century shows lawlessness in the countryside, the absolute autocracy of the governor and a thriving river trade, yet near the centre of government the administration was, within its limits, "not lacking in practical efficiency" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 94, folio 85).

The eighteenth century loosened the empire's hold and brought the Kalhora to power. Sorley describes the Kalhora as a Sindhi tribe of obscure origin, of the type now classed as Jamot, that is, non-Baluch original inhabitants of the Indus valley (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 36, folio 27). For Schimmel, "The first obvious sign of the struggle for power in the Lower Indus Valley becomes visible in 1717", in the conflict around the Sufi Shah Inayat; "The Kalhora clan from Upper Sind, particularly active in the struggle against Inayat, soon afterwards became independent in Sind", and the army sent against Jhok came "from various places in Sind and Hind" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 25, folio not stated). In the religious life of the province, "A strong Naqshbandiyya influence in the beginning of the eighteenth century, however, changed the spiritual climate of southern Sind to a certain extent": Makhdum Muhammad Hashim of Thatta and his relatives attacked the Sufis who defended the emotional side of religion, and "this Naqshbandi group contributed to promoting a more sober mystical piety" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 428, folio 397). Sorley sets out the stages of the century: Kalhora governors from 1701; semi-independence by 1736; Persian suzerainty and tribute after the invasion of Nadir Shah in 1739; about 1747 the passing of suzerainty to the Pathan kingdom at Kabul; and from 1778 the civil war between the Kalhora and the Talpurs (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 35, folio 26).

Sindh, Sorley writes, remained an integral part of the empire until the disastrous advent of Nadir Shah in 1737, when it fell first under Persian and then, in 1747, under Afghan dominance; the Mughal governors appointed from Hindustan gave way to Sindhi governors bowing the knee "as little as they dared" to Mughal, Persian and Afghan in turn, and by 1760 the ruling Kalhora dynasty was "almost in fact but not at all in theory independent" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 18, folio 9). The Talpurs after them, he adds, were never in fact completely independent of their Afghan overlords until the first decade of the nineteenth century (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 18, folio 9). Schimmel writes that after Aurangzeb's death "the native Kalhoro dynasty took over the rule in Upper and later in Lower Sind, only to become the vassals of the invading Nadir Shah, who first conquered Sind and the Panjab before he reached Delhi in 1739", and that Ahmad Shah Abdali's influence "on the scene in Sind is not as important as it was in the northern areas of present day Pakistan" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, pp. 30–31, folio not stated). A Company letter of 8 April 1758 has the Pathans subduing "the whole Principality of Scindy" and making it tributary to them, as it had been to the Persians since Nadir Shah's conquest (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 63, folio 54). In the poet's own life, Sorley writes, Nadir Shah made Sindh tributary to Persia and Ahmad Shah Durani made it subject to Kabul (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 180, folio 171). The tribute, he records, fell from 21 lakhs under Nadir Shah to 14 lakhs under Ahmad Shah in 1747, to 11 lakhs about 1760 and to 7 lakhs under Timur Shah about 1775, several of the printed figures imperfect, and it "was always in arrears" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 154, folio 145). The government of Sindh regarded the Afghan government at Kabul as a form of unwarranted alien oppression (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 57–58, folios 48–49).

Sorley's table carries the Kalhora and Talpur years, its figures imperfectly printed: Yar Muhammad Kalhoro obtains a grant from Aurangzeb and defeats the Daudpota in Upper Sindh in 1701, and extends his power in Upper Sindh in a year read as 1711; Nur Muhammad Kalhoro becomes Subedar of Sindh on a line with no year that stands opposite 1739; Nadir Shah invades Sindh, which becomes subject to Persia, in 1740; Sindh becomes subject to the Afghan kingdom of Ahmad Shah Durani in 1750; Ahmad Shah Durani invades Sindh in 1754, and Nur Muhammad Kalhoro flees and dies; and in 1757 to 1758 commotion and civil war end in the ascendancy of Ghulam Shah Kalhoro (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 201, folio 192). It continues with misgovernment in Sindh in 1775, the deposition of Sarfaraz Khan Kalhoro for misgovernment in 1776, Ghulam Nabi Khan Kalhoro becoming ruler of Sindh and the beginning of the civil war between the Kalhora and the Talpurs in 1778, and the final establishment of Talpur ascendancy in 1803 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 202, folio 193). From 1756 to 1758, Sorley writes, Sindh was in "a pitiable condition of unrest", though its troubles in 1754 to 1758 were "merely typical of what was happening elsewhere" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 39, folio 30), and in 1758 "Sind no less than the rest of India was in confusion" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 46, folio 37). Robert Erskine wrote in 1760 that it would profit the Company if Sindh were under as good and quiet a government, and as plentiful in grain, as it had been until about 1757, the year imperfectly printed (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 66, folio 57). Callendar wrote in 1775 that "Scindy tho a small track of country is naturally fertile and was rich and populous when it unfortunately fell under the government of this family about 35 years agoe, but has gradually declined ever since", and that "Its chief political connections are with the Patan and Siccs" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 61–62, folios 52–53).

The Kalhora fell to the Talpurs. "It was certainly unfortunate for the masses of the Sind population", Sorley writes, that a dynasty of their own people proved so poor a substitute for the government of the Mughals, while the Talpurs "did succeed in giving a distracted land peace for forty years" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 40–41, folios 31–32). In Schimmel's account the Kalhora were overthrown by "the former disciples of their saintly ancestor, Adam Shah Kalhoro", and "The Talpurs, who thus came to power in 1781, ruled the country until it was conquered by the British in 1843" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, pp. 30–31, folio not stated). Most of the books of the scholar Makhdum Abdur Rahim Girhori "were, however, destroyed during the Afghan raids in Upper Sind in 1781" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 28, folio not stated). The Baluchis, settled in Sindh in considerable numbers, helped the Talpurs destroy "the failing powers of the Sindhi ruling family" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 35, folio 26); by then, Sorley writes, they had become the strongest military force in the land, the earlier Kalhora having encouraged them to settle in Sindh (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 40, folio 31). Boivin writes that the Baluchis had provided since the eighteenth century "the bulk of the troops of the Kalhoras, who had ruled Sindh before them", and that under the Talpurs they stood at the top of local society and held *jagirs* (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 38–41, folios 25–28). Under the Kalhora, in Sorley's account, the government became a sort of cooperation between the ruling house and the chief Baluchi tribes, and the Talpurs welded Sindh into a more complete unity than it had ever known, but only by asserting a Baluch hegemony that bore hard on the non-Baluchi elements of the population, especially the Hindus; under the Mughals and the Kalhora, he writes, Sindh was never at peace, under the Talpurs it was, and the price of that peace was the tyranny of a Baluch minority (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 151–152, folios 142–143).

Boivin adds the religious and social shape of these two reigns. The power of the *pir* "was reinforced in the eighteenth century when the Kalhora developed the irrigation system" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 38–41, folios 25–28). Before 1843 the Sunnis were the majority of Muslims, "but the sovereigns, the Talpur, were themselves Shiites" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 38–41, folios 25–28). In an endnote Boivin reports Ingvild Flaskerud's view that the first portrait of Ali was drawn in Sindh and then sent as a gift to Fath Ali Shah of Persia, who had friendly relations with the Talpurs, "the emirs of Sindh, who were themselves Shia" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 213, folio 200). Under the Talpur rule the Amils, a class of scribes, "almost monopolized the function of kardar", and "when the British conquered Sindh, they had already formed a separate social class" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 44, folio 31). Jürgen Schaflechner adds that the established rule of the Kalhora in the eighteenth century brought new business openings for merchants, one reason why the Lohanas were the most important trading community of Sindh, Kutch and Saurashtra in the nineteenth century (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 80, folio not stated); and Sindh's place "between trading routes connecting Afghanistan, Khorasan, Arabia, Persia, Turkey, and other places made the province appealing to a wide array of merchants from the entire continent" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 81, folio not stated).

Between them, in Sorley's account, the Kalhora and the Talpurs established in Sindh a petty Muslim state which kept, until the British conquest in 1843, the characteristic features of Mughal administration, adding to them "a peculiar brand of Islamic quasi-theocracy" and "a policy of splendid isolation" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 19, folio 10). "The aim of all Sind policy from 1701 onwards was to make Sind independent of Moghul, Persian and Pathan", to diminish the tribute and "to preserve the land as a closed terrain into which no foreigners of any sort were allowed entrance except with the utmost difficulty"; in 1843 Sindh "was then to all intents and purposes a sovereign and petty Muslim state" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 35–36, folios 26–27). The two houses, he writes, kept Sindh shut to the influences of the outside world for a hundred and fifty years (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 41, folio 32). Beneath these changes he finds a deep continuity: there is little reason to doubt, he says, that the peoples of Sindh in the first half of the eighteenth century lived much as Sir Charles Napier found them in 1843 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 19, folio 10), and "the main administrative structure of the government remained largely unaltered from the days of Shah Jahan to the days of Sir Charles Napier" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 30, folio 21). The economic foundation changed little, he writes, despite the rise in the second half of the eighteenth century of the four large towns of modern Sindh, Karachi, Hyderabad, Sukkur and Shikarpur, which displaced Thatta, Nasarpur, Rohri, Sehwan and Kandiaro (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 30, folio 21). The Sindh of Napier was not very different in essentials from the Sindh of Ghulam Shah Kalhoro, which in turn did not differ from the Sindh of Akbar, Jahangir, Shah Jahan and Aurangzeb (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 31, folio 22), and "the framework of the Moghul administration in Sind remained little altered in essentials from 1600 to 1840, the change in spirit was considerable" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 153, folio 144).

Of the British period the books record the conquest of 1843, a new script and the politics of the pirs. The British, Sorley writes, entered on Sindh "as if it were almost an unknown land" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 23, folio 14). For Shackle, only with the British conquest of Sindh in 1843 did the literary culture of the Sufi tradition come to be overlaid by the new patterns of modernity (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, pp. 12–13, folio not stated), and he notes that Sindh was administered from Bombay rather than Calcutta (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 25, folio not stated). Boivin's account turns on colonial Sindh. The historical context of the region at the turn of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, he writes, "is dominated by British colonization", and the shared culture he studies rested on "interaction between new local elites and colonial administrators, as early as the middle of the nineteenth century" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 17, folio 4). The first European movement to reach Sindh was Freemasonry, "since the conqueror and first governor of Sindh, Sir Charles Napier, built a Masonic lodge in 1843 in Karachi" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 46, folio 33). The political history of the *hurr* and the Pir Pagaro belongs with the state, below.

The new government changed the languages of the province. When Persian was given up as the official language in the 1850s, Sindhi and English became the two languages of administration, and the Amils led the great campaign of translation launched by Sir Bartle Frere, the commissioner in Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 44, folio 31). Schimmel writes that "Sindhi continued to flourish after the British occupation in 1843. With the introduction of a new Arabic alphabet in 1852, a considerable literature grew up that included mystical poetry and prose" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396). Boivin concludes that the province's main social structure was "a quasi-feudal system, with a civil servant class and the groups involved in trade, but the policy implemented by the British commissioners, especially Sir Bartle Frere, gave birth to the Sindhi intelligentsia" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 63, folio 50). After the uprising of 1857 the Sufi Imdadullah fled through the Punjab and Sindh visiting Sufi shrines, none of which Kugle names, and sailed from Karachi into exile in Mecca (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 280, folio 259). After 1857 many Kayasths went to work in the princely states, including the Khairpur emirate in Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 26, folio 13). With the growth of new trading centres in Sindh, especially Karachi, the Lohanas invested in agricultural land, mainly cotton, and by the end of the nineteenth century had become powerful landowners, known locally as *zamindars* (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 43–44, folios 30–31).

Boivin gives the census figures for Sindh from the first census of 1872 as Hughes reported them: a population printed as 2,333,527 million, with 1,718,688 Muslims, 393,092 Hindus, 5,034 Christians and 86,663 persons in a category of other castes such as Sikhs, Parsis and Jews, although the same sentence opens by saying that Sindh's population statistics are not available in that census (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 38, folio 25). In another place he describes Sindh as "a largely Muslim-dominated country, making up about 75 per cent of the whole population, where the power had been in the hands of Muslims for centuries" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 199, folio 186). In 1941, he writes, the Hindus made up about 21 per cent of the population of Sindh, including 6 per cent of the scheduled castes, so that they would have numbered under a million, exactly 0.9 million (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 61, folio 48).

The twentieth century brought a politics of the province's own and a breach in its communal peace. Schaflechner traces Sindhi nationalism, following Oscar Verkaaik, to "the struggle for independence from the Bombay Presidency in the 1920s and 1930s", and names as important to its later growth the 1947 publication *Save Sind, Save the Continent*, by Ibrahim Joyo, whose first name the book prints as Ibrahmin (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 88, folio not stated). From the 1920s onwards riots between Hindus and Muslims increased, a growing tension that culminated with Partition (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 64, folio 51). Boivin notes that "it is a common trope to claim that Sindh did not suffer the violence experienced by Punjab", but reports from Punjab were reaching the province, and there was a precedent in the Sukkur riots, which spread beyond the city to Rohri and to other parts of Sindh such as Lakki (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 61, folio 48). Even if it never reached the level of Punjab's violence, he concludes, "it is irrelevant to claim there was no communal violence in Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 62, folio 49). The narrative of Rochaldas, one of his subjects, lays the spread of violence through Sindh not on the Muslim Sindhis but on the Muhajirs, who wanted the houses of the Hindus, at least in Karachi; some Hindus had already sent their wives and children to India while they themselves stayed on in Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 70–71, folios 57–58).

Shackle carries the story to "after independence in 1947, when the mass emigration of the Hindu population from Pakistan to India took place" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, pp. 12–13, folio not stated). Boivin follows the migration in numbers. By early November 1947, 246,000 Hindus had already left Sindh by sea, air or rail; by 1 January 1948 nearly 500,000 people were estimated to have sought refuge in India; and Hindus from interior Sindh travelled in special trains chartered by the Sindh government (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 62, folio 49). In December, with an added train, 140,000 Hindus crossed the border, and on 14 January the government of India appointed a director general of evacuation to assist non-Muslims who wished to leave Sindh for India (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 63, folio 50). The Hindu Sindhis left especially after the Karachi riots of January 1948 (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 66, folio not stated). He puts the migrants at closer to 0.8 million, if not fewer, against a general consensus of about a million (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 61, folio 48).

For Schaflechner, "The partition of the subcontinent brought major changes to the province of Sindh": the departure of the Hindu elite and the arrival of Muslim refugees from India altered its demography, and the Sindhi community came to define itself by a sense of being shut out and subjugated by Muhajir power (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 87, folio not stated). Even so, Boivin writes, "Sindh is the province of Pakistan with the most significant Hindu population", and "The province of Sindh now belongs to Pakistan, a country that has been through different steps of islamization" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 176–177, folios not stated and 164). So the books bring the province to the present day.

## The Land and Its Livelihood

The river made the land. Sorley writes that "Sind is one of those lucky lands that are not seriously troubled if the monsoon" rains fail, "because Sind is the creation, the slow geographical accretion, of a river", its calamities being unchecked inundation, drifting sand and changing river channels (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 118, folio 109). Within its limits, he writes, it has always supported a tolerably well-fed population in modest comfort, and "The land was considered a sort of Arabia Felix with a quiet, industrious and intelligent people living in isolation from the rest of the Indian continent" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 119, folio 110). Greenery, Sorley writes, is not common in the arid land except close to the river and its large branches (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 182, folio 173), and his glossary gives Sawan, the word uncertainly printed, as the rainy season in Sindh, July and August (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 430, folio 421). The country, he adds, is deficient in building stone (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 101, folio 92), and metal does not exist in it (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 108, folio 99).

Famine and sickness are weighed against each other in his account. Famines such as devastated less fortunate parts of India "had ordinarily no terrors for the Sindhi peasant" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 129, folio 120), and "Sind was relatively immune from famines" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 143, folio 134). On the same page he records Aurangzeb's jagir yielding little because of drought, locusts, plague and floods in three successive seasons, from 1648 to 1652, and Hamilton's plague of 1696, and judges that "in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries public health in Sind was not good" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 143, folio 134). Burnes wrote of the unhealthiness of Lower Sindh, its epidemics, agues, fevers and ophthalmia from the dust (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 144, folio 135).

Irrigation, for Sorley, is the key. Its place in Sindh, where rainfall is scanty and never regular, cannot be exaggerated (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 121, folio 112). The canals, Persian wheels and wells of Sindh "were constructed by the natives of the land without any assistance from the Empire of Delhi" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 122, folio 113), skilful irrigation has existed there since the earliest days (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 135, folio 126), and "Sind has always been a pioneer of irrigation in India" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 138, folio 129). He thinks Kalhora irrigation probably superior to that of the Talpurs, and quotes Lieutenant-Colonel Scott in 1853 that "the whole system of canals in Scinde is one of makeshifts and expedients" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 138, folio 129). The old lift methods, he writes, are still in use and can be seen all over Sindh (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 139, folio 130), and the canal system of Mughal and Kalhora days must, within its limits, have been a very important source of the country's wealth (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 142–143, folios 133–134). Forced labour, *begari*, had always been practised in Sindh, and several of the biggest canals were dug by labour conscripted in this way (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 130, folio 121).

The crops followed the water. The Commissioner in Sindh reported in 1847 that bajri and jowari were more common in the upper part of the country and wheat and barley in the lower, rice growing most extensively in the low southern parts (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 127, folio 118). "The Chandookah and the Lar were the two great rice producing areas just as they are today", and cultivators were then thought better off than in most parts of India (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 128, folio 119). Sindh has always been noted for its fresh and salt water fish, the *Ain-i-Akbari* giving rice and fish as the staple food of Lower Sindh, and the Lower Sindh littoral was, with Gujarat, Kanara and Malabar, one of the great sources of India's seafaring population (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 108–109, folios 99–100).

Sorley's Sindh of the seventeenth century was a place of trade. With its internal waterway system of the Indus, its easy connexion with Lahore and Multan, its thriving cotton industry, "and its vast emporium at Tatta with its busy port at Laribunder, was soon envisaged as a profitable centre of trade with Europe" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 97, folio 88). Fremlen wrote in 1636: "For of all Indian goods none are in such request as those of Synda" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 51, folio 42). His table records an English factory established at Thatta in 1636 and the English factory in Sindh given up in 1662 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 200, folio 191), and a factory established again at Thatta in 1758 and given up in 1775 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 202, folio 193). In the Company's press lists he finds "a complete blank till 25 November 1744" after a letter of 13 October 1657, references to Sindh growing more numerous but not common until the factory's re-establishment in 1758, a regular record of the Sindh factory from August 1762 to July 1764, and a list of covenanted servants for 1712 to 1738 that names no servant residing in Sindh (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 204, folio 195). "The Company was always very much the timid guest of the Sind Government", he writes, and its customs and transit dues were "as unreasonable and oppressive as they had been in the Moghul days" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 58, folio 49; Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 57–58, folios 48–49).

Within Sindh, Sorley writes, transport was all by pack animals, mostly camels, and wheeled traffic was practically unknown (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 98, folio 89). Yet "trade in Sind was on a very well-organized basis", by land and by water, the business soundly established and the government's supervision very thorough (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 100, folio 91). Pirates in those days infested the Gujarat, Kathiawar and Sindh coasts (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 58, folio 49), and their presence on the Sindh coast caused shippers continual anxiety (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 71, folio 62); a Company letter of 1758 says that the whole coast are rogues on both sides (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 63, folio 54). Three kinds of rupee were in circulation in Sindh (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 70, folio 61), and the lari was in common use in southern Persia, Sindh and western Asia (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 133, folio 124).

"During Moghul and Kalhoro times the chief industries were the manufacture of cotton goods, saltpetre and indigo, leather and fish", Sorley writes, and hand flour-milling, oil-pressing and the potter's craft can still be seen in Sindh exactly as they were then (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 101–102, folios 92–93). The popular modern idea of Sindh as a desert is, he says, as mistaken as the view that it was a purely agricultural country, for "Sind supported half a dozen thriving industries", with camels and buffaloes supporting much of the population; he quotes Crowe that "Camels are bred in every part of Sind" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 110, folio 101). Thatta and Nasarpur, Sorley writes, were the main centres of the cotton industry in Sindh (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 102, folio 93). Saltpetre production was estimated at up to 10,000 maunds a year (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 104, folio 95), but "The supply from Sind was never more than a useful auxiliary of the main supply obtained from Bengal and Bihar" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 105, folio 96). Indigo was grown mostly in Middle Sindh, and its quality was inferior (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 106, folio 97). "Sind has always been a land with large numbers of buffaloes and oxen", with a plentiful supply of hides, and its saddlery and ornamental leatherwork were famous (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 107, folio 98). "In Sind, a Muslim land, this prejudice against leather is not felt by three-quarters of the population" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 108, folio 99).

Skilled hands were harder to find. The Company's letters of the 1760s complain of "the paucity of skilled workmen available in Sind" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 67, folio 58), of "The inability of Sind to provide suitable artisans" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 72, folio 63), and Sorley writes that "It was impossible to get Sindhi craftsmen capable of making the kind of article needed" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 75, folio 66); artificers for the saltpetre coppers could not be procured in Sindh (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 105, folio 96). Of the Company's own servants he observes, in his own voice, that "As Sindhi Muhammadans are notoriously unhappy away from Sind", their fondness for desertion perhaps made them unsuitable in Bombay (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 64, folio 55).

The trade declined. By 1770, Sorley writes, "Sind suffered with the rest of cotton manufacturing India", and the later Kalhora turned it into a self-contained Muslim state closed to Englishmen for nearly three-quarters of a century, so that it relapsed into lethargy until after 1843; yet he holds that the decline of its trade from 1775 was not due to the incapacity of the Kalhora or the parochialism of the Talpurs (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 116, folio 107). About the time of the British annexation, after half a century of Talpur rule, Sindh had for nearly a hundred years ceased to supply cottons to the European market, and "In fact Sind was now importing English chintzes and calicoes instead of exporting her own"; its exports were rice, ghi, opium, indigo, dried fish and leather, Upper Sindh sending dry grains, ghi, cotton and indigo to Lower Sindh and receiving rice, fish, leather goods and arms (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 100–101, folios 91–92).

The people were fewer than in Sorley's day. "In the Moghul and Kalhoro days Sind did not support one quarter of the population it now supports", he writes (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 118, folio 109). Del Hoste calculated in 1832 that the population was 850,000, about a quarter of them Hindus, and Burnes in 1828 put it at about one million (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 126, folio 117). Between 1740 and 1800, the second figure imperfectly printed, the chief concentration of population shifted from Thatta to Shikarpur, Hyderabad and Karachi; the Commissioner in Sindh in 1847 put the population at 1,274,732 on 48,000 square miles, both figures imperfectly printed, and in Mughal and Kalhora days, Sorley judges, it cannot have exceeded one million (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 127, folio 118). "The society was an agricultural and pastoral one with cottage industry well-diffused and with town-life, as we understand it today, concentrated in Tatta only" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 121, folio 112).

The village, in Sorley's account, changed little. The agricultural and pastoral population lived in huts that could be put up and moved with the floods, and nineteenth century accounts "accord very much with present-day conditions in agricultural and pastoral areas in Sind" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 123, folio 114). The Talpurs, like true Baluchis, set little store by magnificent buildings, and Baluch influence was, he thinks, partly responsible for a decline in the standard of building (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 123–124, folios 114–115). Of von Orlich's picture of the clay houses of Lower Sindh in 1842 he writes that "All who are acquainted with Lower Sind today will recognize the truthfulness of this picture" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 124, folio 115). Wind scoops are "still in universal use in Lower Sind to catch the south-west wind" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 49, folio 40). "The Sindhi village must in fact have reminded one strongly of the villages of early medieval Europe", he writes, a description still true of the small villages of rural Sindh in his day (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 125, folio 116), with Hindu traders in the bazaar and "the Musulman cultivators" in *landhis* round it (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 126, folio 117).

The lot of the poor, in his view, altered as little as their houses. "The labourers, artisans and peasants were therefore relatively little worse off in Moghul and Kalhoro times than they are today" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 131, folio 122), the moneylender advancing money against a share of the crop, a system that still prevails, and children herding cattle are still one of the chief obstacles to the education of the peasant class (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 132, folio 123). Yet throughout the Mughal and Kalhora periods, he writes, circumstances continually conspired to grind the poor in their poverty (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 135, folio 126).

Government rested on the land. The system at the time of the British occupation, Sorley writes, "did not differ greatly from the system of the Moghuls", the state's share taken in kind and taxation as a whole "extraordinarily comprehensive" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 153–154, folios 144–145). Sindh was then approaching a natural and self-contained economy that argued a great increase in administrative efficiency from 1740 onwards (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 155, folio 146). Local cesses were not abolished under the Kalhora, and under the Talpurs their collection was tightened (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 156, folio 147). Tax farming, he writes, was one of the great curses of Mughal, Kalhora and Talpur rule alike, the farmers being mostly Hindus (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 157, folio 148), and a poll tax, the *sar shumari*, fell on non-Baluchi and Jat Muslims (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 155, folio 146). This taxation, backed by the Baluch soldiery, made the government far from popular with the common man and enriched the ruling house (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 159, folio 150).

Land tenure in Sindh, so far as is known, "has always been feudal" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 163, folio 154). In 1847 jagir and inam land was put at one-tenth of the government land; jagirs were held mostly by Baluch chiefs, and *puttadaris*, in Upper Sindh especially, mostly by Afghan settlers dating from the time of Nadir Shah and earlier (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 164, folio 155). The zamindar's representative was called Mukhadam or Arbab, "a title that has not yet died out in Sind" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 165, folio 156).

## Saints and Pilgrimage

The books open the Sufi history of the province with a traveller. Schimmel's Sindh is a land of mystics from the first: it is the country Hallaj "traversed shortly after 900 to call people to God" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 175, folio 157); "From Gujerat he wandered through Sind, the lower Indus valley, which had been a part of the Muslim Empire since 711. The seeds he sowed there grew in later centuries in the mystical poetry of this province" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 98, folio 67); and "In 905, a mystic like Hallaj traveled extensively throughout Sind and probably discussed theological problems with the sages of this country" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 376, folio 345). Boivin's glossary likewise says that Hallaj visited Sindh in 905 (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 219, folio 206), and he writes that "In Sindh, Hallaj is a very popular figure, and many Sufi poets mention his name in their verses" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 102, folio 89). Abbas writes that "The Sufi mystical traditions came to the subcontinent of Pakistan and India through the Sind province", and that after Muhammad bin Qasim's conquest "a long line of Sufi mystics came to the region through Baluchistan, Mekran, Multan, and Sind itself" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 50, folio not stated). Rizvi, by contrast, finds that the spread of Sufism seen in Transoxiana and Khurasan is not matched in the early province: "no such developments in Sind are recorded" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 127, folio 109).

For Sorley, Sindh drew holy men to it. "The extent to which Sind attracted contemplative and religious-minded persons during the middle age of Islam is well known and is the foundation of its school of Sufi thought" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 173, folio 164). Sufi mysticism, he writes, was "introduced into Sind centuries ago and found there a congenial home" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 236, folio 227), Sufistic thought has been very prominent in Sindh at various times (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 249, folio 240), and "The fact that Sind was the home of several skilled exponents of Sufi thought during the middle ages accounts fully for the mastery of Sufi expression exhibited by Shah Abdul Latif" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 252, folio 243). "From the thirteenth century onwards there was a great immigration into Sind of learned and religious-minded men from the centre of the Muslim world", and "The great ascendancy of the Sayids in Sind doubtless dates from about this time" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 302, folio 293). Most of these Sayyids, he writes, were Shia, and "it is possible that Sind was for a time a congenial home for these immigrants" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 302, folio 293). He adds that "down the centuries Sayids in Sind have produced a number of men famous for their learning and their saintliness" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 212, folio 203).

Sorley is nonetheless cautious about the history of Sufism itself. "It is not possible to trace in any detail the progress of tasawwuf in Sind", he writes, and there is no reliable account of the movement's progress there (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 298, folio 289). "That there has been any consistent progress of Sufism in Sind I do not believe" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 302, folio 293); in his view it has been handed down from age to age by a handful of learned men (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 303, folio 294). Between the thirteenth century, when Lal Shahbaz Qalandar flourished, and the eighteenth, he writes, "there is very little that can be said to be securely established in the way of verified historical fact" about the Sufi poets of Sindh (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 299, folio 290). Shackle gives a different emphasis: as in other regions of South Asia, he writes, a strong Sufi presence was rapidly established across Sindh with the arrival of charismatic figures, often associated with one of the main Sufi orders, such as the Suhrawardis and the Qadiris (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 12, folio not stated).

The landscape of shrines took shape from the thirteenth century. In *Pain and Grace*, from the day that Lal Shahbaz Qalandar settled in Sehwan in the thirteenth century, and from Baha'uddin Zakariya's Suhrawardi centre "at the northernmost border of the country, in Multan", "Sind had produced an immense number of mystics, whose shrines are scattered all through the Indus Valley, and who invented short songs in their mother tongue to express the mysteries of love and union" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 25, folio not stated). Rizvi records the belief that "the Sumirahs of the lower Sind who were Isma'ilis, adopted Sunnism under the influence of the Saiyid", Makhdum-i Jahaniyan, and that "A Jat chief of Sind, later named 'Abdu'llah, is also said to have embraced Islam through a supernatural feat performed by Makhdum Jahaniyan" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 301, folio 279). In the sixteenth century, Schimmel writes, "a group of mystics migrated from Sind to Burhanpur in central India, where they founded a new branch of the Qadiriyya tariqa; they are reported to have used Sindhi verses" in their sessions of *sama'*, and "From that time on the indigenous Sindhi tradition contains innumerable stories of saints, mainly of those who are buried on Makli Hill near Thatta" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384). Bhandari, at the Mughal conquest, records a different kind of devotion. A great plague broke out in those parts, and dervishes dreamt that it came from the rebelliousness of the people of that country, and everyone made vows (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 174).

The orders of the province are named differently in different books. Boivin finds three Sufi orders dominant among the Hindu Sufis of Sindh. The Qadiriyya was "the most influential"; the Qalandariyya, in Sindh, reflects the radiance of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar and the pilgrimage centre of Sehwan Sharif; and the Sohrawardiyya was "the oldest Sufi order that settled in Multan and was predominant in the Indus Valley from the thirteenth century onwards" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 227–228, folios 214–215). "Maybe because of the Sohrawardiyya's hold on Sindh, the Chishtiyya could not spread in Sindh as it did in the rest of South Asia" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 227–228, folios 214–215), and elsewhere he states simply that the Chishtiyya was not widespread in Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 217, folio 204). The Sufis of Sindh he studies were mostly in touch with the Sohrawardiyya and the Qadiriyya (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 139, folio 126), the brotherhoods "that are dominant in the landscape of Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 98, folio 85). The Naqshbandiyya, he writes, has several important centres in the province, especially Lowari Sharif in Southern Sindh, near Badin (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 228, folio 215). Sorley's division is another: "The Sufi fakirs of Sind belonged to the two great orders of Jelali and Jemali darwishes" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 299, folio 290). The Jemali *faqirs* of Sindh belonged to four orders, the Qadiri, the Naqshbandi, the Suhrawardi and the Chishti, and beside them were the Qalandars and the "so-called Sufi beggars who wandered far and wide over Sind taking their toll of the superstitious country people" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 300, folio 291).

Affiliation to an order counted for less than the bond with a master. Boivin adds a caution: "in Sindh, Sufi practice was more based on a personal allegiance with an individual than any affiliation with a Sufi tariqa", Sufis could belong to several orders at once, and the subject "deserves to be studied further in Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 200, folio 187). Shah Abdul Latif, Shackle writes, belonged to one of the many lineages of hereditary Sufi saints established in the countryside of Sindh, "where they have always enjoyed great prestige and power as pīrs or holy men with a special spiritual authority" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 8, folio not stated). Unlike many of the pirs of Sindh, he adds, the poet was not formally affiliated, through a chain of spiritual descent, to any of the great Sufi orders (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 9, folio not stated). Schimmel observes that in Shah Abdul Latif's *Risalo* none of the great Sufi orders is named, "nor any of the sanctuaries to which the inhabitants of Sind flocked at many occasions" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 190, folio 190).

The pir stood at the centre of religious life in the countryside. Sorley writes that the practice of Sufism by the common people "was a very different thing from the refined system of the poets and theologians" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 298, folio 289). He quotes Elliot on the saints of the *Tuhfat al-Kiram*, "so numerous that there is scarcely a village in that priest-ridden country which has not its tombs of holy men" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 298, folio 289). "The Pirs held positions of great personal authority", he writes (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 299, folio 290), and the countryside "became in this way the home of an immense number of these petty imperia" of the pirs (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 176, folio 167). Of the Kalhora's descent from a sainted mendicant, Sorley writes that such a figure has always appealed vividly to the Muslims of the lower Indus valley (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 36, folio 27). The belief that the saints sleep in their tombs is, he writes, "the solid foundation of the extraordinary devotion paid in Sind to the tombs of the deceased saints, pirs and holy men", bound up with the bond of pir and disciple (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 302, folio 293).

Sorley gathers the travellers' testimony to the veneration of holy men. The extent to which they "were reverenced and pampered in Sind" is, in his words, almost beyond belief, and a Persian proverb about Multan, that it consisted of heat, dust, beggars and tombs, applied equally to Sindh; "The social religion of Sind was built up round the reverence of the murid for the murshid" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 166, folio 157). Dr Burnes wrote that "There is no country in Asia or rather on earth that is so perfectly priestridden", the country being said "to possess no fewer than one hundred thousand tombs of saints" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 167, folio 158), and Sir Alexander Burnes that "The mendicants in Sind are more numerous than in any other country in Asia" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 168, folio 159). Abbas writes, without giving a source, that "In the province of Sind alone there are said to be more than a hundred thousand Sufi shrines" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 50, folio not stated). The tendency, Sorley writes, grew more pronounced under the Kalhora; yet "The real form and meaning of Islam in Sind is not to be found in these ebullitions", but "is to be read in the beautiful spiritual poems of Shah Abdul Latif" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 169, folio 160).

The tombs were places of gathering and of trade. "The oriental system of markets and fairs on saints' days at saints' tombs was well established", Sorley writes, so that in this respect the commerce of Sindh followed the old medieval system (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 98, folio 89). Of the superstitious practices he lists, "Examples of all could be cited from Sind" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 177, folio 168). Of the province's trees, Schimmel writes that "still in our day the trees near Sindhi shrines are decorated with many pieces of cloth, preferably red, in the shape of little flags or tiny cradles, which are attached there for the sake of a vow, as it is usual almost everywhere in the Islamic world" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 168, folio 168).

The cult of ʿAbd al-Qadir Gilani runs through the province. "Old Sindhi songs" describe how his "spiritual realm extends from Istanbul to Delhi"; Richard Burton, writing about 1850, "says that there were about a hundred large trees in Sind, all called after Gilani. Each had a pole and a flag hung upon it to fulfill some vow made in adversity or sickness"; sweetmeats were distributed to the poor in the saint's honour, and "the fruit and leaves of the trees were not allowed to be touched even by cattle"; and "Proper names like Ghauth Bakhsh, 'gift of the help,' in Sind and Balochistan point to his popularity" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 279, folio 248). Schimmel finds hymns to Gilani in both Sindh and the Punjab (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, pp. 416–417, folios 385–386), and "One of the first 'geographical' poems in Sindhi was composed in his honor" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 417, folio 386).

The river had its own saint. In Sindh, under the name of Khwajo Khizr, the saint "has always exercised a great power" and has in popular belief become identified with the spirit of the waters and the deity of the river Indus, and "The Daryapanthis of Sind are believers in the sanctity of the River God or the God of Waters, and the belief affects Hindu and Muslim alike" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 301, folio 292). The cult of Khwajo Khizr, Sorley writes elsewhere, was linked with the worship of the Indus and supported by Muslim and Hindu alike (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 177, folio 168), and in Sindh Khizr is called Zinda Pir (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 178, folio 169). Omar Kasmani writes that "In most historical accounts, it is the figure of Udero Lal who is conflated with Khizr in Sindh" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 9), and that "Offerings to the Indus River can still be documented in the wider region of Sindh" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 11).

The piety of the province is also a matter of books. "It is said that some mystics in Sind", and it may be true of other provinces as well, "kept only three books for spiritual nourishment": the Koran, the Mathnawi and the Diwan of Hafiz (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 358, folio 327). "In Sind there were many pious who, by their recitation of the Mathnawi, 'kept the marketplace of union warm', and Rumi's work was quoted by stern Naqshbandis as much as by members of the more emotional orders" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 165, folio 165). At Shah Abdul Latif's own tomb today dervishes perform his music on Thursday nights, "and the melodies are well known all over Sind" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 421, folio 390).

It is a matter of women too. "The province of Sind, generally famous for its saint worship, is particularly rich in legends connected with women saints", and Schimmel quotes Burton: "To the credit of the Sindhi it must be said that they do not refuse to admit the religious merits of the softer sex"; Burton speaks of the *faqirani*, the female *faqir*, of whom "a few of them occasionally rise to the high rank of a murshid" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 464, folio 433). His remarks on Bibi Fatima Hajram, a *hafiza*, are corroborated by the many books Sindhi scholars have devoted to the religious life of their country, and in Sindh, as elsewhere, there are whole groups of women saints, such as the Seven Chaste and the veiled women in the rank of the *abdal* (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 465, folio 434). Schimmel writes in an annotation that she has "learned more about Islamic piety from women in Anatolia and Sind than from most of my learned colleagues or from sophisticated books" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 281, folio 263), and that in Sindh women often use endearing diminutives in addressing the saints, with poems in honour of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar of Sehwan and, in more recent times, verses devoted to the Pir Pagaro (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 287, folio 269).

Women's singing at the shrines has its own geography. Abbas's informant Naseer Mirza told her that the *lotevali* gypsy women sang only at the shrine of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar, "certainly, that is the only place in the Sind now where the loṭevālīs come", and that he had seen no such tradition at Bhit Shah, at Sachal Sarmast's shrine, at Rikhail Shah's or at any other Sufi *dargah* in Sindh (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 61, folio not stated). Asked whether it is more a tradition of Sindh that women sing at Sufi shrines, the singer Reshma said that it was so at the shrines, less so in Rajasthan, but above all at the Lal Shahbaz Qalandar shrine (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 60, folio not stated).

Sindh was also crossed by Hindu pilgrims and renunciants. Three of the *Risalo*'s surs, Shackle writes, are devoted to "the wandering yogis who traversed Sindh during their pilgrimage to the shrine of the goddess at Hinglaj in Balochistan", and as a young man Shah Abdul Latif is believed to have spent time with them (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 20, folio not stated). Sur 18, *Ramakali*, praises the groups of Hindu yogis "who wandered from one place to another in Sindh or beyond as ideal practitioners of the spiritual life" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 334, folio not stated). The *Risalo*'s many references to places in and around Sindh, Shackle adds, support the claim that the poet travelled widely as a young man (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 10, folio not stated). The Nathpanthis, known locally as Verangins or Berangins, played a role "in many forms" in the province (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 77, folio 64), and Shah Abdul Latif's vocabulary for the yogis shows that they were "an important religious community in eighteenth-century Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 92, folio 79). The name *sami* is generally given in Sindh to a group of wandering *faqirs* (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 77, folio 64). Raja Bharthari, who renounced his throne and made a hermitage at Sehwan, "is also known as Raja Virag by Hindus of Sindh" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 15).

Schaflechner, writing of the goddess temple of Hinglaj, sees the same landscape from the side of pilgrimage: "The south of Pakistan, and especially Sindh, offers a whole range of salvific spaces that are frequently visited by different religious communities and form a network of pilgrimage routes or ritual journeys" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 107, folio not stated). Legends linking Shah Abdul Latif and the Goddess, he writes, "connect Hindu and Muslim sacred spaces in Sindh and show that imagined landscapes do not necessarily have to be separated using clear-cut religious taxonomies" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 109, folio not stated). Most of the visitors to the festival he studied are Kohlis, Menghwar and Bhil, which, he observes, reflects the Hindu population of Sindh as a whole (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 154, folio not stated). The deified heroes most popular in Sindh were Rama Pir, Pithoro Pir, Pabuji and Guga Pir (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 42–43, folios 29–30); Ramdeo Pir is still known in the province as Rama Pir and has today been taken into the temples of the pan Indian divinities (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 192, folio 179).

Boivin sorts the ways in which the Hindus of Sindh are related to Sufism into three kinds, each with an example. The most common is a Sufi tomb visited by both Muslims and Hindus, such as Bhit Shah; also relatively common is a saint with a double identity, Muslim and Hindu, such as Udero Lal; and the third is a Sufi *darbar* managed by Hindus, such as that of Pithoro Pir (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 177–178, folios 164–165). "In Sindh, we already know that a number of sacred figures have a dual religious identity, Muslim and Hindu. The Muslim identity is always related to Sufism, while the Hindu side usually belongs to the Nathpanth" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 187, folio 174). Hindus have been coming to the Sufi mausoleums of Sindh since the nineteenth century (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 183, folio 170). "The large majority of the Hindus used to pay a visit to the Sufi shrines, but does it mean they are Sufi? Of course not. It does mean that, for them, the Sufis are a part of their religious legacy" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 192–193, folios 179–180). Among the Muslim disciples of Pithoro Pir in Sindh are Sindhis such as Sammas and Panhwars and Baluchis such as the Maris and the Legharis, and "The disciples of Pithoro Pir mix in Sindh", whereas in Kutch the untouchable disciples worship separately (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 191, folio 178). The Pithoro Pir case shows "the variety of the negotiations between Sufism and Hinduism in Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 192, folio 179).

The holy places of the province, in Boivin's account, are its *darbars*. In Sindh another Persian word was mostly used, *darbar*, and "In Sindh, the darbar was the sacred territory in which the Sufi culture was at work"; the term was "used by the Sikhs, the Muslims and the Hindus" alike (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 37–38, folios 24–25). Colonial rule, he argues, brought "a renewal of religiosity in relation to the spread of a composite middle class. It was marked by the emergence of the darbars, spreading a darbar culture" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 64, folio 51). Some *darbars* of the Nanakpanth had been founded in the late eighteenth century, but "there was a renaissance of the Nanakpanthi darbars in the second half of the nineteenth century" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 53–54, folios 40–41), and in his general conclusion he speaks of "the building of many new Nanakpanthi darbars all over Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 199, folio 186). Two of the Hindu Sufis he studies came from Northern Sindh, from Shikarpur and Rohri, and he takes this as a possible clue that "the devotional centres of Sindh had shifted north sometime after the British conquest" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 60, folio 47). Guru Nanak's own passage is remembered as well. For Haroon Khalid, Iqbal Qaiser's *Historical Sikh Shrines of Pakistan* identifies a temple at Clifton as "the temple which Nanak had visited on his tour of Sindh" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 217, folio 191), and tradition has it that "Nanak visited Uch Sharif on his way to Sindh, towards the end of his travels, after he had already visited the East, South, North and West" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 278, folio 252).

Jhulelal has a geography of his own in the province. Jhulelal was already worshipped by some groups among the Hindus of Sindh, though most of them were Nanakpanthis (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 158, folio 145). Before Partition his temples in Sindh were built with a dome, as were some Sikh *samadhis*, and his priests, the Thakurs, lived in several cities of the province before migrating to India from 1947 onwards (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 211, folio 198). In colonial Sindh there was an attempt to create a centralised path devoted to Jhulelal, then known as Amar Lal or Udero Lal, with a meeting of several Hindu *panchayats* of Sindh held at Udero Lal (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 53–54, folios 40–41). In April 2016 Boivin and a team toured Southern Sindh to map the shrines devoted to Jhulelal there (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 184, folio 171), and he places Thano Bula Khan in the Kohistan, "the western part of Sindh that stands close to Baluchistan" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 184, folio 171).

Seen from India, the province remains the source of holiness, and its forms of devotion mark it off from the Sindhi *darbars* that migrants founded there. Those Indian *darbars* "are not centred on tombs, unlike those of Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 131, folio 118), and in them the photographic portrait carries the sacredness of the space in place of the tomb, "as is the case in the dargahs of Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 132, folio 119). The *shahnai* is very common in any religious ritual performed in Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 145, folio 132), and in India some typical Sufi rituals performed in Sindh, such as the *alam* and the *dhamal*, have been suppressed (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 152, folio 139). The *bayragins*, as relics, are in Sindh, "once again irrespective of religious belonging", the mark of renunciation (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 120, folio 107). The *darbar* of Rochaldas is made a territory extending from an original sacred territory in Sindh, the holy city of Sehwan Sharif, and invoking Jhok Sharif builds "a network of sacred territories which are all located in Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 141, folio 128). The *mehndi* of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar keeps a direct ritual link "with the lost territory of Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 147, folio 134), and the living link is "with the Master who stays in Sindh, nowadays in Pakistan" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 203, folio 190). Following Thapan, Boivin observes that the spiritual masters, the *sajjada nashins* or *pirs*, of Daraza, Tando Jahaniya and Jhok Sharif are still in Sindh, Pakistan (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 27, folio 14).

## Communities

Eaton gives the long view of how the province became Muslim. Sindh is one of the western "wings of India that became Muslim-majority regions", where, from the fourteenth century through the Mughal period, "the growth of Muslim societies correlated with the adoption of sedentary agriculture", in a frontier society not yet deeply penetrated by Brahmanical values, and where "shrines of great Sufis attracted and integrated pre-agrarian and non-Muslim pastoral clans" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 48, folio 36). He follows Irfan Habib on the Jats, pastoralists who "had been moving up from Sind into the Multan area between the seventh and eleventh centuries": Hsuan Tsang wrote of river groups in Sindh who "gave themselves exclusively to tending cattle and from this derive their livelihood" and "have no masters", and the Chach-nama "styled these groups 'Jatts', located them in the wastes of the Indus valley in Sind, and noted an absence among them of social hierarchy" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 224, folio 212). Eaton sums this up as the Jats' "migration north from Sind to Punjab" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 227, folio 215).

In seventeenth and eighteenth century Sindh, Sorley writes, "a great gulf yawned between Islam's ideal and the practical working" of Islamic belief, in a Muslim state that struggled to semi-independence in the mid-eighteenth century (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 172, folio 163). It was a state with a population "composed for the most part of converted Jats"; "The majority of the population were followers of the Sunnat. But very influential sections held Shia tenets" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 172, folio 163). The Sayyids, he writes, were nearly all Shias, and "The Sindhi is by nature an easy-going being, not given to religious subtlety" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 173, folio 164). The mass conversions, he argues, broke down the clear distinction between Sunni and Shia and left the Islam of the common people much influenced by Hindu predilections, with "Hindus visiting Mussulman shrines and making votive offerings" and "the general easy-going character of Sind orthodoxy" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 175, folio 166). He traces what he calls the debasement of Islam to the popular religion of the indigenous Sindhis, sometimes called Jats, who were the majority of the population (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 178, folio 169). From a simple conviction of the tenets of a tolerant Sufism, he writes, flowed a Sufi school of thought, a more pantheistic conception of Islamic doctrine than elsewhere, "an absence of bitterness between Sunni and Shia", and a kind of rapprochement between the deeper mystical ideas of Muslim and Hindu thought (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 225, folio 216).

The books see Sindh as a province where traditions met. Schimmel writes that Hindu sharing in the Muslim orders is "more visible in Sind than in the Punjab": "Hindu writers wrote mystical poems in which they used the imagery of the Muslims; they composed poems in honor of the Prophet" and even wrote *taʿziya* for the Muharram mourning of the Shia community of Sindh, or devoted ballads to the fate of famous Sufi martyrs (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 418, folio 387). In *Pain and Grace* she warns against taking Makhdum Abdur Rahim Girhori as typical of Sindhi Sufism: on the whole there was, in H. T. Sorley's words, "a common basis of understanding, by Muslim and Hindu alike, of the mystical message of Islam"; Sorley also found in eighteenth century Sindh an "almost incredible veneration for sayyids as such and a great accretion of superstitious practices in the belief of the multitude"; the understanding between the communities "may perhaps have been facilitated by the activities of the Ismaili missionaries who came to Sind in the 14th and 15th centuries"; "neither Hindus nor Muslims are orthodox in Sind", and still in the 1920s there were "numerous Hindus and amongst them some of the best brains of Sind, old and new, who are Sufi by religion"; "a number of Hindus became devoted disciples of Muslim saints" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 28, folio not stated). Shah Abdul Latif, "as did his Panjabi contemporaries and his successors in Sind, accepted the Hindus who were striving on the way to perfection as true pilgrims in the eternal Light" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 267, folio 267).

The books divide the society of the province in different ways. Bhandari maps the peoples of his *suba* by its landscape: north of a line running from Multan and Uch to Thatta and Kech Makran lie hills of hard rock, home of the Baluchis and some Afghans; to the south, from Uch to Gujarat, are hills of sand and the Bhati, whose chief has his seat at Jaisalmer, and other Rajput tribes; and from Bhakkar to Nasirpur and Umarkot live the Sabuda, the name uncertain in the printed text, and the Jareja (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529). Sorley quotes Pottinger's division of the population into three classes: the privileged, Amirs, chiefs, Baluchis, Sayyids and religious mendicants; the pastoral, agricultural and other Muslim tribes who made up the majority; and the Hindus, tyrannised over in the most infamous and open manner (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 170, folio 161). Boivin's picture of the society of the province in 1843 divides Muslim society along ethnic lines between Ashrafs and Ajlafs (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 38–41, folios 25–28). The Baluchis stood at the top; the Sayyids were mostly landowners, *zamindars*, but were also *pirs*; and "The pirs of Sindh play the role of mediators between the tribes, and between the rulers and the governed" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 38–41, folios 25–28). Apart from a few groups of Afghans and Pathans in the northwest, the bulk of the Muslims were Sindhis, most of them landless farmers, *haris*; the trading Muslims were the Memon, the Khojah and the Bohrah; and excluded groups such as the Shikaris and the Sidis stood at the bottom (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 38–41, folios 25–28). Burton asserted that Arabic was known only to Muslim scholars (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 38–41, folios 25–28).

The Baluchis, Sorley writes, have been in Sindh for many generations, tribes of them settled in various parts of the country long before the Kalhora called them in during the eighteenth century to stiffen the forces of the prince of Sindh, the moment from which their political importance dates (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 255, folio 246). In 1613 the Baluchis of the lower country had no good reputation for law-abiding habits, and Upper Sindh rather than Lower Sindh drew them most; in his own day most of the Baluchi population was found in the Upper Sind Frontier, Larkana and Sukkur districts, the first still a kind of Baluchi preserve (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 255, folio 246). The modern Baluchi in the Sind of Sorley's day, he writes, "prefers the open fields to a house in a street" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 256, folio 247). Baluchis and Sindhis, he writes, had by then lived together long enough for their poetry to share common ground, and "The Doms visited the villages of Sind wherever there were" Baluchis to listen to them (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 257–258, folios 248–249). The Baluchis "as a class are not at all intolerant", he writes, tracing intolerance in Sindh instead to the Kalhora "as fanatical Muslims" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 152, folio 143). The Jatois, the name imperfectly printed, and the Mazaris are, in his words, "tribes strongly represented in Sind" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 256–257, folios 247–248). Hindu races who came from the Punjab, he adds, form an important element in the population (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 270, folio 261).

The Hindus kept the money and the trade of the province. Even in the 1640s, Sorley writes, "big business was in the hands of Hindu traders", some of whom acquired considerable wealth (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 54, folio 45); the brokers, agents and dalals were mostly Hindus, who could not display opulence (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 100, folio 91), and "In Sind there were many considerable Hindu traders on whom" the Company's agents relied (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 113, folio 104). Under the Kalhora, he writes, "the true Sindhis, the Jats and camelmen, the fishermen and hunters" were fully exploited, and the position of the Hindus deteriorated further; yet they continued to live in Sindh, advancing money to the zamindars, financing trade, farming the revenue and owning all the petty shops in the villages (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 171, folio 162). Shackle writes of the long-distance sea voyages "annually undertaken by the Hindu traders of Sindh to locations like Aden, Gujarat, or Lanka" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 16, folio not stated), glosses Aden as "The port in Yemen that was a regular destination for traders from Sindh", and observes that Portuguese pirates had an evil reputation in the seas around Sindh (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 323, folio not stated). He writes that it was the custom for the wives of the Hindu traders of Sindh to make offerings to the water deity for their husbands' safe return, that one distant place was famous for its wealth, "which attracted sailors from distant Sindh", and that the Hindu festival of lamps in the autumn marked the beginning of the trading season (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 324, folio not stated).

Among the Hindus, Boivin writes, British knowledge was mostly limited to the mercantile castes, from which arose two points, "the absence of a real system of castes and the small role played by the Brahmans" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 40–41, folios 27–28). The Saraswat Brahmans accepted food from the Lohanas, who made up half of the whole Hindu population and were divided into Amils and Bhaibands (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 40–41, folios 27–28). In Eastern Sindh the Rajputs had kept their feudal privileges under the Talpurs, and the British restored them in return for loyalty; "The panchayat system prevailed among the Hindus of Sindh. In rural Sindh, the panchayat of the Lohanas could have authority over other castes" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 40–41, folios 27–28). The Sodhas, the dominant Rajput group, came by their own tradition from Ujjain in Malwa in 1226, and their chief, Parmar Sodha, was established as king, *rano*, at Umarkot; most became landowners and farmers, and the Gazetteer of 1907 describes them as worshippers of Shiva (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 191, folio 178). "Contrary to popular belief, there were many untouchables in Sindh", among them Bhils or Kohlis and the Menghwar, mostly settled around Umarkot (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 40, folio 27). The printed sentence on whether these peoples were long established in the east of the province, "the ultimate extension of the great Thar Desert", reads that "it is certain whether" they were, which seems to have lost a negative; the first census reports that many had come from Gujarat or Rajasthan between 1843 and 1872, most worked in agriculture, and crafts were largely in Muslim hands (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 40, folio 27).

Schaflechner gives the Lohanas the same central place, through older witnesses. Burton wrote in 1851 that a description of the Lohanas "will be a general portrait of the Sindh Hindoos", and McMurdo that "The Lowannas or Lohannas compose the great body of Hindus in Sindh"; over the past few centuries the community absorbed many other groups and clans and now takes in a large share of the upper classes of Pakistani Hindus (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 79, folio not stated). Thakur, too, calls them "the general body of the Sind Hindus" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 80, folio not stated). Zahid-Khan explains the merging by the small number of non-Muslim Sindhis drawing together against the majority, and around the eighteenth or nineteenth century the merchant groups of Sindh divided: Lohanas who worked for the local rulers became Amils, at first 10 to 15 per cent of them, and those who went on trading were Bhaibands (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 81, folio not stated). In Boivin's account the Amils were "probably from the eighteenth century onwards" in the administration (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 44, folio 31), a subcaste of the Lohanas who became the official scribes of the Kalhoras and the Talpurs (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 214, folio 201). Yet "Due to the heterogeneity of the Lohanas in Sindh, it is not possible to make any universal claim for the whole community" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 216, folio not stated). "The Lohanas, once the dominant Hindu community in pre-partition Sindh, are still mostly involved in trade", and many of them, the Amils especially, "have played a leading role in integrating Sufism as a main component of Sindhi identity" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 193, folio 180).

Two paths dominated among the Lohanas and related merchant groups, the Nanakpanth and the Daryapanth (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 42–43, folios 29–30). The Daryapanth, "in all probability the oldest of the two", rests on the deification of the Indus and is served by a priestly caste, the Thakurs; the Lohanas practised animal sacrifice at Navratri and for Khetrapal, while the Rajputs followed neither path (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 42–43, folios 29–30). The colour saffron, which in Sindh corresponds to a bright red, is for the Daryapanthis a symbol both of religion and of marriage (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 181, folio 168). "In nineteenth-century Sindh, the Nanakpanth, or 'the path of Nanak', was probably the dominant religious path among the Hindu Sindhis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 90, folio 77), and many Nanakpanthis in Sindh belonged to a subgroup known as the Udasis (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 53, folio 40). After the Singh Sabha reforms, "The Sindhi Nanakpanthis were thus not included in the Sikh community, and they identified themselves as being a Hindu religious sect" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 200, folio 187). Schaflechner shows the same blurring in the census: many Lohanas in Sindh followed Guru Nanak's teachings, so that in the 1881 census Lohanas from Shikarpur and Hyderabad were counted as Sikhs, and ten years later, in 1891, as Hindus (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 80, folio not stated). Today, he writes, many middle and upper class Hindus in Sindh follow the Nanakpanth (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 257, folio not stated), and it is common to cover one's head before entering a *tikana*, the temple holding the Guru Granth Sahib (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 286, folio not stated).

Belonging in the province was long a matter of locality rather than creed. Burton wrote that certain populations of Sindh could be counted neither Muslim nor Hindu, Raikes spoke of peoples recognised by neither, and this category, Boivin argues, matched the creed of the Sufis of Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 19–20, folios 6–7). "As Richard Burton noted as early as 1851, it is indeed the worship of the intercessors that constitutes the common religious idiom of Hindus and Muslims" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 51, folio 38). In rural Sindh Muslims worshipped the smallpox goddess Sitala when epidemics struck the village, and "In the religiosity of Sindh, the devotional relationship of a follower with a spiritual Master was prevalent, rather than the focus being on affiliation to an institutionalized religion" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 51, folio 38). The colonial step changed this: because of new factors such as the census, people had more and more to identify themselves by their affiliation to the great religions, where before they had identified themselves "according to their locality, caste and ethnicity" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 23, folio 10). The Sindhis of the late nineteenth century were increasingly inclined to name themselves by a religion the colonial power would recognise (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 51, folio 38), and the migrants, coming from a Muslim dominated region, identified more and more as Hindu in the later censuses (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 33, folio 20). The colonial order and Partition changed the balance in another way too. It was the Sindhi Hindus, Shackle writes, who were first drawn to participate most actively in the colonial system, much of the new secondary literature being "the work of Hindu scholars", a situation that lasted until the mass emigration of the Hindu population from Pakistan to India after independence in 1947 (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, pp. 12–13, folio not stated).

The Hindu Sufis were the meeting point. Narsain, as Boivin reports him, "clearly states that the integration of Sufism inside of the religious environment of the Hindu Sindhis dates back to the nineteenth century, and that the main actors involved were the Amils, the literary class" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 27, folio 14). Since the Amils were the bulk of the literati in Sindh, they were also instrumental in bringing Sufism into the religion of the local Hindus (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 45, folio 32). Boivin explains why Hindus took Muslim Sufi masters by a "cultural islamization" working beyond religious affiliation, to which the Hindus were surrendering without knowing it, and by the fact that "the Muslim Sufi market was far more developed than the Hindu market"; to be a Sufi, for a Hindu, "was to participate fully in the process of building a modern Sindhi province" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 199, folio 186). No formal relation between Sindh and the Hindu branch of the Naqshbandiyya has been found (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 26, folio 13).

Krishna and the Vedanta entered the same meeting. Krishna's worship had developed among the Amils by the late nineteenth century, some of whom accepted the Vallabhachari sect (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 135, folio 122), and Sufis of the period such as Sachal, Qutub Ali Shah and Bedil brought Krishna into their poetry, so that "despite the fact that Sindh was Muslim dominated, the Krishnaite Bhakti was influential among wujudi Sufis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 198, folio 185). In an endnote Boivin reads wall paintings of Krishna in Hindu temples as a possible sign of a Krishnaite revival among the Sindhi Hindus in the nineteenth century (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 213, folio 200). Conversely, "the Muslim Sufis of Sindh were mostly attracted by the Vishnuite school of Hinduism" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 107, folio 94). A main factor in the religious renewal of the province was the Neo-Vedanta of Ramakrishna (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 200, folio 187), and before Partition "there are many stories where businessmen decided to give up all of their worldly goods and activities to become renunciants" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 205, folio 192).

Some communities stood low. The castes of fishermen named in sur 16, *Kamod*, as Gandiri, the first name uncertain in the printed text, Mangar, Me and Muhano, "were traditionally ranked as the lowest of the low in Sindhi society" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 333, folio not stated). Schimmel likewise writes that "The Mohana traditionally belonged to the lowest social strata in Sind, and their women were noted for beauty and not too strict morals" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 175, folio 175). Professional musicians in Sindh, Sorley writes, are drawn mostly from the minstrel class, which is held in low esteem (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 232, folio 223).

Abbas's ethnography names the peoples of the province. "The Kolhis are the indigenous peasant communities of Sind who are sometimes migratory. Usually they are temporary farm laborers in the large feudal holdings. They are non-Muslims, and together with the Bhils they are the native inhabitants of the Sind-Rajasthan continuum" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 46, folio not stated); because Shah Abdul Latif wrote "in the Sindhi vernacular, the Kohli and Bhil male and female peasants can understand and relate to it" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 47, folio not stated). Her glossary calls the Bhils and the Kohlis aboriginal landless roving peasants of Sindh (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 179, folio not stated; Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 184, folio not stated), and an annotation describes them as non-Muslim migratory peasants who work on large landholdings in Sindh and Indian Gujarat (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 200, folio not stated).

The Sidis came to the province from Africa. They "were initially brought as slaves from Africa to work in the subcontinent of Pakistan and India. Although they are now free, some of them are attached to the feudal households in the Sind province of Pakistan. Their native language is Sindhi" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 67–68, folios not stated). Abbas's informant Qalandar Shah said that Africans are present in Sindh in large numbers because of the slave trade, were freed there too when slavery was abolished, and had been coming through the ages (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 69, folio not stated). Abbas writes that "the Sidis in Sind and Kacch claim descent from Hazrat Bilal" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 167, folio not stated); they are also known as Sheedi or Syedi in Sindh and Gujarat (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 188, folio not stated), and their *mugarman* drum is held sacred by the Sidi communities of Sindh, Baluchistan, Rajasthan and Gujarat, who use it in their shrines and rituals (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 185, folio not stated). On a visit to the Sidis of Karachi Mori Abbas was served "fish cooked in curry, a traditional Sindhi lunch eaten with rice and flatbread" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 71, folio not stated).

The common life of the country was shared. "The peasantry of Sind are, and always have been, a happy-go-lucky people fond of laughter and amusement", Sorley writes, fond of conversation and of listening to songs and stories, and keeping the Id and Holi and Diwali with whole-heartedness (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 145, folio 136). Wrestling was as popular then as in his day, when it had a claim to be the most popular sport of the country; Sindh possessed and still possesses a great variety of folk dances; polo does not seem to have been played there (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 148–149, folios 139–140). Intoxication with liquor and drugs, he writes, was undoubtedly a curse of the country (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 146, folio 137), very prevalent everywhere on Burton's evidence (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 147, folio 138).

Kin and custom shape security and the place of women. "In rural Sindh, in particular, the clan or the barādarī serves as a guarantee of security rather than the nation state" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 18, folio not stated). Kasmani records how custom shapes women's place. Evoking the idea of the *niyani*, the little sister in Sindhi society, the husband of one of her companions explained that among locals the greatest conflicts can be resolved if one feuding party can get a *niyani* to intervene for it, so that "Women fakirs were therefore perfect instruments for intercession in his opinion" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 94, folio 77). She cites a study by Lubna Chaudhry finding that in parts of Sindh and Punjab unmarried girls in their teens and early twenties were "most vulnerable", facing "discriminatory food portions within the family and limited access to health care" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 191, folio 174, ch. 2 n. 24).

Some communities carry their histories as legends. In the Brahmakshatriya story, as Schaflechner reports it, Parashurama "arrives in the province of Sindh" to kill Kshatriyas, and "The mighty king Ratnasena, the ruler of Sindh" flees (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 61–62, folio not stated). In the Charan story of Avar, famine drives her family to Sindh, then ruled by Umar Sumra, a recent convert to Islam known as Hamir; the Samma and Bhati Rajput kings, who had long wanted the country, help Avar kill him and then divide Sindh between them; another story has Avar kill fifty two Hun demons and so save the people of Sindh (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 72, folio not stated). The Vagris' own history, he writes, has the community moving back and forth between Sindh and Gujarat for many generations until Partition forced them to settle on one side of the border, though with no written account "the clan's detailed history can only be based on speculation" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 308, folio not stated).

The Hindus of present day Sindh, as Schaflechner and Boivin describe them, are mostly of the lowest castes. Sindh is "home to the majority of Pakistan's Hindus", whom Schaflechner numbers at between 6 and 10 million, an estimate that varies widely by source, and of whom over 90 per cent belong to underprivileged tribes or subcastes such as the Menghwar, the Bhil and the Kohli, "who frequently work as manual laborers for the powerful landowning elite in Sindh" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 13, folio not stated). The estimate comes from the Human Rights Commission of Pakistan in Sindh (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 28, folio not stated, endnote 1), and the share of over 90 per cent from NGOs in the province (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 276, folio not stated). "In Sindh, the Kohli community is mainly a disempowered scheduled caste whose members work as agricultural peasants on the fields owned by Sindh's feudal elite" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 154, folio not stated). Boivin likewise writes that, despite the mass migration, the Sindhi Hindus of Pakistan number several millions, most of them from the scheduled castes, mostly Menghwars, Bhils and Kohlis (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 175, folio 162); some call them Dalits and some even claim they are not Hindu, yet "the Sufi legacy is still alive among the Dalits of Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 194, folio 181). Among the Hindus of Sindh there is now a debate on whether the term Dalit is apt (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 187, folio 174). The way they take in the Sufi legacy depends, he writes, "on the social milieu, the caste system and the urban/rural context" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 194, folio 181).

Khalid's books describe the Hindus and Sikhs of the province today. The majority of the remaining non-Muslims "are Hindus living in the 'faraway' 'peripheral' regions of Southern Punjab, inner Sindh and Baluchistan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 12, folio not stated); "according to the census of 1998, the Hindu minority is the largest minority in the country, with Christians in the second place, most of the Hindus are scattered in Sindh, Baluchistan, and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 87, folio not stated). "The majority of the Hindu community is based in Sindh and Southern Punjab, where they have a proportionate representation from the lower and the upper castes", and there they "are a part of the social fabric of the area", though Khalid says he could not go into the community's experience in these regions in detail (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 233, folio not stated). After the demolition of the Babri Mosque on 6 December 1992, "Hindu communities in Sindh and Quetta were attacked" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 22, folio not stated). Nanakpanthi Sikhs "belong to the areas of South Punjab and Sindh"; their temples in Sindh and Southern Punjab hold portraits of Guru Nanak beside Hindu deities, and "They are found in very large numbers in the province of Sindh" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 197, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* thousands of such Sikhs live in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Punjab and Sindh (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 98, folio 80); the communities who refused the Khalsa and called themselves followers of Guru Nanak "still exist in Sindh and in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. Baptised Sikhs look down upon them", and at Manora Khalid saw them merging into the larger groups of Sikhs (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 230, folio 204). An informant of Khalid's in *In Search of Shiva* speaks of "several shrines in the Multan region and in Sindh where people still practise Hinduism and give it the name of Islam" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 10, ch. 4, folio not stated).

Caste remains a contested matter in the province. Thakur wrote that the caste system "is almost absent in Sind", a view Schaflechner links to the assimilating role of the Lohanas and to nationalist portraits of Sindh as a place of religious harmony; devoted Sindhi Hindu nationalists told him caste discrimination exists today only in India or in Pakistan's other provinces, while "Many upper-caste Sindhi Hindus often deny or ignore acts of segregation", which Dalit organisations fiercely oppose (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 277–278, folio not stated). Boivin, too, records the frequent claim that there was no caste system in Sindh, or no rigid one, because the Brahmans were so few, and allows that Sindh may have had a social fabric of its own, though colonial rule renewed it deeply there as everywhere in India (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 36–37, folio not stated / folio 24). Burton records the unusual use in Sindh of the term Vaishiya, for operatives and mechanics as against merchants and shopkeepers (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 98–99, folio not stated, endnote 16), and says that the Saraswat Brahmans were devoted to the Devi, "who, in most areas of Sindh, was called Hinglaj" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 100, folio not stated, endnote 28).

Rites and formulas mark the communities off and draw them together. Schaflechner sets the reformers' ideas of progress against "low-caste rituals in Sindh such as the sacrifice of animals or the consumption of alcohol as prasād" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 278, folio not stated), and observes that many other Hindu groups in Sindh, and all over Pakistan, sacrifice animals for the fulfilment of wishes or at rites of passage (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 310, folio not stated). Among the Hindus of Sindh the formula that all have one Lord, *Sab ka malik ek hai*, has for some years been very popular and marks a new policy of theirs, and the Hindu *dargah* shows how strongly the middle and upper Hindu classes of Sindh hold to their Sufi legacy (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 186, folio 173; Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 187, folio 174).

## Language, Poetry and Music

For Boivin poetry is the heart of the province's religion: "Sufi poetry is the backbone of Sufism in Sindh, including for the Hindu Sufis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 26, folio 13). The books agree that the Sufis made Sindhi a literary language. Schimmel writes: "Sind, as other countries, had, and still has, a big treasure of folk poetry, ballads, and working songs; folk tales are located in the different parts of the country, be it the hill slopes of Balochistan, the desert region of Thar Parkar, the fertile plains that were watered by the Indus, or the shifting creeks near the Indus mouth. It seems that in Sind, as in other parts of the Muslim world, the Sufis are responsible for the development of the spoken language into a literary one" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 161, folio 161). She dates native language mystical poetry in Sindh, as in the Punjab, from the fifteenth century (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384), and writes that in Sindh, too, "the 18th century can be called the most important period for the formation of mystical poetry and prose" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, pp. 30–31, folio not stated). Shackle writes that there was already an established culture of vernacular Sufi poetry in Sindh before Shah Abdul Latif, with poets such as Shah Abdul Karim, who died in 1623, and Shah Inat Rizvi, who died in 1711, though it tends to be overshadowed in the literary histories by Shah Abdul Latif's unique reputation (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 9, folio not stated).

Boivin knew before he began that the Hindus of Sindh "had gone to Sufi shrines, and that they shared their passion for Sufi poetry with Muslim Sindhis and others, especially through Shah Abd al-Latif's Shah jo Risalo" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 14, folio not stated). The works of the Sufis of Sindh, known as *kalams*, "constituted the foundations of Sufi culture" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 19–20, folios 6–7), and "In Sindh, save the Naqshbandis, Sufism is dominated by poetry expressing the concept of wahdat-e wujud" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 162, folio 149). The Sufis to whom the Hindus look mostly belong to Upper Sindh, where Siraiki is used for Sufi poetry besides Sindhi, and all belong to the *wujudi* school of the Sufism of Sindh, which leaves out the Naqshbandis (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 108, folio 95). This trend, he writes, "seems to have developed in the nineteenth century, during colonial domination" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 109, folio 96).

The province's religious culture, Boivin argues, was made in its own language. "Manuscripts and early lithographed books provide evidence that the religious culture of Sindh had been vernacularized", and the first phase was usually a translation from Arabic and Persian into Sindhi (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 23, folio 10). In the early eighteenth century Naqshbandi scholars from Lowari Sharif wrote treatises in Sindh showing that the Muslim Sindhis did not know the Quran, wrote paraphrases and then a Sindhi translation of it, and condemned as un-Islamic some rituals they called Hindu or Shia (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 22–23, folios 9–10). Their later treatises led to the conclusion that the Sindhis, even when officially Muslim, did not practise orthodox Islam (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 228, folio 215). "Devotional literature in Sindhi thus formed the basis of a culture that transcended social, religious and ethnic divisions", and it was carried not by the *ulema* or the *pandits* but to the villages by the caste musicians, the Manganhar or *mirasi* (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 52–53, folios 39–40). The Shia devotional poetry of the *marsiya* "is cherished by both Muslims and Hindus, the latter being performers as well as authors" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 42, folio 29).

The province's learned languages were not its spoken one. The five native histories of Sindh, Sorley writes, are all in Persian, "which was the Court language of the ruling houses of the country up to the days of the British conquest" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 24, folio 15). The learned men of Mughal and Kalhora Sindh, though not few, do not make a company imposing in achievement, and they wrote "in Persian and Arabic and not in the language of the countryside" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 217, folio 208). Most of them were Sayyids, Kazis and Mullas, or men concerned with teaching the Quran, and the writers belonged largely to families that had immigrated into Sindh; "They were not Sindhis by birth" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 218–219, folios 209–210). In the days of the Afghans before the Mughals, and earlier, "Sind proved a favourite home for learned men", drawn by the reputation Sindh had gained for its Sufi philosophy, and the age of the Arghuns and Tarkhans was specially notable for the entry of "this kind of intellectual aristocracy", which had "but little effect upon the unlettered cultivator class"; "In this respect Sind was merely typical of Muslim India generally" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 219, folio 210).

Arabic and Urdu left little mark on the province's verse. Arabic, Sorley writes, has always been known and spoken in Sindh by a few learned men, and the Quran "is known by heart by large numbers of devout Muslims in the province", but the effect of Arabic on the poetical literature of Sindh is negligible (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 248, folio 239). "Urdu has never been a language which has taken kindly to Sind soil", and "the literary currents which played in Sind came from Persian and Arabic sources" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 258, folio 249). The Sindhi language itself, in his view, suits "a peace-loving agricultural and pastoral culture elaborated by an industrious and non-martial people" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 120, folio 111). Schimmel, for her part, writes that "The province continued to produce mystics who wrote either in Persian or in their mother tongue" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396).

Learning reached few. Sindh has always had a number of persons called *hafiz*, which does not mean, Sorley writes, that they know Arabic as scholars (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 219, folio 210). There is no contemporary account of the working of its educational system in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, the best description being Burton's, gathered orally (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 220, folio 211). Sindh in Mughal days, he concludes, showed merely the working of the Islamic educational system, and "The great mass of the people remained illiterate" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 222, folio 213). Sindh, he writes, has never been distinguished for learning and could have had few chances in medieval times of keeping anything like a Muslim university (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 224, folio 215). The teaching profession, he writes, does not seem ever to have been held in great respect in Sindh, teachers seldom being paid more than half a rupee a month by each pupil; in the time of the Talpurs Sindh had six madrasas, at Sehwan, at Trippat, at a place north of Sehwan whose printed name cannot be read with certainty, at Matiari, at Mohar or Walhari near Umarkot, and at Chothiyari on the Nara (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 225, folio 216). The Sindhi language "was much neglected as a medium of instruction except by Hindus, who used a non-Arabic script", and conditions were unlikely to have been better under the Mughals and the Kalhora (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 225–226, folios 216–217).

Northern Sindh had a second language of verse. Siraiki, in Boivin's account, is "the Sindhi dialect mostly spoken in Northern Sindh, and especially among the Baluchi population", and many Sindhi Sufi poets, especially from Northern Sindh, composed in several languages (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 171, folio 158); the glossary calls it a language of Northern Sindh and Southern Punjab (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 220, folio 207). Schimmel writes in the same sense that "Many later Sindhi Sufi poets, mainly those of Baloch origin, used also the northern dialect of Sindhi, Siraiki", and that the imagery of the two linguistic areas is closely related, as one would expect among the people of predominantly rural provinces (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 154, folio 154). She finds the mystical literature of Sindh and the Punjab closely alike, with the motif of spinning and weaving "in a cotton-growing country" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, pp. 416–417, folios 385–386). On the road between Bahawalpur and Uch, Khalid found that "the cultures of Punjab and Sindh merge": a Punjab Hotel beside a Sindh Hotel, Siraiki as an amalgamation of the two languages, and the Siraiki of Uch more like Sindhi (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 276, folio 250).

The province's Hindus shared in this poetry. Ajwani held that the Bhakti reached Sindh only in the sixteenth century, because of its isolation, but Boivin finds no evidence in him of the influence of the *sants* on the province's spiritual life (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 90, folio 77). Jotwani, cited in the book as Motwani, called Shah Abdul Latif and Sachal "the late participaters in the Bhakti movement which had a major impact in Sindh through the spread of Guru Nanak's teaching" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 91, folio 78), and the *slokas* of Sami were probably widespread among the Hindus of Northern Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 104, folio 91). Sachal Sarmast had many followers among the Hindu trade castes of Northern Sindh, and some of them took their love of singing his poetry to India when they migrated after Partition (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 150, folio 137). For the Sindhis who left, singing Sindhi Sufi poetry keeps "a link, through language and music, with Sindh as a fatherland" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 205, folio 192).

Above this tradition stands one book. For Sorley, Shah Abdul Latif's *Risalo* "is the only classic which the language of Sind has yet produced in the realm of deeply imaginative literature", its poems held in universal and popular esteem and known in some form to all classes (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 7, folio not stated). Shackle writes that the *Risalo* "is universally acknowledged to be the greatest classic of Sindhi literature in both Sindh itself and other parts of Pakistan, as well as among the Sindhi émigré population in India and the wider diaspora" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 8, folio not stated). The poetry "exercises over Hindus in Sind a spell almost as complete as that exercised by it over Muslims" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 175, folio 166), and its appeal lies "deep in the hearts of all classes of Sind's population, Muslim and Hindu, lettered and unlettered" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 178–179, folios 169–170). The *Risalo*, Sorley says, is understood and loved in Sindh by Hindus as much as by Muslims (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 225, folio 216). The poet's achievement came in days when Sindh was still medieval in outlook, and "the probabilities are all against Sind's producing a second Shah Abdul Latif", though his poems "still bind Sind with a powerful spell of love and admiration" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 215, folio 206). His poetry is "the only gem cast up from the sea of Sind's vernacular literature" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 216, folio 207).

Sorley explains the poem's place by the province's circumstances. The cultural heritage of Sindh, he writes, came to fruition in Shah Abdul Latif's poetry at a time when the Mughal Empire was showing clear signs of its dissolution; the province's peculiar circumstances were the late conversion of most of its people to Islam and the absence of any considerable body of native literature in Sindhi, because its intelligentsia was drawn mostly from the learned and privileged classes, many of them immigrants rather than original inhabitants (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 245, folio 236). During the Muslim domination of Sindh from the fourteenth century onwards, he writes, "the thoughts and religion of the Hindu population received but scant acknowledgement in the literary forms of the day" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 246, folio 237), while the Sufi strain of thought has in Sindh always appealed to Hindus (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 247, folio 238). The poem's stories, in his reading, "are all Hindu in origin. It is plain that most of them relate to the Sind of pre-Islam days" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 269, folio 260); apart from the Punjab and Kutch tales they are stories of Lower Sindh current among the Hindu peoples on the borders of the Thar before their general conversion to Islam, so that the Hindu peoples of Sindh in his day feel that their culture, no less than that of Islam, went into the making of the poem (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 271, folio 262).

Shackle, for his part, writes that the heroines' stories "are loosely set in the pre-Mughal period when Sindh was ruled by the Muslim Rajput dynasties of Sumiros and Samos, but they are not related in detail" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 17, folio not stated), and that other surs are based on male characters "from the heroic Rajput period of Sindhi history" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 21, folio not stated). These legends give the province a heroic past. Shackle glosses Lakho Phulani as "a legendary bandit from the heroic age of Sindh, who is remembered for his fearless attacks on the ruling Jarejo Samos", and Jakhiro as "a Rajput ruler of medieval Sindh famous for his bravery and generosity", whose verses are to be read as an extended panegyric to the Prophet; in a famous episode of these legends, the invasion of the Muslim emperor of Delhi, Sultan Alauddin, who died in 1316, was resisted by Jakhiro, who took under his protection the women of his fellow Rajputs, the Sumiros (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 342, folio not stated).

The great tales of the province are love stories, and its poets made them teach. Mystical poets "in Sind and the Punjab" developed "the art of utilizing simple folk tales as media for mystical instruction" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 154, folio 136), and "In Sind, the range of folk tales utilized for mystical purposes is even wider": the first allusions appear, "however veiled, in Qadi Qadan's verse" in the first half of the sixteenth century, become more obvious in the short verses of Shah Abdul Karim of Bulri, and reach full bloom in the *Risalo* of his great-grandson Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit in the eighteenth; "Since the original tales were known to everyone, he usually begins his poetical renderings with the most dramatic moment" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 171, folio 153). The Lila Chanesar story "can be dated back to the time of Jam Chanesar, one of the Summa rulers in 14th century Sind", and the Mumal Rano tale "originated in Lower Sind some time in the 15th century" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 157, folio 157). "Sohni is one of the favorite folk tales in both Sind and the Panjab" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 156, folio 156), and Abbas writes that the Sohni myth "is sung in Sufi poetry in all the regions of the Sind, the Siraiki-speaking area of the Panjab" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 126–127, folios not stated).

Of Sassi, the annotator of Usborne's Waris Shah writes: "Sassi-Punnoo is a story of old Sind." Sassi belonged to Bhambore, an old city, and Punnoo was a Baluch chief, and the romances of Sohni and Sassi have travelled beyond their first setting to become popular "both in the old Punjab and Sind" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 12, folio 11, note 5). For Abbas the Sassi myth is "told in the lands of Sind and Baluchistan. In the oral traditions of the region the legend is sung with all the emotion that manifests Sassi's grief" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 118, folio not stated), and performers love to sing it in the regional languages and dialects of Sindh, Baluchistan and the Siraiki speaking regions (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 120, folio not stated). Schimmel recalls Sassi's run through the desert at sunset in Shah Abdul Latif's verse as "a scene which can be appreciated by those who know the glowing evenings at the western border of Sind, when all of a sudden, darkness overtakes the wayfarer, and nothing is left but the sounds of a few animals" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 170, folio 170).

The *Risalo* makes a world out of Sindh. It "draws upon a wide-ranging set of interlocking references to the scenery, society, and legends of Sindh to create a whole new imaginative world", Shackle writes (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 15, folio not stated). In sur 22, *Sarang*, "the transformation of the landscape in Sindh and far beyond is interpreted as a manifestation of the universal extent of divine grace" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 21, folio not stated), the monsoon rains being conceived as a manifestation of divine power both in Sindh, where the normally dry countryside is transformed by them, and across the rest of the world (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, pp. 337–338, folio not stated). After a catalogue of the places the rains reach, ending "Some have passed over Umarkot and have made the ground green", the verse, in Shackle's translation, asks God to make Sindh flourish forever, and closes: "Dear friend and sweet beloved, make the whole world prosperous" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 265, folio not stated). That final invocation of God's blessings on Sindh, Shackle writes, is one of the most often quoted verses of the *Risalo* (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 338, folio not stated). Schimmel reads the same sur: the cloud of mercy, rising over the desert of Eastern Sindh, the Thar Parkar, is seen floating from Africa to China, and the poet, coming closer to his home, prays: "My Lord, be kind over dear Sind, / Friend! Sweet darling! You may make the whole world happy!" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 259, folio 259).

The countryside fills the poem. In sur 18, *Ramakali*, the yogi's horn is praised: "There is nothing like it in the north, in Sindh, or in Hind" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 244, folio not stated), and Shackle explains that "Hind denotes the Gangetic region, as opposed to Sindh and the Indus valley" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 336, folio not stated). "The crane (S. kūṉja) comes to Sindh as a winter migrant", he writes in a note to sur 28, *Dahar*, its plaintive cry heard as the pain of separation from the flock (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 341, folio not stated). Sorley too finds the countryside in the poem: "few countries are as rich as Sind is in bird life", and in Sindh the crow is "a harbinger of good tidings" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 266, folio 257). For Schimmel, likewise, "the crow, kang, is the typical messenger bird in Sind, as was the pigeon in high Persian poetry", and the spinning of cotton is "one of the most important occupations of women" and a frequent topic of the poetry of Sindh and the Punjab (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 160, folio 160). Shah Abdul Latif, she writes, "offers in his verse a vivid picture of the conditions of the Sind countryside during the early 18th century"; "The imagery of the swan must have been popular in Sind for a long time", and Lila's swinging bed recalls the "colorful, laquer-work beds" that "are still a specialty of Sind" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 168, folio 168).

Schimmel traces other habits of the province's verse. The complaint that no letter has come "is general among the Sindhis, so that most of the girls who never learned how a pen looks or knew even a single letter always complain that they do not receive letters from their friends", and it occurs often in wedding songs (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 172, folio 172); "In the folk poetry of Sind the heroines always complain that no letter from the beloved has reached them" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 455, folio 424). In the Golden Alphabets words with the same letters were combined in one verse, "since alliteration was a frequently used poetical device, particularly in Sind" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 454, folio 423), and the mystical bards of Sindh and the Punjab knew the tradition of *Ahmad bila mim* (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 451, folio 420). An Arabic saying "was very popular in 18th century Sind": "Affliction is for saintliness the same as are flames for the gold" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 222, folio 222). Schimmel also writes that "Even the poetry of the eighteenth-century mystic" Shah Abdul Latif, in "the remote province of Sind", calls the reader to admire Iblis as the one true lover (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 226, folio 195).

The Prophet is a theme of the province's poets. Poets "in the plains of Sind and the Punjab wrote tender little poems or instructive Golden Alphabets to instil love for him" in the souls of the villagers (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 222, folio 204), and "The great mystical poets of Sind have devoted wonderful songs to the Prophet, who appears as the dawn between the night of created human life and the Divine day" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 223, folio 205). Abbas writes that *virahini* poetry, in which the devotee is a young bride awaiting her bridegroom, who is equated with the Prophet Muhammad, "is also sung in the Sindhi devotional contexts, such as the mauluds that celebrate the Prophet's birth" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 149, folio not stated).

The poet names the province in lament as well as in prayer. In the lament of Marui, in Schimmel's translation, he says that Marui is no more in Malir, nor Hamir; that "Punhun is no longer in Kech", nor is there in Sindh an Amir; and that in the convents there is no Pir (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 235, folio 235). The singer Alan Faqir recited to Abbas the line "Punnu is not in the Kec / Sind is not the amir", which her notes explain to mean that Sindh does not lead the land politically, that it is not in the position of leadership or amirship; they add that these are all Alan Faqir's political interpretations of the female myths (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 156, folio not stated; Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 210–211, folios not stated).

Under British rule, the poem acquired a scholarship and an examination syllabus. The poems, Sorley writes, lived as sung verse in the days of the Kalhora and the Talpurs, but "it was only after the settled days of the British occupation of the country that there was born that type of literary curiosity which calls forth books of comment and criticism"; the literature on the poet owes its origin largely to English education in the eighty years before he wrote, Trumpp having worked in the days when Bartle Frere was Commissioner in Sindh, and the vast growth of general education in Sindh under British rule has spurred the study of the poems (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 233, folio 224). Shackle writes that after 1843 Persian was quite rapidly replaced as the language of education, administration and elite literature by English and by Sindhi, "whose development was actively encouraged by the colonial authorities in Bombay", and that the *Risalo*, a classic that had always appealed to all sections of the Sindhi population, "was pressed into service for the examinations prescribed by the new education syllabus" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, pp. 12–13, folio not stated). Because Sindh was administered from Bombay rather than Calcutta, he adds, Urdu was not installed as the immediate successor of Persian (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 25, folio not stated). Taufiq Rafat's selection reports Asaf Khan's account of the many Sindhi dialects and of how the Hyderabadi dialect became "standard when the British in 1853 asked the Hydrabadi scholars to write the textbooks for the whole of Sind" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 236, folio 216). In colonial Sindh there was also an attempt to impose a script of its own, the Khudawadi (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 53–54, folios 40–41).

Print and the colonial state remade this heritage. "In Sindh, a printing press was brought to Karachi in the 1850s, but the main event in relation to Sufism came with the printing in 1866 of the Shah jo Risalo", which was followed by a rationalisation of the poetry begun by Hindu literati in the 1890s (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 47–48, folios 34–35). Hindu Sindhis played a leading part in publishing Sufi books in late nineteenth century Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 173, folio 160), and Boivin stresses "the key role played by the Hindus of Sindh in the transmission of the Sufi legacy up through partition" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 170, folio 157). For most Sindhi intellectuals before Partition, Hindu or Muslim, the Theosophical Society gave the framework in which the Sufi legacy of Sindh was read anew (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 157, folio 144), and in the late nineteenth century that legacy was built on the *wujudi* ideology, recast in Theosophical terms through its likeness to *advaita vedanta* (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 204, folio 191). After the lectures of the Reverend Bambridge in Karachi, "In Sindh, the lectures led to a revival of religious and theological studies" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 46, folio 33), and Annie Besant was known in Sindh as Basanti Devi (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 47, folio 34).

Shah Abdul Latif, whom Schaflechner dates 1690 to 1751 (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 95), stands at the centre of the province's modern self image. Verkaaik, as Schaflechner reports him, organises Sindhi nationalist narratives around the "Sindhi language, the poetry of [national poet] Shah Abdul Latif, the Indus, the peasant or hari, and above all, Sufism and mysticism", with the link to Mohenjo-Daro besides (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 88, folio not stated). According to Verkaaik it was G. M. Syed who was chiefly responsible for making Shah Abdul Latif the most important poet of Sindh (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 101, folio not stated, endnote 52). Abdul Qadir Mangi writes: "Every Sindhi, of whatever faith he may be, believes in the greatness of Bhitai. From a Kohli Bheel to a Syed, every one considers Bhitai his spiritual guide" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 95, folio not stated). Sindhi nationalists see Shah Abdul Latif as the ideal Sindhi, the perfect example of the tolerant and syncretic history of the province, while more orthodox writers seek to explain away his journey as youthful recklessness (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 95, folio not stated).

Music was everywhere, and it came early. The *Ain-i-Akbari*, Sorley writes, describes the music of Sindh as *kami*, amatory (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 229, folio 220). Whatever the reason for the Chishtiyya's absence, "music appeared very early in the Sufism of Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 227–228, folios 214–215), and Shah Abdul Latif's poetry shows "the importance of music both to him personally and, historically, to the region" and the role of the musician in the society of the region (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 92, folio 79). "Most of the musicians in Sind have been Hindus and not Mussulmans", one reason, Sorley thinks, why the poetry "exercises so strong a spell over the non-Muslim inhabitants of the land" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 231, folio 222). "There is nothing distinctive in the music of Sind", whose instruments are chiefly the ektar, the sitar, the sarangi, the tambur and the pipes and drums used elsewhere in India, yet "Sind has long had a reputation for proficiency in musical execution prevailing amongst all classes" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 232, folio 223). He quotes Crowe: "The Sindians are excessively fond of singing" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 146, folio 137). The Naqshbandis stand apart: "the majority of the Naqshbandis in Sindh are strongly opposed to music and to the singing of devotional songs" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 228, folio 215).

The poetry lives in its singing. Schimmel writes that "It is moving to listen to illiterate folk musicians in remote corners of Sind" repeating the name of Hallaj in ecstatic songs, and to hear them "claim that he was the true lover who paid with his life for his love" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 175, folio 157). The visitor who goes on to Sindh "will enjoy listening to the moving mystical songs" of Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit (1689 to 1752) or the ardent poems of Sachal Sarmast (d. 1826), which are almost as popular today as they were a hundred and fifty years ago (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 414, folio 383). The translator Naveed Alam writes that "Even to this day, most Punjabis and Sindhis are more likely to have heard a kafi than read one" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 15, folio 15). Abbas writes that Shah Abdul Latif and other Sufi poets of the Sindh region composed in the vernacular languages and dialects, in which they sang as roving mendicants within an Islamic framework, and that "For fear of the clergy mystic poetry sung to music remained confined to the minstrel class" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 50, folio not stated). *Sufiana kalam* "is usually sung in the vernacular and is a feature of the shrines in Sind, especially those of Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar and Shah Abdul Latif at Bhit" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 85, folio not stated). "Among musician communities in the Sind, Shah's Risālo is treated with great respect because of its sacred Quranic context, and the book is not allowed to lie around on the floor" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 51, folio not stated); "Since large sections of the Quran are quoted in his Risālo, musicians in Sind hold Shah's written text in the utmost veneration" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 166, folio not stated).

Abbas describes the province's musicians. "In Sind there can be no ritual without the Manganhar, be it a birth, a wedding, or a death. They are the designated community musicians, and each community has its own Manganhars" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 77, folio not stated); "In Sind, the Sidis of African descent and the Manganhar musicians render the ceremonial singing of devotional narratives" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 169, folio not stated). Her glossary calls the Manganhar hereditary professional musicians of Sindh and Rajasthan and gives *mangiari* and *mangti* as names for a female musician there (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 184, folio not stated). "There is evidence of roving minstrels in the Sind-Rajasthan area. Among them are the jogī snake charmers and flute players" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 77, folio not stated).

She records the forms they sing in. "In Sind this form of choir singing is called sūng" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 40, folio not stated), and "The speech and song create ecstasy, which is a very special context of some shrines in Sind" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 55, folio not stated). Sikander Baloch told her that there is a school of thought in Sindhi Sufi poetry, the *sung*, in which the lover sings to win back the estranged beloved, and that this is called *manana* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 63, folio not stated). Baloch and Naseer Mirza claim that Abida Parvin's use of the *dohra* or *bait* within her narrative, a style she took from her male mentors, is unique: "They claim that no other woman musician in the Sind has this style, which is essentially a unique male style" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 55, folio not stated).

Women sing too, often for women. Among the areas of women's participation Abbas lists "Women as singers/musicians/participants of Sufi songs, sometimes even called the faqīrīanī in the Sindhi shrines" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 21, folio not stated). The Sidi, Mohana and Manganhar women attached to feudal households in Sindh told her that they sing devotional Sufi poetry at their mentor's shrine only on special occasions such as his *urs*, that they perform there only before women, and that their female patrons pay them well (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 95, folio not stated). These musicians sing in the Sayyid households of Sindh to women only (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 112, folio not stated), and "Sidi women render ritual musical services in the households in the Sind" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 167, folio not stated). The singer Taj Mastani deals with television stations and with clients "from the affluent feudal households in Sind, who can reach her for ritual ceremonies" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 34, folio not stated). In the Siraiki belt around Multan, Bahawalpur, Mianwali and northern Sindh, "the female voices are again prominent" in the Sufi poetry of the oral traditions (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 148, folio not stated).

Abbas places the province in a wider world of song. "The mystical lore of the region that includes Sind, Baluchistan, and the coastal belt of Iran shares myths, melodies, and linguistic codes", among them "the famous 'Mast Qalandar' dhun" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 47–48, folios not stated); the *dhun* is "a tune reported to be sung all along the Rajasthan, Sind, Baluchistan, and Iran continuum", and "Almost every folksinger in the region can sing it" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 57, folio not stated). She cites Michael Wood's film *In the Footsteps of Alexander*, which shows "the Indus along the Baluchistan coast back through Persia as a route that seems to have been used for centuries" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 210, folio not stated).

Her glossary records the ceremonial music of Sindh. It names *gic*, songs usually sung by women at births and weddings (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 182, folio not stated); *jamalo*, a folk tune and also a dance, and *kapati*, the songs of the cotton spinners (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 183, folio not stated); *olang*, a drum rhythm played at the bridegroom's home (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 186, folio not stated); and *sadmana*, a melody for ceremonial occasions (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 187, folio not stated). It gives *candar* both as a ceremonial Sindhi melody for auspicious occasions and as the wooden castanets that keep rhythm in Sindhi folk music (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 180, folio not stated). It has *haswari*, a ceremonial tune played with drums and bagpipes, which Sindhi musicians claim to play for the pirs and murshids when they appear at festive ceremonies such as a wedding, and *laon*, a Sindhi nuptial song (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 182, folio not stated; Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 184, folio not stated). *Dhammal* is the vigorous ritual dance performed at Sufi shrines in Punjab and Sindh (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 180, folio not stated).

## Shrines and the State

In Sorley's account religion and government were bound together in Sindh for centuries. Since the time of the Sumiros in the thirteenth century, he writes, "the land has been predominantly Muslim in population and the government had a strong theocratic basis with much virtual power in the hands of the priestly class of Sayids" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 23, folio 14). Saints and rulers were bound together in Sindh from early on: Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya's support for Iltutmish's annexation, the governors who sought Makhdum-i Jahaniyan's help, and the ruler of Sindh who became Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus's disciple are told in the history above. The Kalhora's deference to the priestly class "led to the extraordinary reverence for pirs, Sayids and holy men, which may be said to have been the predominant characteristic of eighteenth century Sind" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 163, folio 154). MacMurdo put ecclesiastical expense at one-third of the gross revenue (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 167, folio 158). Of the order under the Kalhora, in which the true Sindhis were exploited and the position of the Hindus grew worse, Sorley writes that a quasi-theocratic state could have taken no other form (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 171, folio 162).

The saints were a treasure of the state and an instrument of it. Diwan Gidumal, "the minister of the Kalhoro princes", offered an invader two bags which he said "contain the most valuable of Sind's gifts; they contain the holy dust from the tombs of numerous saints and Pirs of Sind" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 25, folio not stated). In *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* Schimmel tells the same story as a mark of the country's pride in its mystical heritage: "Did not Diwan Gidumal, the minister, give the invading Nadir Shah (1739) the most precious treasure Sind had to offer, some of the dust of its saints?" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396). Burton, as Sorley quotes him, saw Sufism in Sindh as a power in politics: *tasawwuf* "under the native governments was as formidable a political engine as most of the secret confraternities recorded in history"; "Even among the mild Sindhis a noted Pir" might formerly safely order one of his disciples to murder an enemy; "Yet the native princes encouraged it partly from superstition and partly because the price of every Pir was well known to them" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 299, folio 290).

The law was the law of a Muslim state. "Sind was a Muslim state and in theory justice was administered according to the tenets of the Koran", Sorley writes, though in Mughal and Kalhora days every man was to some extent the defender of his own rights and the police were lamentably insufficient (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 159, folio 150). Collective village responsibility, as nineteenth century officers described it, might apply word for word to the Sindh of Mughal and Kalhora days, and crime, he writes, was comparatively little rife in Sindh, apart from cattle stealing and the wildness of the Baluchi tribes, though the country held many expert thieves and equally expert trackers (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 160–161, folios 151–152). Trial by ordeal was practised, and under the Talpurs there were no regular civil courts (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 161, folio 152). Burnes wrote that as the state religion of Sindh was taken from the Quran, so was its system of jurisprudence; Hindus settled disputes "by panchayats", and the Baluchis seemed generally to take the law into their own hands (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 162, folio 153). Learning, too, was supported through religious property. Sorley finds no evidence that a single educational institution in Sindh owed its origin to any act of an emperor of Delhi (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 221, folio 212), and writes that the chief support of the literary groups came from revenues granted as *inams* to mosques and to the *maktabs* attached to them (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 222, folio 213).

Under British rule the pirs became landlords in the eyes of the state. Robert Rozehnal quotes Katherine Ewing: "British policy, especially in the Punjab and Sind, was to treat them in essentially the same way that landlords and tribal leaders were treated", the hereditary saints, the *sajjada nishin*, being meant (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 33, folio 23). Jamal Malik finds the cult of holy men "particularly evident in the countryside, where major landlords and large-scale land concentrations are crucial features, especially in Punjab and Sindh", where "the local pîr represents the local landlord or is at least closely related to him" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 199, folio 188). Charles Lindholm argues that under indirect rule in South Asia "Sufis were usually equally content to maintain the peace as long as they were allowed to continue their religious routines and keep their estates and authority" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 237, folio 226), and notes as exceptions "the two Hur rebellions against the British in the Sind by the followers of pir Pagara, a landlord, political leader and hereditary head of a Sufi brotherhood who still retains great authority in present-day Pakistan" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, pp. 241–242, folios 230–231). Schimmel writes that "The attitude of some mystical leaders in the lower Indus valley" proved "quite decisive in the political arena": the *hurr*, the Free, the loyal and absolutely obedient disciples of the Pir Pagaro, were gathered first to support Ahmad Brelwi's struggle against the Sikhs between 1829 and 1831 and later joined actively in the struggle against the British; "The hurr even played a minor role in the Indian-Pakistani war in 1971"; and "This was not the first time that mystical leaders in Sind, or in other parts of the Muslim world, were engaged in politics or in social reforms" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 428, folio 397).

The tie between land and shrine reaches the lives of musicians and fakirs. Abbas's glossary defines the *vadera* as a feudal landlord in Sindh (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 189, folio not stated), and the singer Alan Faqir told her that the *vaderas* have disputes with one another and come to the *faqir* for a prayer (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 78, folio not stated). Khalid explains that "Saeen is a title used to refer to a feudal lord or a spiritual leader from lower Punjab and Sindh, where the shrine culture thrives" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated).

The wealth of the shrines has drawn criticism and reform. Schimmel writes that "the majority of the masses continued, and partly still continue, in the veneration of their saints, which is echoed in hundreds of simple folk songs and popular verses. The exaggerated veneration of a saint's tomb has been made the target of some of the best modern Sindhi short stories. The discrepancy between the wealth of the shrines and their guardians and the poverty of the masses indeed provides an impressive theme for social and religious criticism. That holds true not only for Sind, but for other parts of the Muslim world as well" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 429, folio 398). "In Bengal as in Sind, the Naqshbandiyya and some more militant mystically inspired groups tried to curb the overflowing emotionalism expressed in the lovely folk songs" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 432, folio 401; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 433, folio 402). In Khalid's present the reformers come from outside the shrines: Tableeghi preachers "focus on southern Punjab and rural Sindh where syncretistic religion held sway" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 10, ch. 4, folio not stated), and, as Tahir Kamran puts it, in recent years "the focus of the Deoband has been on South Punjab and Sindh where there is an ubiquitous saint and shrine culture" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated).

Partition divided the custodians from many of their followers. After Partition, Boivin writes, "The darbars in Sindh were most of the time preserved with different arrangements regarding their management", often with *gaddi nashins* or followers in India paying the keepers, and "despite the fluctuant diplomatic relations between Pakistan and India, it was always possible to travel across the border" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 68, folio 55). Yet one of his subjects, Molchand, worried after Partition that it had become difficult for Hindu Sindhi Sufis to visit the Sufi places in Sindh, now in Pakistan (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 124, folio 111). In Sindh, he notes, the groups of Sufis and Nanakpanthis had no central organisation ruling over a whole body of followers (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 202, folio 189).

The Pakistani state has shaped which saints the province celebrates. Kasmani writes that "Sindhi publications of the state hardly mention the saint or the shrine of Sehwan until the late 1960s", while "other saints of Sindh (especially Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai, Sachal Sarmast, and Shah Inayat) were regularly featured during this time, and their annual fairs were marked by special editions. Only from the 1960s onward do Urdu and Sindhi newspapers offer detailed reportage on the annual fair of Sehwan" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 70, folio 53). The publications she means are "two prominent state-run journals, Nayin Zindagi and Mehran, published by the Information Department, Ministry of Information and Broadcasting, Sindh, and the Sindhi Adabi Board, respectively" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 189, folio 172, ch. 1 n. 28), and today "State institutions like the Department of Culture or the Sindhi Adabi Board routinely publish books and brochures dedicated to the saint's life as well as to Sehwan's history and heritage" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 20). Rozehnal records the Sufi Shaykh Wahid Bakhsh, who died in 1995, warning against nationalist slogans, "Sindhu Desh", the ethnic Sindhis' country, among them: "They do not realize that four smaller countries, which they wish to create, will be swallowed by India overnight"; in Rozehnal's reading, "In Wahid Bakhsh's view, the homegrown divisions of ethnic rivalry are as destructive as any external enemy" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 127, folio 118).

The modern state appears in Schaflechner's fieldwork through representation and money. The Sindh province, he notes, "has 9 minority seats out of 168" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 28, folio not stated, endnote 5). Government funding for the places of minority communities shows in a wave of temple restorations across Sindh within the past decade: "While in Sindh, I visited many temples that were renovated within the past decade, coinciding with changes in the political landscape" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 16, folio not stated). He also records how violence against a temple was settled outside the state: in one of the rumours about the attack on a temple at Larkana in March 2014, told him by NGO friends in Hyderabad, "According to tradition in Sindh, the culprits took the Quran and asked the Hindu family for remission" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 17, folio not stated). The instability of the country and "the feudal environment in Sindh", he suggests, have kept scholars away from the province (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 13, folio not stated).

Boivin sees a growing threat. "A possible explanation is that the Hindu population of Pakistan, including Sindh, is more and more under the threat of the radical Muslims of the country" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 127, folio 114). "Despite the islamization, though, and despite the spread of Islamist terrorism in Sindh as in other parts of Pakistan, I argue that the Sufi culture still pervades Sindhi society", and he aims to balance the view that the integrating role of Sufism is weakening in Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 177, folio 164). He is plain that Sufism does not of itself mean tolerance: "the most active people working for the conversion of young Hindu girls to Islam are well-known Sufi pirs" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 177, folio 164), and "the Sufi dargahs are now targets of Islamist terrorism" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 186, folio 173).

## The Province in the Books

Bhandari's description of the *suba* of Thatta, written in the author's own time, is the fullest account of the province as a Mughal unit. It measures two hundred and fifty *kos* in length, from Bhakkar to Kech Makran, and one hundred *kos* in breadth, from the town of Badin to the port of Lahari; it is bounded on the east by Gujarat Ahmadabad, on the west by Kech Makran, on the north by Bhakkar and on the south by the salt sea (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529). Four *sarkars*, Thatta, Siwistan, Nasirpur and Umarkot, make up the *suba*, with fifty seven *mahals* and five ports, and a revenue given as ten crore, forty nine lakh and seventy thousand *dams*, the first two words of the sum uncertain in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 528–529). In this country, he writes, a third share is taken from the cultivator (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530). Bhandari places the province at the edge of Hindustan. In his own day, he writes, this realm adjoins Bengal to the east and the Deccan to the south, and on the west Thatta reaches the salt sea (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 579). In his account of Gujarat, Kutch is a separate country, two hundred and fifty *kos* in length and breadth, with Sindh to its west (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 531). Among his sources he names the *Tarikh-i Bahadurshahi*, on the sultans of Gujarat Ahmadabad and of the *wilayat* of Sind known as Thatta, lands that at various times had passed out of the hands of the throne holders of Delhi and come each under an independent ruler of its own (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 581).

The province, in his eyes, lives on its river. The greatest river, in his words, is the Sind; merchants from Multan and Bhakkar ship their goods down it to Thatta, travellers and even heavy armies go by water, and large armies rarely go by land because of the jungle and the want of water (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529). Most things turn on the boat, and there must be forty thousand boats, large and small (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530). Salt and iron mines are plentiful, and at a distance of six *kos*, the figure uncertain in the printed text, is a quarry of yellow stone that is cut to any size for building (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530). There are wild ass, hare, hog deer, pig and fish; the people live on rice, curds and fish; dried fish is shipped to the ports and fish oil, the word uncertain in the printed text, is used on the boats; and a fish called the *palla*, its name also uncertain in the printed text, comes up from the salt sea into the Sind, is caught in nets, and is found in no other country (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530). Curds keep for four months, there are flowers and many fruits, the mango above all, and small melons grow in the desert (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530).

Bhandari also sets down what was said of the province's liver-eaters, the *jigar khwara*, more famous here than anywhere round about. By a spell, he relates, they take the livers of people, especially of children and especially at mealtimes; some say a trance comes over them; they share the liver with their fellows and the victim dies (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530). They make others like themselves by feeding them a piece and teaching them the spell, and they ride the hyena and bring news from far away (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530). When one is caught, those who know the art cut open the calf of the leg and take out a seed like a pomegranate seed, which is fed to the victim, who recovers (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 529–530). A liver-eater, he reports, will not sink with a millstone tied to the neck, nor burn in fire; to cure one, the experts brand both temples, fill the eyes with salt, hang the person up in a house for forty days, give food without salt and recite spells (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529). Most of them are women, some men; they are found in every land, but more in the lands of Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529).

Sorley dedicated his book "TO THE PEOPLE OF SIND" with a verse of his own on a peace that followed an age of war: "The drums of peacefulness are struck", runs one line, and the next, "Your land a full abundance yields" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 9, folio not stated). For him, eighteenth century Sindh was a survival of an older world. In the days when the verses were composed, he writes, "Islam in Sind preserved the true native form of its characteristic power"; the ideas that then permeated popular thinking, he adds, penetrated the Muslim consciousness much more deeply than in the Sindh of his own day, when that territorial dominion had been lost; and the Muslim India of the Middle Ages, "of which eighteenth century Sind was a relic", recalled in some features medieval Christian Europe (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 213, folio 204). The Sindh from which the poetry could spring, he writes, was more like a "Merrie England" of village self-sufficiency: "It was a society of landholders, petty cultivators, herdsmen, craftsmen and traders, knowing little of the outside world except what could be seen of it at markets and fairs, in the gatherings of strangers at tombs on saints' days or in the company of fellow pilgrims" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 213–214, folios 204–205). Summing up his history, he writes that Sindh in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries "was a land of cultivators and herdsmen, keepers of camels, buffaloes, sheep, goats and oxen, of tombs and Sayids, of dust and heat, of the annual rise and fall of the waters of the Indus but inefficiently controlled by a crude system of irrigation" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 265, folio 256). Of the poem's rustic scenes, he writes, "No one familiar with Sind will dispute how true a portrayal this is of a simple rustic civilization of husbandmen and cattle herds" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 267–268, folios 258–259), and the poems are loved by all classes in Sindh (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 268, folio 259).

Sorley argues with the nineteenth century writers. He holds that "many of the criticisms of Victorian writers are unjust" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 30, folio 21). Burton, he writes, "despised Sind for what he considered its lax Muhammadanism" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 120, folio 111), and Burton's view that Sindh came into British hands in an almost exhausted state cannot be accepted "without considerable qualification" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 143, folio 134). Against such critics he quotes Assistant-Surgeon Heddle in May 1836: "The large masses of the indigenous population are particularly industrious", the merchants of Sindh are active and intelligent, and "The people are orderly and obedient and the laws are respected" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 116–117, folios 107–108). He sets his own judgement that "The peoples of Sind, whether indigenous or immigrant, have never shown any military genius", and that "The people as a whole have always been peaceful and industrious" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 23, folio 14). Lieutenant James, reporting in 1847 on the Chandookah pargana, wrote of Sindh as one continued battlefield, the scene of usurpation, tyranny and bloodshed, "till finally the British bayonet gave the worn-out country peace" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 36–37, folios 27–28).

The word itself is not always the province. Baluch poetry, Sorley writes, has many references to Sindh, but the word so used means not the modern political division of that name but the valley of the Indus, though some places named are actually Sindhi places in the country south of Multan that reaches far down the river towards the sea (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 256, folio 247). One Baluchi bard, as Longworth Dames records him, sings that drunkards "are the young men of the Sindh country", since "there is much water and bhang is cheap" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 256–257, folios 247–248). Schimmel notes that in discussions of Bayezid's master, said to be Abu ʿAli as-Sindi, "i.e., from Sind", the name may point to the lower Indus valley or to a village called Sind close to Bistam, and that even in the first case "not every man from Sind could be expected to know all the intricacies of Hindu monistic philosophy" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 78, folio 47).

For Schimmel, Sindh is above all a mystical country. In 711 the Arabs conquered it, "and this province never ceased to be a center of Islamic studies and a seat of Islamic mysticism" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 6, folio 2). "Sind has always been praised as the country with a particularly strong inclination towards mysticism", and she quotes a Sindhi author, J. Parsram: "Sind has been flattened and has been in a state of negation. This is the cause of the many disadvantages from which it suffers; but it has been also the cause of a great blessing", the blessing being "something of which India is in need", namely the mystical tradition (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, pp. 24–25, folio not stated). In *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* she writes that "The country had always been proud of its mystical heritage" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396). She closes *Pain and Grace* with the province's light: "the villages in Sind are covered with a veil of golden dust; the walls, built of unbaked clay, look like bronze, and the blue and white tiles which cover a lonely saint's tomb match the purity of the blue sky", and the approaches of Dard and Shah Abdul Latif are "as different as are the sunsets in their native provinces" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 263, folio 263). Other voices in the same book are harsher. Schimmel quotes E. Trumpp, in his introduction to Sur Sorathi, on "how much even the strong monotheistic deism of Islam has become modified and corrupted in Sind, as in other parts of India, by its contact with Hinduism" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 158, folio 158), and Sorley on the province's veneration of sayyids and accretion of superstitious practices (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 28, folio not stated).

Later authors of the province described it as a meeting of civilisations. Jethmal Parsram, in *Sind and its Sufis* of 1924, summarised the many religions that had flourished in Sindh, Buddhism, Jainism and Hinduism, to which in more recent times Sikhism and Sufism had been added, and wrote that "there is hardly a country in the whole of Asia, including India, in which the mystic thought of two great civilizations, the Indian and the Arabic-Iranian, is seen as in so beautiful a union as in Sind" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 49, folio 36). Ram Panjwani was already convinced, while still in Sindh, "that Sufism was the core of Sindhi culture, a culture shared by all creeds and social milieus" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 157, folio 144). His preface set out to acquaint the Sindhis "with their past and the glorious cultural heritage of Sind which was inhabited by their forefathers" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 159, folio 146), and his vision of Sindh was that of "a land of the mystic poets, who are humanists as well" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 160, folio 147). When he spoke of the religious culture of Sindh, he meant the spirit of Sufism (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 161, folio 148).

Boivin's own view is that "Sindh hosted a specific shared religious culture resulting from a process of vernacularization, which is not contradictory of the fact that some of its features can also be found in neighbouring provinces" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 37–38, folios 24–25). "The Sufi culture of Sindh was nonetheless the most shared product, but this does not prevent the society from being highly fragmented" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 19, folio 6). He reports Kothari's explanation, which finds the cause in Sindh's place as a frontier, with a constant influx of migrants, far from the main imperial centres (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 28, folio 15), and adds: "It is a fact that Sindh, because of its geographical location both as an isolated and a buffer zone, has hosted many unorthodox versions of normative religions, especially Hinduism and Islam" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 29, folio 16). The impact of British rule was twofold: it objectified Sufism, "enthroning it as the fundamental of Sindhi culture at large", and it renewed religion through a composite middle class (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 64, folio 51). Sindh, like most regions of India, underwent this objectification of the Sufi legacy (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 43–44, folios 30–31), but in his judgement it "had drawn" for centuries "a kind of specific religiosity based on devotion" which placed faith beyond any formal belonging to an institutional religion (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 66, folio not stated). He notes that "In the field of Sindh studies, however, there is no published research related to Sufism and the Hindu Sufis in Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 177–178, folios 164–165).

Schaflechner reads the same idea as a nationalist one. In nationalist writing found "on both sides of the border", Sindh is presented "as a distinct entity lacking any cultural links to other provinces or the state of Pakistan generally" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 87, folio not stated). He quotes K. R. Malkani, a Sindhi Hindu who left Hyderabad after Partition for New Delhi: "Sindh has always been there, Pakistan is a passing show", and "A Sufi Sindh and an Islamic Pakistan cannot coexist"; he calls the book full of rants against Pakistan (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 87, folio not stated). Nationalist writers also blame British rule, which they see as having victimised Sindh; Hussain Soomro writes that the province was once so prosperous that it was said to abound in milk and honey, and in these narratives Pakistan and the British become "the culprits in the case of lost harmony and affluence in Sindh" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 87–88, folio not stated). Sindhi nationalist discourse ties Hinglaj itself to a real Sindh that prospered before outsiders began to suppress the province (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 97, folio not stated), and his chapter plan calls the shrine a hallmark of Sindh's golden past for modern Sindhi nationalism (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 51, folio not stated).

The nationalist case, as Schaflechner presents it, joins the province to Sufism. G. M. Syed, a politician who spent many years in prison for his separatist views, "directly linked the province of Sindh with a tradition of Sufism", found Sufism in its purest form in the province, and took as proof the shrines of Sufi saints that are "to this day, visited by Hindus and Muslims alike" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 88–89, folio not stated). Verkaaik writes that "both Sindhi Hindus and Muslims were influenced by mysticism and religious tolerance. This was the Sindhi essence that both Hindus and Muslims shared" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 89, folio not stated). Ishtiaq Ansari ties Mohenjo-Daro directly to today's Sindhis, claims that the Veda was composed on the shores of the Indus, and holds that Shiva and the Mother Goddess "were already known and honored in the ancient civilization of Sindh" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 93, folio not stated). Badar Abro defines the real Sindh, *asal sindh*, as the valleys between Rohri and eastern Baluchistan where the Sindhis once lived in prosperity, and argues that because Sindh remained under the influence of Sufi mysticism the area is, in his English word, secular, so that its religious places belong to the shared heritage of that real Sindh (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 94, folio not stated). Khadim Soomro writes: "A Sindhi has a special place in human civilization because he stands for good and liberal attitude and behavior" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 96, folio not stated).

Two of the books set limits to this picture. Schaflechner's own verdict is that the common notion among Sindhis today "that in the history of Sindh, there is no religious tension between the two communities" is "merely a nationalist utopian ideal, as Aggarwal (2013) has shown" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 90–91, folio not stated). Shackle's Sindh, for its part, is a land read through its poet, and he warns against reading the poet too narrowly as its voice. Sur 15, *Marui*, he writes, "also allows for a nativist interpretation that exalts the authenticity of the values and landscape of Sindh" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 332, folio not stated), but his introduction cautions against seeing Shah Abdul Latif "too narrowly" as "an authentic spokesman of the Sindhi folk tradition or as an advocate of Sindhi nationalism" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 13, folio not stated).

For Abbas the province is part of a larger cultural region. She speaks of "the Sind-Rajasthan continuum" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 46, folio not stated), of the lore of "the region that includes Sind, Baluchistan, and the coastal belt of Iran" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 47–48, folios not stated), and of the Sassi myth as belonging to "the area currently referred to as a continuum in ethnomusicology" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 156, folio not stated). Her informant Alan Faqir spoke of Sindh as a land invaded again and again, when the Mongols, the Tartars and the Sassanids came, looted and plundered and assaulted, which yet goes on and will not die; "Sindhri is beaten", he said, and yet Sindhri goes on (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 156–157, folios not stated). Her notes explain that Sindhri is a name used for Sindh in the poetic traditions (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 210–211, folios not stated).

For Khalid, Sindh is a region he names but did not travel in for *In Search of Shiva*: "southern Punjab and Sindh are excluded, where there are many such shrines. These shrines also represent similar characteristics as discussed in this book" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 6, Introduction, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* he looks from Manora at "the Makran mountain range, the boundary line between Sindh and Baluchistan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 228, folio 202).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The date of the Arab conquest.** Schimmel and Eaton give 711 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 61, folio 30; Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 30, folio 18); Rizvi has Muhammad bin Qasim begin his advance in 710 and conquer Sindh "between 711 and 712" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 127, folio 109); Chishti has the country of Sindh conquered under Walid between 705 and 715 of the Christian era, both numbers uncertain in the printed text, after earlier attacks under Uthman and a partial conquest under Ali (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 802; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 802–803).
- **Who held Mansura in the tenth century.** Rizvi writes that Mansura, the capital of Sindh, was ruled by the Sumira dynasty, which had accepted the overlordship of the Fatimid Caliphs of Egypt (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 128, folio 110); Schimmel has the Carmathians rule northern Sindh and Multan (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 98, folio 67); Sorley has the early dynasties of Multan and Mansura, under Arab influence, hold Upper and Lower Sindh until nearly the end of the tenth century (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 247, folio 238).
- **Whether Sufism came early to Sindh.** Abbas writes that the Sufi traditions came to the subcontinent through the province of Sindh and that a long line of Sufi mystics followed Muhammad bin Qasim's conquest (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 50, folio not stated); Rizvi writes that "no such developments in Sind are recorded" in the early period and explains the lack of information about Sufis there by slow land routes and Fatimid overlordship (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 127, folio 109; Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 128, folio 110); Schimmel finds the pious of the region interested in the early centuries mainly in hadith and in transmitting Indian learning westward (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 375, folio 344).
- **Who the Sumra were.** Sorley calls them a local dynasty of Rajput origin (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 247, folio 238); the *Tarikh-i-Tahiri*, as he quotes it, calls them a Hindu tribe (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 270, folio 261); Shackle speaks of the Muslim Rajput dynasties of the Sumiros and Samos (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 17, folio not stated).
- **The dates and length of Sumra rule.** Sorley dates the Sumra regime from about 1025 to about 1360 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 174, folio 165); the *Tarikh-i-Tahiri*, as he quotes it, gives the Hijri years 700 to 843, and the *Ain-i-Akbari* a line of thirty-six princes reigning for five hundred years (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 270, folio 261); Rizvi has the Sumira rule Lower Sindh until the middle of the fourteenth century (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 128, folio 110); Bhandari has the Sumra take over from the descendants of Bani Tamim Ansari, who ruled for one hundred and thirty nine years, and keep the government in their family for five hundred years before it passed to the Samma (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 173).
- **When Samma rule began.** Boivin writes that the Sammas officially took power in 1351, while the Sumras kept control of some outlying areas of Sindh such as Umarkot (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 189, folio 176); Bhandari's list has the first Samma ruler, Jam Anzar, reign from the year 735 of the Hijra, itself uncertain in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 172–173).
- **Who ruled Sindh when Firuz Shah of Delhi came against it.** Boivin writes that in 1367 Firuz Shah Tughluq laid siege to Thatta when Jam Juna II controlled Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 189, folio 176); Bhandari sets Firuz Shah's three campaigns against the Jam in the time of Jam Bani Thatta son of Jam Anzar, and makes Jam Junan Anzar's brother and predecessor of Bani Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 173; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 172–173).
- **When the people of Sindh became Muslim.** Sorley in one place says most of the population was converted in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 174, folio 165), with the mass of the common people becoming Muslim under Jam Nizamuddin about A.D. 1461 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 175, folio 166); in another, that conversion came from the twelfth century onwards through the central Asian peoples, the agricultural population and landholding class being almost entirely Muslim by the time of the Lodi Afghans in the sixteenth century (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 248, folio 239). Boivin writes that historical sources show that before the eighteenth century the people who identified themselves as Muslims were few, probably because the name was restricted to the *sayyids* and their dependents (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 22–23, folios 9–10).
- **How the Sufi presence grew in the province.** Shackle writes that it was rapidly established across Sindh with the arrival of charismatic figures of the main orders (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 12, folio not stated); Sorley writes that no reliable account of its progress exists and that he does not believe there was any consistent progress of Sufism in Sindh (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 298, folio 289; Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 302, folio 293).
- **The Chishtiyya in Sindh.** Boivin writes that, maybe because of the Sohrawardiyya's hold on Sindh, the Chishtiyya could not spread there as it did in the rest of South Asia (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 227–228, folios 214–215), and that it was not widespread in Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 217, folio 204); Sorley counts the Chishti among the four orders to which the Jemali *faqirs* of Sindh belonged (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 300, folio 291).
- **The year of the Mughal annexation.** Sorley writes that Akbar added Sindh to the empire in 1592 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 18, folio 9), and his chronological table records the annexation in that year (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 200, folio 191); Schimmel writes that Sindh was part of the Mughal Empire since 1595 (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 25, folio not stated); Bhandari has Mirza Jani Beg come to court with the Khankhanan in the year 1001 of the Hijra, a year uncertain in the printed text, and the country enter the protected dominions of the empire (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 171).
- **The unit Sindh formed under the Mughals.** Sorley makes it part of the Subah of Multan, divided into the sarkars of Bakhar and Tatta (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 31–32, folios 22–23; Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 150, folio 141); Callendar's account of 1775, which he quotes, calls Scindy a province of the Empire (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 61, folio 52); and his chronological table names Aurangzeb governor of Multan and Sindh (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 200, folio 191) and Nur Muhammad Kalhoro Subedar of Sindh (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 201, folio 192). Bhandari knows it as a *suba* of its own, the *suba* of Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530), made up of four *sarkars*, Thatta, Siwistan, Nasirpur and Umarkot (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 528–529), with governors of its own appointed by the emperor (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 105; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 64).
- **The year of Nadir Shah's coming.** Sorley gives the disastrous advent of Nadir Shah as 1737 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 18, folio 9); elsewhere Persian suzerainty follows the invasion of Nadir Shah in 1739 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 35, folio 26); and his chronological table has Nadir Shah invade Sindh in 1740 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 201, folio 192).
- **When Sindh passed under the Afghans.** Sorley gives 1747 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 18, folio 9) and about 1747 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 35, folio 26); his chronological table has Sindh become subject to the Afghan kingdom of Ahmad Shah Durani in 1750 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 201, folio 192).
- **To which invader Divan Gidumal gave the dust of Sindh's saints.** In *Pain and Grace* the gift of two bags holding the holy dust of the tombs of the saints and pirs of Sindh is set at Ahmad Shah Abdali's invasion, which took place in Shah Abdul Latif's lifetime (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 25, folio not stated); in *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* the dust is given to "the invading Nadir Shah (1739)" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396).
- **When the Talpurs came to power.** Schimmel writes that the Talpurs came to power in 1781 and ruled the country until the British conquest of 1843 (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, pp. 30–31, folio not stated); Sorley's chronological table has the civil war between the Kalhora and the Talpurs begin in 1778 and the final establishment of Talpur ascendancy in 1803 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 202, folio 193).
- **Peace under the Talpurs.** Sorley writes that they gave a distracted land peace for forty years (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 40–41, folios 31–32) and that under them Sindh was at peace, at the price of a Baluch minority's tyranny (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 151–152, folios 142–143); Lieutenant James, whom he quotes, saw Sindh as one continued battlefield until the British bayonet gave it peace (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 36–37, folios 27–28).
- **Famine.** Sorley calls Sindh relatively immune from famines (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 143, folio 134; Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 129, folio 120); he also records the famine and plague of 1659 and 1660, of which a Company letter says no famine ever raged worse (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 56, folio 47), and drought, locusts, plague and floods in three successive seasons from 1648 to 1652 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 143, folio 134).
- **The population before the conquest.** Del Hoste put it at 850,000 in 1832 and Burnes at about one million in 1828 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 126, folio 117).
- **The state of Sindh at the British conquest.** Burton held that it came into British hands almost exhausted; Sorley says this cannot be accepted without considerable qualification (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 143, folio 134).
- **The census of 1872.** Within one sentence of Boivin's, the account opens by saying that Sindh's population statistics are not available in the first census of 1872, then gives a population printed as 2,333,527 million and a breakdown by religion (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 38, folio 25).
- **How many Hindus left Sindh at Partition.** Boivin puts them at closer to 0.8 million, if not fewer, and reports a kind of consensus of around a million (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 61, folio 48).
- **Whether the province's history knew religious harmony.** G. M. Syed, as Schaflechner reports him, found Sufism in its purest form in Sindh, proven by shrines visited by Hindus and Muslims alike, and Verkaaik calls mysticism and tolerance the essence Sindhi Hindus and Muslims shared (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 88–89, folio not stated; Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 89, folio not stated); Schaflechner calls the notion that the history of Sindh holds no religious tension between the two communities a nationalist utopian ideal (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 90–91, folio not stated); Boivin, against the trope that Sindh did not suffer Punjab's violence, cites the Sukkur riots and the rising riots from the 1920s (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 61, folio 48; Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 64, folio 51).
- **When Hindus began visiting Sufi shrines.** Boivin writes that Hindus have been coming to the Sufi mausoleums of Sindh since the nineteenth century (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 183, folio 170); Sorley, writing of the Islam of the common people in the Sindh of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, speaks of "Hindus visiting Mussulman shrines and making votive offerings" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 175, folio 166).
- **Caste.** Thakur wrote that the caste system is almost absent in Sindh (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 277–278, folio not stated); Boivin writes that, contrary to popular belief, there were many untouchables in Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 40, folio 27), while allowing that Sindh may have had a social fabric of its own (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 36–37, folio not stated / folio 24).
- **The caste make up of the Hindus of Sindh today.** Schaflechner writes that over 90 per cent of them belong to underprivileged tribes or subcastes (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 13, folio not stated; Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 276, folio not stated), and Boivin that most of them are from the scheduled castes (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 175, folio 162); Khalid writes that the Hindu community of Sindh and Southern Punjab has a proportionate representation from the lower and the upper castes (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 233, folio not stated).
- **The share of the Lohanas among the Hindus.** Boivin gives them half of the whole Hindu population (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 40–41, folios 27–28); McMurdo, as Schaflechner quotes him, calls them the great body of Hindus in Sindh, and Thakur the general body of the Sindh Hindus (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 79, folio not stated; Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 80, folio not stated).
- **The Shikaris and the Dheds.** Burton held that they were neither Hindus nor Muslims, while other authors count the Shikaris as Muslims and the Dheds as Hindus (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 187, folio 174).
- **Shah Abdul Latif's dates.** Schaflechner dates him 1690 to 1751 (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 95); Schimmel gives 1689 to 1752 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 414, folio 383), and so does Sorley (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 28, folio 19).
- **Sindhi literature before Shah Abdul Latif.** Sorley writes of the absence of any considerable body of native literature in Sindhi (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 245, folio 236) and calls the *Risalo* the only classic of the language (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 7, folio not stated); Shackle writes that there was already an established culture of vernacular Sufi poetry in Sindh, overshadowed by Shah Abdul Latif's reputation (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 9, folio not stated); Schimmel dates native language mystical poetry in Sindh from the fifteenth century (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384), and finds the first allusions to the folk tales in Qadi Qadan's verse in the first half of the sixteenth century (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 171, folio 153).
- **The setting of the *Risalo*'s stories.** Sorley holds that they are all Hindu in origin and relate mostly to the Sindh of pre-Islamic days (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 269, folio 260); Shackle writes that the heroines' stories are loosely set in the pre-Mughal period of the Muslim Rajput dynasties of the Sumiros and Samos (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 17, folio not stated).
- **Where the Sassi story belongs.** The annotator of Usborne's Waris Shah calls it "a story of old Sind" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 12, folio 11, note 5); Abbas writes that it "originated in Baluchistan and Sind" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 156, folio not stated).
- **What *sung* means.** Abbas calls *sung* the form of choir singing in Sindh (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 40, folio not stated); her informants Sikander Baloch and Naseer Mirza use it for the practice in which the lover woos the estranged beloved through a serenade on the castanets (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 55, folio not stated); her glossary has a choir that sings at the Sufi shrines in Sindh (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 188, folio not stated); and a note records that a native Sindhi informant corrected the term to mean a choir of mendicants who sing mystical poetry (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 198, folio not stated).
