The material is thin. Of the ten books, only Bhandari's *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* describes the place at any length (its names, a battle, its armies, the three roads to Kabul, its produce and its Afghans). Every other book names Peshawar in one to four passing sentences, and several of the source sections say so themselves. The article is short for that reason and is not padded.

Dashes: there are no em dashes, and no en dash in any prose. Three en dashes remain, all inside page references that the sources print as ranges and that the checker matches character for character: `pp. 504–503` (Bhandari, twice: the Mohmand sentence in the Overview and in Communities) and `pp. 128–129, folios not stated` (Schaflechner). Nankana Sahib, Karachi and Multan do the same. Bhandari's other range, `pp. 502–501` (the three roads), is not cited as a range: the opening sentence carries the two single pages the source gives for the roads themselves, `p. 502; p. 501`. No hyphenated compounds of my own; the only hyphens in the prose are in the names *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* and *Sirr-i dilbaran*. "Hyderabad-Sind" (Ernst and Lawrence) is written "Hyderabad in Sind".

No prose paragraph holds Arabic script (checked by script). There is no verse paragraph.

## Weak material and how I treated it

- **Dhillon, "He visited Kabul before reaching Peshawar" (p. 224, folio 246)**, which the source calls a "Bare itinerary name". Stated as what it is, in The Place in the Books: the retelling "names the city as a stage on the return from Baghdad". The source does not say who "He" is, so the article quotes the sentence and does not name the traveller.
- **Schaflechner, Peshawar "only as the home of robbers inside Abadhut's novel" (pp. 128–129, folios not stated)**, which the source calls "mechanical". Given as what it is, in The Place in the Books.
- **Schimmel's two demonyms.** The sources say neither book discusses Peshawar. They go into The Place in the Books only, as the one way each book uses the word, quoted exactly. The sentence "In both books Peshawari stands beside Hindu and Turk as a distinction that the mystical verse she paraphrases does away with" is my summary of the two quoted lines. It is cited to both and adds no fact, but V1 may want it cut.
- **Bhandari's bare march (p. 201)**, which the source calls a "transit mention". Given as a march: "the court turned toward Peshawar, and from that stage a *farman* of counsel was sent to Hakim Mirza". The source does not name the ruler, so the article does not either.
- **Bhandari's "office mention" (p. 77)**: Dara's robes of honour to the armies of Peshawar and Kabul under Mahabat Khan. Used in History as a seat of command, with nothing added.
- **Chishti, Ranjit Singh's conquest (p. 169)**: "mentioned only as the occasion on which a Qur'an was sent from there to the shrine". The source does not name the shrine, so the article says "to a shrine". See question 2.
- **Rozehnal, the woman disciple (p. 219, folio 211).** The source says she "names no Peshawar shrine", and the article says so. It also follows the source in setting the frontier, where women may walk into the sanctum, against Pakpattan, where they may not.
- **Rozehnal, Shahidullah Faridi "placed there once" (p. 77, folio 67).** The source does not say on what occasion, so the article says only that Rozehnal places him in Peshawar.
- **Khalid's informants.** The Source line says informant names may be pseudonyms, so Prem Gupta and Tarvinder Kaur are introduced as "an informant Khalid calls". Surjeet Singh is given "as Khalid names him", and Diyal "as he gives the name". The source does not say whether Diyal is a man or a woman, so the article does not either.
- **Khalid's band (p. 51)** is "the musical band 'have also come from Peshawar'". The source does not say where the band played, so the article says only "Of a musical band Khalid writes that its members 'have also come from Peshawar'".
- **Tarvinder Kaur's "1971 attacks" (p. 184).** The source does not say where the attacks were, so the article says "After the attacks of 1971".
- **Signpost sentences without a citation** (body only, never in the Overview) introduce what the cited sentences after them say: "Bhandari's chronicle gives the city its older names twice"; "The books set a battle at Peshawar"; "Bhandari also names the city as a seat of command"; "In the twentieth century the city has a place in the story of the Chishti Sabiri order"; "Bhandari describes the country around the city"; "The shrines of the frontier, in one woman's account, are open to women"; "Khalid's Peshawar is a city to which Sikhs move"; "Khalid records one festival as a reason to go to Peshawar"; "The city's Hindus also travel as pilgrims"; "Most of the books name Peshawar in passing".

## Fixed on reread (after the first checker pass)

- The Overview had five uncited framing sentences ("In the chronicles it is a field of battle and a gate to Kabul", "Its religious life reaches across traditions" and others). They were removed, so every Overview sentence now carries the citation of its body sentence.
- "The first event the books set at Peshawar is a battle" became "The books set a battle at Peshawar". "First" was a chronological judgement the sources do not make.
- "Rizvi sets Peshawar at the head of a wide religious movement" became "Rizvi makes Peshawar the centre of the Nath yogis", which is his word.
- "The city's Hindus appear in Khalid as travellers to the holy places of the Punjab": "of the Punjab" came from general knowledge and was removed.
- Tarvinder Kaur's family went to Peshawar "because" of its Sikh population: "because" was my inference and was removed. The source's "where there was a considerable Sikh population" stands. The History paragraph that repeated the Sikh refugees was removed, and that material now sits in Communities only.
- "a man he names Diyal" became "Diyal, as he gives the name"; "Of the people of other faiths he met, one, Diyal" was invented framing and was removed.
- "For Schimmel, then, to be of Peshawar is one of the marks of a separate self that the mystic leaves behind" was my interpretation. It became the plainer sentence quoted under weak material.
- "in the words of one of his disciples, a frontier town of open shrines" became a quotation of her words. The source does not say whose disciple she was.
- Dhillon "names the city once" became "names the city as a stage". The source does not say once.
- "The older histories see the city as a frontier" became "Among the older histories, Bhandari's chronicle gives the city the most room". "And its Afghans" was taken out of that list, so that it cites only single pages.

## Where the Sources Disagree (as in the article)

1. What became of Raja Jaipal: Bhandari, as printed, has him taken captive with fifteen of his sons, brothers and kin and put to death (p. 424, which the source marks "printed as quoted ... see Doubts"). Chishti has Mahmud make him prisoner (p. 805).
2. The city's older names inside Bhandari: Farshawar, Parshawar and Bagram (p. 424), against Parshawar and Farshawar (p. 503). This is a difference inside one source rather than a contradiction. It is listed because the p. 424 list includes Bagram, which p. 503 does not.
3. The name of Gorakhtari inside Rizvi: "Gorakhtari", "either 'Gorkhattari' or perhaps 'Gorakhtari'" (p. 413, folio 389), "Gorkhattri" on the plate (p. 392), and "Gorakhattri" in the illustration list (p. 10).

Not listed as a disagreement:
- How the Mahmud encounter began. Chishti has Mahmud come to Peshawar with ten thousand horsemen in August (p. 805). Bhandari has Jaipal reach Peshawar to oppose him, in the year 391 (p. 424). The two accounts do not conflict.
- Zauqi Shah's cities. Ernst and Lawrence name four cities of newspaper work (p. 139, folio 126). Rozehnal names seven cities of his travels (p. 62, folio 52). They count different things.

## Questions for Rauf

1. **Bhandari's "طریق مال گذاری و رعیت گیری ... دارند" (pp. 504–503), said of the Mohmand and other Afghans.** The source prints it with an ellipsis and gives no gloss. I rendered it "who pay revenue and live as subjects". *Raiyat giri* could also mean taking on tenants or subjects, which would make them landholders rather than subjects. Which reading should the public version use, or should it keep only "who pay revenue"?
2. **The shrine to which a Qur'an was sent at Ranjit Singh's conquest (Chishti, p. 169).** The source does not name it. *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* is a book about Lahore, so it is probably a Lahore shrine, but the article says only "a shrine". Should someone read p. 169 to name it?
3. **Bhandari's "تومان بنگرام[OCR?] ان مشهور به پشاور" (p. 504).** It may read "the *tuman* of Bagram, which is known as Peshawar", which would agree with p. 424's list of Bagram among the city's older names. I kept the uncertainty. Should a look at the page settle it?
4. **The year 391 (Bhandari, p. 424).** The source prints "در سنه ۳۹۱" and does not say "AH". The article says "the year 391" as printed. May the public version say "391 AH"?
5. **The three en dashes inside page references** (`pp. 504–503` twice, `pp. 128–129`). This drafter's brief says both "no en dashes anywhere" and "copy page references exactly", and the checker needs the exact range. I kept the ranges, as Nankana Sahib does. Should they stay, or should the rule be relaxed for page references explicitly?
