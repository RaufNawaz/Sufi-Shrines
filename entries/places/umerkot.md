## Overview

Michel Boivin places the hamlet of Pithoro, with the tomb of Pithoro Pir, between Mirpur Khas and Umarkot, as he spells it (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 189, folio 176), and writes that the area of Umarkot "was at the centre of conflicts of local, regional and central interests" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 189, folio 176). In the chronicle of Sujan Rai Bhandari the fortress of Amarkot is where an emperor halted after crossing the desert and where Prince Muhammad Akbar was born (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 284). Boivin names the Menghwar as an untouchable group "mostly settled around Umerkot" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 40, folio 27), and Jürgen Schaflechner writes of walking pilgrims from "a village near Umerkot in Sindh" to Hinglaj (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 152, folio not stated).

## History

Boivin describes the area as contested ground. "The area of Umarkot where he lived had been a place of rough combat between two groups of Rajputs, the Somras and the Sodhas", he writes, in the pages he gives to Pithoro Pir (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 188, folio 175). He adds that "The area of Umarkot was at the centre of conflicts of local, regional and central interests", and that "Although the latter officially took power from the first in 1351, the Somras had preserved their control of certain outlying areas of Sindh, including, precisely, the area of Umarkot" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 189, folio 176).

Boivin speaks with caution of a chief: "Their chief, Parmar Sodha, would have been established as king (rano) at Umarkot" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 191, folio 178).

The Persian chronicle of Sujan Rai Bhandari gives the town one of its fullest scenes. After the crossing of the desert, it says, the emperor's party came after thousands of hardships and toil to the fortress of Amarkot, and Rana Parshad, its ruler, counted the arrival his own good fortune and performed the duties of service (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 284). After they reached that fortress Prince Muhammad Akbar was born, on the fifth of Rajab of a year the printed text gives as 949, though the reading of the year is uncertain (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 284). His mother was Hamida Banu Begum, whose descent the chronicle traces to Ahmad Jam, and whom the emperor had married after reaching the neighbourhood of Thatta; the astrologers foretold a long reign and a long life for the child (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 284). The emperor stayed some while in that land and then left, meaning to go to Qandahar and to leave the women of his household there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 284).

## Communities

Boivin writes that "Another important untouchable group was made of the Menghwar, mostly settled around Umerkot. Part of their devotional literature was and still is in Sindhi." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 40, folio 27).

The Rajput lineages of his account belong to the same ground: the Somras and the Sodhas, whose fighting made the area of Umarkot "a place of rough combat" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 188, folio 175). Of the people whose chief this Parmar Sodha was, he records that "The Gazetteer of 1907 describes them as worshippers of Shiva." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 191, folio 178).

Bhandari's chronicle names other peoples of the country around. From Bhakkar to Nasirpur and Amarkot, it says, live the Sabuda, a name uncertain in the printed text for which the editor's footnote gives Suda, together with the Jarija and others (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529). The passage belongs to the chronicle's account of the suba of Thatta and its four sarkars (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529).

## Holy Places and Pilgrimage

The one shrine the books place near the town is Pithoro Pir's. "The tomb of Pithoro Pir is located in the hamlet of Pithoro, between Mirpur Khas and Umarkot, on the railway line which formerly connected Hyderabad (Sindh) to Barmer and Jodhpur, in Rajasthan. As for many other saints, the site of their sanctuary bears the name of the saint." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 189, folio 176). Boivin places the same stretch of country again "between Mirpur Khas and Umerkot" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 178, folio 165). Of the Gazetteer of 1907 and its account of the people of Parmar Sodha he adds: "No mention of Pithoro Pir appears there." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 191, folio 178).

Devotion also went out from the district on foot. Schaflechner writes of a walking pilgrim group from "a village near Umerkot in Sindh" which covered "around 600 kilometers" on foot to Hinglaj (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 152, folio not stated).

## The Place in the Books

Each book spells the town its own way. Boivin writes both Umarkot and Umerkot (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 188, folio 175; Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 40, folio 27), Schaflechner Umerkot (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 152, folio not stated), and Bhandari's Persian chronicle Amarkot, the fortress of Amarkot (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 284). Shemeem Burney Abbas, too, uses both Umarkot and Umerkot (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 185, folio not stated; Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 216, folio not stated), and Annemarie Schimmel's index writes Omarkot (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 359, folio 341).

For Bhandari's chronicle, Amarkot is a fortress on a royal journey, the birthplace of a prince, and a place in the description of the suba of Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 284; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529). For Boivin it is the setting of Pithoro Pir's tomb, in a country of Rajput rivalries, and the home of an untouchable community whose devotional literature is partly in Sindhi (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 189, folio 176; Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 40, folio 27). For Schaflechner it is the home district of pilgrims who walked to Hinglaj (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 152, folio not stated).

The town also belongs to romance. Abbas gives Umarkot as the seat of the vizier Rano in the Mumal myth (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 185, folio not stated), and in her list of primary sources Umerkot appears as a place of fieldwork or recording (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 216, folio not stated). Schimmel's book does not discuss the place; its index carries Omarkot as a headword and, from it, Omar of Omarkot, a figure of romance and not a saint (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 359, folio 341).
