## Overview

Michel Boivin calls Hyderabad "the late capital of the independent Sindh before the British conquest" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 30, folio 17). In his account it is a city of Amil families and of Sufis, among them Qutub Ali Shah, whose dargah stands in Jahaniyya, a neighbourhood of the city (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 119, folio 106; Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 95, folio 82). For Shemeem Burney Abbas it is a city of Sufi music, where the radio and local recording companies let singers of Sufi lyrics, women among them, earn a living (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 34, 63, folios not stated; Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 35, folio not stated). Jürgen Schaflechner names it as a place from which pilgrims travel to Hinglaj, and reports an attack on a temple in the city in 2014 (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 291, folio not stated; Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 17, folio not stated).

## History

Boivin calls Hyderabad "the late capital of the independent Sindh before the British conquest" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 30, folio 17). The Talpur mirs appear in his account beside a renunciant: of the *astano* that Dalpat Sufi founded in the city he writes that "the Talpur mirs are said to have looked after it" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 118, folio 105). He also mentions "the railway line which formerly connected Hyderabad (Sindh) to Barmer and Jodhpur" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 189, folio 176).

Boivin's Hyderabad is also a city of lectures, printing and schooling. Annie Besant, he writes, came to Hyderabad, "where she delivered a number of lectures", and "in Hyderabad, her speech was about Hindu religion" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 46–47, folios 33–34). He records a printing house founded in Hyderabad that "was named Blavatsky Printing" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 48, folio 35). "In 1901, aged 22, Rochaldas married and settled in Hyderabad to enrol as a student at the medical college of the city" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 56, folio 43), and Mulchand "studied up to class 6 at the Hiranand Academy in Hyderabad" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 59, folio 46).

Schaflechner reports an attack on "a temple in Hyderabad" on 28 March 2014, without naming the temple (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 17, folio not stated).

## Holy Places

Boivin names "Tando Jahaniyya in Hyderabad" as one of three Sufi traditions (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 60, folio 47). Qutub Ali Shah, he writes, "was a famous Sufi from Hyderabad", and "His dargah is located in Jahaniyya, a neighbourhood in Hyderabad" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 95, folio 82). The dargah's custodian travelled to perform its rites: Boivin writes of two figures who "both had the dastarbandi ceremony performed by the sajjada nashin of Qutub Ali Shah's dargah from Hyderabad", and adds that "The Sufi pir came for it from Pakistan twice, in 1957 and in 1980" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 167, folio 154).

Abbas places another Sufi shrine in the city. She names as a pir, a spiritual mentor, Qutb Din Darya, "whose shrine is called Tando Jahana and is in the Civil Lines at Hyderabad" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 71, folio not stated).

A renunciant's house became a place of pilgrimage. Dalpat Sufi, Boivin writes, "founded an astano, a place of residence for a renunciant, in Hyderabad, on the Sipahimalani Lane"; after his death it "became a pilgrimage centre under the guidance of his successor, Keshavram Sipahimalani", and "The place was known as Guru Sehwani's place" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 118, folio 105). In a note Boivin puts it more cautiously: "Apparently, he used to stay in a place in Hyderabad which can be called a darbar" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 209, folio 196, Ch. 1 n. 11).

Boivin speaks too of "the huge sanctuary close to Hyderabad" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 184, folio 171). The city also has its musicians' graveyard, described under music below (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 62, folio not stated).

## Communities

The families Boivin follows in Hyderabad are mostly Amils. "The Sipahimalanis were a well-known Amil family of Hyderabad" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 119, folio 106), and it was on the Sipahimalani Lane that Dalpat Sufi's *astano* stood, with Keshavram Sipahimalani as his successor there (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 118, folio 105). Hiranand Advani "was born in Hyderabad in a family of Khudabadi Amils" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 103, folio 90), and Molchand Kripalani was "born in Hyderabad in 1887 in a Krishnaite family" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 123, folio 110).

Dalpat Sufi drew followers of both faiths: Boivin writes that "He is said to have attracted many followers, Muslims and Hindus" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 118, folio 105). Schaflechner names Hyderabad as a place from which visitors come to Hinglaj at *mīlād-al-nabī* and *ʿāśūrah* (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 272, folio not stated), and from which pilgrims come at *ʿīd* and in *muḥarram* (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 291, folio not stated).

## Music, Radio and Women's Voices

Abbas makes Hyderabad a city where Sufi singers can live by their art. Taj Mastani left Sehwan for Hyderabad "because it is a large urban center", and "affirmed that in Hyderabad she can earn a living from the radio. She can further negotiate business contracts with television stations and her clients from the affluent feudal households in Sind" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 34, 63, folios not stated). Abbas writes that "within areas such as Hyderabad or Faisalabad the local recording companies have assisted women musicians in earning a living from recording their Sufi lyrics with music"; their songwriters are male relatives or men in the community, and "this small industry has become a source of income for many women and men" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 35, folio not stated).

Radio Pakistan Hyderabad stands behind much of what Abbas heard, through its director, Sikander Baloch, and its music director, Naseer Mirza (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 44, 55, 61–63, 80, 93, 95, 214–218, folios not stated). "The radio in Pakistan has played a significant role in identifying musicians and providing them a livelihood", she writes (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 55, folio not stated), and "Baloch and Mirza at Radio Pakistan in Hyderabad affirmed that they no longer have to go to melās at the Sufi shrines to hunt for talent. Many educated female and male performers come themselves to the radio and television for auditions and are contracted to sing" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 80, folio not stated).

The city keeps musical lineages and a resting place for its musicians. Abbas records that there are "two gharānās in Hyderabad", the Indore *gharānā* and the Gawalior *gharānā* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 61, folio not stated). Zeena Bai, she writes, "is buried in the Hyderabad graveyard where all the great musicians of the city are buried" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 62, folio not stated).

The city is also a place where the music is recorded and studied. "The Institute of Sindhology in Hyderabad, Sind, is one such source" for documentation, Abbas writes (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 175, folio not stated), and the Institute publishes a cassette of the musicians of Bhit Shah and several of the works she draws on (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 197, 216–217, folios not stated).

## The Place in the Books

For Boivin, Hyderabad is the late capital of independent Sindh and a city of Amil families, of Sufis and their dargahs, and of a renunciant's *astano* that became a place of pilgrimage (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 30, folio 17; Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 119, folio 106; Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 95, folio 82; Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 118, folio 105). In one passage he writes "Hyderabad (Sindh)" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 189, folio 176).

For Abbas, who writes "Hyderabad, Sind", the city is a place of radio, recording companies, musical lineages and scholarship, where women who sing Sufi lyrics can earn a living (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 175, folio not stated; Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 35, folio not stated; Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 61, folio not stated).

Schaflechner's Hyderabad is a city where a temple was attacked in 2014 and from which visitors and pilgrims come to Hinglaj at *mīlād-al-nabī*, *ʿāśūrah*, *ʿīd* and *muḥarram* (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 17, folio not stated; Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 272, folio not stated; Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 291, folio not stated).

In Regula Burckhardt Qureshi's study of *qawwali* the city appears only as a place of publication: "Hyderabad, Pakistan", for a work by Baloch dated 1966 in her bibliography (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 267, folio 247).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The shrine at Tando Jahaniyya or Tando Jahana.** Boivin names "Tando Jahaniyya in Hyderabad" as one of three Sufi traditions and places the dargah of Qutub Ali Shah in Jahaniyya, a neighbourhood of the city (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 60, folio 47; Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 95, folio 82). Abbas gives the shrine called Tando Jahana, in the Civil Lines at Hyderabad, to Qutb Din Darya (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 71, folio not stated). Neither book says whether the two names mean one shrine.
- **What Dalpat Sufi's place in Hyderabad was.** In his text Boivin writes that Dalpat "founded an astano, a place of residence for a renunciant, in Hyderabad, on the Sipahimalani Lane", which became a pilgrimage centre known as Guru Sehwani's place (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 118, folio 105). In a note he writes only: "Apparently, he used to stay in a place in Hyderabad which can be called a darbar" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 209, folio 196, Ch. 1 n. 11).
