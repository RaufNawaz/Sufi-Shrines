- **guru-gurpat-mandir-db-80-sirey-ghat** (the book's "Hyderabad", the archive's only Hyderabad row) —
  **place-level.** "the late capital of the independent Sindh before the British conquest" (p. 30,
  folio 17); Annie Besant "visited this city as well as Hyderabad, where she delivered a number of
  lectures" and "in Hyderabad, her speech was about Hindu religion (Sadhu Vaswani 2001: 31)" (pp. 46–47,
  folios 33–34); "the printing house he founded in Hyderabad was named **Blavatsky Printing**" (p. 48,
  folio 35); "In 1901, aged 22, Rochaldas married and settled in Hyderabad to enrol as a student at the
  medical college of the city" (p. 56, folio 43); Mulchand "studied up to class 6 at the **Hiranand
  Academy** in Hyderabad" (p. 59, folio 46); Qutub Ali Shah "was a famous Sufi from Hyderabad… **His
  dargah is located in Jahaniyya, a neighbourhood in Hyderabad**" (p. 95, folio 82) and "both had the
  dastarbandi ceremony performed by the sajjada nashin of Qutub Ali Shah's dargah from Hyderabad. The
  Sufi pir came for it from Pakistan twice, **in 1957 and in 1980**" (p. 167, folio 154); Hiranand Advani
  "was born in Hyderabad in a family of **Khudabadi Amils**" (p. 103, folio 90); Dalpat Sufi "founded an
  **astano**, a place of residence for a renunciant, in Hyderabad, **on the Sipahimalani Lane**. He is
  said to have attracted many followers, Muslims and Hindus, and the **Talpur mirs are said to have
  looked after it**" and, after his death, it "became a pilgrimage centre under the guidance of his
  successor, Keshavram Sipahimalani. The place was known as **Guru Sehwani's place**" (p. 118, folio 105)
  — qualified by the author's own note, "Apparently, he used to stay in a place in Hyderabad which can be
  called a darbar" (p. 209, folio 196, Ch. 1 n. 11); "The Sipahimalanis were a well-known Amil family of
  Hyderabad" (p. 119, folio 106); Molchand Kripalani "born in Hyderabad in 1887 in a Krishnaite family
  (Kripalani 1986: 3)" (p. 123, folio 110); "the huge sanctuary close to Hyderabad" (p. 184, folio 171);
  "the railway line which formerly connected **Hyderabad (Sindh)** to Barmer and Jodhpur" (p. 189,
  folio 176); and "Tando Jahaniyya in Hyderabad" as one of three Sufi traditions (p. 60, folio 47),
  indexed "Tando Jahaniyya, Hyderabad 14, 47, 82, 85, 99, 132" (p. 251, folio 238). Further bare
  occurrences at pp. 54, 56, 59, 60 (folios 41, 43, 46, 47), p. 78 (folio 65) and pp. 95, 103 (folios 82,

## From Shemeem Burney Abbas, The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual

### Place-level material: Hyderabad / Hyderabad, Sind

*Attached to 1 row(s): guru-gurpat-mandir-db-80-sirey-ghat*

*The book's place-level material on "Hyderabad / Hyderabad, Sind" (1 rows), from its list of rows attached at place or person level, copied into every existing entry that list names (ruling (a); Rauf, 10 October 2026). It is about the place in general, not about this shrine alone.*

- **guru-gurpat-mandir-db-80-sirey-ghat** (the book's "Hyderabad", "Hyderabad, Sind" — 1 row)
  - **Hyderabad is the book's media capital for Sindhi devotional music, and the material is
    substantial.** Taj Mastani left Sehwan for it "because it is a large urban center" and "affirmed that
    in Hyderabad she can earn a living from the radio. She can further negotiate business contracts with
    television stations and her clients from the affluent feudal households in Sind" (pp. 34, 63, folios
    not stated). "within areas such as Hyderabad or Faisalabad the local recording companies have assisted
    women musicians in earning a living from recording their Sufi lyrics with music. Their songwriters are
    male relatives or men in the community. In fact, this small industry has become a source of income for
    many women and men" (p. 35, folio not stated).
  - Radio Pakistan Hyderabad is the institution behind much of the book's testimony: Sikander Baloch,
    director, and Naseer Mirza, music director (pp. 44, 55, 61–63, 80, 93, 95, 214–218, folios not
    stated). "The radio in Pakistan has played a significant role in identifying musicians and providing
    them a livelihood" (p. 55, folio not stated); "Baloch and Mirza at Radio Pakistan in Hyderabad
    affirmed that they no longer have to go to melās at the Sufi shrines to hunt for talent. Many educated
    female and male performers come themselves to the radio and television for auditions and are
    contracted to sing" (p. 80, folio not stated).
  - **Musical lineages and a musicians' graveyard.** "there are two gharānās in Hyderabad—the Indore
    gharānā and the Gawalior gharānā" (p. 61, folio not stated); Zeena Bai "is buried in the Hyderabad
    graveyard where all the great musicians of the city are buried" (p. 62, folio not stated).
  - "The Institute of Sindhology in Hyderabad, Sind, is one such source" for documentation (p. 175, folio
    not stated); it publishes the Bhit Shah musicians' cassette and several of the book's sources
    (pp. 197, 216–217, folios not stated). Another shrine is placed in the city without an archive match:
    "Their pir (spiritual mentor) is Qutb Din Darya, whose shrine is called Tando Jahana and is in the
    Civil Lines at Hyderabad" (p. 71, folio not stated).
