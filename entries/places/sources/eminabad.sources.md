## From Haroon Khalid, Walking with Nanak

### Place-level material: Eminabad / Saidpur

*Attached to 3 row(s): gurdwara-chakki-sahib, gurdwara-khoohi-bhai-lalo-bhai-lalo-di-khooi, gurdwara-rori-sahib*

*The book's material headed "Eminabad / Saidpur (place-level: 3 rows)", copied into every existing entry that heading lists (ruling (a); Rauf, 10 October 2026). It is about the place in general, not about this shrine alone.*

The book's "Eminabad" and, in the reconstruction, "Saidpur" — mapped here on the book's own identification (p. 115, folio 97), NOT to the Islamabad Saidpur rows.

- **Shrine / Darbar**
  - "When Babur took over the city of Saidpur, he renamed it Eminabad." (p. 115, folio 97); Babur destroyed "the cities of Eminabad and Lahore" (p. 15, folio not stated).
  - Travelogue: "The cone like spires of ancient Hindu temples" rise among the houses, "beautiful, in contrast to the monolithic Muslim architecture that now dominates the city of Eminabad"; "the dusty roads and worn down houses highlighted the fact that the city had lost it[s] economi[c] prowess" (top of page badly damaged); "Once, it was an important trading centre on the Grand Trunk Road that connected with Lahore and then Delhi", the highway since shifted south; "a new city, not far from there—CGrjranwala [OCR?] [Gujranwala] … has replaced Eminabad as an important trading centre" (p. 115, folio 97).
  - Travelogue, one temple: "Despite being abandoned in 1947, the temple still looked sturdy"; its paint worn off, "a corrosive layer had developed"; these temples "had been taken over by migrants from the other side of the border following the creation of Pakistan", whose descendants stared back; the author wondered whether they had been attacked "in 1992, after the destruction of the Babri mosque in India", and "searched for signs of attack but found none" (p. 115, folio 97); he "marvelled at how much of the historical architecture had stil[l] been preserved" (p. 116, folio 98).
  - Author's reconstruction of Saidpur in Nanak's day: "magnificent houses and havelis", huge temples with gold tridents, idols in every shop ("Laxmi" most popular, then "Ganesh") (p. 103, folio 85); "Saidpur was an ancient city", its temples said to be built "thousands of years ago, long before the birth of Islam", grown into complexes with pilgrims' rooms, leased shops, stables and "an army of servants" (p. 103, folio 85); "the Brahmin pandits were the bosses", offerings kept, distributed or sold, guards keeping out "naked and starving beggars" (pp. 103–104, folios 85–86); "a Hindu-majority city with a few Muslim household[s]", Hindus mostly traders, Muslims aristocrats; "one of the most important trading centres between the Chenab and the Ravi. After Delhi and Lahore, this was the most important [s]top for traders", with "some of the tallest temples in the Punjab and also the most beautiful courtesans" (p. 104, folio 86); Malik Bhago's "four-storey haveli known as the Shahi Qilla at Saidpur", frescoed, "a minister of the Rai of Saidpur", holding a wedding feast for "all Brahmins and members of the high castes" (p. 109, folio not stated).
- **Practices & Events**
  - Eminabad is listed among cities with Sikh communities that "Only recently" can "exert themselves in a way that was unthinkable earlier" — university enrolment, "many have their own cars" (p. 88, folio 70; printed "Eminabyg [OCR?]").
  - Nanak was questioned "at the court of Malik Bhago in Eminabad" (p. 144, folio 126). The author and Iqbal Qaiser went on to Sialkot "After our visit to Eminabad" (p. 191, folio 165).
- **Legends & Miracles**
  - The city was renamed "in her honour" after "a woman known as Eni [OCR?]", who offered Babur roasted grain when he had not eaten for days, shortly before he reached Saidpur (p. 116, folio 98).
