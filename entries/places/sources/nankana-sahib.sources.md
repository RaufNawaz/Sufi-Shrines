The book identifies the place once: "Nanakana Sahib (earlier called Talwandi)" (p. 175, folio "l92" [text-layer?] [192]). On that identification every bare "Talwandi" in the book is carried here, place-level; the book names no gurdwara at Talwandi.
- Location and history, in Dhillon's retelling: "the village of Talwandi, which lay between the rivers Ravi and Chenab, directly in the path of the armies that had set their eyes on Delhi"; "Thirteen times was the village of Talwandi destroyed and thirteen times - like the fabled phoenix - it rose again from its ashes"; "Its last rebirth took place under the supervision of Rai Bhoe, a Bhatti Rajpur [text-layer?] [Bhatti Rajput], who had converted to Islam" (pp. 15–16, folios 17–18). It also "lay in the path of numerous mendicants, sadhus and holy men", who camped "in a grove of trees on the outskirts of the village" (p. 24, folio 28).
- Families: Rai Bhoe and his son Rai Bular (Bhatti); "ofShiv Ram" [text-layer?] [of Shiv Ram], head of a Bedi family and accountant to Rai Bhoe, succeeded by his son Kalyan Chand Das Bedi (p. 17, folio 19).
- Nanak's birth there "On 14 April 1469" (pp. 17–18, folios 19–20); at school "a brilliance that had never before been seen in any child in Talwandi" (p. 18, folio 20).
- Sacha Sauda: goods to be sold in Talwandi (p. 25, folio 29); the story ends in "the forest" and "Talwandi" (p. 38, folio 42).
- Distances: Sultanpur is "around 60 km from Talwandi" (p. 44, folio 49); Kartarpur is "180 km from Nanakana Sahib (earlier called Talwandi) via Lahore" (p. 175, folio "l92" [text-layer?] [192]).
- Returns: the first udasi passes "From Sultanpur… to Talwandi" (p. 56, folio 62); "Nanak and Mardana finally returned to Talwandi in I 506 [text-layer?] [1506]"; "Mata Tripta felt great joy"; he spent time daily with "Rai Bhoe", who "looked upon" Nanak "as his Guru" (p. 123, folio 137); Mehta Kalu was "a little in awe of his son" (p. 124, folio 138).
- After Pak Pattan, "Nanak travelled back to Talwandi"; Talwandi "was now no longer an obscure little village but had become a place of pilgrimage"; he sat by the dying Rai Bular, "sick and his condicion [condition] was deteriorating rapidly" (p. 172, folio 188). Kartarpur became a pilgrim town "like Talwandi and Sulcanpur [text-layer?] [Sultanpur]" (p. 176, folio 193).

## From Haroon Khalid, A White Trail

### Place-level material: Nankana Sahib

*Attached to 7 row(s): gurdwara-balila-sahib-bal-lila-sahib, gurdwara-malji-sahib, gurdwara-panjvi-chati-patshahi, gurdwara-patti-sahib, gurdwara-sri-kiara-sahib, gurdwara-tambo-sahib, gurudwara-janam-asthan-nankana-sahib*

*The book's material headed "Nankana Sahib (place-level: 7 rows)", copied into every existing entry that heading lists (ruling (a); Rauf, 10 October 2026). It is about the place in general, not about this shrine alone.*

The book's "Nankana Sahib", "Nankana", "the historical city of Nankana Sahib" (7 chunks); the four Nankana chapters, pp. 172–232. Place-level: where the book says only "the Gurdwara" (Gobind Singh birthday ceremony, p. 179; the 1985 attack, p. 216; the New Year pavilion, p. 215) Pass 1 left it unnamed and kept it here. Reportage unless marked; the festivals are dated 2011.

- **The city** (book's historical prose and reportage)
  - "Originally called Rai-Bhoi-Di-Talwandi, this city was rechristened Nankana Sahib in honour of Guru Nanak"; Nanak spent his early years here, hence "several Gurdwaras in the city", all of which pilgrims visit (p. 222, folio not stated); "Guru Nanak was born in a city now called Nankana Sahib, Pakistan" (p. 168, folio not stated); "the historical city of Nankana Sahib, associated with the birth of the founder of the Sikh religion" — near Itan wali, Aasia Bibi's village (p. 90, folio not stated).
  - "a city considered to be one of the holiest cities in the world for the Sikhs, as the founder of their religion, Nanak, was born here" (p. 183, folio not stated); "considered to be one of the holiest cities in the world for the Sikhs, if not the holiest" (pp. 221–222, folio not stated).
  - "There are seven Gurdwaras in Nankana Sahib, most of which are associated with incidents from the life of Baba Guru Nanak" (p. 192, folio not stated); "All year round most of them remain locked, only to be opened at the time of the festival. There are security guards employed at the entrance to all of them." (p. 227, folio not stated); Kar Seva's name is on "yellow dustbins and other boards that have been placed in different Gurdwaras of the city" (p. 223, folio not stated).
  - "There is one main road that runs through the entire city"; "The entire city needs a major uplift. The buildings are old and the road has pot holes"; "Most of the buildings are from the late British or the early Pakistan era" (p. 186, folio not stated). Roof view: mosque minarets and "small white domes of the Gurdwaras"; "remains of a few Hindu temples; cone like structures rising from houses abandoned at the time of partition"; the railway station (p. 225, folio not stated).
  - "During the Musharraf era (2001-2008), newly constructed roads and other government facilities made their way into the city. In 2005, Nankana Sahib was separated from Sheikhupura and made into an independent district, with this city becoming the district headquarters"; Sikh expats demand an international hotel and visa-free "holy city" status, with "no progress" yet (p. 226, folio not stated). A city "with a population of over a million" (p. 176, folio not stated).
  - Contents list four Nankana chapters: "Guru Gobind Singh's birthday at Nankana Sahib" (p. 5, folio not stated), "Lohri at Nankana Sahib", "Sikh New Year at Nankana Sahib", "Guru Nanak's birthday at Nankana Sahib" (p. 6, folio not stated).
- **The Sikh community and its migration**
  - Author: "My research on the Sikh community was limited to the Sikhs based in Nankana Sahib"; "these are not indigenous people from the city, but recent migrants from Khyber-Pakhtunkhwa (KPK) where there is a considerable Sikh population" (p. 235, folio not stated).
  - Population figures, kept side by side: "In the past ten years, the population has increased almost sevenfold. There were only about thirty to forty Sikh houses in Nankana ten years ago", families arriving "following the alienation after the wars of 1965 and 1971 as well as the demolition of Babri Mosque in 1992" (pp. 174–175, folio not stated); refugee families moved to "Peshawar, Hassan Abdal and Nankana Sahib, and started living at Gurdwaras"; "In about ten years, the population in Nankana Sahib increased to about two hundred houses" (p. 175, folio not stated); Kartar: "Just a few years ago, there were only about fifty Sikh houses, but now there are almost two hundred to two hundred and fifty Sikh homes" (p. 193, folio not stated).
  - "Most of the newcomers were incorporated within the Gurdwaras, in rooms which were generally reserved for pilgrims"; the local community helped with small businesses or rent; the influx gave "a confidence boost to the Sikhs who were until now dominated by the Muslim majority" (p. 176, folio not stated).
  - Kartar (informant): "Ours was one of the first families to move to Nankana Sahib ... There was no Sikh left here after partition. Our Gurdwaras became barren and were in a bad state"; "Giani Pratap" came with an Indian pilgrimage party "sometime in the sixties", stayed "to look after the Gurdwara", housed the family inside it briefly and made Kartar's father "a sewaadar (worker) at the Gurdwara"; he "died recently in Lahore" (p. 184, folio not stated).
  - Rajbir (informant): Chauhan's speech "invited Pakistani Sikhs to move to Nankana Sahib to take care of the Gurdwaras"; from Parachinar "Within two months, my cousin and I moved to Nankana"; "There were hardly any Sikhs here at that time" — during the Khalistan Movement, "one of the most tumultuous times in the city's history" (p. 213, folio not stated).
  - Surjeet and "Udam" speak Urdu "with a Pasthu accent"; their families "moved to Nankana a few years ago"; "not all Sikhs here can speak Urdu or Punjabi"; the commentator "shifts between Urdu and Pashtu" (p. 174, folio not stated); Baljeet's is "one of the latest families to have moved to Nankana" (p. 175, folio not stated).
  - Livelihoods: "A number of Sikhs living here are engaged in this profession [hakeem], following a practice that goes back several generations" (p. 174, folio not stated); "A lot of the Sikh families at Nankana Sahib are involved in hikmat (traditional practice of medicine)"; Dr. Manoj Singh, of "one of the Pathan families at Nankana Sahib, who settled there after the unrest in the tribal areas", now in Lahore, "travels to Nankana Sahib regularly", treating old people "free of charge" (p. 202, folio not stated). "There has been a growing migration of Sikhs boys and men from Nankana Sahib to bigger cities like Lahore, Faisalabad, and Gujranawala", keeping "grocery shops, cloth trading and hikmaat"; Surjeet's cousin Manjeet "has done his MBA and is now working in Lahore"; Udham Singh: "I am originally from Nankana but we have our business there [Gujranawala]" (p. 174, folio not stated).
  - "Guru Nanak is celebrated all over Nankana Sahib. There is a Guru Nanak High School, a Guru Nanak grocery store and a Guru Nanak cloth shop, all run by Sikhs. The Muslims generally refrain from eulogizing Guru Nanak in this particular way"; at Guru Nanak Government College "there are only a few Sikh boys studying here; the majority of the students are Muslim" (p. 172, folio not stated).
  - "46 year old Madan Singh, a former head of the PSGPC", "A successful businessman from Nankana Sahib", runs "Guru Nanak High School, the only school in the country where the Granth Sahib is taught to Sikh children"; of the current PSGPC head: "He never visits the city" (p. 203, folio not stated); his family "has been living at Nankana since the late seventies", "one of the richest Sikhs from the city" (p. 215, folio not stated). Kartar: "I was in class at Nankana Sahib during my undergraduate studies when a professor of mine commented that Punjabi is a language of the unrefined" (p. 202, folio not stated).
  - Separate utensils: at a juice shop Kartar shows the separate glasses — "There are a few shopkeepers here who keep this distinction between Muslim and non-Muslim utensils" (p. 186, folio not stated); Aslam Chaudary, restaurant on "the main artillery road" for ten years, refuses Sikh customers ("We do not have your utensils") and complains "We become prisoners within our own city"; not all traders are resentful, as pilgrims boost the local economy (p. 226, folio not stated).
- **Guru Gobind Singh's birthday and Lohri** (January 2011)
  - "The date is 10th of January 2011 and the occasion is the birthday celebration of Guru Gobind Singh": a twenty-over cricket tournament on the Guru Nanak Government College ground, reserved that day for the Sikh community — "Khalsa XI", "Nankana XI" and "Singh XI" (lost the semi-final to Nankana XI); live commentary by two Sikh boys, a dhol-wala, a dismissed batsman (Daljeet Singh, out to Sukhvinder Singh) dancing bhangra, women in a tent, a few Muslim students watching (pp. 172–173, folio not stated). Surjeet: "For the first time, this year we have arranged this cricket tournament" (p. 176, folio not stated).
  - "The concluding ceremony of the festival is held on the morning of 11th January 2011" "at the Gurdwara at eight in the morning"; "People working out-of-station have come to Nankana"; trophies brought "from the main shrine"; "Khalsa XI" receives the tallest; a communal prayer follows (pp. 179–180, folio not stated).
  - Traditional war games on Guru Gobind Singh's birthday "ended here at the time of partition"; events kept alive by foreign pilgrims included "Guru Nanak's birthday at Nankana Sahib" (p. 176, folio not stated). Kartar: Lohri and Guru Gobind Singh's birthday "have only begun in the last couple of years", made possible "by the increase in the Sikh population as a result of the migration from the tribal areas after the war"; "Earlier, there was only a prayer at the Gurdwara." (p. 193, folio not stated). "The celebration of Lohri at Nankana Sahib this year is an attempt to revive this cultural tradition of Punjab" (pp. 193–194, folio not stated). During the procession Muslims in shops and on bikes watch "with burning curiosity" (p. 192, folio not stated).
- **Sikh New Year, 14 March 2011, and the 1985 attack**
  - "unlike the other festivals, this is not publicized as it is not only a religious event but also a political one, with no precedent in Pakistan. The date is 14th March 2011."; the Sindhi pilgrims' visit "coincides" with it (p. 215, folio not stated). Pakistani Sikhs like Kartar keep Purewal's original calendar: "This explains why March 14th was being celebrated as Sikh New Year here, while Indian Sikhs continue to celebrate New Year on April 13th" (p. 217, folio not stated).
  - The Granth reading in a pavilion ends in prayer; kirtan with tablas and harmonium; Kartar Singh from a pulpit: "We Sikhs are a separate nation and we will not let the Indian sarkar, which is a Hindu lobby, take our identity away"; the crowd "listen indifferently" (p. 215, folio not stated).
  - Six qawwals in brown shalwar kameez and Muslim caps sing first to "the Muslim Saint Baba Fareed" (pp. 217–218, folio not stated); Kartar: "This is the first time since partition that Muslim Qawwalis have been invited to perform at Nankana Sahib. We have called them from Faisalabad" (p. 218, folio not stated).
  - "It was the November of 1985, Guru Nanak's birthday, a year after Operation Blue Star": "Thousands of Sikh pilgrims had come to Nankana from all over the world"; after Akhand Path and bhog, "Sardar Ganga Singh Dhillion, an American Sikh", spoke for a separate Sikh state (p. 215, folio not stated). The PSGPC "had not been formed as yet"; the SGPC, "based in Amritsar", managed the Pakistani Gurdwaras through the Indian High Commission in Islamabad, whose staff received pilgrims at the border and arranged their stay "inside the Gurdwaras" (pp. 215–216, folio not stated). During the speech "the gate of the Gurdwara was closed" and "Counsellor B. Jain and First Secretary K. K. Khanna" were attacked, barely escaping alive; Madan Singh, "only 19 at that time", was among the attackers: "I was arrested along with twenty-five other people and held at Sheikhupura Jail for ten days"; "Khalistan Zindabad" banners and pictures of "Sant Jarnail Singh Bhindrawale" were then "all over the Gurdwara" (p. 216, folio not stated). "The religious festivals at Nankana and other cities of Pakistan have gone back to being religious festivals" (p. 216, folio not stated).
- **Guru Nanak's birthday, November 2011**
  - Kartar: "thousands of people come to Nankana Sahib from all over the world" (p. 182, folio not stated). Figures and dates, side by side: "According to official estimates, ten to fifteen thousand people come to Nankana Sahib for this festival every year, which is celebrated during the last days of November"; in 2011 "According to media reports, only three thousand people are attending"; "This year it began on the 8th of November and will conclude today, on the 10th of November"; called "Guru Nanak Jayanti or Gurpurab" (p. 222, folio not stated).
  - "a heavy presence of police officials at all the entrances to the city. No car is permitted inside" (p. 220, folio not stated); devotees walk barefoot (pp. 221–222, folio not stated); "Nankana still has Pepsi bottles with the older logos" (p. 221, folio not stated). Mahtab Singh, 72, "became a celebrity when his interview was broadcast on national television at the 2010 festival", vowing to go shoeless throughout his pilgrimage (p. 222, folio not stated).
  - Trains from India (pilgrims from "Delhi, Gujarat and Amritsar") cross at Wagah and wait at Nankana three days; ETPB and PSGPC representatives receive them at Wagah, where a Langar is arranged (pp. 222–223, folio not stated). Three days of Akhand Path; the Nagar Kirtan follows bhog on the final day; pilgrims then go to other cities; richer pilgrims from Britain, America and Canada come by chartered plane and hire buses; travel-company posters promise "all" the Gurdwaras in Pakistan (p. 223, folio not stated).
  - Souvenir buying; women with mangalsutra and sindoor; "STD" banners for the Indian pilgrims; "The entire city looks like a mini-India during these few days" (p. 226, folio not stated). "Things in Nankana Sahib will soon calm down. Atleast till the next year…." (p. 232, folio not stated).

The book's "Nankana Sahib", "Nankana", "Rai Bhoi di Talwindi", "Talwindi" [OCR?] [Talwandi]. **Talwandi is mapped here throughout, including the chunk-005, -006 and -013 mentions that Pass-1 left unmapped**, on the book's own identification: "Born Talwandi (now known as Nankana Sahib in Pakistan)" (p. 314, folio 288); also "The name of Rai Bhoi di Talwindi has been changed to Nankana Sahib in honour of the first Sikh Guru who was born there" (p. 31, folio 13).

- **Saint**
  - Guru Nanak "Born Talwandi (now known as Nankana Sahib in Pakistan) on October 20, 1469" (p. 314, folio 288). "Rai Bhoi di Talwindi which later became Nankana Sahib", "where there are several gurdwaras marking events from his childhood"; Meherban "claims that Guru Nanak was born in Dera Chahal and not Rai Bhoi di Talwindi", while other Janamsakhi writers and Sikh scholars "are unanimous that Nanak was born in Rai Bhoi di Talwindi" (p. 43, folio 25).
