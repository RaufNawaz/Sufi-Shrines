## From Harish Dhillon, Janamsakhis

### Place-level material: Sialkot

The book's "Sialkot".
- Puratan first udasi "(1499-1506)" ends at "Saidpur, Pasrur and Sialkot (all three in Pakistan)" (p. 54, folio 60).
- "a bustling city that was situated in the foothills of the Himalayas"; legend has it "founded by Raja Sela, the uncle of the Pandavas" (note 30), rebuilt by "Raja Satya Vachan, when it became part of the kingdom of Kashmir"; "The earliest recorded mention of the city is found in ancient Greek texts going as far back as 322 B.C., where it is referred to as Sagala"; "famous for its silk" (p. 136, folio 151).
- "Sialkot became a part of the Muslim Sultanate when Shahabuddin Muhammad Ghori captured Punjab in 1192", with a garrison left there (p. 136, folio 151); "many Sufi missionaries settled here thus making the Sialkoc [text-layer?] [Sialkot] region a predominantly Muslim one and giving it a rich Sufi tradition"; "an important post on the trade route between West Asia and Ddhi [text-layer?] [Delhi]" (p. 137, folio 152).
- In Dhillon's retelling: a city "inhabited predominantly by Muslims" with a powerful Hindu trading presence (p. 137, folio 152); the faqir Hamza Ghaus's curse in "the centre of the market square" (p. 150, folio 165); "almost the entire citizenry of Sialkoc [text-layer?] [Sialkot] was there co [text-layer?] [to] see him on his way" (p. 159, folio 174).
- The third udasi starts and ends here (p. 191, folio 210). During "the Mughal Emperor Babur's third incursion into India", "Sialkot had offered no resistance and had been spared the usual bloody massacre and plunder" (p. 224, folio 246).

## From Haroon Khalid, Walking with Nanak

### Place-level material: Sialkot

*Attached to 2 row(s): gurdwara-babay-de-ber, shrine-of-imam-ali-ul-haq*

- **Shrine / Darbar**
  - Author's reconstruction of its past: "Puran Bhagat was the son of the King of Sialkot, Raja Salivahan"; "It is believed that Sialkot was founded by this king"; "The well of Puran Bhagat was at a little distance from the city of Sialkot", and devotees raised a temple around it, "the most important temple in the ancient city of Sialkot" (p. 184, folio 158). Sialkot is "the city in the foothills of Jamin [OCR?] [Jammu]" (p. 185, folio 159). Maula Karar is "one vendor in the city of Sialkot" who tells Nanak of "the various shrines here" (pp. 188–189, folio 162 / folio not stated); "Sialkot is not very far from Eminabad" (p. 189, folio not stated); Karar, hiding from Nanak, is bitten by a snake "escaping the harsh winter outside" and "died on the spot", and Nanak and Mardana take "part in the final rites" (p. 190, folio 164).
  - "'Allah O Akbar'" on barren rocks is "more prevalent in places where there is a contested history, like in Sialkot, where there used [t]o be a major Sikh shrine" (p. 267, folio 241).
- **Practices & Events** (travelogue)
  - They took "the newly constructed motorway that connects the Grand Trunk Road with Sialkot" after Eminabad — "Nanak too must have followed a similar route"; "Sialkot has become one of the most important cities in Pakistan"; its business community "financed its own airport, which now handles international flights", and the highway; "several factories" and "a lot of mounds", "probably archaeological ruins, converted into graveyards" (p. 191, folio 165).
  - Fields "flooded, this being the start of the monsoon season" [OCR?], which Iqbal Qaiser puts down to India releasing dam water; "Sialkot is located right on the border", and "Historically, Sialkot provided Punjab with a connection to Kashmir"; days later, firing on the LOC was reported, with shells "on many villages in the Sialkot district" [OCR?] (p. 193, folio 167); "one can see the mountains of Jammu … from the roof tops of the city" [OCR?] (p. 194, folio 168).
  - Pasrur lies "About thirty kilometres from Sialkot", on "perhaps one of the worst roads" (pp. 199–200, folios 173–174). The author of *Tarikh Guru Khalsa*, Bhai "Se sagh" [OCR?], "belonged to Sialkot" (p. 203, folio 177).
- **Legends & Miracles**
  - Sialkot "was gripped by fear" of Hamza Ghous, who told "the people of Sialkot" he would pray forty days; the quarrel began when a Hindu man broke a promise to give his first-born son (pp. 197–198, folios 171–172) — the rest is in the Guru Nanak group.

*Place-level attachment (ruling (a)): the book's "سیالکوت", "قلعه سیالکوت", "شهر سیالکوت", also "شلکوت(۱)" and "سالکوت", in c. 014, 015, 016, 024, 032, 041, 042. **The town as the author describes it (c. 041)**
- "در دوابه رچنا شهر متقدمین سیالکوت است" (p. 517).
- It was called "شلکوت" (editor's footnote (1) is [illegible]); its founding is attributed to "راجه شل[OCR?] طغائ[OCR?] پاندوان"; the city and the raja are mentioned in the Mahabharat, "که از تصنیف ان قریب پنجهزار سال میگذرد" (p. 517).
- It is also called "سالکوت" and attributed to "راجه سالباهن"; "قلعه پخته از و یادگار است" (p. 517).
- "درزمان سابق دار الحکومت ولایت پنجاب بود و دو سه کروه آبادی داشت"; now known as Sialkot, and "از جمیع قصبات اینصوبه درابادی افزون است" (p. 517). After Lahore fell into ruin in early times, "دار الحکومت این ولایت شهر سیالکوت گردید", until Sultan Mahmud's conquest and Malik Ayaz's rebuilding of Lahore (p. 525, c. 042).
- "راجه مانسنگه اکبرشاهی", faujdar of Jammu and jagirdar of Sialkot, repaired the fort and improved the city; then "صفدرخان جهانگیری", also faujdar of Jammu and holding this pargana in jagir, rebuilt the fort and its tower; most later governors made repairs too (p. 517).
- "عمارت قانونگویان قوم بدهیره[OCR?] و بعضی مردم دیگر بغایت مطبوع و دل کشا است" (p. 517).
- Paper: "کاغذ مانسنگهی و هم حریری و خاصه جهانگیری", white, durable and sent to other regions (p. 517). Embroidery: karchikan in silk and kalabatun, items including "فوطه و سوزنی و عدشقه[OCR?] و دستارخوان و خوانپوش"; "هر سال قریب لکهه روپیه از کارچکن دوزی به بیع و شرا می آید", and it goes all over the world (p. 517). Jamdhar, katari and barchhi are well made (p. 517). Gujrat produces more embroidery than Sialkot (p. 514).
- Gardens surround the city, especially "باغ نظر محمد بهوته[OCR?]" (p. 517).
- The stream "ریک[OCR?]" rises in "کوه جمو" and flows below the city; ten kos beyond, "زمین همین[OCR?] میشود", it takes the name "کهیلری[OCR?]" and spreads out until it loses its name (p. 517). When it floods in the rains "جمیع مردم سیالکوت از اکابر و اصاغر" play in the water, naked except for a loincloth and carrying leather water-skins; anyone from the city who is far away remembers this with pleasure (pp. 517–516).
- "دار العلوم و جامع علما و معدن فضل و مسکن فضلاست" (p. 516). Under Akbar "مولاناے کمال الدین حسین", "خان مرزبان کشمیر رنجیده", came to Sialkot "درسنه نهصد و هفتاد و یک هجری" and established teaching (p. 516). Under Shah Jahan "مولوی عبدالحکیم" spread the sciences, wrote glosses ("حاشیه"), and students came to his "مدرسه متبرکه" (p. 516). His second[OCR?] son "مولوی عبدالله" ran the madrasa, was called "امام وقت", and died "درسنه ۲۶[OCR?](۱) عالمگیری" (editor's footnote: "سنه ۲۶[OCR?]") (p. 516).
- As a reference point: Dhonkal[OCR?] is 12 kos from Sialkot (see sakhi-sarwar) and Purmandal[OCR?] 15 kos (p. 516); the Chenab passes "دیهات پرگنه سیالکوت" (p. 512); Gujrat's villages were taken out of "پرگنه سیالکوت" (p. 515); Shah Daula's first master lived in Sialkot (p. 515). *basis: directional mention*

**Earlier history**
- Maulana Kamal at Sialkot (c. 015): in the reign of Husain Khan (Chak) of Kashmir, "افضل الفضلا و اکمل العلما مولانا ئے کمال که در ان زمان از فضیلت درویشی مشهور [illegible] الاکناف بود" left Kashmir, "در سیالکوت رسیده بدرس مقید گشت" (p. 187), and became famous "در هندوستان[OCR?] بفضایل[OCR?] کمالات خداشناسی و ایزد پرستی" (p. 187). No tomb, date of death or silsila is given (not stated).
- Shahi Khan (later Sultan Zain al-Abidin) "از کشمیر برامده بسیالکوت رسید", joined "جسرته[OCR?] کهوکهر[OCR?]", who had escaped Timur's custody "در پنجاب", and marched against Ali Shah (p. 191). Nothing further is said about the town. *basis: passing mention*
- Shihab al-Din's fifth campaign: "درمیان دریاۓ راوی وچناب قلعه سیالکوت را که بناۓ آن از قدیم است وشکست وریخت که درارکان آن راه یافته بود بتجدید وترمیم لتعمیر[OCR?] نموده نایب خود را گذاشته برگشت" (p. 404); "وخسرو ملک باتفاق طایفه کهوکهران مدتے محاصره قلعه سیالکوت نموده بے نیل مقصود بلاهور عود کرد" (p. 404). The suba description: "درسنه پانصد و هشتاد(۲) هجری", having failed at Lahore, he came to Sialkot, repaired and rebuilt the old fort, and left his army there (p. 517); editor's footnotes: (2) "[illegible] پانصد[OCR?] و هشتاد[OCR?] [illegible]"; (3) "[illegible]".
- Babur (c. 024): Daulat Khan and Ghazi Khan gathered "چهل هزار سوار و پیاده", took Kalanaur, and "از مردم بادشاهی سیالکوت نیز انتزاع نموده اند" (p. 304). Babur camped by the Chenab near "قصبه بهلول پور", and "حکم شد که سیالکوت را خراب کرده ساکنان آن جا را در بهلول پور آباد گردانند" (p. 304). Alam Khan and others reached "سرهند[OCR?]", heard of "نزول رایات عالیات حضرت بادشاه در حدود سیالکوت", and came over (p. 304). "آن حضرت از سیالکوت متوجه پیشتر شده براه پسرور[OCR?] کلانور تشریف بردند" (p. 304). *basis: campaign mention*
- Only as an office (c. 016): Kunwar Man Singh "فوجدار سیالکوت بود" when he relieved the Lahore fort (p. 203). *basis: office mention*
- Akbar (c. 014): the land later made pargana Gujrat "داخل سیالکوت بود" (p. 177). On the year-42 Kashmir journey, "رعایاے ٹپہ[OCR?] الوور[OCR?] اٹیچ[OCR?] تابع سیالکوت" complained of "محمد بیگ کروڑے[OCR?]"; "براے عبرت پذیرے اعمال ستمگار جفا پیشہ", "خنجر بجاق[OCR?] کشیدند" — apparently he was put to death, but that is a reading (p. 177).
