## Overview

The books know Islamabad as the seat of the state. In *Embodying Charisma* it is "Pakistan's capital city, Islamabad" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 151, folio 140), and Haroon Khalid writes that "Rawalpindi happens to be the twin city of Islamabad, the federal capital of the country" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 265, folio 239). Shemeem Burney Abbas puts it plainly: "Islamabad is the capital and therefore its population consists of officials linked with the federal government, and the foreign embassies and their support staff" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 100–101, folios not stated).

The books set a shrine on the edge of the capital. The village of Nurpur Shahan and Barri Imam's shrine stand on the outskirts of the capital (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 151, folio 140), and a devotional print described in the same volume names Pir Mehr Ali Shah with the place "Golra/Islamabad" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 159, folio 148). Khalid, in *In Search of Shiva*, places the federal capital in the Margalla hills, in whose heart he sets the ruins of Taxila and the stupa of Dharmarajika (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated).

The books also place religious organisations and circles in the city. Khalid describes Al-Huda as "one of the most prominent Pakistani religious revivalist organizations, based in Islamabad, which aims to educate women about the Quran and the Sunnah" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated). Robert Rozehnal writes that the Association of Spiritual Training, founded by Wahid Bakhsh, publishes an English language journal, *The Sufi Path*, from the city (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 130, folio 121). Khalid also counts Islamabad, with Lahore, among the important cities for Baha'i representation (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 234, folio not stated).

The capital is also a place of patronage. Abbas writes that the singer Abida Parvin lives in Islamabad rather than in her hometown of Hyderabad "because Islamabad is the capital and she can negotiate business from there" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 80, folio not stated), and she writes that invitations to concerts such as one she attended at the Open University, sponsored by the state, are among "the perks that go with being part of the official Islamabad bureaucracy", a practice with a colonial heritage (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 100–101, folios not stated).

Public anger and violence also reach the city in the books. Khalid recalls a day when a mob of thousands headed towards the diplomatic enclave to protest outside the United States' embassy (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 7, ch. 1, folio not stated), and in *A White Trail* he records the news that Shahbaz Bhatti had been murdered in Islamabad outside his residence, months after the Governor of Punjab, Salman Taseer, was shot there by his bodyguard (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 48, folio not stated).

## The Capital

The books know the city first as the capital. In *Embodying Charisma* it is "Pakistan's capital city, Islamabad" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 151, folio 140). Khalid writes that "Rawalpindi happens to be the twin city of Islamabad, the federal capital of the country" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 265, folio 239). Abbas describes who lives there: "Islamabad is the capital and therefore its population consists of officials linked with the federal government, and the foreign embassies and their support staff" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 100–101, folios not stated). Of the singer Abida Parvin she writes that her clients "are the foreign diplomats in the city and state and private agencies" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 80, folio not stated).

The diplomatic quarter appears in Khalid's *In Search of Shiva* on a day of protest. "Horrible news was coming in from Rawalpindi and Islamabad", he writes: a mob of thousands was heading towards the diplomatic enclave to protest outside the United States' embassy, the government had put up barriers to stop it, and "There were hundreds of foreigners in the diplomatic enclave" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 7, ch. 1, folio not stated).

The capital is also where two killings that Khalid records took place. In *A White Trail* an informant Khalid calls Gupta brings him the news, dated 2nd March 2011, that "Shahbaz Bhatti has been murdered this morning in Islamabad, outside his residence", and recalls that "a couple of months ago, the Governor of Punjab Salman Taseer was gunned down by his bodyguard in Islamabad for criticizing the law" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 48, folio not stated).

Institutions of the capital also pass judgement on holy places elsewhere. Jürgen Schaflechner records that an official of the Jinnah Institute, which is based in Islamabad, branded Hinglaj in 2012 as a 'high risk for catastrophe' because of the possibility of extremist attacks (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 50, folio not stated).

## Holy Places

The books set a shrine on the edge of the capital. In *Embodying Charisma* the village of Nurpur Shahan and Barri Imam's shrine stand on the outskirts of Pakistan's capital city, Islamabad (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 151, folio 140). The same volume describes a devotional print on which Pir Mehr Ali Shah is named with his place given as "Golra/Islamabad" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 159, folio 148).

Khalid was told of a sacred tree in the city. The travel writer Salman Rashid told him: "I have heard of a bodhi tree in Islamabad and one near Jhelum" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 9, ch. 3, folio not stated).

In the same book Khalid sets the capital among the Margalla hills, and a Buddhist site with it. He writes of a site "in the heart of Margalla hills, where the federal capital of Pakistan is": in the ruins of Taxila, Dharmarajika, a stupa surrounded by the remains of a monastery and "giant sculptures of Lord Buddha and Bodhisattva", which he dates to the third century BCE and which is "believed to have been constructed by the great Mauryan emperor, Ashoka" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated). He calls it "a UNESCO world heritage site and a major tourist attraction", and he once walked its encircling pathway with "Buddhist monks and the students from the neighbouring monastery" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated).

## Communities and Circles

Khalid writes that the Baha'is "are evenly scattered throughout the country, with Lahore and Islamabad being two of the important cities as far as Baha'i representation is concerned" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 234, folio not stated).

A movement of religious revival among women has its centre in the city. Khalid describes Al-Huda as "one of the most prominent Pakistani religious revivalist organizations, based in Islamabad, which aims to educate women about the Quran and the Sunnah" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated). It was established in 1994, he writes, and "in the past few years, it has spread rapidly across the educated middle class of Islamabad" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).

A Sufi association publishes its journal from the city. Rozehnal writes that since 1989 the Association of Spiritual Training, founded by Wahid Bakhsh and administered by a number of his senior disciples, "has published an English language journal titled The Sufi Path", and that "Based in Islamabad, it has produced fourteen issues to date" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 130, folio 121). He cites its thirteenth book with the imprint "Islamabad: Association of Spiritual Training, Pakistan, 1995" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 244, folio 237), and his bibliography lists the association in Islamabad as the publisher of Books 13 and 14 of *The Sufi Path*, of 1995 and 1996 (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 263, folio not stated).

Rozehnal also records the end of a gathering in the city: "Another female murid used to host a similar weekly halqa in Islamabad, but this practice has recently been discontinued" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 201, folio 193).

## Music, Patronage and the State

Abbas describes the capital as a place where a singer does business. Abida Parvin, she writes, "lives in Islamabad and not in her hometown of Hyderabad, because Islamabad is the capital and she can negotiate business from there" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 80, folio not stated).

In Abbas's account the state is the patron. She attended Abida Parvin's concert at the Open University in Islamabad, where she sang for three hours in the summer heat of August; the university sponsored the performance, and since the Open University is an institution sponsored by the state, the concert was held for the benefit of senior government officials and faculty (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 100–101, folios not stated). "Invitations to such concerts are among the perks that go with being part of the official Islamabad bureaucracy", Abbas writes, and "This practice has a colonial heritage" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 100–101, folios not stated). The year of the concert does not survive in the printed text (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 116, folio not stated).

"That evening this woman performer sang in a fiery mood and blasted the orthodox government of the time in the presence of its official bureaucracy by using the frames of Sufi poetry", Abbas writes, without naming the government (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 101, folio not stated). Abbas writes that she sang the story of Layla and Majnun "before an august audience of government bureaucrats", with the intention "to entertain and also to create sociopolitical awareness through myth about the minority provinces or marginalized groups in the country" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 134–135, folios not stated).

The state's cultural institutions in the city hold archives of the music and have sent its performers abroad. "The key archives from which I collected the materials were the Institute of Folk Heritage and the Allama Iqbal Open University in Islamabad", Abbas writes (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 23, folio not stated). Lok Virsa, "the Institute of Folk Heritage in Islamabad", contacted a performer through Radio Pakistan in Hyderabad, and through Lok Virsa she was able to go to international concerts in the Middle East and Europe; "as a state organization, Lok Virsa could afford to pay for her trips abroad" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 34, folio not stated).

## The Place in the Books

Each book sees the city from its own side. For Khalid it is home: he moved to Islamabad "after my marriage" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 157, folio 139) and now lives there (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 282, folio 256). He finds its drivers "much more courteous" than those of Lahore (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 159, folio 141), and he writes of driving back to Islamabad with Anam after a weekend in Lahore (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 245, folio 219).

For Rozehnal the city is a place of fieldwork. He travelled frequently to Islamabad, as to Bahawalpur, Allahabad in southern Punjab and Karachi, to meet the Chishti Sabiri disciples (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 26, folio 15). His interviews there are dated 22 and 24 March 2001 (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, pp. 109, 186, 190, folios 100, 178, 182), and he records a journey to visit disciples (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 91, folio 81).

For Abbas it is where much of her material was gathered, in the archives of the Institute of Folk Heritage and the Allama Iqbal Open University (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 23, folio not stated). Her first interview was with Ustad Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan at the Marriott Hotel in Islamabad; its year does not survive in the printed text (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 22, folio not stated; Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 195, folio not stated).

In the other books the city is a place of publication and learning. Annemarie Schimmel's bibliography cites *The Message of the Prophet*, a selection of articles read at the First International Congress on Seerat in 1976, issued by the Ministry of Religious Affairs, "Islamabad: Government of Pakistan, 1976" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 330, folio 312), and an essay by Gerhard Bowering on the Prophet in the same publication (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 316, folio 298). Carl W. Ernst and Bruce B. Lawrence cite an imprint of the Persian research centre in the city, the *Markaz-i Tahqiqat-i Farsi-i Iran u Pakistan*, of 1398/1978 (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 234, folio 224), and they cite an Islamabad manuscript (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 235, folio 225). Scott Kugle cites *Shah Hussayn*, by Shafqat Tanwir Mirza, published in Islamabad by Lok Wirsa Isha'at Ghar in 1989, the author's name being damaged in the printed text and read as Tanwir (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 340, folio 319). *Embodying Charisma* also gives the city as a place of publication (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 169, folio 158). Schaflechner names the city once, in the sentence on the Jinnah Institute (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 50, folio not stated).
