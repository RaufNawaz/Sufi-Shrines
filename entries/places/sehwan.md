## Overview

Michel Boivin calls Sehwan Sharif "a very reputed centre of Sufi pilgrimage" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 113, folio 100), and Jürgen Schaflechner calls Sehwan the *dargāh* town of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 107, folio not stated). Shemeem Burney Abbas describes "the historic city built within a large fortress from the time of Alexander the Great" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 56–57, folios not stated). The histories know it also as Siwistan: Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi writes "Siwistan (Sehwan) in Sind" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 172, folio 154), and Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian chronicle uses both names (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 172; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 50).

In Bhandari's chronicle Sehwan is a fort. Mirza Shah Beg, ruler of Qandahar, took the fort of Bhakkar and after it the fort of Sehwan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 172); the Khankhanan's army took the fort of Sehwan first on its way to Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 175); and Dara's boats slipped past the fort of Siwistan on his flight to Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 50). Bhandari counts the *sarkar* of Siwistan, on the bank of the river Sind, as one of the four *sarkars* of the province of Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529). Rizvi records that Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq made Makhdum-i Jahaniyan the head of a *khanqah* in Siwistan "known as the Khanqah-i Muhammadi to which several villages were attached" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 299, folio 277).

Haroon Khalid calls the shrine of Sehwan Sharif "one of the most important Muslim shrines in Sindh, which was once a Hindu temple dedicated to Lord Shiva" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 32, folio 14). Boivin writes that before partition the Hindus made up about half of the town, which had two main Hindu temples, one devoted to Shiva and one to Jhulelal, and that from 1947 almost all of its Hindus migrated to India (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 179–180, folios 166–167). The Thakurs, the priestly caste of Jhulelal, left Sindh to settle in India, especially in Bombay and Ulhasnagar, and in Bombay a Sehwani *panchayat* has published directories of the Sehwanis in India and overseas since partition (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 180, folio 167).

Women whom Abbas interviewed told her that during the *urs*, which lasts more than a week, the narrow streets of the city are almost impossible to walk in, and she writes that female musicians converge on Sehwan in large numbers to earn a living (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 56, folio not stated; Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 56–57, folios not stated). Annemarie Schimmel's account of the place speaks of its *malang*s and of a fair whose dates she gives in the month of Shawwal (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 386, folio 355).

## History

Bhandari's chronicle first brings Sehwan into its story under the Samma rulers. In the time of Jam Nizam al-Din, known as Jam Nanda, Mirza Shah Beg, the ruler of Qandahar, sent Mirza Isa Tarkhan against the Jam and then came himself; he took the fort of Bhakkar, and after that he took the fort of Sehwan as well and went back to Qandahar (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 172).

The chronicle returns to the fort in the Khankhanan's campaign against Thatta. The Khankhanan was joined by Rawal Bhim, lord of the land of Jaisalmer, and by Dalip, son of Rai Rai Singh of Bikaner, though the printed readings of both names are uncertain; the army first conquered the fort of Sehwan and then moved on (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 175). Mirza Jani Beg, the chronicle says, made peace and brought the country of Sehwan into the imperial dominions, and gave his daughter in marriage to Mirza Iraj, the Khankhanan's son (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 174).

The fullest scene the chronicle sets at Siwistan is Dara's flight to Thatta. On the twelfth day, of Safar as the narrative runs, Saf Shikan Khan reached a point thirteen *kos* from Siwistan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 50). Muhammad Salih, the *faujdar* and keeper of the fort of Siwistan, whose second name reads Tarkhan though the reading is uncertain in the printed text, wrote that Dara had come within five *kos* of the fort and that his treasure boats were following (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 50). Saf Shikan Khan sent a thousand horsemen with musketeers, camel guns and what the uncertain printed text appears to call rockets, together with diggers and water carriers, to build a battery near the fort at a spot where the river was narrower (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 50). The guns fired on the boats, but the distance was too great for them to do any harm, and the boats passed the fort unhindered (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 50). Bhandari judges that Muhammad Salih failed in his service, and that had he blocked the way Dara would probably have been taken (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 50). Dara passed Siwistan, which the chronicle calls a place of dread and of mortal danger, and reached Thatta; one word of the passage cannot be read in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 50).

In one passing line the chronicle also names Siwistan as a place of flight: a Jalal al-Din, defeated before Lahore, escaped toward Sind and Siwistan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 399).

Rizvi names Siwistan twice in his history of the Sufis. He names "Siwistan (Sehwan) in Sind" as a posting for Shaikh ʿArif (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 172, folio 154). He writes that Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq appointed Makhdum-i Jahaniyan Shaikhu'l-Islam and made him the head of a *khanqah* in Siwistan "known as the Khanqah-i Muhammadi to which several villages were attached" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 299, folio 277).

Abbas gives the city an older past still, as "the historic city built within a large fortress from the time of Alexander the Great"; she gives no other date for the fortress (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 56–57, folios not stated).

## The Town and Its Country

Bhandari places Siwistan on the river. After the fort of Bhakkar, he writes, the Indus enters the districts of Thatta by way of the country of Siwistan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 510). In his description of the provinces, Siwistan is one end of the length of the province of Multan, which runs four hundred *kos* from Firozpur to Siwistan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525). The *sarkar* of Siwistan itself, on the bank of the river Sind, is one of the four *sarkars* of the province of Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529).

In the same description Bhandari tells of a great lake in these parts, two days' journey long, which people call Manchur, though the printed reading of the name is uncertain (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529). Fishermen live on the water in dwellings built over it, he writes, and live mostly by fishing; he compares Kashmir, where land is made on the face of the water (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529).

Schaflechner names "the mountains of Sehwan" as a feature of the landscape (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 113, folio not stated). Boivin places the town "in central Sindh where Vicholi was spoken (a Sindhi dialect which was officially proclaimed to be the official Sindhi language by the British)", and writes that to be born there "was different from being born in Hyderabad" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 30, folio 17). He takes Sehwan Sharif as his example of a small city, against Karachi as a big one (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 67, folio 54).

## Holy Places

Schaflechner calls Sehwan the *dargāh* town of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 107, folio not stated). Writing of a man whose native town it was, Boivin calls Sehwan Sharif "a very reputed centre of Sufi pilgrimage" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 113, folio 100), and elsewhere he speaks of "the strong Sufi ambiance of the city" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 161, folio 148).

Khalid writes: "Perhaps the most popular example is that of the shrine of Sehwan Sharif, one of the most important Muslim shrines in Sindh, which was once a Hindu temple dedicated to Lord Shiva" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 32, folio 14). Schimmel's account of Sehwan has sentences on a sanctuary of Shiva and on a lingam (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 386, folio 355).

The town also had Hindu temples of its own. Boivin writes that before partition it "hosted two main Hindu temples. The first one was devoted to Shiva and settled in the north-eastern part of the town. The other one was devoted to Jhulelal and was established on the north-western side of the town" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 179–180, folios 166–167). He does not name either temple, date it, or say whether it survives (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 179–180, folios 166–167).

Rizvi's Siwistan also held a *khanqah* of Makhdum-i Jahaniyan, the Khanqah-i Muhammadi, "to which several villages were attached" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 299, folio 277).

## Communities

Boivin writes that "Before partition, the Hindus made up about half of the total population" of the town, and that "From 1947 onwards, almost all of the Hindu population migrated to India" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 179–180, folios 166–167).

Among them were the Thakurs. "All the Thakurs, the priestly caste of Jhulelal, left Sindh to settle in India, especially in Bombay and Ulhasnagar", Boivin writes, and "In Sehwan, they could have been the most powerful Hindu group" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 180, folio 167). He counts among those who migrated "the Thakurs who were running Jhulelal's temple in the northwestern part of the town" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 71, folio 58). In Bombay, he writes, "there is still a Sehwani panchayat which has published several directories of the Sehwanis in India and overseas since partition", and for some time now it has had a Facebook page (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 180, folio 167). In a note to his second chapter he writes: "Here, the Thakurs came from Sehwan Sharif" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 210, folio 197, Ch. 2 n. 6).

Boivin follows several people of Sehwan through his book. Nandirani Merani was "an Amil from Sehwan Sharif who translated many books on different matters such as history, geography and so on" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 45, folio 32). Boivin writes that "through his family, Mulchand was extremely well integrated into the mystical network of Sehwan Sharif", and he names "the Shahi Bazar in Sehwan" in the same passage (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 125, folio 112); Sehwan Sharif was where Mulchand decided to stay (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 128, folio 115). Paru Chawla "was born as Lalwani in Sehwan Sharif in 1932. Despite the strong Sufi ambiance of the city, she left at about fifteen years old" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 161, folio 148).

## Festivals, Music and Pilgrimage

Abbas describes the city at the time of the *urs*. Women informants who live around the heavily populated area of the shrine told her that "during the urs celebrations, which last for more than a week, it is almost impossible to walk in the narrow streets of the city" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 56, folio not stated). Sehwan, she writes, "is flooded with large numbers of female musicians who converge on the city to earn a living" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 56–57, folios not stated). The singer Alan Faqir "is reported to have sung on the buses and trains between Rohri and Sehwan Sharif in his youth" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 78, folio not stated).

Schimmel's account of the place speaks of its *malang*s and of a fair whose dates she gives in the month of Shawwal (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 386, folio 355).

Sehwan is both a goal and a starting point of journeys. Bedil, Boivin writes, went to Sehwan Sharif in his teens, "where he is reported to have been vouchsafed spiritual visions"; Boivin does not describe what he saw (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 97, folio 84). In Schaflechner's study of Hinglaj Devi, Sehwan is the destination that Raj Kumar, as Schaflechner names him, gave up in order to go to Hinglaj (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 160–161, folios not stated), and the place from which a pilgrim whom Schaflechner met at Lahoot la Makan in 2010 had set out (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 147, folio not stated).

## The Place in the Books

The books see Sehwan from very different distances. For Bhandari, in his Persian chronicle, Sehwan or Siwistan is a fort taken in campaigns, a *sarkar* of the province of Thatta, and a country through which the Indus passes (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 172; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 175; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 510). He says nothing of any shrine or saint there. For Rizvi it is "Siwistan (Sehwan) in Sind", a place to which Shaikh ʿArif was posted and where a *khanqah* held villages (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 172, folio 154; Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 299, folio 277).

The modern writers see a town of pilgrimage. Schimmel's page on Sehwan speaks of its *malang*s, its fair, a sanctuary of Shiva and a lingam (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 386, folio 355). Abbas, who interviewed two informants at Sehwan Sharif in a February whose year cannot be read in the printed text, one of them a medical doctor, writes of it as a historic fortress city crowded at the *urs* and full of women musicians (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 214, 217, folios not stated; Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 56–57, folios not stated). Khalid calls its shrine one of the most important Muslim shrines in Sindh, and writes that it was once a Hindu temple dedicated to Shiva (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 32, folio 14). For Schaflechner it is the *dargāh* town of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar, a destination one pilgrim gave up for Hinglaj, and the place another pilgrim set out from (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 107, folio not stated; Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 160–161, folios not stated; Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 147, folio not stated).

In Boivin's book on the Sindhis in India the town appears again and again: as a small city of central Sindh, as a very reputed centre of Sufi pilgrimage, as a town whose Hindus were about half its people before partition, and as the origin of the Thakurs and of the Sehwani *panchayat* of Bombay (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 67, folio 54; Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 113, folio 100; Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 179–180, folios 166–167; Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 180, folio 167).
