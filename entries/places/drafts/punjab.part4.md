## The Province in History

For Christopher Shackle the Punjab's Muslim history begins as an extension of a wider world: "The Muslim conquest of northern India extended this Persianate cultural world to Panjab, where Persian remained the dominant literary language of the ruling elite through the Mughal period and beyond" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 11, folio x). In the same passage he writes that "A strong Sufi presence was rapidly established" in the region with the arrival of charismatic figures associated with the main Sufi orders (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 11, folio x). Annemarie Schimmel, writing of the Mughal age, finds that interest in Rumi's work "seems to have been common to all classes of society", for "the Mogul emperors (especially Akbar) were as fond of him as the simple villagers in Sind and Punjab" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 358, folio 327). H. T. Sorley, writing of Sind under the Mughals, notes two things of its northern neighbour in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries: that, as Sarkar remarked, "with the Punjab, Sind was the home of the leather industry" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 107, folio 98), and that against the general rule that Mughal public money went to tombs and palaces rather than to public works, the seventeenth century canal enterprise in the Punjab was an exception, though he does not say where in the Punjab it lay (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 122, folio 113).

Sorley explains Sind's own isolation by the Punjab's exposure. In his account the invaders who poured over the frontier barriers of the north west passed Sind by and moved rapidly on to the more promising fields of the Punjab rivers and the Gangetic plain, until the rise of the kingdom of Afghanistan and of the Sikhs closed those barriers in the middle of the eighteenth century (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 22, folio 13).

The eighteenth century is the age the books dwell on, and they see the Punjab as a battlefield. Schimmel writes that Nadir Shah "first conquered Sind and the Panjab before he reached Delhi in 1739", and that the influence of Ahmad Shah Abdali was greater in "the northern areas of present day Pakistan" than in Sind (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 30, folio not stated). As the Mughal empire broke into pieces around 1750, the "Sikh threatened the Panjab", and "Nadir Shah's successor, Ahmad Shah Durrani, invaded the Panjab several times, coming from his native Afghanistan" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 50, folio 48). Sorley quotes Sir Alfred Lyall's account of how Ahmad Shah of the Abdali Afghans, after Nadir Shah's assassination, took Afghanistan and from that base seized the whole of the Punjab between 1748 and a closing year that the printed text renders imperfectly, read as 1751 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 17, folio 8). When the East India Company re-entered Sind in 1758, "Afghans, Sikhs and Marathas were at each other's throats" in the plains of the Punjab (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 46, folio 37), and in 1760 "The Afghan and Maratha armies were threatening each other in the Punjab" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 70, folio 61). His chronological table of events in Indian history records under 1756 to 1758, a span whose digits are broken by a space in the printed text, that the "Sikhs rise in the Punjab" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 201, folio 192), and under 1758 that the "Marathas occupy Punjab" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 202, folio 193). Schimmel, writing of a later generation, remembers Shah Ismaʿil Shahid and Sayyid Ahmad of Bareilly as "known for their brave fight against the Sikh, who had occupied the whole of the Punjab and part of the northwestern frontier" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 403, folio 372).

Shackle reads the same century through Bulleh Shah, who died in 1758 and lived "at a time of great social upheaval" in the Punjab, "as the central authority of the declining Mughal empire came under increasing challenge from armed rebellion by the Sikhs and from local notables" establishing their autonomy; these unsettled conditions, he writes, are occasionally reflected in the poetry (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 8, folio vii). He glosses one verse as "Taken to be an allusion to the Sikhs, whose guerilla horsemen ultimately destroyed the Mughal authority in Panjab" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 193, folio 168), and notes that "The Ranghars were a tribe of Muslim Rajputs who achieved prominence in eighteenth-century Panjab, and who were proverbial for their arrogance" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 179, folio 154). He reads the allusive verses of one *kafi* as appearing "to refer to a separation of Bullhe Shah from Shah Inayat due to the troubled conditions then prevailing in Panjab", and points to another *kafi* "for the allusions to the disturbed state of contemporary Panjabi society"; the twelfth century of the Hijri era of which the poem speaks, he explains, corresponds to the eighteenth century of the common era (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, pp. 165–166, folios 140–141). In that poem, in Shackle's translation, the poet says of the new century: "The door of doom and torment has opened, and Panjab is in a bad state. It has been struck by fear of the pit of hell." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 52, folio 27). Shackle also warns that this critique of the social upheavals of the early eighteenth century, which so interests modern readers, "represents only a very small, if vividly expressed, part of his poetry" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 15, folio xiv).

For Shackle the colonial conquest marks the next turn: "Only with the British conquest of Panjab in the 1840s did the literary culture of the Sufi tradition come to be overlaid by new patterns of modernity", as Persian was quite rapidly replaced by Urdu and English as the language of education, administration and elite literature (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, pp. 11–12, folios x–xi). In the twentieth century, among the new middle class of the colonial period, the formulation of nationalist understandings in the Punjab was no simple process, "given the complexities created by the simultaneous currency of three closely related languages, Urdu, Hindi, and Panjabi, whose differences were exploited to sharpen the rival cultural identities of Muslims, Hindus, and Sikhs, respectively" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 12, folio xi). Michel Boivin sets the Punjab inside a larger colonial economy: "During the Pax Britannica imposed by the colonial rule, the exchange of goods and products was reinforced between the different regions of the north-western Indian subcontinent, including Punjab, Rajasthan, Gujarat and Sindh", and beyond the merchants there were "networks of circulating knowledge, and especially religious knowledge" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 196, folio not stated).

Boivin also reminds the reader that the southern Punjab has not always been reckoned to the Punjab. Of the Siraiki speaking south, with Multan as its capital, he writes that "this area was a part of Sindh during the Middle Ages" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 216, folio 203), and he describes a shared Sindhian culture, a set of social, religious and cultural traits that existed in the Sindhi area "at the time when it encompassed the southern present Punjab" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 53, folio 40). He does not say whether by present Punjab he means the Pakistani province (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 53, folio 40).

## Saints and Pilgrimage

The books tie the spread of Islam in the Punjab to its saints. Schimmel writes that in Nizamuddin's time "Sufism became a mass movement in northwestern India", and that "A number of Punjabi tribes still claim to have been converted by Fariduddin Ganj-i Shakar" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 381, folio 350). Shemeem Burney Abbas reports, as tradition and with her own hedges, that the Bibi Pak Daman, whose shrine is in Lahore, "involved the men in disseminating Islam among the lay non-Muslim populations in the Panjab", the women speaking to the male scholars and their audiences from behind the veil, and that "seven hundred male scholars came with the Bibi Pak Daman to Hind, as the land was known then" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 161, folio not stated).

Of the orders, Boivin writes that the Chishtiyya "is dominant in Punjab and North India" while it was not widespread in Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 217, folio 204), and that it "is usually considered to be one of the most powerful Sufi orders in Northern India and Punjab" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 227, folio 214). Robert Rozehnal shows how a Sufi master's domain could be drawn on the map of the province in the twentieth century. Shahidullah Faridi's last will and testament says that Wahid Bakhsh, whose *khilafat* was publicly confirmed in 1975, "has initiated halqa zikr and guidance in Bahawalpur" and "should spread the silsila in this place only"; Rozehnal reads this: "Here Shahidullah effectively delineates the southern Punjab as Wahid Bakhsh's personal spiritual domain (wilayat)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 90, folio 80). The will itself names only Bahawalpur; the southern Punjab is Rozehnal's reading of it (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 90, folio 80).

Not every holy place in the Punjab belongs to a named saint. Pnina Werbner and Helene Basu, introducing Lukas Werth's chapter, point to "the countless shrines to unknown saints found on the outskirts of Punjabi villages", whose anonymity is, in Werth's suggestion, a key feature of "their placement as mediators between the dangerous forces of nature and cultural continuity" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 23, folio 12). Werth himself notes that Khidr "is often called Zinda pir in Punjab, because he also is thought to have disappeared alive" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 101, folio 90). Abbas defines the *dhammal* as "The vigorous ritual dance performed at Sufi shrines in Panjab and Sind" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 180, folio not stated).

## Communities

The books describe a Punjab in which the boundaries between communities were porous. Schimmel calls it a strange fact, "more visible in Sind than in the Punjab", that Hindus shared in the Muslim orders: "Some saints were claimed by both communities" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 418, folio 387). Shah Abdul Latif of Sind, she writes, "as did his Panjabi contemporaries and his successors in Sind, accepted the Hindus who were striving on the way to perfection as true pilgrims in the eternal Light" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 267, folio 267).

Boivin follows the Sikh tradition outward from the Punjab and back. "The Nirguni tradition was more influential in Northern India, especially in Punjab, where the Sikh tradition historically derived from the Nirguni tradition of the Bhakti" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 89, folio 76). He suggests, as a possibility only, that "the Aroras imported this cult from Punjab during their migrations in Sindh in the eighteenth century", the cult being the Nanakpanth, "a form of Sikhism that does not recognize the authority of the Khalsa" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 43, folio 30). In the second half of the nineteenth century, he writes, came the first attempt to rationalise and make uniform the Sikh tradition, and "This process of normalizing the Sikh tradition was centred on Punjab, and it did not concern the Sindhi Nanakpanthis who were heterodox Sikhs" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 53, folio 40). Sorley, for his part, explains the Punjab tale of Suhini and Mehar in Sind by a migration: "it must have come into Sind through the immigration from the Punjab of those Hindu races which form an important element in the population", as the Census of India volume for 1931 relating to the Bombay Presidency makes clear (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 270, folio 261).

The land's people appear in the books by caste and clan. Abbas writes that "The Jats of the Panjab are zamindars (landowners), known for practicing widow-marriage", and that the Siyal tribe "is one of the few tribes in the Panjab that allow women to inherit property under customary law" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 121, folio not stated). Her glossary calls the Siyal, Hir's family, "powerful landowners of the Panjab" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 188, folio not stated), names the Kheras as "A powerful clan of landowners in the Panjab mentioned in the Hir-Ranjha myth" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 183–184, folios not stated), and adds that "Some sections of Indian society, such as the Siyal Jats of the Panjab, permitted a widow to remarry, and she was not forced into satti" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 189, folio not stated). Of the *arain*, she writes in her text that "The arāĩ in the Panjab were gardeners or small cultivators who worked on their own land" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 108, folio not stated), while her glossary calls them "A caste of landowners in the Panjab" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 178, folio not stated). Shackle sets the legend of Hir and Ranjha "in the world of the Jat pastoral tribes of western Panjab" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 20, folio xix).

The modern books look at the hardships of the countryside. Omar Kasmani writes that "Being a child in a poor rural family in central Punjab, one who is regarded as intersex means being given to particular bodily horizons, oriented to certain futures more than others", and that the most likely path for such a child would have led to a life among the *khwaja-sara* (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 73, folio 56). He cites Lubna Chaudhry's study of women and poverty in parts of Sindh and Punjab, which finds that "unmarried girls in their teens and early twenties are most vulnerable", pointing to "discriminatory food portions within the family and limited access to health care" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 191, folio 174).

## Language, Poetry and Music

The Punjab of these books is above all a land of mystical poetry in its own tongue. Schimmel writes that "Reliable tradition traces the use of the native language for mystical poetry both in Sind and in the Punjab back to the fifteenth century", and that Madho Lal Husayn's allusions to the stories of Hir and of Sohni Mahinwal seem to inaugurate, or to continue from unknown beginnings, "a tradition typical of the religious poetry of the two provinces: the insertion of folk tales into the context of mystical thought" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384). In another book she suggests that "In the Panjab, popular mystical poetry may have emerged as early as in the days of Fariduddin Shakarganj", the Chishti saint of Pakpattan who died in 1265, and that "Numerous folk songs and ballads existed but were never committed to writing" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 16, folio 12). In a third she places the development of folk tales as media for mystical instruction by the poets of Sind and the Punjab only in the centuries after the death of the historian Badaʾuni (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 154, folio 136).

"The golden age of Muslim Panjabi poetry", in Schimmel's account, "is the late 17th and the 18th century, e.g., the time when the province was invaded one time after the other by Mahrattas, Sikhs, and, in between, by the liberating Muslim armies" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 17, folio 13). Comparing Shah Abdul Latif of Sind, she finds the similarities with "Panjabi mystical folk poetry, which reached its apex with Bullhe Shah during exactly the same period" conspicuous, and notes that Siraiki "forms the transition to Panjabi", so that the imagery of all three linguistic areas is closely related, as one would expect among the people of mainly rural provinces (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 154, folio 154). Latif, she writes, represents the rural mystical tradition "in which his contemporaries in the Panjab and, to a certain extent, in the Pashto speaking areas were rooted" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 267, folio 267).

Schimmel draws the Punjab and Sind together as one literary landscape. "The Punjab and the Indus valley have a common tradition of mystical folk literature" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 414, folio 383), and "The Punjab and Sind show close similarities in the types of mystical literature that nourished there"; Panjabi and Sindhi "were excellent media to express mystical feelings, though not mystical theories", their themes "sung time and again in forms inherited from the Indian past", not in the Persian *ghazal*, forms "closely connected with indigenous musical modes" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 416, folio 385). The mystics of the north west of the Subcontinent took up the attack on the *molla*, and such verses "appealed particularly to the peasants and the illiterate" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 416, folio 385). "In both provinces, a preference for the motif of spinning and weaving can be observed, a natural propensity in a cotton-growing country"; both also produced hymns praising the great masters of the orders, above all ʿAbd al-Qadir Gilani, in imagery that "could easily be understood by everybody in the village" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 417, folio 386). In *Pain and Grace* she adds that "A topic which is not rarely found in the poetry of Sind and the Panjab is the spinning of cotton, one of the most important occupations of women", sung to traditional folk melodies, whose printed name the text renders uncertainly as Kapa'iti, that the womenfolk sang while spinning (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 160, folio 160).

While the urban poets of India praised the Prophet in grandiose hymns, "their contemporaries in the plains of Sind and the Punjab wrote tender little poems or instructive Golden Alphabets to instil love for him into the souls of the villagers" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 222, folio 204). The tradition of *Ahmad bila mim* "has been repeated in high literature as much as in popular mystical poetry, be it among the Turkish Bektashis or among the mystical bards of Sind and the Punjab" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 451, folio 420), and the story of Muhammad ibn al-Hanafiyya, "a son of 'Ali by another wife than Fatima", "is found in the Panjab, in Bengal, South India, and Malaysia" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 284, folio 266). Schimmel also finds in Sind and the Punjab "a special aspect of mystical appreciation of women" rarely met elsewhere in the Muslim world, the longing soul represented as a woman, so that the tragic heroines of the Sindhi and Panjabi folk tales, Hir, Sassui, Sohni and many others, "represent the human soul in search of the beloved" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 465, folio 434).

The romance of Hir and Ranjha stands at the centre of this world. Schimmel writes that it was elaborated in more than a hundred versions in Panjabi, Urdu, Sindhi and Persian and given its final form in the late eighteenth century by Waris Shah: "It is his verse which is sung, time and again, wherever Panjabis meet" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 171, folio 153). "The visitor who stays in a Panjabi village will certainly find somebody to sing for him the old romance of Hir Ranjha in the classical form given it by Warith Shah" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 414, folio 383), and "even today a Panjabi will be deeply touched when this moving story is sung" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 17, folio 13). Abbas writes that Waris Shah "is said to have recited it with great emotion among his speech communities in the Panjab", and that "Many renowned folk artists in the Panjab, such as Reshma, Zahida Parvin, Nazir Begum, Sabiha Khanum, Tufail Niazi, and Iqbal Bahu, have sung it" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 123, folio not stated). Hir's lament as she is carried off in the palanquin "is traditionally sung at almost every Panjabi wedding at the time of the bride's departure to her husband's home" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 124, folio not stated), and the palanquin itself carries a strong emotional charge in Panjabi ritual (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 206, folio not stated).

Other tales travel across the Punjab's borders. "Sohni is one of the favorite folk tales in both Sind and the Panjab" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 156, folio 156); Abbas hears the Sohni myth sung in Sufi poetry in all the regions of Sind and in the Siraiki speaking area of the Punjab (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 126–127, folios not stated), and calls Mahival "The hero in the Sohni-Mahival myth of the Panjab" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 184, folio not stated). Sorley calls "The story of Suhini and Mehar" "a Punjab tale which has found a home in Sind", one of the stories of Shah Abdul Latif's Risalo that came from the Punjab (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 270, folio 261; Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 271, folio 262).

The books also follow the province's languages through the colonial age. Shackle notes that the lyrics of Bulleh Shah and the other Sufi poets, "who lived at different times and in different areas of Panjab", were never systematically preserved by an organised faith community, and were for the most part recorded and gathered into printed collections only in the late nineteenth century (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, pp. 9–10, folios viii–ix), when publishers in Lahore brought the older Sufi poetry into the new print culture (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, pp. 11–12, folios x–xi). In the Punjab, he writes in his translation of Shah Abdul Latif, Urdu was installed as the immediate successor of Persian, which did not happen in Sindh, administered from Bombay rather than Calcutta (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 25, folio not stated). Schimmel writes that the literary heritage of Panjabi "was rarely continued by Muslims after 1800", though in the twentieth century a number of mystical treatises were written and published in Panjabi, among them a partial translation of Rumi's *Mathnawi*; the language "was used almost exclusively by the Sikhs as their literary medium, whereas the Panjabi Muslims resorted to Urdu", "though they enjoyed and still enjoy listening to the simple mystical verses and ballads in their spoken mother tongue" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396).

Boivin describes Siraiki as "a language spoken in Northern Sindh and Southern Punjab" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 220, folio 207), "mainly spoken in Southern Punjab, with Multan as capital" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 216, folio 203).

Regula Burckhardt Qureshi treats the Punjab as one of "the two principal regions of the Qawwali tradition", set beside Uttar Pradesh, whose tunes can be told apart mainly by their rhythmic setting: "A predominantly quantitative emphasis characterizes tunes from Uttar Pradesh, while Panjabi tunes tend to be rhythmically organized according to 'quality' or stress", the drum articulating the metre more strongly in the Punjab, in keeping with each region's drumming tradition (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 73, folio 53). Her musical example of the Punjab style is a song sung by "a Panjabi Qawwal, Rahmat Khan" (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 57, folio 37), which she takes to exemplify "the 'Panjabi style'" (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 58, folio 38). She does not say whether her Punjab is the Indian or the Pakistani side, or both (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 73, folio 53).

## Shrines and the State

The books see the Punjab's shrines bound up with land and power. Jamal Malik, writing in *Embodying Charisma*, finds the cult of the holy man and its folk piety "particularly evident in the countryside, where major landlords and large-scale land concentrations are crucial features, especially in Punjab and Sindh", and adds that "In many cases, the local pîr represents the local landlord or is at least closely related to him" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 199, folio 188).

Rozehnal, quoting Katherine Ewing, describes how the British dealt with the hereditary saints, who "were seen primarily in terms of their economic power": "British policy, especially in the Punjab and Sind, was to treat them in essentially the same way that landlords and tribal leaders were treated", and "They tried to maintain the traditional social structure intact, securing the loyalty of the pirs, landlords, and chiefs by reinforcing their economic positions and educating them in the British tradition" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 33, folio 23). Kasmani, drawing on Umber bin Ibad's work on central Punjab, sets the colonial state, which in Ibad's words "did not try to control shrines directly through its bureaucracy", against the postcolonial state, which has gone further, driven by a desire "to harness the pluralistic traditions of shrines" in favour of "representing a Singular Islam" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 69, folio 52).

## The Province in the Books

Each author draws the Punjab differently. Schimmel most often speaks of Sind and the Punjab together, as "the two provinces" of one tradition of religious poetry (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384), and of a "cotton-growing country" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 417, folio 386); in *Pain and Grace* "the province" is the land invaded by Marathas, Sikhs and Muslim armies in the late seventeenth and eighteenth centuries (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 17, folio 13). Shackle writes of Panjab without the article, and narrows it to "western Panjab" for the world of Hir and Ranjha (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 20, folio xix). For Sorley, writing of eighteenth century Sind, the Punjab is the theatre of the armies of Afghans, Sikhs and Marathas (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 46, folio 37) and a source of migrants and tales (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 270, folio 261).

The modern ethnographers work with parts of the province. Kasmani writes of central Punjab (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 73, folio 56), Rozehnal of the southern Punjab as a Sufi master's domain (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 90, folio 80), and Boivin of a southern Punjab that was once part of Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 216, folio 203). Boivin also criticises scholars of Sindhi studies who treat Sindh's religious encounters "as if comparable dynamics were not implemented in other regions, be they neighbouring Sindh, such as Punjab, Rajasthan or Gujarat, or elsewhere" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 196, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **What the *arain* of the Punjab were.** In her text Abbas calls them "gardeners or small cultivators who worked on their own land" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 108, folio not stated); her glossary calls them "A caste of landowners in the Panjab" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 178, folio not stated).
- **When mystical poetry in the Punjab's own language began.** Schimmel writes in one book that "Reliable tradition traces the use of the native language for mystical poetry both in Sind and in the Punjab back to the fifteenth century" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384); in another, that popular mystical poetry in the Punjab "may have emerged as early as in the days of Fariduddin Shakarganj", who died in 1265 (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 16, folio 12).
- **Whether the Siraiki of Sindh and of the Punjab is one language.** Boivin's glossary calls Siraiki "a language spoken in Northern Sindh and Southern Punjab" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 220, folio 207); in his text he reports that for some specialists the Siraiki of Northern Sindh is "a distinct language from the Siraiki of South Punjab" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 171, folio 158).
