## Festivals and Practice

Kanhaiya Lal dates the Muslim observances of the city one by one. On the twenty eighth of Shaban Hindus and Muslims hold a mela with fireworks, if the word is read rightly, at Shah Bilawal's khanqah (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 236, folio 239). Mian Mir has five melas a year, one of them the *ʿurs* of two days and two nights and four on the four Wednesdays of Sawan, and the offerings come in abundance (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 245, folio 248). There is an annual mela at Ghore Shah (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 237, folio 240), and at Shaikh Musa's grave the blacksmiths, many of them his family's *murids*, keep the yearly *ʿurs* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 246, folio 249). At Hamid Qari's khanqah the *ʿurs* falls on the thirteenth and fourteenth of Jumada al-Thani, the second figure uncertain, the date on which, the book says, he died (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 253, folio 256). There is an annual *ʿurs* at Shaikh Mahmud Shah Naqshbandi's tomb (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 261, folio 264), and Hassu Teli's annual *ʿurs* is held on the third of Shawwal (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 169, folio 172; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 269, folio 272).

Faqir Sain Qutb Shah Qadri's *ʿurs* is held twice a year, with a *bhandara* and *qawwali* all day (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 271, folio 274). Fateh Shah Mast's falls on the seventh of Shawwal (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 274, folio 277); Mauj Darya's annual *ʿurs* draws a great crowd (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 280, folio 283); and Shah Chiragh's falls on the seventeenth of Rabi us-Sani (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 282, folio 285). At Shah Abu Ishaq Qadri's, on the fifth of Muharram, the Quran is read all night and then, by an uncertain reading, songs are listened to (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 283–284, folios 286–287). Abul Muali has three melas a year, with illumination and dancing courtesans on the first night and *qawwali* on the second, besides both Eids (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 285, folio 288). Taj Shah Majzub's *ʿurs* falls on the date of his death (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 286, folio 289). That festival was paid for privately: its managers, a man called Nur Muhammad and his son Bota, hold the annual mela on his date at their own expense, in fulfilment of a prayer that was granted (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 286, folio 289).

At Data Ganj Bakhsh there are a Friday mela, the *ʿurs* on the eighteenth of Safar, the figure uncertain, the mela of the Last Wednesday and a Thursday night vigil (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 291, folio 294). Muhammad Fazil's *ʿurs* falls on the thirteenth of Rajab and Shah Sharf's on the thirteenth of Safar, both figures uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 297, folio 300), and Shah Sarwani's on the ninth of Safar and the ninth of Rabi al-Awwal (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 303, folio 306). Shah Yaqub Zanjani had a Thursday mela once and has a great mela in Rajab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 301, folio 304). The Huzuri khanqah holds a two day annual mela, lamps on the first day and *qawwali* on the second (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 167, folio 170; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 267, folio 270). At a Qadiri and Shattari mazar a mela is held every year at which Sufis gather to hear *sama* and fall into ecstasy (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 144, folio 147). And at Shah Abu al-Maali's *rauza* there are the melas of both Eids, offerings, and an annual *ʿurs* attended, by an uncertain reading, in thousands (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 87, folio 90).

Muharram was kept in the great houses. At the Qizilbash household there was *taʿziyadari* for the ten days, with food distributed day and night to Sunni and Shia alike; on the seventh, the day of the *mehndi*, the *alam* bearers came to the house and received one to ten rupees each (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 71, folio 74). At Mubarak Haveli the seventh of Muharram drew the *mehndi* and *alam* processions of the whole city (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 332, folio 339). Chishti records how Ranjit Singh's rule reached into the city's observances: it was proclaimed in the city that whoever brought out a *ta'zia* would lose his head (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 274). At Nur Jahan's tomb, its basement left open after the British repairs, people go inside, and the printed word for what they do there can be read either as holding a fair or as soiling the place (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 324, folio 331).

The Hindu and Sikh calendars are recorded as closely. On every Ekadashi one temple holds a great mela, devotees gather in numbers, bhagats sing bhajans all night and offerings are made in abundance (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 120, folio 123), and at an Ekadashi assembly inside a thakurdwara *qawwals* come and delight the listeners with songs of divine unity (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 462, folio 469). At the Kali Mata mandir a mela gathers every day of the nine days of Navratri and offerings are many; hundreds of Hindus come to bow their foreheads, and Hindus from other lands too come there and are blessed (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 133, folio 136). On the day of Basant a mela is held at the samadh of Haqiqat Rai, and the income from its offerings is good (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 208, folio 211). On the night of the fourteenth of Phagan a great mela is held at the shivala by a well whose name, Badhu-wala, is uncertain, and countless people come from the city to worship (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 214, folio 217). The Devi Dwara of Rajo and Dharman holds two annual melas, on the Ashtami of Chait and the Ashtami of Asoj, the month names uncertain, when great crowds gather and offerings are abundant, and through the year it holds vigils at which the bhagats sing the Devi's hymns all night; the book does not say who takes the offerings (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 439, folio 446). The Mata temple holds eight vigils a month, one every Sunday, the day uncertain, and one every Tuesday, and another on the *panchami*, at which *karah prashad*, the word uncertain, is distributed (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 457, folio 464). At Mozang the Sikhs gather on the fifth day of every bright half of the moon, when *prashad* is distributed and lamps are lit (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 226, folio 229).

The greatest mela of the city is Bhadar Kali's at Niaz Beg, held every year in the month of Jeth; no mela in Lahore is greater, and Muslims too mostly attend (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 229, folio 232). Because it falls in the hot season people suffer, and relief comes largely from the city's nobles, the word uncertain: at the mela stalls of sharbat, water and pounded bhang are set up and sharbat is given to all alike; there is little shade and tents are pitched; the crowd stays a night and a day; and shopkeepers move their shops there in advance and prepare sweets (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 230, folio 233). Inside the walls, on the day of Bhadar Kali's mela at Niaz Beg, the city temple of Bhadr Kali also draws an immense gathering of women and men, those who do not go out to the village coming there to worship (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 126, folio 129). Than Bhairo's great mela falls on a Sunday at the new moon of Bhadon, with offerings and an all night vigil of bhajans, and besides it hundreds of people come from the city every Sunday to bow their heads there (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 232, folio 235).

Many melas, *ʿurs* and *sama* gatherings were in decline when Kanhaiya Lal wrote, and he keeps the tenses apart. At the mazar of Miran Badshah inside the Wazir Khan mosque, in the time of the Sikhs, a mela was held every Thursday and thousands gathered, and the annual *ʿurs* and *sama* were held with great pomp; now Hindus and Muslims still attend on Thursdays and the annual *ʿurs* is still held, but the *sama* is spoken of only in the past (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 161, folio 164). At Imam Ghulam Muhammad's tomb an annual mela and a *sama* gathering used to be held, and both have now stopped entirely (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 163, folio 166). At Sayyid Sarbuland's mazar crowds gathered on Thursdays in the Sikh period and the annual *ʿurs* was held with pomp, but that brisk trade is no more (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 164, folio 167); he uses the same formula of the tomb at the Masjid Suf (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 163, folio 166) and of the bookbinders' shops (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 162, folio 165). At Fateh Shah Mast's there was a great mela in the time of the Sikhs, but now not such a crowd (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 274, folio 277). At Shah Husain Zanjani's mazar a great annual mela was held in the Sikh period, but little by little its splendour has gone (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 275, folio 278).

Shah Yaqub Zanjani's melas were killed by his neighbours' building: on two sides Lala Ratan Chand's sarai and the buildings of his tank have hidden the khanqah, it is hemmed in on all four sides, and its graveyard too has been closed (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 301, folio 304). At Fazl Shah Majzub's khanqah the *ʿurs* that was kept with great pomp in Raja Dina Nath's lifetime has dwindled to an annual *fatiha* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 325–326, folios 332–333); Kanhaiya Lal says it shrank to that with the death of his patron (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 325, folio 332). Against these, two melas grew: Mian Mir's, with the population of the garrison (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 245, folio 248), and the *chiraghan* mela, whose crowds come from Lahore, Amritsar and distant cities because of the railway (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 351, folio 358).

Two modern scholars write of a festival of lamps in the city. Madho Lal's anniversary, Schimmel writes, is "still celebrated in Lahore near the Shalimar Garden on the last Saturday in March" and is famous as *Melā Chirāghān*, the 'Fair of Lamps' (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384). Abbas records the *Melā Chirāg* as held annually at the Shalimar Gardens (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 95, 185, folios not stated).

Vows were tied to sickness, and some of these rites were shared across the traditions. At Shah Dargahi's well a Sunday rite was shared by Hindus and Muslims, and Kanhaiya Lal describes it twice. Once: on Sundays Hindus and Muslims take their children who suffer from a sickness, its printed name not certainly readable, to the well and bathe them; each person brings four sweet loaves and four salted ones, and after the bath the zamindar of the well takes the loaves, together with one paisa for the lamp (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 82, folio 85). And again: whoever brings his child to the well cooks four sweet loaves, the word uncertainly printed, and four salted ones and brings them with him, distributes them to the people, and gives one paisa to the zamindars of the well (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 299, folio 302). At Sitla Mata worship goes on all the time, but the special worship comes when smallpox is raging, and thousands whose children have recovered from it come in joy; while the sickness is at its height people give offerings, each as much as he can (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 187, folio 190). At the Sitla Devi mandir cash and a goat are offered after recovery from smallpox, following the bath of recovery (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 452–453, folios 459–460).

Offerings were also made in kind. At Ghore Shah clay horses are still offered at the mazar in thousands on the day of the mela, so that heaps of them always lie on the parapets of the tomb (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 237, folio 240). At one grave a vow of bread is current in Kanhaiya Lal's own time, told as a *karamat*: whoever goes to the grave in need vows a number of loaves, and when at last by God's grace his work is brought to completion, he cooks the loaves, brings them to the grave and shares them out; the number of loaves and who receives them are illegible in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 294, folio 297).

Daily and weekly rites are timed. At the Kotwali shiwala *arti* is performed at four in the morning and seven in the evening, and also whenever a notable comes, so that no hour is empty of worship (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 95, folio 98), with special worship on Mondays (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 96, folio 99). The great kettledrum, the pipe and the gong are its instruments of *arti* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 95, folio 98), and the pipe, the gong and the conch sound so loudly that no one can hear anyone else speak (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 96, folio 99). *Arti* is performed twice daily at the Bhagat Ram shiwala (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 100, folio 103), and the Granth is read twice daily at the Guru Ram Das gurdwara (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 102, folio 105). Worship is performed twice daily at the Radha Kishan thakurdwara (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 134, folio 137) and in Chhajju Bhagat's house, whose great hall, Kanhaiya Lal says, should be thought of as a shivala (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 135, folio 138). At Chor Mor Wala the mahant himself gives the *katha* every evening, being a learned man (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 125, folio 128).

At the Ram Dwara the *katha* of the Bhagavata, the Ramayana and other holy books is recited twice daily (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 128, folio 131). There every attender is handed a rosary of *tulsi* by the mahant and told to sing the praise of Ram; a heap of rosaries lies in the middle of the circle, and one man is appointed solely to look after them (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 128, folio 131). A lamp burns night and day at the Bhairo ji *asthan* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 128, folio 131) and at Than Bhairo's fire place, never going out (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 231, folio 234). A naqara is played at two times of day at the Baoli (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 109, folio 112) and at *arti* time at Banke Bihari (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 118, folio 121), and mystic songs are sung in the evening at the Kuka dharamsal (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 105, folio 108). At a Hindu bhagat's samadh, on every Monday and Tuesday, Hindu devotees, women and men, attend and *qawwali* is sung in the mystic raga (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 185, folio 188).

Some rites gathered around ordinary places. A saint's shop became a second site of his cult: a flag still flies over it, a lamp is lit there every day, and on Thursdays many lamps are lit (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 168–169, folios 171–172; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 269, folio 272). At Pir Bhola's grave boys play all day and climb up on the grave as though it were a horse, the word uncertain, and every Friday the boys of the city make a mazar of Pir Bhola in lane after lane, offering flowers on it and lighting lamps (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 150, folio 153).

Congregations are counted only once. Six or seven thousand worshippers came to gather for the Eid prayers at the Badshahi Masjid (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 142, folio 145). Elsewhere visitors are not numbered except in round terms, thousands at Miran Badshah's Thursday mela (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 161, folio 164), hundreds of Hindus at Kali Mata (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 133, folio 136) and hundreds every Sunday at Than Bhairo (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 232, folio 235), and about eight hundred seats, by estimate, at the Mian Mir church (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 394, folio 401).

The *langar* fed the poor across the traditions. At the Ram Dwara *langar* is given twice a day, the verb uncertain: by former custom any poor person who attends is fed, and any traveller who comes is given food too, and the offerings go to two heads only, the necessary expenses of Pandit Kanshi Ram and the cost of the daily *langar* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 128, folio 131). Shah Bilawal Qadri spent thousands of rupees a day on a public *langar*, and thousands of the poor ate at meal times from his guest house, the last words uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 235, folio 238). A public *langar* ran continuously at Mauj Darya's house (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 279, folio 282). At Than Bhairo a masonry building is called the *langar* house, the word uncertain, because *langar* is served there on mela day (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 231, folio 234); the temple's mahant feeds the faqirs and jogis who gather for the mela, and lay visitors cook their own food (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 232, folio 235). At both of Shah Sharf's melas faqirs gather and food is distributed (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 297, folio 300); at Shah Sarwani's tomb faqirs gather on both *ʿurs* dates to eat and to hear *qawwali* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 303, folio 306); at Faqir Sain Qutb Shah Qadri's a *bhandara*, the word uncertain, is distributed among the faqirs (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 271, folio 274); and at Mubarak Haveli the food given to the poor and faqirs for the ten days of Muharram is cooked in one part of the building (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 332, folio 339).

Land and mills stood behind the kitchens. A dharamsal has forty bighas and a running well, whose income Nihang Singh takes and with which he feeds travellers, and a mill built to grind flour for them (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 213–214, folios 216–217). A mill runs continuously inside Mian Wada's enclosure (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 238, folio 241), and another was built for grinding flour on the Bhauri Sarkar estate (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 201, folio 204). Mian Wada's madrasa was a charity for the disabled with a headcount and a curriculum: under the Sikhs the blind, the lame, the crippled, a hundred dervishes and faqirs, and students in hundreds received bread and clothing from it, and in the author's own day about two hundred blind and lame and five dervishes are maintained by the khanqah, all are taught the Quran, and the blind are made to learn it by heart (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 239, folio 242). Hospitality was the mahant's rule at one establishment (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 204, folio 207). One dharamsala lived wholly on offerings, its faqirs supported only by the service and gifts of devotees, with bhajans daily in the third watch, offerings made on the Granth and food for any travelling faqir (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 103, folio 106). Before moving the image of Thakur ji from Rangilpur to Lahore with great honour, Hari Narayan first spent a great deal of money there feeding sadhus and Hindus (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 120, folio 123).

Bhang had its place in shrine practice. At the samadh of Guru Arjan a building called the Sikhs' cauldron, the word uncertain, has a stone mortar sunk in the ground and a pestle for grinding bhang (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 206, folio 209). At Shahid Ganj Nihang Sikhs grind bhang all day in a roadside hall where its cauldrons stand (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220), and the stalls of the Bhadar Kali mela serve pounded bhang (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 230, folio 233). One *mahant*'s own offering, his *nazr*, is a little bhang that he calls *prashad* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 213, folio 216).

*Sama* and music run through the record. Kanhaiya Lal notes *sama* and *qawwali* where they are held and where they have stopped (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 144, folio 147; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 161, folio 164; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 163, folio 166; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 167, folio 170; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 185, folio 188; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 271, folio 274; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 303, folio 306), with Punjabi *kafis*, *barah mah* and *abyat* sung in assemblies by singers whose name is uncertain in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 144, folio 147). Ernst and Lawrence record a sixteenth century anecdote in which "a man of Lahore came and said" that in his time there was no one worthy of listening to music, *samaʿ*: "In this time there is no one worthy" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 27, folio 13). Music at the city's shrines has a long history in the sources Abbas uses. She cites Tahir Lahori, who "also mentions women musicians who performed hazrī at the Sufi shrines in Lahore, especially at the time of the urs", and who uses the term *hazrī* of qawwals who sang at the Sufi shrines in Lahore both before the partition and after it; the year of the partition is lost in the printed text (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 90, folio not stated). Lahori's essay carries the practice in its title, *Aulīyā ke Urs*, published in *Sohna Shehr Lahore* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 227, folio not stated). Abbas herself interviewed a grassroots musician, Zarina Khatoon, in Lahore (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 218, folio not stated). Regula Burckhardt Qureshi's discography of other recordings of qawwal parties lists four from Lahore: Kausar Jabin, Mubarak Ali and Fateh Ali, Muhammad Siddiq and Alaf Din Pathan, with no shrine or assembly attached to any of them (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 282, folio 262).

Rozehnal describes the quieter practice of a modern order in the city. His fieldwork included "frequent attendance at the zikr circles in Lahore" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 141, folio 133), and the full repertoire of remembrance he prints was recorded "at a gathering on a Thursday evening in October 2000" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 202, folio 194). Hospitality is refused at these gatherings: "That is why serving any food or even a cup of tea is strictly forbidden, because some people then might come just to socialize" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 203, folio 195).

Lahore drew pilgrims in and sent them out. Visiting the city's shrines was itself a practice of pilgrims passing through: Fakhr-ud-Din, on his first journey toward Ajmer, visited all the mazars of Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 756). Kasmani describes the city at the time of a saint's festival from the side of a fakir who arrived at Data Darbar: "It was the time of the saint's festival, and countless other fakirs had gathered in the city." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 65, folio 48). He also met a "visiting fakir from Lahore who in the autumn of 2011 was in Sehwan to perform his duty at the shrine of Lal", who kept his retreat by the river there (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 9). A Lahore woman of Shahdara is a regular at the Maryabad festival (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 97, folio not stated), where on the second day "the Bishop Sahib comes from Lahore" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 86, folio not stated).

Khalid traces the Milad procession to a single year and a single mosque. "In 1935, a group of about a hundred people left the premises of the historical Wazir Khan Mosque", singing praises of the Prophet, and walked "around the alleys of the walled city" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). The mosque's prayer leader, Maulana Syed Dedar Shah, had argued: "If the Hindus can arrange festivals and hold processions for their fake gods then can't we ... do the same for our Prophet [PBUH]", though "Most of his followers rejected his idea" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). "Soon after a man called Karam Elahi, also a resident of Lahore, organized his own little procession", and "Inayatullah Qadri, another religious scholar, started holding an annual Mehafal-e-Millad"; the event "crossed the boundaries of the walled city and spread to neighbouring villages and towns" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). In time the procession "had to be moved outside Delhi Gate, where a permanent platform was constructed", and "The junction where the procession converged every year got the name of Millad Chowk" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).

The minorities keep their own calendar in the city. Khalid's chapters follow Shri Valmiki's birthday, Krishna Janamashtami, Palm Sunday and Good Friday at the Sacred Heart Cathedral, Easter at the Cathedral Church of Resurrection, Navroz and Hazrat Bab's birthday in Lahore (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 5, folio not stated), and the commemorations of Guru Arjan's martyrdom at Gurdwara Dera Sahib and of Maharaja Ranjit Singh's death anniversary (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 6, folio not stated). "On religious festivals like Holi, Diwali and Shri Valmiki's birthday, the Neela Gumbad Mandir becomes an island of religious syncretism in Lahore" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 70, folio not stated). Before 9/11, a Catholic recalls, "a huge procession used to leave from the Don Bosco School" on Railway Road for the Sacred Heart Cathedral, and "All the Catholic Churches in the city would merge into that one procession"; the city's yearly religious conferences of about three thousand people were stopped at the government's request "Following the threats of rising terrorism" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 114–115, folio not stated).

Easter breakfast at the Cathedral Church of Resurrection is "halwa puri and channa, typical Lahori nashta" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 134, folio not stated). At Navroz, Daria Boatwala says, "Nothing really happens here as you can see" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 142, folio not stated), and tambola "is the only game they play in Lahore now" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 143, folio not stated). On a Sunday on Mall Road, cricket in all the grounds is "a usual Sunday feature" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 100, folio not stated).

Older practices and humbler customs linger at the edges of the record. "In Lahore, I met a man who claimed to have spent forty days inside a grave", Khalid writes in a passage on the forty day retreat (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated). Chishti records a Lahore custom of a humbler kind: the Shakir Shahi *konda* is famous in Lahore, made by Shakir Shah Kahar at two annas a *konda* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 275). The calendar of the saints is itself a Lahore product: Ernst and Lawrence write that "today one can still acquire current Urdu almanacs printed in Bombay and Lahore that prominently feature the death anniversaries of Sufi saints of the Indian subcontinent" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 105, folio 92). Of al-Busiri's *Burda*, the devotional ode, Schimmel writes: "Copies are still being sold in the shops of Lahore and elsewhere." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 256, folio 225). And in Bhandari's chronicle the army of Khalilullah Khan and Raja Jai Singh camped outside the city on the day of Eid al-Adha (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 67).

## In Verse

Lahore was a city of Persian poets under the Ghaznavids. Mas'ud-i-Sa'd-i-Salman's family came from Hamadan in Persia, but, as Browne's abridgement of Mirza Muhammad puts it, "he himself was born at Lahore, of which, in several passages in his poems, he speaks as his native place" (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 135, folio 117). He was the panegyrist of five Ghaznavid kings, Ibrahim, Mas'ud III, Shirzad, Arslan and Bahramshah (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 135, folio 117), and his special patron was Sultan Ibrahim's son Mahmud, "who was made governor of India in A.H. 469 (A.D. 1076-7)" (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 135, folio 117). "He was probably born about A.H. 440 (A.D. 1048-9) and died about A.H. 515 (A.D. 1121-2)" (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 135, folio 117).

He shared his master's disgrace, was imprisoned, was freed, and "returned to his father's estate in India"; when Mas'ud III gave the government of India to his son Shirzad, the commander in chief made the poet "governor of Jalandar, a dependency of Lahore" (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 135, folio 117). Imprisoned again, he was released about A.H. 500 and, being then sixty, "he retired from public life and spent the remainder of his days in seclusion" (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 135, folio 117). Sana'i, "who first collected and edited his poetry", is among those who bear witness to his pre-eminence (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 135, folio 117), and the poet Rashidi was "his contemporary and rival" (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 134, folio 116). Another poet of the age, Abu'l-Faraj-i-Runi, is tied to the city by his name: Runa, "from which he took his nisba, was a place near Lahore", and not, as other works assert, in Khurasan or near Nishapur (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 135, folio 117).

Madho Lal Hussain is Lahore's own poet in Naveed Alam's translation: "drunkenly he roams the streets of Lahore in the company of fellow carousers" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 6, folio 6). His verse was first printed in the city: an unknown Sindhi editor's Gurmukhi collection was "published in Lahore around 1900" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 17, folio 17), and the Punjab University library holds a manuscript, number 374, of 1804 (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 17, folio 17; Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 186, folio 186). Alam also names Najm Hosain Syed's play "Takht L'hore (Throne of Lahore)" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, pp. 13–14, folios 13–14; Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 179, folio 179).

The Mughal chronicle carries verse spoken in the city. Bhandari records a Persian couplet that Akbar recalled on the river below the fort. Setting out for Kashmir in the rains, the emperor, as he crossed the Ravi below the fort of Lahore, asked about whom this verse had been written (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 179):

کلاه خسروی و تاج شاہی
بہر گل کی رسد حاشا و کلا

Bhandari gives no rendering of the couplet; he tells it as part of the second journey of Akbar's court from Lahore to Kashmir, in the thirty seventh regnal year (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 179). In the same chronicle the blinded Shahryar, a prisoner in the fort of Lahore, spoke a quatrain whose last line says that the eye of the sun has gone blind (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 107), and Jahangir's palace at Lahore is followed by a ruba'i in its praise (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 135).

Kanhaiya Lal copies the verse inscriptions of the city's mosques. Over the gate of the mosque of Muhammad Salih Kambo three niches carry two lines naming its founder, and the year 1058 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 152, folio 155):

بانی این مسجد زیبا نگار
بنائے آل محمد صالح است

The lines name the founder of the mosque, and the book dates it 1058, uncertainly (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 152, folio 155).

The outer gateway of Masjid Chinian Wali carries a chronogram couplet:

طرفہ معمار خرد تاریخ سال
گفت زیبا مسجد از افراز خان[OCR?]

Kanhaiya Lal reads it as 1082 AH, against 1080 on the inner mihrab, both readings uncertain, and the last word of the couplet is uncertainly printed; from the two dates he concludes that two years passed between the building of the interior and of the outer gateway (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 153, folio 156).

A named local poet supplied one chronogram: Kanhaiya Lal says the quatrain is the composition of Farid, a poet of Lahore, and that Farid's line is written beneath it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 182, folio 185). Three of the four chronograms in those pages of the book call the mosque a second Kaaba (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 177, folio 180; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 178, folio 181; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 182, folio 185), and all four put the date in the mouth of the unseen voice, the *hatif* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 177, 178, 180, 182; folios 180, 181, 183, 185).

At Roshnai Gate he reads a fatal accident as recompense. A parapet fell suddenly on two men and dashed out their brains, and he quotes a Persian couplet on the requital of deeds; the two dead are not named (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 222, folio 225):

از مکافات عمل غافل مشو
ہو[OCR?] گندم از گندم بروبید[OCR?] جو ز جو

*A Persian couplet warning against heedlessness of the requital of deeds, in the image of wheat that grows from wheat and barley from barley; two of its words are uncertainly printed.* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 222, folio 225)

## The Place in the Books

Two of these books describe Lahore as a seat of poets and of rulers. For Mirza Muhammad, as Browne abridges him, Lahore is the birthplace and the native place of Ghaznavid poets, and the seat on which Jalandar depended (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 135, folio 117). For Bhandari, Lahore is a great city and a mighty metropolis, where craftsmen of every land live and the goods of the seven climes and the wares of sea and land are sold, one word of that passage uncertainly printed (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 524). He calls it the seat of the sultanate (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 512) and the capital of the realms (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 177), and Aram Shah's Lahore is a land that delights the heart (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400). His city is a capital of rulers, a store of treasure and arms, and a place that Islam Shah feared for its very greatness (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 260). His only description of the city itself, as distinct from its battles, is the passage in his account of the suba (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 523; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 524; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525).

Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti wrote his account of Lahore on the orders of the commissioner. The commissioner, Mr William Coldstream, the name uncertainly printed, ordered him to write a detailed account of the buildings, mazars, tombs and mosques of the environs of Lahore; Chishti calls the task very hard, because the history of hundreds of years had to be gathered (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 28). Elsewhere he writes that Lala Amin Chand's work on the buildings and people of Lahore was cut short when the same official, his name printed differently there, left for Shahjahanabad (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 734). He saw his book as the first full account of the city's antiquities. In the afterword he claims that no book had yet been written or printed that covered the antiquities of Lahore in full (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 872). He describes it as reporting on the tombs of the Muslim elders, on other buildings and on the old customs of Lahore, while being at root a history of the saints (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 29).

He wrote in conversation, and in argument, with earlier writers on the city. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar, he notes, had long sought an account of the elders of Lahore's environs and could not obtain it (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 31). He cites Lala Amin Chand's *Safarnama*, printed, as he gives it, by Koh Nur, and its account of the city of Lahore, and disputes it (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 733). He was also a witness. Of the heads of Raja Hira Singh and Pandit Jalla, he writes in the afterword that he saw with his own eyes what was done with them in the city (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 868). Naveed Alam, translating Madho Lal Hussain, names Nur Ahmad Chishti's history as "Tehqiqat-i-Chisti: Tarikh-i-Lahore ka Encyclopedia (Researches of Chisti: Encyclopedia of Lahore's History)" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 8, folio 8; Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 178, folio 178).

Kanhaiya Lal writes of Lahore's religious buildings as an engineer, a resident and a historian of property, and his hand shows in the description. He reconstructs vanished features in the presumptive mood: when the mosque was built its founder must have kept the plinth very high, and there must have been shops beneath the northern wall (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 153, folio 156). Where a building has no vocabulary of its own he supplies one, again and again with the phrase that a thing should be called so: the door of a veranda should be called the outer door of the temple (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 199, folio 202), one part should be called the fifth degree (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 197, folio 200), another a three arched seat (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 200, folio 203), and another the northern wall (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 199–200, folios 202–203). A house may rightly be called a collection of temples (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 129, folio 132), and a great hall should be thought of as a shivala (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 135, folio 138).

His judgements of beauty are either warranted by European opinion or delivered flat. The Wazir Khan mosque is famous among Europeans, it may rightly be called the pride and glory of Lahore, great painters learn their art from it, and travellers from far away come to Lahore and carry off copies of its niches (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 155, folio 158). The Badshahi Masjid is larger than the Jami Masjid of Delhi (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 139, folio 142). Of the Sufi Wali mosque he writes that it is a pity its dome was never built, for its beauty would have been doubled (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 173, folio 176). A parapet is shaped so that from a distance it looks like domes (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 172, folio 175), and the unrepaired Shiv Gar shivala in truth needs no repair, so solidly is it built (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 199, folio 202). At Sher Singh's modest samadh he gives a moral reading: the visitor's tears fall unbidden, and he becomes certain that the world is transient (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 210, folio 213). He reports a private donor's structural change with an engineer's interest: Nura Iman Wala repaired the Wazir Khan mosque at a cost of thousands of rupees, and of the eight openings above the dome of each of the four minarets he had four closed and four left open for greater strength (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 174, folio 177). At Nawab Mian Khan's tomb, he notes, when a mosque was built on the west a matching one was built on the east, purely for beauty (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 273, folio 276).

He dates many buildings by the years elapsed since their foundation, reckoned from a present he does not fix. The Teja Singh thakurdwara was built under English rule twenty five years ago on the site of a demolished old haveli (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 103, folio 106); Baikunth Das's thakurdwara was raised by subscription ten years ago (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 114, folio 117); the Shivala Ragnath Mishr is forty years old (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 116, folio 119), the Kuka dharamsal fifteen (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 105, folio 108), and Khuda Singh died eighteen years ago (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 102, folio 105). Bishno Devi's *devi dwara* is forty years old (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 121, folio 124), Chor Mor Wala was founded fifty years ago in Ranjit Singh's time (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 124, folio 127), Jwala Dai's mandir nine years ago (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 127, folio 130), the Ram Dwara six years ago, the figure uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 127–128, folios 130–131), Mehr Das's own mandir not less than two hundred years ago (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 129, folio 132), and the Chhajju Bhagat thakurdwara ten or twelve years ago (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 135–136, folios 138–139). Masjid Muhammad Salih Kambo is two hundred and twenty six years old, the figure uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 152, folio 155), and the Wazir Khan mosque two hundred and fifty one (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 155, folio 158). Shiv Gar died forty years ago (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 199, folio 202) and Daryagar ten (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 209, folio 212); the Ganga Parbat mandirs were founded twenty five years ago, the figure uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 215, folio 218); two hundred years have passed since Shah Bilawal's exhumation (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 235, folio 238); and the Pir Sabz tree is nearly two centuries old (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 162, folio 165). The only statement of his own moment in these pages dates the present time to Samvat 1937, the year uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 130, folio 133), so none of these spans can be turned into a year without fixing when the book was written.

Where he has no year at all he dates by reign: in the reign of Shah Jahan (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 184, folio 187); in Akbar's time, when the foundation of a mandir was laid that still stands, and to which devotees come in numbers because of its antiquity (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 214, folio 217); of former times (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 181, folio 184); built entirely in the Sikh period (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 230, folio 233); built in the reign of Ahmad Shah (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 169, folio 172); in the time of the Tughluq sultanate (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 162, folio 165) and of Sultan Muhammad Tughluq (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 149, folio 152); in the Alamgiri period (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 150, folio 153; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 145, folio 148); and in the Lodi sultanate (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 151, folio 154). In the closing pages of the book the frames of the Chughtai kings, the kings of old, the Sikh government and the English government stand side by side without comment, with the revolution of the kingdom among them, and both 1857 and the fall of the Sikhs are called only a disturbance, *mufsida* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 396, folio 403; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 399, folio 406; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 444–455, folios 451–462). He frames the city's history as a sequence of destructions. His contents list thirteen numbered shocks, *sadme*, running from his printed page 17 to 31, followed by the present form of the city of Lahore at 32 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 2), announced as an account of the ages in which the shocks of plunder and demolition fell upon the city (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 11, folio 6; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 12, folio 7).

He characterises Sikh rule as the period of loss and English rule as the period of restitution, in four separate cases close together: the confiscated shops (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 148, folio 151), the powder magazine (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 150, folio 153), the stripped gateway (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 153, folio 156) and the encroached chowk (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 158, folio 161). The pattern is his own framing. Elsewhere he reports both regimes in the same register with different verbs: the English demolishing cells for some reason of expediency and cutting windows for their own troops (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 140–141, folios 143–144) while also convening *darbars*, subscribing, and restoring mosques by name and date (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 142–143, 146; folios 145–146, 149); and the Sikh regime confiscating, quartering artillery, prising up flooring and transferring endowments (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 140, 143, 147; folios 143, 146, 150). He draws no general conclusion from the contrast. On the city's defences he gives the administrations' own reasons, that so high a wall was thought superfluous, that the work was done to remove a nuisance, and that the time of the English peace had come, without endorsing or contesting them (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 15, 21; folios 10, 16).

Where he vouches for the state's fairness, the words are his own characterisation, not a record of claims. In his account the government, as a matter of justice, paid the subjects full compensation for every loss; land taken from houses was compensated to the subjects' own satisfaction, so that no one was displeased; flood damage was paid to the last penny; and for the rebuilt tank the government paid the full price for the houses, cleared the ground and began building the tank (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 38, folio 41). This was a compulsory acquisition inside the walled city, and he records no dissent and documents no claim or refusal. He is also a party to what he reports. State repairs ran through him as Executive Engineer, Shalamar Bagh's annual repair grant among them (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 348, folio 355), and at the Badshahi Masjid, a building in his own division, he asks the administration in print for a further repair grant, saying that if it would turn its attention to the mosque once more, the mosque would be repaired in full (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 142, folio 145).

His own economic thesis is a counterfactual. Of the Wazir Khan mosque he writes that had the property's income not been attached to it, the very form of its habitation would have been lost (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 158, folio 161). His standing explanation of a shrine's prosperity is the man in charge, not the saint. Dola Wari held its height because some holy man or other always lived there through whom blessing reached all of a company named by a word uncertainly printed (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 80, folio 83). Bhai Prem Singh, he writes, is a blessed and courteous man, and it is because of him that the place is well peopled (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 103, folio 106).

The same reasoning recurs across the traditions. The Teja Singh thakurdwara's splendour was doubled on its sadhu's account until he left (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 103–104, folios 106–107). Of the Brahman Dev Yadatta[OCR?] he writes that because of him the shivala is in great splendour (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 122, folio 125); of Pandit Kanshi Ram, that because of him the place's income, as an uncertainly printed word seems to read, grows day by day (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 128, folio 131); of a mosque, that while its founder lived, the words naming him uncertainly printed, it was in full splendour, and now its splendour is little (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 182, folio 185). Of a lecture platform he writes that while Pandit Sardha Ram Phaluri[OCR?] lived there was great splendour at the place, and that after his death Pandit Gopi Nath, editor of the *Akhbar-e-Aam*, lectures there, but that splendour does not come (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 441, folio 448).

One passage must be read as his judgement rather than as description: a hostile portrait of the patron community of a mosque. Of the Sadhus of Takiya Sadhuwan, Muslim Kashmiris who came under Sikh rule, he writes that they were all tenants once and have now become owners of property and wealthy; that they ply no visible trade or calling, their business being to bring in money by every stratagem; that wherever they go they change their names, one calling himself Sufi Shah, another Shatranji Shah, another Miskin Shah; and that one becomes a faqir on the path, another a *majzub*, another a prince (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 170–171, folios 173–174). A named author here accuses a named Lahore community of impersonating faqirs for a living.

He names the texts he relies on, and where they fail him. *Tuhfat al-Wasilin* is his textual authority twice, its title spelt two ways (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 164, folio 167; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 165, folio 168), and it is silent where he needed it: of Pir Bai, the name uncertain, no one knows the real name of the person buried there, and the author of *Tuhfat al-Wasilin* did not record it in his book either (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 165, folio 168). *Haqiqat al-Fuqara* by Pir Muhammad is a Persian verse life of Lal Husain written by an eyewitness (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 233, folio 236). Munshi Inayatullah, author of *Bahar-e Danish*, was one man's maternal uncle and father in law (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 152, folio 155). One sect has its own granth in a script whose name is uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 186, folio 189). And for the marble of Bahadur Khan's tomb he has only the testimony of old people (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 258, folio 261).

Modern scholars saw Lahore from very different distances. For Rozehnal it was home in the field: he spent the academic year of 1995 and 1996 studying Urdu in the city (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 21, folio 10), and writes, "In Pakistan, I was based in the cultural capital of Lahore" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 26, folio 15). A photograph of the dead shaykh circulated among "several friends in Lahore" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 21, folio 10). Naveed Alam translates Madho Lal Hussain from the city itself: his introduction is signed at its foot "Lahore / August 2015" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 19, folio 19).

For Abbas the city's saints are a genre of local writing, and she cites it: M. Latif Malik's *Aulīyā-e Lahore* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 211, 228, folios not stated), Samina Qureshi's *Lahore: The City Within* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 231, folio not stated), and Kanhyalal's *Tarikh-e Lahore* in a Lahore reprint (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 193, 211, 226, folios not stated). The years of all three are lost in the printed text. Lahore is also the base from which she works: "Kasur is seventy miles from Lahore" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 86, folio not stated); she "started from Lahore on a beautiful spring afternoon" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 105, folio not stated); and "Khalida and Jaleel Naqvi participated in the fieldwork in Lahore and Kasur" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 30, folio not stated). The Naqvis were custodians of the shrine of Hazrat Pir Sabir at Paran Kalyar, interviewed in Lahore (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 217, folio not stated). She places Jhang Siyal "to the southwest of Lahore in Pakistan" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 121–122, folios not stated), and notes that landlords' sons now "live in large urban centers like Karachi, Lahore, or Islamabad" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 81, folio not stated).

Other studies name the city only in passing. In Pnina Werbner and Helene Basu's volume, Lahore marks the far edge of the area from which volunteers come to serve at the *ʿurs* at Ghamkol Sharif (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 122, folio 111). It is also the city to which one woman, Rabi‘a, returns, and the frame for its neighbourhood of Samnabad (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 180, folio 169; Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 188, folio 177). Jürgen Schaflechner's study of Hinglaj names Lahore as a Punjabi city in its survey of Pakistani Hinduism (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 28, folio not stated), and as the home of an informant of the Nath sect (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 138, folio not stated). It attaches no shrine, saint or festival to the city. Ernst and Lawrence record that the twentieth century Chishti Zauqi Shah worked as a journalist in Peshawar, Lahore, Hyderabad in Sind and Karachi (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 139, folio 126).

Rozehnal also shows the city as the publishing centre of the order he studied. A Sufi Foundation founded by a senior murid "published a series of translations and original writings by Wahid Bakhsh during the 1980s"; Bazm-i Ittehad al-Muslimin was "founded in 1984 by the shaykh's nephew"; and "Beginning in 1996, Talifat-i Shaheedi, established by another murid of Shahidullah Faridi, has published four additional texts, three of them in English" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 130, folio 121). Commercial houses carry the rest, among them Ziya al-Qur'an, Qausin Publishers and al-Faisal Publishers, and "At present, some fifty different texts are produced and distributed from a number of publishing houses" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 130, folio 121). The order's books reach readers through "the Urdu bookstores of Karachi and Lahore" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 111, folio 102). He cites the city's press too, "the Lahore-based English language weekly, The Friday Times" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 37, folio 27), and measures another great shrine from it: Pakpattan is "120 miles southwest of Lahore" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 35, folio 25).

The same city of print appears in the older scholarship as a place of publication, and that is all it is there. Schimmel's bibliography lists Lahore houses that printed mystical poetry, among them Ashraf, the University of the Punjab, Urdu Dunya and Allah wale ki qaumi dukan, whose numbered chapbook series carried Panjabi mystical verse (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, pp. 318, 321, 325, 326, 329, 331, 333, 340, 341). That series included a Panjabi version of the *Burda*, *Qasida-i Burda Panjabi* (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 331, folio 313), a commentary on Bullhe Shah (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 333, folio 315), and a collection of Panjabi mystical verse with no author named (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 329, folio 311). She notes two Panjabi verse translations published at Lahore, by Pir Imam Shah and by Maulana Muhammad Shahuddin, the first in a year whose reading is uncertain in the printed text and the second in 1939 (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 254, folio 236). Qureshi names Lahore publishers of the literature on qawwali, among them Sheikh Muhammad Ashraf and the Institute of Islamic Culture (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, pp. 269, 272, 273, 275; folios 249, 252, 253, 255).

Lahore is also a city of manuscripts and collectors. The Punjab University Library holds the "unpublished original manuscript" of *Haqiqat al-Fuqara'* (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 218, folio 197, ch. 4 n. 27), under the shelfmark 3253/248 Farsi (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 338, folio not stated); Kugle searched "libraries in Lahore" for a printing said to date from 1966 and found none (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 328, folio 307, ch. 4 n. 27). Ernst and Lawrence owe one of their manuscripts to "Mr. Khalil al-Rahman Dawoodi of Lahore" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 159, folio 147), the same man who gave them a manuscript copied at Taunsa (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 243, folio 233).

For Khalid, Lahore is home and field. "Based as I am in Lahore, much of my fieldwork took place in the city itself and its vicinity. This is bound to have had its limitations." (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 233, folio not stated). He reads the colonial city on a Sunday drive: "Mall Road, one of the busiest boulevards in the city is deserted", and "A drive along the road is a journey through the colonial era", past the Lahore High Court, "combining European and Eastern architectural techniques" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 100, folio not stated). The National College of Arts was "established in Lahore in 1857" and is "considered to be the most prestigious art college in the country" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 47, folio not stated).

He watches the city change. The Anarkali market "is named after the legendary lover of the Mughal Emperor Jahangir" and "over the years has become the largest tyre market in the city", and "The Pak Tea House, just a few shops away, once famed for its gatherings of the leading intelligentsia of the city, is now on its way to becoming yet another tyre shop" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 66, folio not stated). Rettigan Road is, for one informant, "a prominent locality in colonial Lahore, now taken over by small businesses and shops and no longer the clean spacious area that it once was" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 140, folio not stated). Behind the Roshani gate, "the musicians remain. This is still one of the largest markets for traditional instruments in the city" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 205, folio not stated). The Ravi, which once flowed next to the fort, "has changed its course, moving a few kilometres away" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 195, folio not stated), and today "the mighty Ravi of history flows like a stream in Lahore" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 281, folio 255).

In *In Search of Shiva* the metropolis swallows its villages. The Defence Housing Authority, "the neatest and most expensive residential area of Lahore", "sprawls across a vast tract of land, eating into the small historic villages in the area", whose people, having sold "their abundant land for peanuts to the housing authority", are left "Congested and inflicted with poverty" behind a wall (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated). Hanjarwal is "a village which had been subsumed by the ever-growing metropolis of Lahore" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 7, ch. 1, folio not stated). His guide Iqbal Qaiser "was working on an encyclopedia of Lahore, the first of its kind", in five parts of about a thousand pages each, drawn from land revenue records and government archives, until "administrative changes at the Punjab Cultural Institute put an untimely end to his project" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated).

In *Walking with Nanak* Lahore is a crossroads and a city of crafts. The Grand Trunk Road "connected with Lahore and then Delhi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115, folio 97), and the caravanserai route ran through "Lahore, Satgaraha, Deepalpur, Harrapa, Multan and Uch Sharif" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 119, folio 101); "The Bedian road is the old route to Amritsar" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 38, folio 20). "The Kashmiri binders who used to reside in Lahore were known for their meticulous binding" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 39, folio 21), and Bhai Ram Singh was Principal of "Mayo School of Arts in Lahore" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 117, folio 99). Rohtas, Khalid writes, "was not the kind of fort that was found in Lahore, where only royalty stayed" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 249, folio 223).

Iqbal Qaiser's lecture on Sikh architecture in Lahore argued that "like the Sikh religion, which picks up from both Hinduism and Islam, Sikh architecture draws inspiration from both Hindu and Muslim architecture", and that domes once part of Muslim buildings, made smaller by the Sikhs, are such that "Now such domes are also used in Muslim buildings in Pakistan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 309–310, folio not stated / folio 284). Khalid sees new apartments "built recently to cater to the growing demands of urban Lahore" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 36, folio 18), and indigenous trees "now being replaced by imported ones in the developed areas of Lahore" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 46, folio 28). And he calls it "a city like Lahore, where everyone wears their religion on their" sleeve, the last word lost in the printed copy (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 174, folio 148).
