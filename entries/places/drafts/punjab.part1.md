## The Province in History

Kanhaiya Lal's *Tarikh-e Lahore* is a history of one city, but the Punjab runs through it as the larger unit to which the city belongs, the country whose capital Lahore became and lost and became again. His Punjab begins before Islam. Following Shaikh Ahmad Zanjani, author of the treatise *Tuhfat al-Wasilin*, an account he sets against that of the author of the *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, he relates that when Raja Deb Chand sat on the throne of Delhi he gave the territory of the Punjab in *jagir* to his brother Lohar Chand, part of the passage being illegible in the printed text, and that once his hold over the Punjab was complete he made the city of Lahore his capital (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 14, folio 9).

The Muslim conquest of the province is told through its rulers at Lahore. When Sultan Mahmud and Sabuktigin of Ghazni attacked the Punjab, Raja Jaipal, a Brahman and ruler of the Punjab, held throne and crown at Lahore (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 14, folio 9). Kanhaiya Lal dates the first attack on Lahore to the year 391 AH, the reading of the year uncertain in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 22, folio 17). Mahmud, he writes, wished to drive Raja Jaipal out of Lahore and bring the territory of the Punjab under his own rule, and after the sack of the city Malik Ayaz, his name uncertainly printed, was named to the administration of the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 17, folio 12). The book returns to this later, when Malik Ayaz became ruler of the Punjab by the king's order (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 23, folio 18).

The province was the last refuge of the house of Ghazni. When the Ghurids drove the dynasty out of Ghazni, Khusrau Shah, father of Khusrau Malik, set up his government in the Punjab; Khusrau Malik sat on the throne of Lahore and his rule was established over the Punjab, part of the sentence being illegible; and with his surrender to Sultan Shihab al-Din Ghuri, the Ghurid sultanate in Hind began and the Ghaznavid government came to its end (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 24, folio 19). After Shihab al-Din's death Taj al-Din Yalduz seized Ghazni and then came to the Punjab to take the throne of Hind from Qutb al-Din; in a second rising, in the reign of Sultan Shams al-Din Iltutmish, he took Multan, came to Lahore, plundered the Punjab without restraint, and crossed the Sutlej to advance on Delhi (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 25, folio 20).

The armies of Chingiz Khan's line follow. Kanhaiya Lal reports the author of the *Tuhfat al-Wasilin* as saying that Sultan Jalal al-Din Khwarazmi crossed the river of Sind and entered the Punjab, and that when Chingiz Khan's grandson Quli Khan invaded the Punjab, the army of Delhi and the Mughals fought at Lahore (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 165, folio 168). Mughal is the book's own word for these armies.

Kanhaiya Lal then counts the blows that fell on the province one after another. The fourth came in the time of Sultan Jalal al-Din Firuz Shah Khalji, when a Mughal army sent by a Chaghatai Khan, ruler of Transoxiana, his name partly illegible, came into the Punjab; there was famine in the Punjab at the time, plunder and pillage ruined the whole region, and villages and towns were left deserted (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 26, folio 29). The sultan, named in the same passage, in an uncertain reading, as Shams al-Din Iltutmish, was away on the Gujarat campaign and could do nothing to help the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 26, folio 29). After his victory on the Sutlej the sultan resettled the villages of the Punjab that had been left empty and without a lamp, distributed the Tatars' loot among the poor subjects of the Punjab, and posted a large army on the frontier (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 27, folio 30).

The fifth blow fell in the time of Sultan Muhammad Tughluq. A Tatar army came from the west into the Punjab and both Dipalpur and Lahore were plundered; the rulers of the Punjab made every effort but fled to Delhi; when the army had gone the king came to the Punjab, worked hard to resettle its people, and beheaded the governor of Multan, whose name is printed uncertainly as Bahram, because the Tatar army had come into the Punjab at his signal (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 27, folio 30). The sixth came in the reign of Firuz Shah Tughluq, when the Tatar Mughal army arrived in the Punjab to plunder; the king, then besieging a place whose printed name cannot be read with certainty, came back to the Punjab and after a bloody battle drove the Tatar army out of it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 27–28, folios 30–31).

The seventh came in the reign of Sultan Muhammad Shah son of Firuz Shah, the rest of his name uncertain in the printed text, and with Amir Timur's invasion. A man named Sikha, of the Gakkhar tribe, both names uncertainly printed, came with a great host from the country belonging to Hazara, again an uncertain reading, entered the Punjab, drove the royal officers out of the country and laid waste thousands of settlements; Muhammad Shah sent Prince Humayun with an army to the Punjab; Amir Timur passed through the foothills of the Punjab and entered Delhi; and on his return the people of the Punjab, the word before the name partly illegible, laid before the Amir their complaint of Sikha's killing and plunder (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 28, folio 31). The eighth came in the reign of Sultan Mubarak Shah son of Khizr Khan, after the year 820 AH, the reading of the year uncertain, when Jasrath, Sikha Gakkhar's brother, also raised great disorder in the Punjab, and the king ordered Sultan Shah Lodi, governor of Sirhind, to take his army and drive Jasrath out of the Punjab; there the sentence breaks off at the turn of the page (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 29, folio 32).

Under the Mughal emperors the Punjab is a prince's *jagir* and a *suba*. Humayun attached Langar Khan to his brother Prince Kamran, in whose *jagir* the territory of the Punjab lay (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 76, folio 79). In Shah Jahan's reign Prince Dara Shikoh, *jagirdar* of the Punjab, laid out the Chauk Dara Shikoh in Lahore (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 91, folio 94), and when the government of the Punjab was entrusted to him he put up a great many buildings (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 139, folio 142). The *suba* had its *diwans*: in Shah Jahan's reign, while Nawab Wazir Khan held the governorship, the Andhi Haveli was built by a Hindu amir who was *diwan* of the *suba* of the Punjab, and his descendants held it until the end of the Chaghatai rule (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 336, folio 343). Nawab Zakariya Khan Bahadur, Shahnawaz Khan and other rulers of the Punjab lived in Mughalpura (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 90, folio 93), and Mir Sayyid Bhikari Khan, his name uncertainly printed, was raised by Muhammad Shah's order to the office of deputy of the *suba* of the region of the Punjab, together with a second office whose title cannot be read with certainty (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 146, folio 149).

The last Mughal decades are told through the province's defence against the Durrani. In Muhammad Shah's time Jani Khan, with his brother Mir Muin al-Mulk, defeated Ahmad Shah Durrani, who went back to Kabul; after he had gone Mir Muin al-Mulk ran the administration of the Punjab in his own way, and Jani Khan remained commander of the army of the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 270, folio 273). Kanhaiya Lal gives this account under the tomb where Jani Khan is buried, and prints his death in the year 1194, the reading uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 270, folio 273).

The end of Muslim rule came with famine and plunder. When the rule of Islam passed from the Punjab and the Sikhs gained strength, Kanhaiya Lal writes, the first place the Sikhs plundered was the butchers' quarter of Lahore (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 89–90, folios 92–93). When the Sikh period came, a severe famine struck the Punjab, after which the descendants of the Andhi Haveli's builder left Lahore (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 336, folio 343). When the turn of the age overturned the Islamic state and the Sikhs took Lahore, the whole region of the Punjab was laid waste by famine, after which Mir Jawad's descendants left Lahore and the officials took over his haveli (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 342, folio 349).

Ranjit Singh's Punjab is the province at its widest in the book. Kanhaiya Lal's preface calls him the Maharaja, ruler of the region of the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 9, folio 4), and under his government the whole region of the Punjab came into his hands (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 112, folio 115). He ruled the Punjab for forty years, and besides the Punjab the country of Kashmir, the Derajat and other lands were also in his possession (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220). The infantry of Maharaja Ranjit Singh, ruler of the Punjab, whose number the printed text gives as not less than seventy thousand in wording that is not clean, drew its pay through Bakhshi Bhagat Ram (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 96, folio 99). Ranjit Singh struck coin in the name of Moran, the courtesan, and her coin ran through the whole of the Punjab; Moran Shahi rupees and paisa are still kept as keepsakes (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 179, folio 182). The book says the same in another place: her stamp was struck on rupees and paisa, and Moran Shahi paisa and rupees still exist (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 47, folio 50).

After Ranjit Singh's death his own son, Maharaja Kharak Singh, sat on the throne of the government of the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 221, folio 224). After the deaths of Maharaja Kharak Singh and Kanwar Nau Nihal Singh, Rani Chand Kaur became mistress of the kingdom of the Punjab and was seated on the *gaddi* on the proposal of the Sandhanwalia sardars, who became the managers of the state, while Raja Dhian Singh, the wazir, went off displeased to Jammu (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 443, folio 450). Maharaja Sher Singh, ruler of the Punjab, whose reign lasted two and a half years, is in Kanhaiya Lal's words that emperor of the region of the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 210, folio 213).

Then the English became masters of the Punjab, after the end of Sikh rule (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 33, folio 36). Bakhshi Bhagat Ram's *jagir* had not yet been released when the government annexed the country of the Punjab, after which the Chief Commissioner fixed his pension (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 68, folio 71). When Maharaja Dalip Singh was exiled and English rule over the Punjab was complete, the troops left the Badshahi Mosque (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 141, folio 144).

In Kanhaiya Lal's own day Lahore is the capital of the *suba* of the Punjab. The court of the Lieutenant-Governor and his secretaries sits there, and its authority runs over the whole *suba* of the Punjab: chiefs, rajas and maharajas, commissioners and deputy commissioners are all subordinate to it; the second court is that of the Financial Commissioner and Collector of the Punjab; and the Chief Court gives the final decision in civil and criminal cases, hears the appeals from the commissioners of the *suba*, and must sanction every capital sentence (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 60, folio 63). The book names Egerton as Lieutenant-Governor of the territories of the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 148, folio 151) and McLeod, his name uncertainly printed, as a former Lieutenant-Governor of the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 213, folio 216). Robert Montgomery, Lieutenant-Governor of the territories of the Punjab, was long a ruler of the Punjab and rose from the office of commissioner to Lieutenant-Governor; Colonel Lawrence was Lieutenant-Governor and chief ruler of the Punjab at the time of the Delhi rising, and by his great labour the administration of the Punjab held together and he kept raising a large army and sending it to Delhi, for which he was made Governor General and Viceroy of India; and for the two halls named after them the amirs and officials of the Punjab subscribed most willingly (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 399, folio 406).

The province's institutions gathered in its capital. The Central Jail at Lahore was built as a general jail for the prisoners of the whole region of the Punjab: every district has its district jail for short sentences, but prisoners with long terms are sent here from every district (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 410, folio 417). Women prisoners from all the districts of the Punjab come to the women's prison, built in 1868, the reading of the year uncertain, under the author's own supervision (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 409, folio 416). The Lahore museum building was put up in 1864, when the government first held an exhibition of the wonders of the region of the Punjab, and the great states of the Punjab and its sardars and *jagirdars* sent jewels, textiles, arms and manufactures to it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 402, folio 409). In January 1882, the reading of the year uncertain, an exhibition of the industrial arts of the Punjab was held in the School of Art building under the principal of the School of Art, whose name is uncertainly printed (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 387, folio 394).

## Saints and Pilgrimage

The province's saints appear in the book where their memory touches its history. Kanhaiya Lal continues an account which, he says, the author of the *Hadiqat al-Awliya* gives on the authority of a *Tazkira* whose title is uncertainly printed: when in the year 615 AH the infidel Mughals invaded the Punjab in pursuit of Sultan Jalal al-Din Khwarazmi, and the people of Lahore were put to death for resisting them (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 305, folio 308). That invasion is the setting of the tradition that the Bibis of Bibi Pak Daman prayed to be taken into the earth (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 305, folio 308). The Punjab also drew learned men from afar: in Shah Jahan's reign Abul Khair came from Baghdad touring the countries, and because the climate of the Punjab pleased him he settled in Lahore, where his madrasa stood at Garhi Shahu (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 362–363, folios 369–370).

## Communities

Kanhaiya Lal's Punjab is a country of Hindus and Muslims serving the same state. Under the Mughal *suba* a Hindu amir served as *diwan* of the *suba* of the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 336, folio 343), and in the time of Nawab Zakariya Khan Bahadur, *suba* of Lahore, the brothers Diwan Lakhpat Rai and Jaspat Rai were *diwans* of the government of the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 337, folio 344). Of their wealth he tells that at his son's wedding Lakhpat Rai gave a gift to every Hindu and every Muslim in Lahore, a gift whose printed description, which seems to include a weight of sugar measured in seers, cannot be read with certainty (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 337, folio 344).

The Muslims of the Punjab under Sikh rule appear through the plea of the Fakir sahibs. Pleading with Ranjit Singh for the mosque by the Baoli, they told the Maharaja that nowhere in the mosques of the whole Punjab did the mullas give the call to prayer in a loud voice, and they offered a bond that the mulla would not give the call (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 148, folio 151).

The people of the province moved with famine and fortune. A few years before he wrote, famine fell on Kashmir and thousands of famine stricken Kashmiris came into the Punjab, hundreds of them reaching Lahore itself (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 75, folio 78). Its old ruling class kept its habits: under English rule silk drawstrings went out of use and everyone wears cotton, but the amirs of the Punjab are still accustomed to silk ones (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 47, folio 50).

## Language, Poetry and Music

For Kanhaiya Lal the Punjab had a story and a language of its own. In his preface he writes that the tale of Heer and Ranjha is on the lips of good and bad, young and old, rich and fakir in the country of the Punjab; many books on it exist in the Punjabi language, but no one other than the Punjabis could profit from them, which is why he wrote his own version in Persian (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 10, folio 5).

Books reached the province late. In Sikh times, he writes, no printed book came from Hindustan into the Punjab, and scribes wrote out manuscript books and sold them (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 42, folio 45).

The *suba* of the Punjab is named in verse on the buildings of its governors. On the northern *mihrab* of the mosque of Nawab Zakariya Khan, west of the mazar of Madho Lal Hussain, a tile inscription that Kanhaiya Lal copied carries these lines (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 262–263, folios 265–266).

زبدۂ بارگاہ او نواب
زکریا خان صوبہ پنجاب

The verse names the mosque's builder, Nawab Zakariya Khan of the *suba* of the Punjab, and the inscription places the building in the reign of Muhammad Shah (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 262–263, folios 265–266).

## Shrines and the State

Every ruler of the Punjab left a mark on its religious buildings. In Shah Jahan's reign Munshi Muhammad Salih, *diwan* of the *suba* of the Punjab, built a mosque that was completed in the year 1058, the reading of the year uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 152, folio 155), and Dara Shikoh built a great deal while he governed the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 139, folio 142). Under Ranjit Singh the Moti Masjid in the fort was renamed the Moti Mandir and held the treasury of the state, and the English government too keeps the treasury of the *suba* of the Punjab in it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 339, folio 346). Under the same Maharaja the Fakir sahibs bargained for a mosque by the Baoli by promising that its mulla would not give the call aloud (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 148, folio 151), and the troops left the Badshahi Mosque when English rule over the Punjab was complete (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 141, folio 144).

The new rulers lived in the old buildings. When Sikh rule passed from the Punjab and the country of the Punjab came under the shadow of the English government, an octagonal *kothi* built over a tomb housed in turn Mr Bowring, Deputy Commissioner, Major Macgregor, Deputy Commissioner, both names uncertainly printed, and Montgomery, Commissioner, who in the end became Lieutenant-Governor of the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 395, folio 402).

## The Province in the Books

For Kanhaiya Lal the Punjab is a unit with many names, and he uses them all: the *ilaqa* or territory of the Punjab given in *jagir* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 14, folio 9; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 76, folio 79), the Mughal *suba* of the Punjab with its *diwan* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 152, folio 155), the *khitta* or region of the Punjab that Ranjit Singh ruled (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 9, folio 4), the kingdom of the Punjab that passed to Rani Chand Kaur (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 443, folio 450), the country of the Punjab that the English annexed (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 68, folio 71), and the *suba* of the Punjab whose capital in his own day is Lahore (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 60, folio 63).

He draws its edges by what crosses them. Jalal al-Din entered the Punjab by crossing the river of Sind (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 165, folio 168); the Tatars came into it from the west (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 27, folio 30); Sikha Gakkhar entered it from the country belonging to Hazara, and Timur passed through its foothills on his way to Delhi (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 28, folio 31); the Sutlej is the river Yalduz crossed on his way from the Punjab toward Delhi (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 25, folio 20). Kashmir and the Derajat were Ranjit Singh's possessions besides the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220), the Kashmiris came into the Punjab in their famine (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 75, folio 78), and printed books came from Hindustan into it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 42, folio 45).

He was also a servant of the province he described. The women's prison of the Punjab was built under his supervision (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 409, folio 416), and he describes the province's governors, courts and jails as they stood in his own day (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 60, folio 63). His account of its past leans on earlier writers, whom he names: the *Tuhfat al-Wasilin* of Shaikh Ahmad Zanjani, set against the author of the *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 14, folio 9), and the *Hadiqat al-Awliya* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 305, folio 308).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- The sultan of the fourth blow: Kanhaiya Lal sets it in the time of Sultan Jalal al-Din Firuz Shah Khalji, but names the sultan who was away on the Gujarat campaign, in an uncertain reading, as Shams al-Din Iltutmish (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 26, folio 29).
- The title of Zakariya Khan's office: the tile inscription on his mosque calls him Zakariya Khan of the *suba* of the Punjab, while the book's own heading to the same entry calls him Nawab Zakariya Khan, known as Khan Bahadur, of the *suba* of Lahore (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 262–263, folios 265–266); elsewhere the book calls him Nawab Zakariya Khan Bahadur, *suba* of Lahore (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 337, folio 344) and counts him among the rulers of the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 90, folio 93).
