## Language, Poetry and Music

The Punjab of these books is above all a land of mystical poetry in its own tongue. Schimmel writes that "Reliable tradition traces the use of the native language for mystical poetry both in Sind and in the Punjab back to the fifteenth century", and that Madho Lal Hussein's allusions to the stories of Hir and of Sohni and Mahiwal seem to inaugurate, or to continue from unknown beginnings, "a tradition typical of the religious poetry of the two provinces: the insertion of folk tales into the context of mystical thought" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384). In another book she suggests that "In the Panjab, popular mystical poetry may have emerged as early as in the days of Fariduddin Shakarganj", the Chishti saint of Pakpattan who died in 1265, and that "Numerous folk songs and ballads existed but were never committed to writing" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 16, folio 12). In a third she places the development of folk tales as media for mystical instruction by the poets of Sindh and the Punjab only in the centuries after the death of the historian Badaʾuni (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 154, folio 136). Persian poetry, meanwhile, was common ground in Guru Nanak's time, in Rizvi's argument: "all literate Panjabis, both Hindus and Muslims, at the time of Guru Nanak were conversant with" 'Attar, Rumi and Sa'di (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 420, folio 396).

The *kafi* carried the mystics' teaching. Naveed Alam writes that "Kafis, the stanzaic poems couched in folkloric themes and techniques, were easy to understand and memorize, and thus served as the ideal means to disseminate the mystical teachings among the illiterate masses dependent on the oral transmission of knowledge", and that "Even to this day, most Punjabis and Sindhis are more likely to have heard a kafi than read one" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 15, folio 15). He sets Shah Hussein in the Punjab of Akbar, recalling a rebellion in Punjab led by Abdullah Bhatti, known as Dullah Bhatti, "who was captured and hanged in public on Akbar’s orders" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 14, folio 14). He notes that Najm Hosain Syed's play *Takht L’hore (Throne of Lahore)* "extrapolates the socio-economic conditions of Punjab" in Shah Hussein's time (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 14, folio 14).

"The golden age of Muslim Panjabi poetry", in Schimmel's account, "is the late 17th and the 18th century, e.g., the time when the province was invaded one time after the other by Mahrattas, Sikhs, and, in between, by the liberating Muslim armies" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 17, folio 13). Comparing Shah Abdul Latif of Sindh, she finds the similarities with "Panjabi mystical folk poetry, which reached its apex with Bullhe Shah during exactly the same period" conspicuous, and notes that Siraiki "forms the transition to Panjabi", so that the imagery of all three linguistic areas is closely related, as one would expect among the people of mainly rural provinces (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 154, folio 154). Shah Abdul Latif, she writes, represents the rural mystical tradition "in which his contemporaries in the Panjab and, to a certain extent, in the Pashto speaking areas were rooted" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 267, folio 267).

Schimmel draws the Punjab and Sindh together as one literary landscape. "The Punjab and the Indus valley have a common tradition of mystical folk literature" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 414, folio 383), and "The Punjab and Sind show close similarities in the types of mystical literature that nourished there"; Punjabi and Sindhi "were excellent media to express mystical feelings, though not mystical theories", their themes "sung time and again in forms inherited from the Indian past", not in the Persian *ghazal*, forms "closely connected with indigenous musical modes" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 416, folio 385). The mystics of the north west of the Subcontinent took up the attack on the mulla, and such verses "appealed particularly to the peasants and the illiterate" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 416, folio 385). "In both provinces, a preference for the motif of spinning and weaving can be observed, a natural propensity in a cotton-growing country"; both also produced hymns praising the great masters of the orders, above all ʿAbd al-Qadir Gilani, in imagery that "could easily be understood by everybody in the village" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 417, folio 386). In *Pain and Grace* she adds that "A topic which is not rarely found in the poetry of Sind and the Panjab is the spinning of cotton, one of the most important occupations of women", sung to traditional folk melodies, whose printed name the text renders uncertainly as Kapa'iti, that the womenfolk sang while spinning (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 160, folio 160).

The same poetry turned to the Prophet and to the soul. While the urban poets of India praised the Prophet in grandiose hymns, "their contemporaries in the plains of Sind and the Punjab wrote tender little poems or instructive Golden Alphabets to instil love for him into the souls of the villagers" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 222, folio 204). The tradition of *Ahmad bila mim* "has been repeated in high literature as much as in popular mystical poetry, be it among the Turkish Bektashis or among the mystical bards of Sind and the Punjab" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 451, folio 420), and the story of Muhammad ibn al-Hanafiyya, "a son of 'Ali by another wife than Fatima", "is found in the Panjab, in Bengal, South India, and Malaysia" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 284, folio 266). Schimmel also finds in Sindh and the Punjab "a special aspect of mystical appreciation of women" rarely met elsewhere in the Muslim world, the longing soul represented as a woman, so that the tragic heroines of the Sindhi and Punjabi folk tales, Hir, Sassi, Sohni and many others, "represent the human soul in search of the beloved" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 465, folio 434).

In the south the province's language shades into its neighbour. Boivin describes Siraiki as "a language spoken in Northern Sindh and Southern Punjab" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 220, folio 207), "mainly spoken in Southern Punjab, with Multan as capital" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 216, folio 203). Khalid finds the same blur on the road. "The boundary between Sindh and Punjab becomes a blur as we drive through Bahawalpur"; "This is where the cultures of Punjab and Sindh merge. Some of the roadside hotels are called Punjab Hotel, while others are Sindh Hotel"; Siraiki is "an amalgamation of the two languages", and his guide, the historian Iqbal Qaiser, tells him that "The Siraiki spoken in Multan is closer to Punjabi, while here it is more like Sindhi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 276, folio 250).

The love of Hir and Ranjha is the province's own story. For Kanhaiya Lal the Punjab had a story and a language of its own: in his preface he writes that the tale of Hir and Ranjha is on the lips of good and bad, young and old, rich and fakir in the country of the Punjab; many books on it exist in the Punjabi language, but no one other than the Punjabis could profit from them, which is why he wrote his own version in Persian (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 10, folio 5). The people of Punjab, Bhandari writes, compose wonderful verses and designs on the love of Hir and Ranjha and sing them (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 512–511). Kugle sets the legend against the devotional world further east: "Punjabi poets and their eager audiences poured into Hir and Ranjha the same erotic emotion that was invested in Radha and Krishna in regions closer to the Ganges. The story of Hir and Ranjha became a favorite Sufi device for conveying the value of erotic passion in spiritual life", and Shah Hussein's poems explore divine love "in terms Islamic, Hindu, and indigenous Punjabi" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 228, folio 207). Schimmel writes that the romance was elaborated in more than a hundred versions in Punjabi, Urdu, Sindhi and Persian and given its final form in the late eighteenth century by Waris Shah: "It is his verse which is sung, time and again, wherever Panjabis meet" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 171, folio 153). "The visitor who stays in a Panjabi village will certainly find somebody to sing for him the old romance of Hir Ranjha in the classical form given it by Warith Shah" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 414, folio 383), and "even today a Panjabi will be deeply touched when this moving story is sung" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 17, folio 13).

The romance lives on in performance and in custom. Abbas writes that Waris Shah "is said to have recited it with great emotion among his speech communities in the Panjab", and that "Many renowned folk artists in the Panjab, such as Reshma, Zahida Parvin, Nazir Begum, Sabiha Khanum, Tufail Niazi, and Iqbal Bahu, have sung it" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 123, folio not stated). Hir's lament as she is carried off in the palanquin "is traditionally sung at almost every Panjabi wedding at the time of the bride's departure to her husband's home" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 124, folio not stated), and the palanquin itself carries a strong emotional charge in Punjabi ritual (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 206, folio not stated). In conversation Khalid asks: "Isn't Heer Ranjha part of Punjabi culture? It is the most celebrated folk story here. For centuries it has been sung and dramatized"; Mirza Sahiban is "another famous folk love legend of Punjab", yet "the story of Mirza Sahiban is such a blemish on the legacy of spiritual love stories of Punjab, that it was said that these two brought an end to the era of love legends" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).

Other tales travel across the Punjab's borders. Waris Shah's annotator writes that Sohni and Mahiwal "has its setting In The old Punjab", Sohni belonging to Gujrat on the Chenab, and that this tale and Sassi and Punnoo have travelled beyond their original setting to be popular in both the old Punjab and Sindh (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 12, folio 11). "The Chenab, in the folk culture of Punjab, is known as the river of love", the river of Sohni and Mahiwal and of Ranjha's Takht Hazara (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 9, ch. 3, folio not stated). "Sohni is one of the favorite folk tales in both Sind and the Panjab" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 156, folio 156); Abbas hears the Sohni myth sung in Sufi poetry in all the regions of Sindh and in the Siraiki speaking area of the Punjab (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 126–127, folios not stated), and calls Mahiwal "The hero in the Sohni-Mahival myth of the Panjab" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 184, folio not stated). Sorley calls "The story of Suhini and Mehar" "a Punjab tale which has found a home in Sind", one of the stories of Shah Abdul Latif's Risalo that came from the Punjab (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 270, folio 261; Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 271, folio 262).

The poets made Punjab itself a figure in their verse. Usborne's Waris Shah uses the invasions of the province as similes: "as if armies from Kandahar had swept over the Punjab", "looted him as armies have looted the Punjab", and "today the Punjab has fallen into the hands of Kandaharis", which the annotator takes to refer to Ahmad Shah Abdali (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 7, folio 6; Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 44, folio 43; Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 47, folio 46; Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 48, folio 47). When the poet writes "as if the armies of the Punjab had fallen upon Hindustan", the annotator calls it "an imaginary situation", since the armies of the Punjab are known to have defended Hindustan rather than fallen upon it; even the later Sikh power was contained on the Sutlej by the Anglo Sikh treaty of 1809, and "The poet’s flight of fancy may have been inspired by a mistaken sense of local patriotism" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 7, folio 6; Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 8, folio 7).

Bulleh Shah's lament for the province is the best known of these lines. Rafat's translator's note says that "The full bitterness of Bulleh Shah’s ‘Punjab has gone to the dogs’ will only come through against this backdrop" of upheaval (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 16, folio xvi). In Rafat's translation of the poem Come See Me Once in a While the lines run as follows.

*Now hell has opened its bogs. / Punjab has gone to the dogs. / This hell is the nethermost hell.* (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 149, folio 129)

The province's saints and rulers are named in verse as well. Lahori's book closes with a chronogram for the year 1292 AH by his son, Mufti Ghulam Safdar Lahori, which speaks of the province's saints.

جو خدا کے دوست تھے پنجاب میں
حال ان کا اس سے ہوتا ہے عیان

*A chronogram for the book, on the friends of God in Punjab whose lives it makes known.* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 282, folio 270)

Chishti ends his account of the annexation with a *qita* on Dalip Singh's departure from Punjab, of which he gives two hemistichs likening Dalip Singh to the candle of Punjab and to a new flower from the garden of Punjab; one word of the first hemistich is uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 592). The *suba* of the Punjab is named in verse on the buildings of its governors too. On the northern *mihrab* of the mosque of Nawab Zakariya Khan, west of the mazar of Madho Lal Hussein, a tile inscription that Kanhaiya Lal copied carries these lines (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 262–263, folios 265–266).

زبدۂ بارگاہ او نواب
زکریا خان صوبہ پنجاب

The verse names the mosque's builder, Nawab Zakariya Khan of the *suba* of the Punjab, and the inscription places the building in the reign of Muhammad Shah (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 262–263, folios 265–266).

The province's minstrels carried the songs of every faith. In Guru Nanak and Mardana's time, Khalid writes, the Mirasis went from city to city singing of local chieftains, and landlords showered them with gifts; Mardana "knew by heart several folk stories of Hindu deities and Muslim saints that he would sing during different festivals of Punjab" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 121, folio 103). As a younger man "he would roam the cities and villages of Punjab, singing folk songs and legends", the most popular of them Puran Bhagat's (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 184, folio 158). Two musical instruments, one of them the *nad*, the other's name uncertainly printed, were "popular in the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 39, folio 38). The poetry of the saints lived on in song. In Khalid's reconstruction of the time of Shaikh Ibrahim, Baba Farid's twelfth successor, Farid's poetry "was still sung by qawwals at the shrine and by fakirs in the cities of Punjab" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 142, folio 124). Ernst and Lawrence write that "The name Qalandar continues to be intoned in popular qawwali lyrics of the Punjab", without saying on which side of the border (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 36, folio 22).

Regula Burckhardt Qureshi treats the Punjab as one of "the two principal regions of the Qawwali tradition", set beside Uttar Pradesh, whose tunes can be told apart mainly by their rhythmic setting: "A predominantly quantitative emphasis characterizes tunes from Uttar Pradesh, while Panjabi tunes tend to be rhythmically organized according to 'quality' or stress", the drum articulating the metre more strongly in the Punjab, in keeping with each region's drumming tradition (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 73, folio 53). Her musical example of the Punjab style is a song sung by "a Panjabi Qawwal, Rahmat Khan" (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 57, folio 37), which she takes to exemplify "the 'Panjabi style'" (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 58, folio 38). She does not say whether her Punjab is the Indian or the Pakistani side, or both (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 73, folio 53).

Books reached the province late. In Sikh times, Kanhaiya Lal writes, no printed book came from Hindustan into the Punjab, and scribes wrote out manuscript books and sold them (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 42, folio 45). Shackle notes that the lyrics of Bulleh Shah and the other Sufi poets, "who lived at different times and in different areas of Panjab", were never systematically preserved by an organised faith community, and were for the most part recorded and gathered into printed collections only in the late nineteenth century (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, pp. 9–10, folios viii–ix), when publishers in Lahore brought the older Sufi poetry into the new print culture (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, pp. 11–12, folios x–xi). In the Punjab, he writes in his translation of Shah Abdul Latif, Urdu was installed as the immediate successor of Persian, which did not happen in Sindh, administered from Bombay rather than Calcutta (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 25, folio not stated). Schimmel writes that the literary heritage of Punjabi "was rarely continued by Muslims after 1800", though in the twentieth century a number of mystical treatises were written and published in Punjabi, among them a partial translation of Rumi's *Mathnawi*; the language "was used almost exclusively by the Sikhs as their literary medium, whereas the Panjabi Muslims resorted to Urdu", "though they enjoyed and still enjoy listening to the simple mystical verses and ballads in their spoken mother tongue" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396).

Of the language itself the modern books are gloomy. The introduction to Rafat's selection writes that "Punjabi literature in Pakistan is in a state of neglect. The language has become a purely spoken medium", and that the Punjabis can no longer read it in written form (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 50, folio 30). "In terms of written sources, the Punjab and indeed the whole of the Indus basin, has always" seemed "a wasteland to scholars" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, pp. 46–47, folios 26–27). Writing of how Baba Farid's and Shah Hussein's poetry survived through Sikh collectors, it blames a lack of intellectual tradition among the Muslims of the Punjab for indifference to the classics, adding that "Bulleh Shah was treated no differently" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 47, folio 27). "After this the Punjab awaits a proper" scholarly "assessment of its greatest poet, a tryst that may never take place given the regional antecedents in the realm of letters" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 49, folio 29). The Janus questionnaire it quotes observes that "All regional personalities are moulded by a deep love of their idiom", and that while a Sindhi can name the greatest living Sindhi poet, and so can the Pathan and the Baluch, the Punjabi cannot; Punjabi alone, it says, is not taught in schools and has no dictionary (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 236, folio 216; Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, pp. 237–238, folios 217–218).

The province's language has a low standing among its own educated people, in Khalid's experience. In Pakistani Punjab, he writes, Punjabi "is looked down upon. It is considered to be a language of the boorish and the uneducated". His elite private school fined any student who spoke Punjabi, even in the playground, and "the educated elite of Punjab ends up disowning, in fact, ridiculing anything Punjabi", the poetry of Baba Farid, Guru Nanak, Shah Hussein, Waris Shah and Bulleh Shah among it (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 128, folio 110).

## Shrines and the State

Rulers tied themselves to the province's shrines and its land from early on. Eaton writes that "the Delhi sultans and Mughal emperors granted huge tracts of rich land" for the ritual ceremonies of the great Sufi shrines of Punjab and Sindh, while irrigation "permitted a rapid growth in wheat production" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 48–49, folios 36–37). For Ernst and Lawrence, Akbar used the Chishti saints, Shaikh Salim and "Shaykh Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar in the Punjab", to rule his provinces: "Through a twofold, redoubled Chishti loyalty, Akbar could anchor his imperial legitimacy in two provinces adjacent to Uttar Pradesh, the Punjab and Rajasthan, both of which also happened to be crucial to the political-military ambitions of his reign" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, pp. 112–113, folios 99–100). The book does not state the extent of that Mughal province. Rulers in the books deal with the province's holy men and with its revenue alike. Bhandari writes that while the imperial armies were quartered in Punjab grain was dear and so the assessed revenue of the *parganas* was raised; when the armies left Lahore for the Deccan, grain became cheap, the peasants could not pay, and a reduction was ordered at a rate uncertainly printed; Bhandari gives this as the petition of Guru Arjan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 164).

The Mughal state built across the province and paid for its builders' tombs. Ali Mardan Khan, in Shah Jahan's time, built great and splendid buildings in Hind and Punjab, and his gardens remain his memorial in many places, Chishti writes, following *Mirat al-Hind* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 415). In Shah Jahan's reign the builder of the garden Chishti calls Sanhla Bagh was appointed to an office over the buildings of Punjab, its title damaged in the printed text, and on his death money was granted from the treasury for his tomb (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 727). Every ruler of the Punjab left a mark on its religious buildings, in Kanhaiya Lal's account. In Shah Jahan's reign Munshi Muhammad Salih, *diwan* of the *suba* of the Punjab, built a mosque that was completed in the year 1058, the reading of the year uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 152, folio 155), and Dara Shikoh built a great deal while he governed the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 139, folio 142).

The Sikh years, in Lahori's editor's estimate, were hard on the shrines. Among the merits of the book he counts that the Mufti recorded, as an eyewitness, the disgrace of the notables of Punjab at the hands of the Sikhs and the destruction of the tombs of the shaikhs, his own family having been a target of the Sikh disorders (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 33, folio 21). Ranjit Singh's faith in the *majzub* Mastan Shah, and Mastan Shah's refusal of his thousands of rupees, are recorded by Lahori (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 246, folio 234). Under Ranjit Singh the Moti Masjid in the fort was renamed the Moti Mandir and held the treasury of the state, and the English government too keeps the treasury of the *suba* of the Punjab in it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 339, folio 346). Under the same Maharaja the Fakir sahibs bargained for a mosque by the Baoli by promising that its mulla would not give the call aloud (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 148, folio 151).

The Sikh state endowed the gurdwaras. "When Maharaja Ranjit Singh became the sovereign of Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century he spent large amounts of money on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras", and "attached vast tracts of land to these gurdwaras, so that the revenues from the lands could facilitate their functioning" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16); he renovated "many Gurdwaras all over Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 208, folio not stated). Under the British their custody changed hands: "As a result of sectarian divisions between the Sikh communities and the takeover of Punjab by the British, a large number of important Sikh Gurdwaras were being administered by Hindu mahants" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 189, folio not stated).

The new rulers lived in the old buildings. The troops left the Badshahi Mosque when English rule over the Punjab was complete (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 141, folio 144). When Sikh rule passed from the Punjab and the country of the Punjab came under the shadow of the English government, an octagonal *kothi* built over a tomb housed in turn Mr Bowring, Deputy Commissioner, Major Macgregor, Deputy Commissioner, both names uncertainly printed, and Montgomery, Commissioner, who in the end became Lieutenant-Governor of the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 395, folio 402). Under British rule Chishti argues for the khanqahs before the new government: attention from the government to the khanqah, he writes, would win the goodwill of the subjects of Punjab (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 95). When British rule and its legal settlement were established in Punjab, Jhuggi Shah returned to Dori Shah's site and set about restoring it (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 777).

The books see the Punjab's shrines bound up with land and power under the colonial state and after it. Rozehnal, quoting Katherine Ewing, describes how the British dealt with the hereditary saints, who "were seen primarily in terms of their economic power": "British policy, especially in the Punjab and Sind, was to treat them in essentially the same way that landlords and tribal leaders were treated", and "They tried to maintain the traditional social structure intact, securing the loyalty of the pirs, landlords, and chiefs by reinforcing their economic positions and educating them in the British tradition" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 33, folio 23). Ernst and Lawrence observe that "If we were to follow only the dictates of political history and the criteria of social science, the test case for the Chishti Nizami Sulaymaniyya lineage in the colonial period might seem to be the battles over shrines in the Punjab", with Taunsa as the example, and that "Political power struggles internal to the Sulaymaniyya can be traced to British manipulation and also the rivalry of local Sikhs, Hindus, and Muslim reformers"; they then set this reading aside, since "political contestation is only part of the narrative" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 124, folio printed "Ill" [text-layer?] [111]). Jamal Malik, writing in *Embodying Charisma*, finds the cult of the holy man and its folk piety "particularly evident in the countryside, where major landlords and large-scale land concentrations are crucial features, especially in Punjab and Sindh", and adds that "In many cases, the local pîr represents the local landlord or is at least closely related to him" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 199, folio 188). Kasmani, drawing on Umber bin Ibad's work on central Punjab, sets the colonial state, which in Ibad's words "did not try to control shrines directly through its bureaucracy", against the postcolonial state, which has gone further, driven by a desire "to harness the pluralistic traditions of shrines" in favour of "representing a Singular Islam" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 69, folio 52).

After Partition many non-Muslim holy places lost their purpose. Without the family that cares for it, Khalid writes, the shrine of Ram Thamman "would have also been taken over by the refugees and converted into a household, like the hundreds of temples and Gurdwaras all over Pakistani Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 58, folio not stated). At Makhdoom Pur Pahuran he finds the gurdwara inside a school, its gate locked, with a banner over it describing the Punjab government's new policy for improving education in public schools (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 128, folio 110).

The provincial state appears in the books in many guises. Schaflechner notes that "the Punjab has 8 reserved minority seats out of 371" in its assembly, against Sindh's 9 of 168 (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 28, folio not stated, endnote 5 to p. 15). He also cites a class V Urdu textbook of the Punjab Textbooks Board, published in Lahore in 2002, which teaches that the Hindu has always been an enemy of Islam (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 28, folio not stated, endnote 7), and draws from it, after Nayyar and Salim, that in Pakistani schoolbooks "the Hindu" is not only a danger to national security but "has always been an enemy of Islam" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 20, folio not stated). The road through Changa Manga, Khalid writes, "is much safer now, since the Punjab government introduced the motorway police" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated). He reports that "human rights activists, opposition party leaders and segments of the media have alleged that the Punjab government, dominated by the right-winged Pakistan Muslim League Noon, has been supporting these radical outfits who are based in Jhang", and that "The Muslim League Noon, on the other hand, rubbishes the claims" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).

The killing of the province's governor runs through Khalid's books. "On 4 January 2011 the Governor of Punjab Salman Taseer was shot down by one of his bodyguards, Mumtaz Qadri, for supporting the cause of a Christian woman, Aasia Bibi, accused of blasphemy"; Qadri "became an instant hero, and was hailed by the lawyers' community", and a retired chief justice of the Lahore High Court, Khwaja Sharif, offered to fight his case free of charge, calling him a hero of Islam (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 19, Notes, note 44, folio not stated). In *A White Trail* the governor "was gunned down" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 48, folio not stated), and the Aasia Bibi case "received international coverage after the former Governor of Punjab, Salman Taseer came out in support of this woman and was later killed for his pains" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 90, folio not stated).

## The Province in the Books

Each book draws the Punjab as its own unit, and the earliest of them draws it as a Mughal *suba*. Bhandari, writing about 1107 AH, describes the Mughal *suba* of Lahore as a country of five *doabs*, setting out the famous places of the five *doabs* and then the six rivers of the *suba* that divide them (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 513). He names the *doabs* as Bist Jalandhar between the Sutlej and the Beas, Bari Manjha between the Beas and the Ravi, the *doab* between the Ravi and the Chenab, which he calls Rachna where he places Sialkot in it, the *doab* between the Chenab and the Bihat, which he calls Chunhat where he places Gujrat in it, and Sind Sagar between the Bihat and the Indus, some of the names uncertainly printed (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 511; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 512; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 513; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 515; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). The five rivers of Punjab join the Indus beyond Multan in the Baloch country, and from there the whole stream is called the Sind and becomes a vast sea (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 510). Turning from the capital to the rest of the *suba* of Lahore, he sets out to write of some of its towns (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 523).

To Bhandari the *suba* of Lahore is a country of good water and air. Its summers are very hot and its winters cold; its melons and grapes are like those of Iran and Turan and its mangoes like Hindustan's; its rice is better than Bengal's and its sugarcane better than the Deccan's (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 510). Most of its irrigation depends on wells, worked by a wheel of three hundred and sixty pieces of wood and more than a hundred small pots, turned by a pair of oxen, each turn lifting several hundred maunds of water, while the autumn and spring harvests and cheap grain depend on the rain (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 510). Gold is washed from the sand, especially of the Beas and the Bihat, and the northern hills hold mines of silver, copper and other metals whose names are uncertainly printed; the miners profit and pay the duty to the imperial treasury (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 510). The *suba* measures one hundred and eighty *kos* from the Sutlej to the Indus, and eighty seven *kos* across from Bhera to a place whose name is uncertainly printed; it is bounded by Sirhind to the east, Multan to the west, Kashmir to the north and, to the south, a place whose name is also uncertain (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 510). The five *doabs* comprise three hundred and sixteen *mahals*, yielding eighty nine crore, thirty three lakh and seventy thousand *dams* (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 509).

Beside it Bhandari sets the *suba* of Multan, which he calls the abode of safety. He writes that it runs four hundred *kos* from Firozpur to Siwistan and one hundred and twenty five *kos* across from Khatpur to Jaisalmer, and six hundred and sixty *kos* in length from Sadgahra to Kech Makran; it adjoins the *sarkar* of Sirhind to the east, Kech Makran to the west, Shorkot to the north and the *suba* of Ajmer to the south (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525). Its three *sarkars*, Multan, Dipalpur and Bhakkar, comprise ninety six *mahals* and yield twenty four crore, forty six lakh and fifty five thousand *dams* (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525). The *sarkar* of Dipalpur is a dependency of this *suba* (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 526), and Bhandari bounds the *suba* of Ajmer on the west by Dipalpur, dependent on Multan, without saying whether he means the *suba*, the *sarkar* or the city (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 534). The *suba* of Shahjahanabad has the *suba* of Lahore on its west, but he draws no line between them (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 550).

Bhandari's Punjab is a frontier made safe by union with Kabul. In former times, when Kabul lay outside the rule of the sovereign of Hind, the Turanians raided its borders and the *wilayat* of Punjab lay ruined and waste; since Hind and Kabul came under one ruler, this has been the cause of the prosperity of Punjab and the security of all Hind (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 501). Among his sources he lists a history of the sultans of the *wilayat* of Multan, together with those of Malwa, Daulatabad, the Deccan, Jaunpur, Bengal and Orissa, lands that at various times passed out of the control of Delhi and each had a separate ruler (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 581).

For Kanhaiya Lal the Punjab is a unit with many names, and he uses them all: the *ilaqa* or territory of the Punjab given in *jagir* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 14, folio 9; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 76, folio 79), the Mughal *suba* of the Punjab with its *diwan* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 152, folio 155), the *khitta* or region of the Punjab that Ranjit Singh ruled (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 9, folio 4), the kingdom of the Punjab that passed to Rani Chand Kaur (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 443, folio 450), the country of the Punjab that the English annexed (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 68, folio 71), and the *suba* of the Punjab whose capital in his own day is Lahore (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 60, folio 63). His account of its past leans on earlier writers, whom he names: the *Tuhfat al-Wasilin* of Shaikh Ahmad Zanjani, set against the author of the *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 14, folio 9), and the *Hadiqat al-Awliya* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 305, folio 308).

He draws its edges by what crosses them. Jalal al-Din entered the Punjab by crossing the river he calls the river of Sind (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 165, folio 168); the Tatars, as he names them, came into it from the west (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 27, folio 30); Sikha Gakkhar entered it from the country belonging to Hazara, and Timur passed through its foothills on his way to Delhi (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 28, folio 31); the Sutlej is the river Yalduz crossed on his way from the Punjab toward Delhi (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 25, folio 20). Kashmir and the Derajat were Ranjit Singh's possessions besides the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220), the Kashmiris came into the Punjab in their famine (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 75, folio 78), and printed books came from Hindustan into it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 42, folio 45). He was also a servant of the province he described. The women's prison of the Punjab was built under his supervision (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 409, folio 416), and he describes the province's governors, courts and jails as they stood in his own day (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 60, folio 63).

Lahori, writing in 1875, defines his own province. Friends asked him, he says, for another short collection in the Urdu language holding the lives of the saints of the land of Punjab, that is, of all the territory from Delhi to Peshawar then attached to Punjab, where the tombs of famous saints lie (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 37, folio 25). His fifth chaman holds the elders who were learned and renowned scholars in Punjab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 191, folio 179), his seventh the pious women who lived in Punjab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 247, folio 235), and a later section the elders of Punjab whose dates of death could not be established (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 261, folio 249). In his closing he explains that his Persian *Khazinat al-Asfiya* was so large that those interested in the land of Punjab could not learn from it how many masters of the path had lived in the land of Punjab, a difficulty this short Urdu book removes (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 280, folio 268).

Lahori's modern editor sees the book as filling a gap. Punjab, he writes, is in this respect the most unfortunate of regions, for no regular and researched history of it has yet been written, and those who wrote on its history gave only formal chapters without touching the Sufi tazkiras; the need is to fill the gaps in the political, cultural and spiritual history of Punjab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 23, folio 11). He has compiled a list of the tazkiras, *malfuzat* and letters of the Sufis of Punjab, and no comprehensive tazkira of them has come to his notice; without the Mufti's general tazkiras, he writes, we would see no scholar or gnostic in Punjab today (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 24, folio 12). The book holds the lives of 244 Sufis of Punjab, the numeral uncertainly printed, in seven chamans, from Sultan Mahmud of Ghazni to 1292 AH, 1875 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 31, folio 19), and also takes in some well known Sufis who had no connection with Punjab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 32, folio 20). Some Punjab shaikhs are found only in this book (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 32, folio 20), and by preserving his contemporaries the Mufti did Punjab a great service; yet, the editor adds, he wrote up whichever elders of Punjab came to mind at a single sitting (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 33, folio 21). The modern edition's subtitle calls it an authoritative tazkira of the great Sufis of Punjab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 9, folio not stated).

The editor also weighs the other sources for the province's Sufis. He points to Maulana Muhammad Aqil Lahori's *Tuhfat al-Muslimin*, which records the sayings of Shaikh Hamid Qari and the lives of the great scholars of Punjab from Shaikh Hamid to Shaikh Bahauddin Zakariya Multani, and whose rare manuscript is in his own library (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 278, folio 266). Of another modern work he writes that the author of *Nuzhat al-Khawatir* took *Khazinat al-Asfiya* as his largest source for Punjab and generally included its reports without any research (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 191–192, folios 179–180).

The translators of the province's poets draw it in their own ways. For the annotator of Usborne's Waris Shah the poem's world is "the old Punjab", with its *bars* such as the Nili Bar "in the Montgomery area (now renamed Sahiwal)", a *bar* being a kind of jungle (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 10, folio 9). Naveed Alam reads Shah Hussein's Punjab against W. H. Moreland's account of the Mughal Golden Age, though that account speaks of India taken as a unit rather than of Punjab (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 14, folio 14). Shackle writes of Panjab without the article, and narrows it to "western Panjab" for the world of Hir and Ranjha (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 20, folio xix). Schimmel most often speaks of Sindh and the Punjab together, as "the two provinces" of one tradition of religious poetry (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384), and of a "cotton-growing country" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 417, folio 386); in *Pain and Grace* "the province" is the land invaded by Marathas, Sikhs and Muslim armies in the late seventeenth and eighteenth centuries (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 17, folio 13). For Sorley, writing of eighteenth century Sindh, the Punjab is the theatre of the armies of Afghans, Sikhs and Marathas (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 46, folio 37) and a source of migrants and tales (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 270, folio 261).

For the historians the Punjab is defined by comparison. Eaton sets it against Bengal, where the Mughal officials who arrived around 1620 found that "The Bengali diet of fish and rice, for example, contrasted sharply with the wheat and meat diet of the Punjab and seems to have posed a special stumbling block for immigrants" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 263, folio 251). Rizvi writes against W. H. McLeod's picture of the Punjab of Guru Nanak's time: McLeod, he says, "seems to be unaware of Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Quddus Gangohi who lived in Shahabad, his disciple, Shaikh Jalal of Thaneswar, and the Qadiris who had made the Panjab the main centre of their activities" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 420, folio 396).

Each modern author's Punjab is drawn to the scale of his travels. Khalid limited the research for *In Search of Shiva* to the country around Lahore: "Since this was mostly a self-funded project I could only limit my research around my hometown of Lahore. Therefore only parts of central Punjab are included and southern Punjab and Sindh are excluded, where there are many such shrines" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 6, Introduction, folio not stated). In the afterword to *A White Trail* he explains that he worked in "Central Punjab where I conducted my research", where Hindus live in ghettos, adding: "My perception is that the conditions under which members of the Hindu community live in Central Punjab are the harshest compared to the other regions" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 233, folio not stated). "The majority of the Hindu community is based in Sindh and Southern Punjab, where they have a proportionate representation from the lower and the upper castes" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 233, folio not stated), whereas almost all his interviews were with low caste Hindus "because there are hardly any high-caste Hindus in this region", so that his observations from Central Punjab should not be taken to reflect Hindu communities where the population is large (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 233–234, folio not stated). On the other hand, "The majority of the Christian minority is based in Punjab", so that his Christian material can stand for the community in the country as a whole (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 234, folio not stated).

Khalid's Punjab is also personal. He was "born and brought up in Pakistani Punjab" and knew of Guru Nanak as the founder of Sikhism, a poet and a spiritual leader, "Yet given the Muslim-dominant culture of Pakistani Punjab, there were no avenues available for me within the culture to explore his personality" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 15, folio not stated). On the road he asks whether "is it the bubble that we occupy in the urban centres that is out of context in the culture of Punjab", and remarks that "One rarely finds such a lonely road in the heartland of Punjab, the most prosperous province of Pakistan" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated). At the banyan of Apal Muri, where visitors had left their mobile numbers, he guesses that they were "probably hoping to initiate a secret love affair in this highly monitored social life of rural Punjab" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 9, ch. 3, folio not stated). He notes that "yellow was commonly used for buildings in Punjab during the older days" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 197, folio not stated), and on an abandoned mound he finds karir, "another ancient Punjabi species of plant that is no longer found anywhere except in abandoned places" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 92, folio 74).

In the novelised chapter of *Walking with Nanak* on the Sikh capture of Hassan Abdal under Hari Singh, Khalid imagines the province from its western frontier. The Sikh soldiers "had arrived from the Punjab"; invaders had for almost a thousand years "crossed into the Punjab from the West", and so, in the Punjabi imagination as the damaged sentence can be read, "the West was a fearful place where giants resided" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 258, folio 232). Hari Singh wanted the tribesmen to know "that now it was the time of Punjab to create havoc in the Khyber" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 262, folio 236).

The modern ethnographers work with parts of the province. Kasmani writes of central Punjab (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 73, folio 56), Rozehnal of the southern Punjab as a Sufi master's domain (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 90, folio 80), and Boivin of a southern Punjab that was once part of Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 216, folio 203). Boivin also criticises scholars of Sindhi studies who treat Sindh's religious encounters "as if comparable dynamics were not implemented in other regions, be they neighbouring Sindh, such as Punjab, Rajasthan or Gujarat, or elsewhere" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 196, folio not stated).
