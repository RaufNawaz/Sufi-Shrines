## History

Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti tells the founding of Lahore twice, and the two tellings differ. In his section on Shah Kamal, introducing it as something people say, he relates that Raja Ram Chand had three sons: one, whose printed name may be Lo, built Lahore; a second, whose name is lost in an illegible passage, settled Kasur, the reading of that name also uncertain; and the third, whose name may read Rao, founded Mauza Rawan; the passage closes with the formula that God knows best (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 661). In his dynastic history the raja, Ramchandar, has two sons, Kushu and Lohu, and it is Lohu who settled the city of Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 795).

His account of the city before the Mughals is a history of conquest. When Sabuktigin first invaded Hind, in a Hijri year printed only in part, as a lost number of hundreds and seventy seven, the reading uncertain, Raja Jaipal ruled at Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 804). Sabuktigin, having won the Afghans over, brought Lahore and Multan into his possession (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 804). Chishti tells how Jaipal, defeated, asked that a trusted man of Sabuktigin's come with him to Lahore to collect the tribute, and a Nawab Khair Allah Khan, the name uncertainly printed, went with him; at Jaipal's court the Chhatris stood on the left and the Brahmins and Khatris on the right, the Chhatris urging the raja to keep his word and the Brahmins urging him not to pay; Jaipal broke the agreement and imprisoned the envoy, and Sabuktigin marched on Lahore, where the rajas of Delhi, Ajmer and Kalinjar, the last name uncertainly printed, came to Jaipal's aid, and the Hindus were defeated (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 804). In Sultan Mahmud's time, he writes, a raja of the Tawar family, whose printed name may be Osa, ruled Lahore and Kannauj, both names uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 804).

Lahore was a refuge and then a capital for the sultans who followed. Mas'ud, hard pressed by the Turkmans of Tughril Beg, was forced to set out toward Lahore; his army looted the treasury at the Indus and put the blinded Muhammad back on the throne (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 807). In Maudud's time the Hindu rajas besieged Lahore, which Chishti calls the capital of the people of Islam, for seven months, the numeral uncertainly printed, until the besieged drove them off (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 807). Khusrau Shah son of Bahram came to Lahore and, out of necessity, made it his capital, ruling there for seven years, the numeral again uncertain; his son Khusrau Malik succeeded him; the Ghurids took the country from Khusrau by a thousand tricks and deceptions, and Ghurid rule began here too (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 808–809).

Citing the *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, Chishti writes that after a passage that cannot be read, Lahore lay deserted for a long time, until an amir of a sultan whose name is uncertainly printed settled it again; the name Kamran appears in the damaged continuation (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 430). Settlement then grew (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 431).

Under the Mughals the city becomes a seat of power in Chishti's account. When Humayun returned to Hindustan, the governor of Lahore, Tatar Khan, came out against him, and Bairam Khan overcame him off his guard (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 823). After Hemu took Delhi, Akbar gathered a force at Lahore and marched on Delhi (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 824). A badly garbled passage places Akbar's second enthronement, after Humayun's death, at Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 648). Akbar turned to the settlement of Lahore from the start of his reign: he built a city and a palace, his *daulat khana*, raised large buildings and stayed there for a time (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 431). Chishti calls Lahore the capital in Akbar's reign, when Malik Ali, kotwal in the time of Akbar Badshah, held the whole kotwali of the capital Lahore, in a passage whose surrounding words are uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 194); elsewhere Malik Ali is the kotwal of Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 193). A heavily damaged passage says that Akbar was staying in Lahore in the days when the ancestor of the Gilani family came there (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 525).

Lahore also stands in the story of Nur Jahan's family. Khwaja Ayaz, her father, travelled with his wife and the infant in a caravan as far as Lahore, and there he took service and rose to be *mir munshi* under Akbar (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 827). Four lines on the coin legends of Nur al-Din Jahangir son of Akbar Badshah cannot be read in full; they appear to name Lahore and Ahmadabad among his mints, both readings uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 828). After Mahabat Khan's victory, Nur Jahan fled toward Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 829). Jahangir, ill on his way back from Kashmir, came to Lahore and died there; the date is printed as a day of November in a Christian year of which only 162 can be read, corresponding to a Hijri year of which only the final 36 can be read, both uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 830). He was buried north of the city of Lahore, on the bank of the Ravi, at Mauza Shahdara (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 830); Chishti places the tomb on the bank of the Ravi four miles north of the city (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 855), and writes that from its minarets the whole city of Lahore can be seen, with the Badshahi mosque, Wazir Khan's mosque and its other buildings (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 860).

Shah Jahan, too, is in Lahore in Chishti's pages: the anecdote of Haji Nur has him stopping in the city (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 200). Shah Jahan is said to have had many buildings in Lahore raised, and Nawab Wazir Khan was appointed nazim of Lahore, the title uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 738); Wazir Khan appears again as nazim of Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 749). Chishti calls the Shalamar garden in Lahore Shah Jahan's memorial, and writes that the eye of the age can seldom have seen such a garden (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 833).

The city then served the princes in the wars of succession. After Aurangzeb's accession, Dara Shikoh returned to Lahore, raised a larger army there and crossed the Indus (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 836); the man who betrayed Dara, having handed him over in Delhi, set off toward Lahore and was killed on the road (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 838). At the start of Alamgir's reign, Nawab Khalil Allah Khan held Lahore, a word uncertainly printed, and with a mansab of six thousand was appointed subadar of Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 497). In the succession war after Aurangzeb, Muhammad Mu'izz al-Din, governor of Multan, came to Lahore with a large army and stores to his father Sultan Mu'azzam Bahadur Shah, and both went on to Akbarabad (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 845). Shah Alam died in the city of Lahore in 1712 CE, the numeral uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 846).

Chishti ties the city's great buildings to this age. The great buildings stood until the reign of Alamgir; great amirs had raised large buildings of stone, and the buildings of Abu al-Hasan son of Asaf Khan were among the chief ornaments of Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 431).

He follows the later Mughal governors closely. Abdus Samad Khan was given the title Saif al-Daula Dilir Jang Bahadur and appointed to Lahore by Shah Alam, in a passage whose words are heavily uncertain, and he arrived when his son Zakariya Khan was thirteen years old (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 714). Zakariya Khan was appointed nazim of Lahore through the support of the Sayyid brothers, his father moving to be nazim of Multan, and he continued as nazim of Lahore under Muhammad Shah; he had, Chishti writes, a great passion for building (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 716). In the reign of Muhammad Shah a governor of Lahore is named in connection with the land revenue, but the rest of the passage is damaged (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 372). Mir Momin Khan became nazim of Lahore in Muhammad Shah's time (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 172). Chishti writes that his administration of the subjects of Lahore was good, in words uncertainly printed; that he was not autonomous, the real nazim of Lahore being a Khan whose name cannot be read; that most of the buildings between the Mochi Gate and the Kashmiri Bazaar are his memorials; and that he was the last nazim of Lahore, after whom none was appointed or came (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 173).

Mir Mannu, Chishti writes, governed at Lahore in Muhammad Shah's reign; the Sikhs had grown in number, and there was a general order to seize and kill them (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 121). A Punjabi saying about Mir Mannu is recorded, but it cannot be read (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 121). In the year printed as 1155, the reading uncertain, the suba of Multan was joined to Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 717–718). Under Nawab Abdullah Khan, nazim of Lahore from the year printed as 1161, a famine fell on Lahore and he squeezed the people; Chishti records a proverb that under Nawab Abdullah's rule neither the millstone nor the hearth remained, two of its words uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 717). Khwaja Abid, subadar of Lahore, attacked Charat Singh's fort, the *garhi*, in a year whose printed form cannot be read with certainty, and victory fell to the Sikhs (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 578). A Nawab whose name is damaged came from Kabul to Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 722), and the office of the highest governor of Lahore appears in the career of Rajab Ali Khan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 141).

The invasions that followed fell hard on the city. Nadir Shah plundered Lahore thoroughly after Shahnawaz Khan's defeat, taking cash and goods from Begumpura, while Shahnawaz Khan's petitions to Delhi went unanswered (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 717). At Ahmad Shah Abdali's assault the city was put to slaughter and most of its houses burned, the last words uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 88). In Chishti's telling, Ahmad Shah came to Lahore and Shah Kaku Chishti told him that this was his own city; Shah Kaku was sent as envoy, and the man he went to had him killed, although killing an envoy is forbidden; Ahmad Shah, drowned in grief and anger, crossed the Ravi, the river's name uncertainly printed, and ordered a general massacre (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 218). Thousands died, the number uncertainly printed; Ahmad Shah watched sitting at the gate of what may be the Nakhas, the reading uncertain; Shah Nawaz Khan answered that if he was a merchant he should sell him, if a tyrant imprison him, and if a king forgive him; he was spared, and Ahmad Shah conferred the crown and set off for Hindustan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 219). Ahmad Shah came a second time and ordered a general massacre of Lahore, in which hundreds of thousands of people were killed and Begumpura was looted, in a heavily damaged passage (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 718). Hafiz Lutfullah lived until the time of Nawab Zakariya Khan, after which Ahmad Shah Abdali carried out a slaughter in Lahore and Begumpura was plundered; whenever Ahmad Shah came, the family moved into the city, and went back to Baghbanpura when he left (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 724). When great disorder fell upon the empire, Chishti writes in a damaged passage, Ahmad Shah Abdali rose and the Sikhs began to loot Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 372).

Under Alamgir II the Afghans attacked the Punjab and took Multan and Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 851). Ahmad Shah Abdali had given the government of Lahore to a woman of Pathan descent; Ghazi al-Din arrested her, and Abdali invaded a third time, where the sentence breaks off (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 851). The Marathas, with help from the Sikhs, are said to have conquered Delhi, Agra, Multan and Lahore and driven the Afghans across the river at Attock; no date is given (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 852).

The Sikh period begins, in Chishti's account, in fragmentation. After Mir Mannu's death, rule in Lahore fell apart, in words that cannot be read with certainty, and wherever any Sikh found the chance he set himself up as ruler (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 719). In the time of persecution by the Sikhs, the property of hundreds of people in the city is said to have been lost, in a very damaged passage (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 722). Lahore then had three rulers. In one place Chishti names them, at the arrival of Bawa Parsotam Das, as Gujjar Singh, Lehna Singh and Sobha Singh, the first and last names uncertainly printed, the chiefs of Lahore, and gives the year of that arrival only in part, as a Samvat year beginning 18 (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 251). In another he names only Gujjar Singh, the second and third names not printed, and has the fort built by Gujjar Singh, ruler of Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 326). Elsewhere Gujjar Singh rules a part of the city, in a damaged passage (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 111). Chishti dates other events from the time when the three rulers were appointed in Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 721), among them the resettlement of a village (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 724).

Lehna Singh is one of the sardars of Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 855) and its former ruler (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 858). When Lehna Singh, as ruler, began destroying the monuments of former kings, the elders of the city invited Ranjit Singh to come to Lahore and take possession of it (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 786). Shah Zaman gave Ranjit Singh leave to take Lahore if he could, and Ranjit Singh took it from a ruler whose name is lost (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 579). After Maharaja Ranjit Singh took Lahore, the Nawab's descendants scattered to Shahjahanabad and Kabul, and one of them was brought back and made the resident agent at the court of the Kabul government (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 721). A badly garbled passage on Mehr Muhkam tells of someone coming to Lahore by the Bhati Gate and opening it, and of ten thousand men, horse and foot, entering Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 646); the Maharaja then brought all the chaudhris and numbardars of the country round the city under his shadow (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 647).

Chishti's pages on Ranjit Singh's Lahore are full of works and courtiers. Nura, having gained access to Ranjit Singh's government, was appointed to the building of the moat of Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 423). A weapon made by Ahmad Shah was placed in Lahore at the baradari of Nawab Wazir Khan, where something whose printed name cannot be read with certainty now stands (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 575). The first of the misls, whose printed name is uncertain, was of the Jat people of the district of Lahore, with ten thousand horse, and the sardar of the third misl, his name uncertainly printed, became ruler of the eastern districts of Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 575). Part of one sardar's fourth share was confiscated during a disturbance in Lahore with the Sikh army, in an uncertainly printed passage (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 577). Khushal Singh received havelis and gardens in Lahore and Amritsar, and Dina Nath built a large *shivala* near a part of Lahore whose name cannot be read (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 583). Among the nobles and chiefs of Lahore who visited Mai Bhagi were Raja Dina Nath, the Sandhanwalia sardars and Raja Dhian Singh (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 790).

After Ranjit Singh came the court's wars and the annexation. Maharaja Kharak Singh died in the Bikrami year 1897, the numeral uncertainly printed, and Maharaja Sher Singh was enthroned in the same year; Sher Singh came to Lahore after the deaths of Kharak Singh and Nau Nihal Singh (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 723). Raja Dhian Singh summoned Sher Singh to Lahore so that he might take the throne, and he came and pitched his camp near the cantonment of the Khalsa army (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 588). Suchet Singh was killed in Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 582). The Sandhanwalia party came into the fort and killed Raja Dhian Singh, and Sher Singh's killers were dispatched after the siege of the fort of Lahore and a great battle (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 589). Raja Hira Singh and Pandit Jalla were killed on the bank of the Ravi on the Id of sacrifice, and their heads and bodies were seen hanging, the heads staying there for some days, the number uncertainly printed, while the damaged passage names the bazaar and Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 590). After the defeat at Sobraon, the name uncertainly printed, the troops fled, looting, and entered Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 591). Under the treaty of 1846 territory passed from the government of Lahore, and at the request of the people of the state of Lahore a Resident was appointed in Lahore, while disorder continued (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 591). Raja Lal, the name uncertainly printed, was expelled from Lahore and sent to Agra, also uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 591). In December 1849, the day lost and the year uncertainly printed, Duleep Singh left Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 592).

Of the British city Chishti gives one testimony. Rajab Ali Khan, speaking in the first person, saw that Lahore had been adorned, its surroundings, its clean roads and its lofty buildings, and credited it to the Lieutenant Governor of the Punjab, the title uncertainly printed, finding the city thousands of times more splendid than he had seen it before (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 142).

Chishti also traces the city's extent and fabric. Formerly, he writes, the city of Lahore was settled as far as Mozang (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 115), and of the site of Hashim Pura he says that Lahore once reached that far (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 298). A garbled passage mentions the embankment of the old city and its wall beside the courtyard of a samadh (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 572), and a maulvi bought a house inside the walls of the city of Lahore, in a mohalla whose printed name is uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 372). The city of Lahore, he writes, has twelve gates, and he lists them: the Lahori Gate, the Shah Alami Gate, the Mochi Gate, the Akbari Gate, the Delhi Gate, a gate printed as the Dakki Gate and known by a second name that cannot be read with certainty, a gate printed as the Sarawala Gate which is also called the Kashmiri Gate, the Masti Gate, the Roshnai Gate, the Taksali Gate, the Bhati Gate and the Mori Gate (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 498). Of these twelve, the Delhi Gate, on the side he describes as the east in an uncertain reading, is famous (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 739). Of one tomb he writes that no tomb in the district of Lahore is so lofty or has arches so large, in a partly illegible passage (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 414).

The city's splendour, Chishti writes, comes from its tiled buildings: in the city of Lahore proper, besides the Jami mosque of Nawab Wazir Khan, there are three mosques with tile work, the first being the mosque of Muhammad Salih Kambo (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 751). Its inscription speaks of the Valley of Ayman, of the sanctity of Lahore and of the grace of the Haram (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 753). Khwaja Inayatullah, the name uncertainly printed, is said to have sold thousands of houses in Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 719).

The city's ruin recurs as a theme. In the history of a *gaddi*, Chishti writes that after Jiju Bhagat merged into the spot where he sat, the city was ruined (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 251). Of the mohalla of the Tamarind *takia* he writes that it was deserted at the time of the city's desolation (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 523).

## A City of Saints

Chishti presents his book as a guide to a landscape of saints. It reports, he writes, on the tombs of the Muslim elders, on other buildings and on the old customs of Lahore, but at root it is a history of the saints (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 29). In the afterword he describes its subject as the history of all the mazars on every side of Lahore that are memorials of the elders of the past (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 872). The survey proper opens under a heading announcing an account of the graves, mazars and tombs of the elders of Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 45).

The saints, in his telling, guard the city and claim it. Of a *majzub* faqir buried at a khanqah, he writes that it is said Lahore was kept safe from Ahmad Shah Abdali by his kindness (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 91). When Ahmad Shah came to Lahore, Shah Kaku Chishti told him that this was his own city, before going as envoy to his death (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 218). The people of Lahore revere the mazar of Miran Badshah (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 749). Fakhr-ud-Din, on his first journey toward Ajmer, met in Lahore a Mir whose printed name is uncertain, took him along, and visited all the mazars (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 756).

Even the landscape around the city is holy in these pages. Chishti relates that Mian Natha told Mian Mir that he used to sit in seclusion in Mauza Achhra, the name uncertainly printed, and that he left because all the date palms there, the words uncertainly printed, recited the *tasbih*, glorifying and praising God, which disturbed his concentration (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 287).

Inside the fort, too, there were graves. Describing the Nigah Khana, Chishti notes a similar building in the fort of Lahore, facing the throne on its south side, in which he himself saw a grave built; common people called it the Nigar Khana too (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 787).

Mozang was once part of the city. This Mauza Mozang, Chishti writes, a former settlement outside the city of Lahore, was one of the mohallas of the city of Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 115). In his section on the *takia* of Shah Mir, a garbled passage speaks of a founder of Mohalla Mozang, perhaps someone called Fateh Mozang, the name uncertainly printed, who came with the first Chaghtai kings when they came here (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 207). Elsewhere Mozang is said to have been settled in connection with Pir Shirazi Baloch, and the Baloch people of Mozang came and went to him (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 211).

The burial ground of Miani lies west of Mauza Mozang (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 211). Chishti gives it a section of its own, headed as the graveyard of the Miani tract, Panj Dhera, the last words uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 186), and closes it with a table of the named sites in the graveyard (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 192). Because there are many graveyards and graves in the land of the Miani tract, he promises a plan of the graves for the book (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 211). He says that he has described the graveyard of the Parsis in his account of Miani Sahib (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 733).

He gives several accounts of Miani's name and history. In Akbar's reign, he writes, the place was a village in which only learned men lived, and because Punjabis call the ulama *mian*, it was called Miani; later, when its ulama gained nearness to the king, it was given another name, of which only a first word, perhaps Shah, can be read (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 191). Its chief then was Hafiz Jan Muhammad, father of Hazrat Abu Muhammad Qadiri; the ulama shared a grant, the word uncertainly printed; after his death Abu Muhammad Qadiri was made its head; then a split arose over a point of the *shariat*, many left, and the village was deserted (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 191). Hazrat Mir Ali, the name uncertainly printed, was brought from a place printed as Gumhat, also uncertain, appointed by the king and settled there; the headship was shared between Abu Muhammad and Mir Ali, and the place was settled again (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 191). A Pir whose printed name may be Alima had the coffins of five elders brought from Gumhat and buried here; a grave mound is a *dher* in Punjabi, hence the name Miani Panj Dher, the word uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 191). East and south of one enclosure, he writes, lies a graveyard of the Kanjar, the word uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 191).

The gravediggers of Miani told him their own version. On their word, there was first a mauza here, and the reason it was called by a name printed as Siani is given, but the key word cannot be read (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 211). Their account, fragmentary at this point, speaks of fruit brought from *wilayat*, of digging earth, and of a gravedigger appointed at four rupees, the figure uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 212). The village of Miani was inhabited, they said, in the time of Muqim Sahib of the Hujra (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 212). In his account of the gravediggers, Chishti follows the place through the reigns of Shah Jahan and Alamgir; under Muhammad Shah the graveyard itself became deserted, and under Maharaja Ranjit Singh burials resumed and it grew, though the passage is heavily damaged and the order of events approximate (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 204). The Sikhs, in a damaged passage, looted Mauza Miani and all its people scattered; later a settlement was made, at a rent of one rupee a year for each house, and it grew to about two hundred houses, the figures uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 212). When Miani was deserted, he writes, it became this graveyard (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 213).

## Communities

Chishti claims for his book a breadth that matches the city's. In the afterword he writes that it covers the elders of the Hindus and of the people of Islam alike, and that in its spirit of universal peace, *sulh-e kull*, it respects every religion (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 872).

The book shows the city's communities through the violence that passed between them. Under Mir Mannu, in Muhammad Shah's reign, the Sikhs had grown in number and there was a general order to seize and kill them (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 121). Later, in the time of persecution by the Sikhs, the property of hundreds of people in the city is said to have been lost, in a very damaged passage (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 722). During a disturbance in Lahore, which a garbled passage connects with Sher Singh, the Gilani household raised a flag so that the Sikhs would know theirs as a house of the sayyids (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 525). When the Gilani hazrat moved into a donated haveli, he writes, the whole city was in uproar (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 525), and in a garbled passage all the nobles of Lahore are named at the ancestor's death or funeral (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 526).

He records a community of Jains. Of the panth of Parasnath he writes that most of its disciples in Lahore now come from a group whose printed name is uncertain; they do not tie the mouth cloth and do not marry, and anyone who marries is expelled (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 544). In a largely illegible passage, people of Lahore and elsewhere are said to gather, and the elders of Lahore and Amritsar are named (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 541).

Other households and peoples appear in passing. The Faqir family was known in Lahore as the family of the Faqir sahibs and of the physicians (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 584); every notable of Lahore knows their nobility, and all the people of Lahore, indeed of the Punjab, call their mohalla by a name printed as Qutb Yunan, the reading uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 585). The Baloch of Mozang came and went to Pir Shirazi Baloch (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 211), and Chishti promises a later account of the houses of the people of Delhi in the city of Lahore, the words uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 292).

The gravediggers of Miani were a community of their own. Mian Murad Bakhsh, of the gravedigger people, tired of the work and took one Khwaja as his pupil, and that man's descendants are now among the gravediggers (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 204; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 209). Chishti records their genealogy twice (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 209; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 213), and twice lists the gravediggers working there in his day, once on the word of Sayyid Mansur, the *sajjada nashin* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 209), and once again, with names such as Rahmat, Nabi Bakhsh, Miran Bakhsh, Shahab al-Din, Nur Din and Chiragh Din (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 213).

## Shrines, Endowments and the State

In Chishti's account the city's monuments were at the mercy of its rulers. The elders of Lahore invited Ranjit Singh to take the city when Lehna Singh, as ruler, began destroying the monuments of former kings (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 786). Yet in Ranjit Singh's own time, he writes, the red, black and marble stones of the country around Lahore were torn down (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 296). The buildings of the earlier age survived to the reign of Maharaja Ranjit Singh; they had no guardian, and what people did to them is lost in a damaged phrase; when the cantonments of Ranjit Singh's armies were built, the bricks of old tombs and houses were pulled out, so that thousands of tombs, domes, mosques, gardens and old havelis that were memorials of the amirs of the past were lost (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 431).

Custodians, in his account, did not always share their histories. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar had long sought an account of the elders of Lahore's environs and could not obtain it, because the *sajjada nashins* would not give him word of their own history, the passage partly illegible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 31).

At Miani the land itself was granted and shared. In Akbar's time the ulama of the village shared a grant, the word uncertainly printed, and its headship was later shared between Abu Muhammad Qadiri and Mir Ali, whom the king appointed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 191). After the Sikhs looted the village, the resettled houses paid a rent of one rupee a year each (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 212).

The gravediggers' income shows how a burial ground paid its people. When a body comes, Chishti writes, the owner of the dead gives the gravediggers their due for digging the grave, and beyond that gives the *sajjada nashin* something as he is able (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 213). A gravedigger keeps his share whatever other work he does, and a rupee is first divided into four parts, the figure uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 213); the detailed division that follows is mostly illegible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 214). He also names a well at Miani and the property of Ilahi Bakhsh the gravedigger, in a damaged passage (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 214).

## Festivals and Practice

Ranjit Singh's rule reached into the city's observances: it was proclaimed in the city that whoever brought out a *ta'zia* would lose his head (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 274).

Chishti records a Lahore custom of a humbler kind: the Shakir Shahi *konda* is famous in Lahore, made by Shakir Shah Kahar at two annas a *konda* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 275).

Visiting the city's shrines was itself a practice of pilgrims passing through: Fakhr-ud-Din, on his first journey toward Ajmer, visited all the mazars of Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 756).

## The Place in the Books

Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti wrote his account of Lahore on the orders of the commissioner. The commissioner, Mr William Coldstream, the name uncertainly printed, ordered him to write a detailed account of the buildings, mazars, tombs and mosques of the environs of Lahore; Chishti calls the task very hard, because the history of hundreds of years had to be gathered (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 28). Elsewhere he writes that Lala Amin Chand's work on the buildings and people of Lahore was cut short when the same official, his name printed differently there, left for Shahjahanabad (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 734).

He saw his book as the first full account of the city's antiquities. In the afterword he claims that no book had yet been written or printed that covered the antiquities of Lahore in full (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 872). He describes it as reporting on the tombs of the Muslim elders, on other buildings and on the old customs of Lahore, while being at root a history of the saints (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 29).

He wrote in conversation, and in argument, with earlier writers on the city. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar, he notes, had long sought an account of the elders of Lahore's environs and could not obtain it (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 31). He cites Lala Amin Chand's *Safarnama*, printed, as he gives it, by Koh Nur, and its account of the city of Lahore, and disputes it (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 733).

He was also a witness. Of the heads of Raja Hira Singh and Pandit Jalla, he writes in the afterword that he saw with his own eyes what was done with them in the city (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 868).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The founding of Lahore.** In one telling Raja Ram Chand had three sons, of whom one, perhaps Lo, built Lahore, a second settled Kasur and the third founded Mauza Rawan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 661); in another Raja Ramchandar had two sons, Kushu and Lohu, and Lohu settled the city (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 795).
- **The last nazim of Lahore.** Chishti calls Mir Momin Khan the last nazim of Lahore, after whom none was appointed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 173); he also names Nawab Abdullah Khan as nazim of Lahore from the year printed as 1161 (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 717), Khwaja Abid as subadar of Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 578), and an unnamed ruler of Lahore at the time Ranjit Singh took the city (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 579).
- **The heads of Hira Singh and Pandit Jalla.** In one account their heads and bodies were seen hanging after they were killed on the bank of the Ravi, and the heads stayed there for some days, in a place the damaged passage connects with a bazaar (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 590); in the afterword the heads were hung from a gutter spout opposite the haveli where Jawahar Singh was held, and a Sikh then kicked Pandit Jalla's head through the city of Lahore for several days, which Chishti says he saw himself (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 868).
- **How Miani was emptied and became a graveyard.** One account has the learned village deserted after a split over a point of the *shariat*, then resettled under Mir Ali (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 191); the gravediggers' history has the graveyard itself deserted under Muhammad Shah and growing again under Ranjit Singh (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 204); another passage has the Sikhs loot Mauza Miani and scatter its people, after which a settlement of houses grew up (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 212); and another says that when Miani was deserted it became this graveyard (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 213).
- **The name of Miani.** Chishti derives it from the ulama, whom Punjabis call *mian*, and adds Panj Dher from the five elders' graves (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 191); the gravediggers gave their own reason for a name printed as Siani, but its key word cannot be read (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 211).
- **The gravediggers' genealogy.** In one version Khwaja's two sons are Karim Bakhsh and Ilahi Bakhsh, each with four sons, among them Nabi Bakhsh, Miran Bakhsh and Rahmat (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 209); in another a son Nur Muhammad also dug graves, his sons were Khwaja and Taj, Khwaja's sons Karam Bakhsh and Ilahi Bakhsh, and Karam Bakhsh had five sons, the number uncertainly printed, among them Muhammad Bakhsh, Ahmad Bakhsh and Rahmat (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 213).
- **The gravediggers then working.** Chishti's list on the word of Sayyid Mansur, the *sajjada nashin*, runs to fourteen names, including Ranjha, Kalu and Ludhu, the last uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 209); his second list runs to ten, including Ghulam Husain and a name printed as Ilah Bakhsh, who are not in the first (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 213).
- **The gravediggers' income and the sajjada nashin.** One statement divides the income half and half between two lines and has part of it go to the *sajjada nashin* as wages for digging graves, in uncertainly printed words (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 209); the other has the family of the dead pay the gravediggers their due and give the *sajjada nashin* something besides, the *sajjada nashin* having no connection with the gravediggers' income (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 213).
- **Amin Chand's account of the Parsi graves.** Chishti writes that what is to be found in the Parsi graveyard does not match Lala Amin Chand's description of open pit graves (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 733), and he disputes Amin Chand's account of the city more generally (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 733).
