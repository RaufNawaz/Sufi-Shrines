## The Province in History

For Sujan Rai Bhandari, writing in a work he dates to the year one thousand one hundred and seven of the Hijra (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 581), the country is the *wilayat* of Sind (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 173), the realm of Sind (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 386–387), the lands of Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 175), and, in his own phrase, the *wilayat* of Sind known as Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 581). In his own day it is a Mughal *suba*, the *suba* of Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530). He begins its history with its capitals of ancient times. Brahmanabad, he writes, was a great city and the seat of the throne, and its fort had fourteen hundred towers, each a rope's length from the next; after it Adpur became the capital, and many traces of its towers and walls remain; after that came Dewal, and now Thatta and Debal are the seat of government (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530).

In Bhandari's chronicle of the sultans of Delhi, Sind is first a frontier reached by campaigns. Sultan Mahmud led a great army toward Multan and Sind and had as many as four thousand boats built, each fitted with three iron spikes, one at the prow and one on either side; his enemies sent their families away to the islands and fought, and most of them were killed or drowned (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 411). On another of Mahmud's campaigns a raja, whose name is uncertain in the printed text, abandoned his army out of faintheartedness and poor judgement and made for Sind, and the Sultan's troops pursued and caught him (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 421–420). Late in the year 417, a year uncertain in the printed text, Mahmud set out to punish a group who had behaved insolently toward his soldiers on the bank of the river Sind as he returned from Somnath (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 412). On Shihab al-Din's fourth campaign, in a year of the five hundreds whose last figures cannot be read, the Sultan went against Dewal, which is known as Thatta, brought the whole country along the river Sind under his control, and returned with the rarities of that land and great wealth (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 404). In Iltutmish's reign the fugitive Jalal al-Din fled toward Sind and Siwistan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 399), and under Balban the realm of Sind, with all its dependencies, was fixed as the *jagir* of Prince Muhammad Sultan, who is posted to Multan in the next sentence (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 386–387).

Bhandari gives the country's own rulers a section of their own, and names his source: the history of Sultan Bahadur Shah, he writes, sets down the affairs of the sultans of the *wilayat* of Sind thus (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 173–174). The first rulers were the descendants of Bani Tamim Ansari, the reading uncertain, who ruled for one hundred and thirty nine years; then the Soomras, who stood out among the *zamindars* for their strength and following, took over, in a passage partly illegible; government stayed in the Soomra family for five hundred years, and then passed to the Sammas (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 173). The first Samma ruler was Jam Anzar, and the title Jam, given to their headman and chief, Bhandari traces back, through a word that cannot be read, to Jamshid (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 173). In the time of Jam Bani Thatta son of Jam Anzar, the name uncertain in the printed text, Sultan Firuz Shah, ruler of Delhi, marched against the Jam three times, at last took the *wilayat* of Sind and brought the Jam to Delhi, and then, pleased with his service, gave the country back to him (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 173).

His list of the Samma Jams runs as follows, with many names and figures uncertain in the printed text. Jam Anzar reigned from the year 735, itself uncertain, for a number of years that cannot be read and six months; his brother Jam Junan, fourteen years; Jam Bani Thatta, son of Anzar, fifteen years; his brother Jam Tamachi, thirteen years; Jam Salahuddin, eleven years and some months; Jam Nizamuddin, son of Salahuddin, two years and some months; Jam Ali Sher, six years and some months; Jam Karan, son of Tamachi, a day and a half; Jam Fath Khan, son of Sikandar, fifteen years, raised up by the notables when the country was left without a ruler; Jam Tughluq, son of Sikandar, eighteen years as the numeral is read with some doubt; Jam Mubarak, three days; Jam Sikandar, son of Fath Khan, a year and six months; Jam Sanjar, eight years and some months; Jam Nizamuddin, who was known as Jam Nanda, sixty two years; and Jam Firuz, nineteen years (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 172–173).

The Samma line ended, in Bhandari's account, in a quarrel within the family. Jam Salahuddin, a kinsman of Jam Firuz whose sister was married to Sultan Muzaffar of Gujarat, twice raised armies from Gujarat and won; Jam Firuz called in Mirza Shah Beg Arghun from Qandahar, Salahuddin was killed, and the government of the *wilayat* of Sind passed to Jam Firuz alone (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 172). Then in the year 927, a year uncertain in the printed text, Mirza Shah Beg Arghun led an army from Qandahar and took Thatta; Jam Firuz went to Gujarat, entered the service of Sultan Bahadur Shah, gave him his daughter in marriage and died there, and with that the rule of the Samma line was cut off (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 172).

The Arghuns and Tarkhans followed. Mirza Shah Beg took the *wilayat* of Sind by strength and courage and died in the year 930 after a rule of three years; Mirza Shah Husain fortified Bhakkar anew, took Multan, and died in 962 after thirty two years (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 171). Mirza Isa Tarkhan, one of Mirza Shah Beg's amirs, at Thatta, and Sultan Mahmud at Bhakkar each claimed independence, sometimes at peace with each other and sometimes at war, and Isa Tarkhan died in 965 after thirteen years; Mirza Muhammad Baqi overcame his brother Khan Baba, kept up his father's dealings with Sultan Mahmud of Bhakkar, and ruled eighteen years (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 171). These years, and the eighteen, are all uncertain in the printed text. Bhandari's account of the reign of Sultan Husain Langah shows the same rulers reaching north: Jam Bayazid and Jam Ibrahim, offended with Jam Nanda, ruler of the *wilayat* of Thatta, went over to Sultan Husain and were given a *jagir* whose name is uncertain in the printed text, and Jam Bayazid later helped the Sultan kill his vizier Imad al-Mulk (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 295). When Babur came, his *farman* went to Mirza Shah Husain Arghun, ruler of Thatta, who came up from Thatta to fight for Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 295).

Sind's independent rule ended, in Bhandari, when its ruler entered the empire. The order he records was first to take the *wilayat* of Thatta, and after that to turn to the conquest of Qandahar (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 175). Mirza Jani Beg, son of Painda Muhammad son of Muhammad Baqi, came to court with the Khankhanan in the year 1001 of the Hijra, a year uncertain in the printed text, having ruled for eight years, and the country entered the protected dominions of the empire (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 171). Jani Beg was given the rank of three thousand and the *jagir* of Thatta, and Lahari Bandar, the port on the Thatta side, was made crown land (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 174). Among the deeds of the Khankhanan, Mirza Abd al-Rahim, Bhandari lists the conquest of Gujarat, Thatta and the Deccan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 244). The chronicle's table of contents also lists a section on the conquest of the lands of Thatta and the coming of Jani Beg (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 591).

Thereafter the *suba* of Thatta appears as an office given to the emperor's men. On Shah Jahan's march from the Deccan to his accession, in a year printed as 1037 and uncertain (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 104–106), during a halt of seven days at Ahmadabad, a Mirza Isa Tarkhan received the rank of four thousand *zat* and two thousand horse and the governorship of the *suba* of Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 105). In the pursuit of Dara Shikoh, Qubad Khan, the name uncertain in the printed text, stayed behind by imperial order to hold the governorship of Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 49); at the halt at Lahore he had been raised to the governorship of the *suba* of Thatta with an increase to the rank of four thousand and three thousand horse, being described as one of the foster kin of Shaikh Mir, the words uncertain in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 64). The table of contents of the Aurangzeb reign lists a section on Dara Shikoh's coming from Thatta toward Gujarat (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 589).

Michel Boivin tells the province's medieval history from the other side. Of the fourteenth century in which Pithoro Pir lived he writes that "the period when he lived was turbulent": regionally it was the time of transition between the Somras and the Sammas, and although the Sammas "officially took power from the first in 1351, the Somras had preserved their control of certain outlying areas of Sindh, including, precisely, the area of Umarkot" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 189, folio 176). In 1351 Muhammad Shah Tughluq, sultan of Delhi, "arrived in Sindh in pursuit of a rebel"; in 1367 Firuz Shah Tughluq "returned to lay siege to Thatha", and "Jam Juna II, who controlled Sindh then, sought alliances with the sultan of Gujarat" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 189, folio 176). An endnote gives the book's own measure of how the unit has changed: the Siraiki speaking area of Southern Punjab "was a part of Sindh during the Middle Ages" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 216, folio 203). The Naqshbandiyya, in his account, "came to Sindh from the sixteenth century onwards, with the coming of the Tarkhans and the Arghuns, two Central Asian dynasties that ruled Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 228, folio 215). Historical sources, he writes, show that before the eighteenth century "the people who identified themselves as Muslims were few", probably because the name was restricted to the *sayyids* and their dependents (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 22–23, folios 9–10).

The Kalhoras and then the Talpurs ruled the province before the British. The Baluchis had provided since the eighteenth century "the bulk of the troops of the Kalhoras, who had ruled Sindh before them", and under the Talpurs they stood at the top of local society and held *jagirs* (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 38–41, folios 25–28). The power of the *pir* "was reinforced in the eighteenth century when the Kalhora developed the irrigation system" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 38–41, folios 25–28). Before 1843 the Sunnis were the majority of Muslims, "but the sovereigns, the Talpur, were themselves Shiites" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 38–41, folios 25–28). In an endnote Boivin reports Ingvild Flaskerud's view that the first portrait of Ali was drawn in Sindh and then sent as a gift to Fath Ali Shah of Persia, who had friendly relations with the Talpurs, "the emirs of Sindh, who were themselves Shia" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 213, folio 200). Under the Talpur rule the Amils, a class of scribes, "almost monopolized the function of kardar", and "when the British conquered Sindh, they had already formed a separate social class" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 44, folio 31). Jürgen Schaflechner adds that the established rule of the Kalhoras in the eighteenth century brought new business openings for merchants, one reason why the Lohanas were the most important trading community of Sindh, Kutch and Saurashtra in the nineteenth century (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 80, folio not stated); and Sindh's place "between trading routes connecting Afghanistan, Khorasan, Arabia, Persia, Turkey, and other places made the province appealing to a wide array of merchants from the entire continent" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 81, folio not stated).

Boivin's account turns on colonial Sindh. The historical context of the region at the turn of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, he writes, "is dominated by British colonization", and the shared culture he studies rested on "interaction between new local elites and colonial administrators, as early as the middle of the nineteenth century" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 17, folio 4). The first European movement to reach Sindh was Freemasonry, "since the conqueror and first governor of Sindh, Sir Charles Napier, built a Masonic lodge in 1843 in Karachi" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 46, folio 33). When Persian was given up as the official language in the 1850s, Sindhi and English became the two languages of administration, and the Amils led the great campaign of translation launched by Sir Bartle Frere, the commissioner in Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 44, folio 31). Boivin concludes that the province's main social structure was "a quasi-feudal system, with a civil servant class and the groups involved in trade, but the policy implemented by the British commissioners, especially Sir Bartle Frere, gave birth to the Sindhi intelligentsia" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 63, folio 50). After 1857 many Kayasths went to work in the princely states, including the Khairpur emirate in Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 26, folio 13). With the growth of new trading centres in Sindh, especially Karachi, the Lohanas invested in agricultural land, mainly cotton, and by the end of the nineteenth century had become powerful landowners, known locally as *zamindars* (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 43–44, folios 30–31).

Boivin gives the census figures for Sindh from the first census of 1872 as Hughes reported them: a population printed as 2,333,527 million, with 1,718,688 Muslims, 393,092 Hindus, 5,034 Christians and 86,663 persons in a category of other castes such as Sikhs, Parsis and Jews, although the same sentence opens by saying that Sindh's population statistics are not available in that census (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 38, folio 25). In another place he describes Sindh as "a largely Muslim-dominated country, making up about 75 per cent of the whole population, where the power had been in the hands of Muslims for centuries" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 199, folio 186). In 1941, he writes, the Hindus made up about 21 per cent of the population of Sindh, including 6 per cent of the scheduled castes, so that they would have numbered under a million, exactly 0.9 million (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 61, folio 48).

The communal peace of the province was not unbroken. From the 1920s onwards riots between Hindus and Muslims increased, a growing tension that culminated with Partition (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 64, folio 51). Boivin notes that "it is a common trope to claim that Sindh did not suffer the violence experienced by Punjab", but reports from Punjab were reaching the province, and there was a precedent in the Sukkur riots, which spread beyond the city to Rohri and to other parts of Sindh such as Lakki (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 61, folio 48). Even if it never reached the level of Punjab's violence, he concludes, "it is irrelevant to claim there was no communal violence in Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 62, folio 49). The narrative of Rochaldas, one of his subjects, lays the spread of violence through Sindh not on the Muslim Sindhis but on the Mohajirs, who wanted the houses of the Hindus, at least in Karachi; some Hindus had already sent their wives and children to India while they themselves stayed on in Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 70–71, folios 57–58).

Boivin follows the migration in numbers. By early November 1947, 246,000 Hindus had already left Sindh by sea, air or rail; by 1 January 1948 nearly 500,000 people were estimated to have sought refuge in India; and Hindus from interior Sindh travelled in special trains chartered by the Sindh government (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 62, folio 49). In December, with an added train, 140,000 Hindus crossed the border, and on 14 January the government of India appointed a director general of evacuation to assist non-Muslims who wished to leave Sindh for India (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 63, folio 50). The Hindu Sindhis left especially after the Karachi riots of January 1948 (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 66, folio not stated). He puts the migrants at closer to 0.8 million, if not fewer, against a general consensus of about a million (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 61, folio 48). For Schaflechner, "The partition of the subcontinent brought major changes to the province of Sindh": the departure of the Hindu elite and the arrival of Muslim refugees from India altered its demography, and the Sindhi community came to define itself by a sense of being shut out and subjugated by Muhajir power (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 87, folio not stated).

Even so, Boivin writes, "Sindh is the province of Pakistan with the most significant Hindu population", and "The province of Sindh now belongs to Pakistan, a country that has been through different steps of islamization" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 176–177, folios not stated and 164). Schaflechner traces Sindhi nationalism, following Oscar Verkaaik, to "the struggle for independence from the Bombay Presidency in the 1920s and 1930s", and names as important to its later growth the 1947 publication *Save Sind, Save the Continent*, by Ibrahim Joyo, whose first name the book prints as Ibrahmin (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 88, folio not stated).

## Saints and Pilgrimage

Boivin finds three Sufi orders dominant among the Hindu Sufis of Sindh. The Qadiriyya was "the most influential"; the Qalandariyya, in Sindh, reflects the radiance of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar and the pilgrimage centre of Sehwan Sharif; and the Sohrawardiyya was "the oldest Sufi order that settled in Multan and was predominant in the Indus Valley from the thirteenth century onwards" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 227–228, folios 214–215). "Maybe because of the Sohrawardiyya's hold on Sindh, the Chishtiyya could not spread in Sindh as it did in the rest of South Asia" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 227–228, folios 214–215), and elsewhere he states simply that the Chishtiyya was not widespread in Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 217, folio 204). The Sufis of Sindh he studies were mostly in touch with the Sohrawardiyya and the Qadiriyya (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 139, folio 126), the brotherhoods "that are dominant in the landscape of Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 98, folio 85). The Naqshbandiyya, he writes, has several important centres in the province, especially Lowari Sharif in Southern Sindh, near Badin (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 228, folio 215). He adds a caution: "in Sindh, Sufi practice was more based on a personal allegiance with an individual than any affiliation with a Sufi tariqa", Sufis could belong to several orders at once, and the subject "deserves to be studied further in Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 200, folio 187).

The province's holy places, in Boivin's account, are its *darbars*. In Sindh another Persian word was mostly used, *darbar*, and "In Sindh, the darbar was the sacred territory in which the Sufi culture was at work"; the term was "used by the Sikhs, the Muslims and the Hindus" alike (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 37–38, folios 24–25). Colonial rule, he argues, brought "a renewal of religiosity in relation to the spread of a composite middle class. It was marked by the emergence of the darbars, spreading a darbar culture" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 64, folio 51). Some *darbars* of the Nanakpanth had been founded in the late eighteenth century, but "there was a renaissance of the Nanakpanthi darbars in the second half of the nineteenth century" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 53–54, folios 40–41), and in his general conclusion he speaks of "the building of many new Nanakpanthi darbars all over Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 199, folio 186). Two of the Hindu Sufis he studies came from Northern Sindh, from Shikarpur and Rohri, and he takes this as a possible clue that "the devotional centres of Sindh had shifted north sometime after the British conquest" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 60, folio 47).

Boivin sorts the ways in which the Hindus of Sindh are related to Sufism into three kinds, each with an example. The most common is a Sufi tomb visited by both Muslims and Hindus, such as Bhit Shah; also relatively common is a saint with a double identity, Muslim and Hindu, such as Udero Lal; and the third is a Sufi *darbar* managed by Hindus, such as that of Pithoro Pir (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 177–178, folios 164–165). "In Sindh, we already know that a number of sacred figures have a dual religious identity, Muslim and Hindu. The Muslim identity is always related to Sufism, while the Hindu side usually belongs to the Nathpanth" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 187, folio 174). Hindus have been coming to the Sufi mausoleums of Sindh since the nineteenth century (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 183, folio 170). "The large majority of the Hindus used to pay a visit to the Sufi shrines, but does it mean they are Sufi? Of course not. It does mean that, for them, the Sufis are a part of their religious legacy" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 192–193, folios 179–180). Among the Muslim disciples of Pithoro Pir in Sindh are Sindhis such as Sammas and Panhwars and Baluch such as the Maris and the Legharis, and "The disciples of Pithoro Pir mix in Sindh", whereas in Kutch the untouchable disciples worship separately (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 191, folio 178). The Pithoro Pir case shows "the variety of the negotiations between Sufism and Hinduism in Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 192, folio 179).

Schaflechner, writing of the goddess temple of Hinglaj, sees the same landscape from the side of pilgrimage: "The south of Pakistan, and especially Sindh, offers a whole range of salvific spaces that are frequently visited by different religious communities and form a network of pilgrimage routes or ritual journeys" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 107, folio not stated). Legends linking Shah Abdul Latif and the Goddess, he writes, "connect Hindu and Muslim sacred spaces in Sindh and show that imagined landscapes do not necessarily have to be separated using clear-cut religious taxonomies" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 109, folio not stated). Most of the visitors to the festival he studied are Kohlis, Meghvar and Bhil, which, he notes, reflects the Hindu population of Sindh as a whole (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 154, folio not stated).

Older holy figures belong to this landscape too. "In Sindh, Hallaj is a very popular figure, and many Sufi poets mention his name in their verses" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 102, folio 89), and the book's glossary says that he visited Sindh in 905 (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 219, folio 206). The Nathpanthis, known locally as Verangins or Berangins, played a role "in many forms" in the province (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 77, folio 64), and Shah Latif's vocabulary for the jogis shows that they were "an important religious community in eighteenth-century Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 92, folio 79). The name *sami* is generally given in Sindh to a group of wandering *faqirs* (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 77, folio 64). The deified heroes most popular in Sindh were Rama Pir, Pithoro Pir, Pabuji and Guga Pir (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 42–43, folios 29–30); Ramdeo Pir is still known in the province as Rama Pir and has today been taken into the temples of the pan Indian divinities (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 192, folio 179).

Jhulelal has a geography of his own in the province. Jhulelal was already worshipped by some groups among the Hindus of Sindh, though most of them were Nanakpanthis (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 158, folio 145). Before Partition his temples in Sindh were built with a dome, as were some Sikh *samadhis*, and his priests, the Thakurs, lived in several cities of the province before migrating to India from 1947 onwards (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 211, folio 198). In colonial Sindh there was an attempt to create a centralised path devoted to Jhulelal, then known as Amar Lal or Udero Lal, with a meeting of several Hindu *panchayats* of Sindh held at Udero Lal (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 53–54, folios 40–41). In April 2016 Boivin and a team toured Southern Sindh to map the shrines devoted to Jhulelal there (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 184, folio 171), and he places Thano Bula Khan in the Kohistan, "the western part of Sindh that stands close to Baluchistan" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 184, folio 171).

The forms of devotion mark the province off from the Sindhi *darbars* that migrants founded in India. Those Indian *darbars* "are not centred on tombs, unlike those of Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 131, folio 118), and in them the photographic portrait carries the sacredness of the space in place of the tomb, "as is the case in the dargahs of Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 132, folio 119). The *shahnai* is very common in any religious ritual performed in Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 145, folio 132), and in India some typical Sufi rituals performed in Sindh, such as the *alam* and the *dhamal*, have been suppressed (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 152, folio 139). The *bayragins*, as relics, are in Sindh, "once again irrespective of religious belonging", the mark of renunciation (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 120, folio 107). From India the province remains the source of holiness: the *darbar* of Rochaldas is made a territory extending from an original sacred territory in Sindh, the holy city of Sehwan Sharif, and invoking Jhok Sharif builds "a network of sacred territories which are all located in Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 141, folio 128). The *mehndi* of Lal Shahbaz keeps a direct ritual link "with the lost territory of Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 147, folio 134), and the living link is "with the Master who stays in Sindh, nowadays in Pakistan" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 203, folio 190). Following Thapan, Boivin notes that the spiritual masters, the *sajjada nashins* or *pirs*, of Daraza, Tando Jahaniya and Jhok Sharif are still in Sindh, Pakistan (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 27, folio 14).

Bhandari, at the Mughal conquest, records a different kind of devotion. A great plague broke out in those parts, and dervishes dreamt that it came from the rebelliousness of the people of that country, and everyone made vows (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 174).

## Communities

Boivin's picture of the province's society in 1843 divides Muslim society along ethnic lines between Ashrafs and Ajlafs (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 38–41, folios 25–28). The Baluchis stood at the top; the *sayyids* were mostly landowners, *zamindars*, but were also *pirs*; and "The pirs of Sindh play the role of mediators between the tribes, and between the rulers and the governed" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 38–41, folios 25–28). Apart from a few groups of Afghans and Pathans in the northwest, the bulk of the Muslims were Sindhis, most of them landless farmers, *haris*; the trading Muslims were the Memon, the Khojah and the Bohrah; and excluded groups such as the Shikaris and the Shidi stood at the bottom (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 38–41, folios 25–28). Burton asserted that Arabic was known only to Muslim scholars (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 38–41, folios 25–28). Bhandari maps the peoples of his *suba* by its landscape: north of a line running from Multan and Uch to Thatta and Kech Makran lie hills of hard rock, home of the Baluch and some Afghans; to the south, from Uch to Gujarat, are hills of sand and the Bhati, whose chief has his seat at Jaisalmer, and other Rajput tribes; and from Bhakkar to Nasirpur and Amarkot live the Sabuda, the name uncertain in the printed text, and the Jareja (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529).

Among the Hindus, Boivin writes, British knowledge was mostly limited to the mercantile castes, from which arose two points, "the absence of a real system of castes and the small role played by the Brahmans" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 40–41, folios 27–28). The Saraswat Brahmans accepted food from the Lohanas, who made up half of the whole Hindu population and were divided into Amils and Bhaibands (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 40–41, folios 27–28). In Eastern Sindh the Rajputs had kept their feudal privileges under the Talpurs, and the British restored them in return for loyalty; "The panchayat system prevailed among the Hindus of Sindh. In rural Sindh, the panchayat of the Lohanas could have authority over other castes" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 40–41, folios 27–28). The Sodhas, the dominant Rajput group, came by their own tradition from Ujjain in Malwa in 1226, and their chief, Parmar Sodha, was established as king, *rano*, at Umarkot; most became landowners and farmers, and the Gazetteer of 1907 describes them as worshippers of Shiva (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 191, folio 178). "Contrary to popular belief, there were many untouchables in Sindh", among them Bhils or Kohlis and the Menghwar, mostly settled around Umerkot (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 40, folio 27). The printed sentence on whether these peoples were long established in the east of the province, "the ultimate extension of the great Thar Desert", reads that "it is certain whether" they were, which seems to have lost a negative; the first census reports that many had come from Gujarat or Rajasthan between 1843 and 1872, most worked in agriculture, and crafts were largely in Muslim hands (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 40, folio 27).

Schaflechner gives the Lohanas the same central place, through older witnesses. Burton wrote in 1851 that a description of the Lohanas "will be a general portrait of the Sindh Hindoos", and McMurdo that "The Lowannas or Lohannas compose the great body of Hindus in Sindh"; over the past few centuries the community absorbed many other groups and clans and now takes in a large part of the upper classes of Pakistani Hindus (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 79, folio not stated). Thakur, too, calls them "the general body of the Sind Hindus" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 80, folio not stated). Zahid-Khan explains the merging by the small number of non-Muslim Sindhis drawing together against the majority, and around the eighteenth or nineteenth century the merchant groups of Sindh divided: Lohanas who worked for the local rulers became Amils, at first 10 to 15 per cent of them, and those who went on trading were Bhaibands (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 81, folio not stated). Yet "Due to the heterogeneity of the Lohanas in Sindh, it is not possible to make any universal claim for the whole community" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 216, folio not stated). In Boivin's account the Amils were "probably from the eighteenth century onwards" in the administration (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 44, folio 31), a subcaste of the Lohanas who became the official scribes of the Kalhoras and the Talpurs (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 214, folio 201). "The Lohanas, once the dominant Hindu community in pre-partition Sindh, are still mostly involved in trade", and many of them, the Amils especially, "have played a leading role in integrating Sufism as a main component of Sindhi identity" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 193, folio 180).

Two paths dominated among the Lohanas and related merchant groups, the Nanakpanth and the Daryapanth (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 42–43, folios 29–30). The Daryapanth, "in all probability the oldest of the two", rests on the deification of the Indus and is served by a priestly caste, the Thakurs; the Lohanas practised animal sacrifice at Navratri and for Khetrapal, while the Rajputs followed neither path (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 42–43, folios 29–30). "In nineteenth-century Sindh, the Nanakpanth, or 'the path of Nanak', was probably the dominant religious path among the Hindu Sindhis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 90, folio 77), and many Nanakpanthis in Sindh belonged to a subgroup known as the Udasis (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 53, folio 40). After the Singh Sabha reforms, "The Sindhi Nanakpanthis were thus not included in the Sikh community, and they identified themselves as being a Hindu religious sect" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 200, folio 187). Schaflechner shows the same blurring in the census: many Lohanas in Sindh followed Nanak's teachings, so that in the 1881 census Lohanas from Shikarpur and Hyderabad were counted as Sikhs, and ten years later, in 1891, as Hindus (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 80, folio not stated). Today, he writes, many middle and upper class Hindus in Sindh follow the Nanak *panth* (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 257, folio not stated), and it is common to cover one's head before entering a *tikana*, the temple holding the Guru Granth Sahib (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 286, folio not stated). The colour saffron, which in Sindh corresponds to a bright red, is for the Daryapanthis a symbol both of religion and of marriage (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 181, folio 168).

Belonging in the province was long a matter of locality rather than creed. Burton wrote that certain populations of Sindh could be counted neither Muslim nor Hindu, Raikes spoke of peoples recognised by neither, and this category, Boivin argues, matched the creed of the Sufis of Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 19–20, folios 6–7). "As Richard Burton noted as early as 1851, it is indeed the worship of the intercessors that constitutes the common religious idiom of Hindus and Muslims" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 51, folio 38). In rural Sindh Muslims worshipped the smallpox goddess Sitala when epidemics struck the village, and "In the religiosity of Sindh, the devotional relationship of a follower with a spiritual Master was prevalent, rather than the focus being on affiliation to an institutionalized religion" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 51, folio 38). The colonial step changed this: because of new factors such as the census, people had more and more to identify themselves by their affiliation to the great religions, where before they had identified themselves "according to their locality, caste and ethnicity" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 23, folio 10). The Sindhis of the late nineteenth century were increasingly inclined to name themselves by a religion the colonial power would recognise (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 51, folio 38), and the migrants, coming from a Muslim dominated region, identified more and more as Hindu in the later censuses (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 33, folio 20).

The Hindu Sufis were the meeting point. Narsain, as Boivin reports him, "clearly states that the integration of Sufism inside of the religious environment of the Hindu Sindhis dates back to the nineteenth century, and that the main actors involved were the Amils, the literary class" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 27, folio 14). Since the Amils were the bulk of the literati in Sindh, they were also instrumental in bringing Sufism into the religion of the local Hindus (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 45, folio 32). Boivin explains why Hindus took Muslim Sufi masters by a "cultural islamization" working beyond religious affiliation, to which the Hindus were surrendering without knowing it, and by the fact that "the Muslim Sufi market was far more developed than the Hindu market"; to be a Sufi, for a Hindu, "was to participate fully in the process of building a modern Sindhi province" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 199, folio 186). Krishna's worship had developed among the Amils by the late nineteenth century, some of whom accepted the Vallabhachari sect (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 135, folio 122), and Sufis of the period such as Sachal, Qutub Ali Shah and Bedil brought Krishna into their poetry, so that "despite the fact that Sindh was Muslim dominated, the Krishnaite Bhakti was influential among wujudi Sufis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 198, folio 185). An endnote reads wall paintings of Krishna in Hindu temples as a possible sign of a Krishnaite revival among the Sindhi Hindus in the nineteenth century (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 213, folio 200). Conversely, "the Muslim Sufis of Sindh were mostly attracted by the Vishnuite school of Hinduism" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 107, folio 94). A main factor in the religious renewal of the province was the Neo-Vedanta of Ramakrishna (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 200, folio 187), and before Partition "there are many stories where businessmen decided to give up all of their worldly goods and activities to become renunciants" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 205, folio 192). No formal relation between Sindh and the Hindu branch of the Naqshbandiyya has been found (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 26, folio 13).

The Hindus of present day Sindh, as both authors describe them, are mostly of the lowest castes. Sindh is "home to the majority of Pakistan's Hindus", whom Schaflechner numbers at between 6 and 10 million, an estimate that varies widely by source, and of whom over 90 per cent belong to underprivileged tribes or subcastes such as the Meghvar, the Bhil and the Kohli, "who frequently work as manual laborers for the powerful landowning elite in Sindh" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 13, folio not stated). The estimate comes from the Human Rights Commission of Pakistan in Sindh (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 28, folio not stated, endnote 1), and the share of over 90 per cent from NGOs in the province (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 276, folio not stated). "In Sindh, the Kohli community is mainly a disempowered scheduled caste whose members work as agricultural peasants on the fields owned by Sindh's feudal elite" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 154, folio not stated). Boivin likewise writes that, despite the mass migration, the Sindhi Hindus of Pakistan number several millions, most of them from the scheduled castes, mostly Menghwars, Bhils and Kohlis (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 175, folio 162); some call them Dalits and some even claim they are not Hindu, yet "the Sufi legacy is still alive among the Dalits of Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 194, folio 181). Among the Hindus of Sindh there is now a debate on whether the term Dalit is apt (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 187, folio 174). The way they take in the Sufi legacy depends, he writes, "on the social milieu, the caste system and the urban/rural context" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 194, folio 181).

Caste remains a contested matter in the province. Thakur wrote that the caste system "is almost absent in Sind", a view Schaflechner links to the assimilating role of the Lohanas and to nationalist portraits of Sindh as a place of religious harmony; devoted Sindhi Hindu nationalists told him caste discrimination exists today only in India or in Pakistan's other provinces, while "Many upper-caste Sindhi Hindus often deny or ignore acts of segregation", which Dalit organisations fiercely oppose (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 277–278, folio not stated). Boivin, too, notes the frequent claim that there was no caste system in Sindh, or no rigid one, because the Brahmans were so few, and allows that Sindh may have had a social fabric of its own, though colonial rule renewed it deeply there as everywhere in India (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 36–37, folio not stated / folio 24). Schaflechner sets the reformers' ideas of progress against "low-caste rituals in Sindh such as the sacrifice of animals or the consumption of alcohol as prasād" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 278, folio not stated), and notes that many other Hindu groups in Sindh, and all over Pakistan, sacrifice animals for the fulfilment of wishes or at rites of passage (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 310, folio not stated). Burton records the unusual use in Sindh of the term Vaishiya, for operatives and mechanics as against merchants and shopkeepers (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 98–99, folio not stated, endnote 16), and says that the Saraswat Brahmins were devoted to the Devi, "who, in most areas of Sindh, was called Hinglaj" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 100, folio not stated, endnote 28).

Some communities carry their histories as legends. In the Brahmakshatriya story, as Schaflechner reports it, Parashurama "arrives in the province of Sindh" to kill Kshatriyas, and "The mighty king Ratnasena, the ruler of Sindh" flees (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 61–62, folio not stated). In the Charan story of Avar, famine drives her family to Sindh, then ruled by Umar Sumra, a recent convert to Islam known as Hamir; the Samma and Bhati Rajput kings, who had long wanted the country, help Avar kill him and then divide Sindh between them; another story has Avar kill fifty two Hun demons and so save the people of Sindh (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 72, folio not stated). The Vagris' own history, he writes, has the community moving back and forth between Sindh and Gujarat for many generations until Partition forced them to settle on one side of the border, though with no written account "the clan's detailed history can only be based on speculation" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 308, folio not stated).

Security in the countryside rests on kin. "In rural Sindh, in particular, the clan or the barādarī serves as a guarantee of security rather than the nation state" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 18, folio not stated). Among the Hindus of Sindh the formula that all have one Lord, *Sab ka malik ek hai*, has for some years been very popular and marks a new policy of theirs, and the Hindu *dargah* shows how strongly the middle and upper Hindu classes of Sindh hold to their Sufi legacy (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 186, folio 173; Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 187, folio 174).

## Language, Poetry and Music

For Boivin poetry is the heart of the province's religion: "Sufi poetry is the backbone of Sufism in Sindh, including for the Hindu Sufis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 26, folio 13). He knew before he began that the Hindus of Sindh "had gone to Sufi shrines, and that they shared their passion for Sufi poetry with Muslim Sindhis and others, especially through Shah Abd al-Latif's Shah jo Risalo" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 14, folio not stated). The works of the Sufis of Sindh, known as *kalams*, "constituted the foundations of Sufi culture" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 19–20, folios 6–7), and "In Sindh, save the Naqshbandis, Sufism is dominated by poetry expressing the concept of wahdat-e wujud" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 162, folio 149). The Sufis to whom the Hindus look mostly belong to Upper Sindh, where Siraiki is used for Sufi poetry besides Sindhi, and all belong to the *wujudi* school of the Sufism of Sindh, which leaves out the Naqshbandis (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 108, folio 95); this trend "seems to have developed in the nineteenth century, during colonial domination" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 109, folio 96).

The province's religious culture, Boivin argues, was made in its own language. "Manuscripts and early lithographed books provide evidence that the religious culture of Sindh had been vernacularized", and the first phase was usually a translation from Arabic and Persian into Sindhi (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 23, folio 10). In the early eighteenth century Naqshbandi scholars from Lowari Sharif wrote treatises in Sindh showing that the Muslim Sindhis did not know the Quran, wrote paraphrases and then a Sindhi translation of it, and condemned as un-Islamic some rituals they called Hindu or Shia (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 22–23, folios 9–10). Their later treatises led to the conclusion that the Sindhis, even when officially Muslim, did not practise orthodox Islam (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 228, folio 215). "Devotional literature in Sindhi thus formed the basis of a culture that transcended social, religious and ethnic divisions", and it was carried not by the *ulema* or the *pandits* but to the villages by the caste musicians, the *manghanar* or *mirasi* (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 52–53, folios 39–40). The Shia devotional poetry of the *marsiya* "is cherished by both Muslims and Hindus, the latter being performers as well as authors" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 42, folio 29).

Siraiki, in Boivin's account, is "the Sindhi dialect mostly spoken in Northern Sindh, and especially among the Baluchi population", and many Sindhi Sufi poets, especially from Northern Sindh, composed in several languages (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 171, folio 158); the glossary calls it a language of Northern Sindh and Southern Punjab (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 220, folio 207). Ajwani held that the Bhakti reached Sindh only in the sixteenth century, because of its isolation, but Boivin finds no evidence in him of the influence of the *sants* on the province's spiritual life (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 90, folio 77). Jotwani, cited in the book as Motwani, called Shah Latif and Sachal "the late participaters in the Bhakti movement which had a major impact in Sindh through the spread of Guru Nanak's teaching" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 91, folio 78), and the *slokas* of Sami were probably widespread among the Hindus of Northern Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 104, folio 91). Sachal Sarmast had many followers among the Hindu trade castes of Northern Sindh, and some of them took their love of singing his poetry to India when they migrated after Partition (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 150, folio 137).

Print and the colonial state remade this heritage. "In Sindh, a printing press was brought to Karachi in the 1850s, but the main event in relation to Sufism came with the printing in 1866 of the Shah jo Risalo", which was followed by a rationalisation of the poetry begun by Hindu literati in the 1890s (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 47–48, folios 34–35). Hindu Sindhis played a leading part in publishing Sufi books in late nineteenth century Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 173, folio 160), and Boivin stresses "the key role played by the Hindus of Sindh in the transmission of the Sufi legacy up through partition" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 170, folio 157). In colonial Sindh there was also an attempt to impose a script of its own, the Khudawadi (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 53–54, folios 40–41). For most Sindhi intellectuals before Partition, Hindu or Muslim, the Theosophical Society gave the framework in which the Sufi legacy of Sindh was read anew (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 157, folio 144), and in the late nineteenth century that legacy was built on the *wujudi* ideology, recast in Theosophical terms through its likeness to *advaita vedanta* (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 204, folio 191). After the lectures of the Reverend Bambridge in Karachi, "In Sindh, the lectures led to a revival of religious and theological studies" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 46, folio 33), and Annie Besant was known in Sindh as Basanti Devi (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 47, folio 34).

Music came early. Whatever the reason for the Chishtiyya's absence, "music appeared very early in the Sufism of Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 227–228, folios 214–215). Shah Latif's poetry shows "the importance of music both to him personally and, historically, to the region" and the role of the musician in the society of the region (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 92, folio 79). The Naqshbandis stand apart: "the majority of the Naqshbandis in Sindh are strongly opposed to music and to the singing of devotional songs" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 228, folio 215). For the Sindhis who left, singing Sindhi Sufi poetry keeps "a link, through language and music, with Sindh as a fatherland" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 205, folio 192).

Shah Abdul Latif, whom Schaflechner dates 1690 to 1751 (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 95), stands at the centre of the province's modern self image. Verkaaik, as Schaflechner reports him, organises Sindhi nationalist narratives around the "Sindhi language, the poetry of [national poet] Shah Abdul Latif, the Indus, the peasant or hari, and above all, Sufism and mysticism", with the link to Mohenjo-Daro besides (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 88, folio not stated). According to Verkaaik it was G. M. Syed who was chiefly responsible for making Bhittai the most important poet of Sindh (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 101, folio not stated, endnote 52). Abdul Qadir Mangi writes: "Every Sindhi, of whatever faith he may be, believes in the greatness of Bhitai. From a Kohli Bheel to a Syed, every one considers Bhitai his spiritual guide" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 95, folio not stated). Sindhi nationalists see Latif as the ideal Sindhi, the perfect example of the tolerant and syncretic history of the province, while more orthodox writers seek to explain away his journey as youthful recklessness (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 95, folio not stated).

## Shrines and the State

The modern state appears in Schaflechner's fieldwork through representation and money. The Sindh province, he notes, "has 9 minority seats out of 168" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 28, folio not stated, endnote 5). Government funding for the places of minority communities shows in a wave of temple restorations across Sindh within the past decade: "While in Sindh, I visited many temples that were renovated within the past decade, coinciding with changes in the political landscape" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 16, folio not stated). He also records how violence against a temple was settled outside the state: in one of the rumours about the attack on a temple at Larkana in March 2014, told him by NGO friends in Hyderabad, "According to tradition in Sindh, the culprits took the Quran and asked the Hindu family for remission" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 17, folio not stated). The instability of the country and "the feudal environment in Sindh", he suggests, have kept scholars away from the province (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 13, folio not stated).

After Partition, Boivin writes, "The darbars in Sindh were most of the time preserved with different arrangements regarding their management", often with *gaddi nashins* or followers in India paying the keepers, and "despite the fluctuant diplomatic relations between Pakistan and India, it was always possible to travel across the border" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 68, folio 55). Yet one of his subjects, Molchand, worried after Partition that it had become difficult for Hindu Sindhi Sufis to visit the Sufi places in Sindh, now in Pakistan (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 124, folio 111). In Sindh, he notes, the groups of Sufis and Nanakpanthis had no central organisation ruling over a whole body of followers (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 202, folio 189).

Boivin sees a growing threat. "A possible explanation is that the Hindu population of Pakistan, including Sindh, is more and more under the threat of the radical Muslims of the country" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 127, folio 114). "Despite the islamization, though, and despite the spread of Islamist terrorism in Sindh as in other parts of Pakistan, I argue that the Sufi culture still pervades Sindhi society", and he aims to balance the view that the integrating role of Sufism is weakening in Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 177, folio 164). He is plain that Sufism does not of itself mean tolerance: "the most active people working for the conversion of young Hindu girls to Islam are well-known Sufi pirs" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 177, folio 164), and "the Sufi dargahs are now targets of Islamist terrorism" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 186, folio 173).

## The Province in the Books

Bhandari's description of the *suba* of Thatta, written in the author's own time, is the fullest account of the province as a Mughal unit. It measures two hundred and fifty *kos* in length, from Bhakkar to Kech Makran, and one hundred *kos* in breadth, from the town of Badin to the port of Lahari; it is bounded on the east by Gujarat Ahmadabad, on the west by Kech Makran, on the north by Bhakkar and on the south by the salt sea (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529). Four *sarkars*, Thatta, Siwistan, Nasirpur and Amarkot, make up the *suba*, with fifty seven *mahals* and five ports, and a revenue given as ten crore, forty nine lakh and seventy thousand *dams*, the first two words of the sum uncertain in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 528–529). In this country, he writes, a third share is taken from the cultivator (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530).

The province, in his eyes, lives on its river. The greatest river is the Sind; merchants from Multan and Bhakkar ship their goods down it to Thatta, travellers and even heavy armies go by water, and large armies rarely go by land because of the jungle and the want of water (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529). Most things turn on the boat, and there must be forty thousand boats, large and small (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530). Salt and iron mines are plentiful, and at a distance of six *kos*, the figure uncertain in the printed text, is a quarry of yellow stone that is cut to any size for building (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530). There are wild ass, hare, hog deer, pig and fish; the people live on rice, curds and fish; dried fish is shipped to the ports and fish oil, the word uncertain in the printed text, is used on the boats; and a fish called the *palla*, its name also uncertain in the printed text, comes up from the salt sea into the Sind, is caught in nets, and is found in no other country (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530). Curds keep for four months, there are flowers and many fruits, the mango above all, and small melons grow in the desert (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530).

Bhandari also sets down what was said of the province's liver-eaters, the *jigar khwara*, more famous here than anywhere round about. By a spell, he relates, they take the livers of people, especially of children and especially at mealtimes; some say a trance comes over them; they share the liver with their fellows and the victim dies (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530). They make others like themselves by feeding them a piece and teaching them the spell, and they ride the hyena and bring news from far away (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530). When one is caught, those who know the art cut open the calf of the leg and take out a seed like a pomegranate seed, which is fed to the victim, who recovers (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 529–530). A liver-eater, he reports, will not sink with a millstone tied to the neck, nor burn in fire; to cure one, the experts brand both temples, fill the eyes with salt, hang the person up in a house for forty days, give food without salt and recite spells (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529). Most of them are women, some men; they are found in every land, but more in the lands of Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529).

Bhandari places the province at the edge of Hindustan. In his own day, he writes, this realm adjoins Bengal to the east and the Deccan to the south, and on the west Thatta reaches the salt sea (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 579). In his account of Gujarat, Kutch is a separate country, two hundred and fifty *kos* in length and breadth, with Sind to its west (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 531). Among his sources he names the *Tarikh-i Bahadurshahi*, on the sultans of Gujarat Ahmadabad and of the *wilayat* of Sind known as Thatta, lands that at various times had passed out of the hands of the throne holders of Delhi and come each under an independent ruler of its own (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 581).

Later authors of the province described it as a meeting of civilisations. Jethmal Parsram, in *Sind and its Sufis* of 1924, summarised the many religions that had flourished in Sindh, Buddhism, Jainism and Hinduism, to which in more recent times Sikhism and Sufism had been added, and wrote that "there is hardly a country in the whole of Asia, including India, in which the mystic thought of two great civilizations, the Indian and the Arabic-Iranian, is seen as in so beautiful a union as in Sind" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 49, folio 36). Ram Panjwani was already convinced, while still in Sindh, "that Sufism was the core of Sindhi culture, a culture shared by all creeds and social milieus" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 157, folio 144). His preface set out to acquaint the Sindhis "with their past and the glorious cultural heritage of Sind which was inhabited by their forefathers" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 159, folio 146), and his vision of Sindh was that of "a land of the mystic poets, who are humanists as well" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 160, folio 147). When he spoke of the religious culture of Sindh, he meant the spirit of Sufism (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 161, folio 148).

Boivin's own view is that "Sindh hosted a specific shared religious culture resulting from a process of vernacularization, which is not contradictory of the fact that some of its features can also be found in neighbouring provinces" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 37–38, folios 24–25). "The Sufi culture of Sindh was nonetheless the most shared product, but this does not prevent the society from being highly fragmented" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 19, folio 6). He reports Kothari's explanation, which finds the cause in Sindh's place as a frontier, with a constant influx of migrants, far from the main imperial centres (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 28, folio 15), and adds: "It is a fact that Sindh, because of its geographical location both as an isolated and a buffer zone, has hosted many unorthodox versions of normative religions, especially Hinduism and Islam" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 29, folio 16). The impact of British rule was twofold: it objectified Sufism, "enthroning it as the fundamental of Sindhi culture at large", and it renewed religion through a composite middle class (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 64, folio 51). Sindh, like most regions of India, underwent this objectification of the Sufi legacy (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 43–44, folios 30–31), but in his judgement it "had drawn" for centuries "a kind of specific religiosity based on devotion" which placed faith beyond any formal belonging to an institutional religion (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 66, folio not stated). He notes that "In the field of Sindh studies, however, there is no published research related to Sufism and the Hindu Sufis in Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 177–178, folios 164–165).

Schaflechner reads the same idea as a nationalist one. In nationalist writing found "on both sides of the border", Sindh is presented "as a distinct entity lacking any cultural links to other provinces or the state of Pakistan generally" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 87, folio not stated). He quotes K. R. Malkani, a Sindhi Hindu who left Hyderabad after Partition for New Delhi: "Sindh has always been there, Pakistan is a passing show", and "A Sufi Sindh and an Islamic Pakistan cannot coexist"; he calls the book full of rants against Pakistan (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 87, folio not stated). Nationalist writers also blame British rule, which they see as having victimised Sindh; Hussain Soomro writes that the province was once so prosperous that it was said to abound in milk and honey, and in these narratives Pakistan and the British become "the culprits in the case of lost harmony and affluence in Sindh" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 87–88, folio not stated). Sindhi nationalist discourse ties Hinglaj itself to a real Sindh that prospered before outsiders began to suppress the province (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 97, folio not stated), and his chapter plan calls the shrine a hallmark of Sindh's golden past for modern Sindhi nationalism (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 51, folio not stated).

The nationalist case, as Schaflechner presents it, joins the province to Sufism. G. M. Syed, a politician who spent many years in prison for his separatist views, "directly linked the province of Sindh with a tradition of Sufism", found Sufism in its purest form in the province, and took as proof the shrines of Sufi saints that are "to this day, visited by Hindus and Muslims alike" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 88–89, folio not stated). Verkaaik writes that "both Sindhi Hindus and Muslims were influenced by mysticism and religious tolerance. This was the Sindhi essence that both Hindus and Muslims shared" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 89, folio not stated). Ishtiaq Ansari ties Mohenjo-Daro directly to today's Sindhis, claims that the Veda was composed on the shores of the Indus, and holds that Shiva and the Mother Goddess "were already known and honored in the ancient civilization of Sindh" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 93, folio not stated). Badar Abro defines the real Sindh, *asal sindh*, as the valleys between Rohri and eastern Balochistan where the Sindhis once lived in prosperity, and argues that because Sindh remained under the influence of Sufi mysticism the area is, in his English word, secular, so that its religious places belong to the shared heritage of that real Sindh (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 94, folio not stated). Khadim Soomro writes: "A Sindhi has a special place in human civilization because he stands for good and liberal attitude and behavior" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 96, folio not stated). Schaflechner's own verdict is that the common notion among Sindhis today "that in the history of Sindh, there is no religious tension between the two communities" is "merely a nationalist utopian ideal, as Aggarwal (2013) has shown" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 90–91, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- On whether the province's history knew religious harmony: G. M. Syed, as Schaflechner reports him, found Sufism in its purest form in Sindh, proven by shrines visited by Hindus and Muslims alike, and Verkaaik calls mysticism and tolerance the essence Sindhi Hindus and Muslims shared (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 88–89, folio not stated; Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 89, folio not stated); Schaflechner calls the notion that the history of Sindh holds no religious tension between the two communities a nationalist utopian ideal (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 90–91, folio not stated); Boivin, against the trope that Sindh did not suffer Punjab's violence, cites the Sukkur riots and the rising riots from the 1920s (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 61, folio 48; Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 64, folio 51).
- On caste: Thakur wrote that the caste system is almost absent in Sind (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 277–278, folio not stated); Boivin writes that, contrary to popular belief, there were many untouchables in Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 40, folio 27), while allowing that Sindh may have had a social fabric of its own (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 36–37, folio not stated / folio 24).
- On the share of the Lohanas among the Hindus: Boivin gives them half of the whole Hindu population (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 40–41, folios 27–28); McMurdo, as Schaflechner quotes him, calls them the great body of Hindus in Sindh, and Thakur the general body of the Sind Hindus (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 79, folio not stated; Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 80, folio not stated).
- On who ruled Sind when Firuz Shah of Delhi came against it: Boivin writes that in 1367 Firuz Shah Tughluq laid siege to Thatta when Jam Juna II controlled Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 189, folio 176); Bhandari sets Firuz Shah's three campaigns against the Jam in the time of Jam Bani Thatta son of Jam Anzar, and makes Jam Junan Anzar's brother and predecessor of Bani Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 173; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 172–173).
- On the Shikaris and the Dheds: Burton held that they were neither Hindus nor Muslims, while other authors count the Shikaris as Muslims and the Dheds as Hindus (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 187, folio 174).
- On the census of 1872, within one sentence of Boivin's: it opens by saying that Sindh's population statistics are not available in the first census of 1872, then gives a population printed as 2,333,527 million and a breakdown by religion (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 38, folio 25).
- On the number of Hindus who left Sindh at Partition: Boivin puts them at closer to 0.8 million, if not fewer, and reports a kind of consensus of around a million (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 61, folio 48).
