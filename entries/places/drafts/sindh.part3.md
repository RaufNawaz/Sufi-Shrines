## The Province in History

Most of these authors write "Sind", Haroon Khalid, Omar Kasmani and Scott Kugle "Sindh", and Shemeem Burney Abbas often "the Sind"; the unit each of them means is the one described in the passage cited, and none of them draws its boundary as a modern map would. For Annemarie Schimmel the Sind of the Arab conquest is "the lower Indus valley" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 61, folio 30), and in *Pain and Grace* it is "the southern part of present day Pakistan" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 6, folio 2). An unattributed note in Charles Frederick Usborne's translation of Waris Shah reports a different reckoning: "The Arab historians speak of Hind and Sind separately. Sind, In their description, is bounded by Multan, Jaisalmere and Gujrat and Hind, or Hindustan, begins where Sind ends." The same note gives the Arabic title of the Chach Nameh, which it calls one of the earliest histories of Sind, as The History of Hind and Sind, though one word of the transliterated title is uncertain in the printed text (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 55, folio 54, note 1). The note seems to gloss Ranjha's blessing on a Raja that he may rule over horses, camels, elephants, Hindustan and Sind (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 54, folio 53).

The books begin the province's Muslim history with the Arabs. Schimmel writes that in 711 the Arabs "also reached Sind, the lower Indus valley, and laid the foundation of a Muslim rule that still continues in present Pakistan" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 61, folio 30), and that 711 was "the year in which the Arabs conquered Sind and the adjacent provinces northward up to Multan" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 375, folio 344). Richard Eaton writes that "Muslim navies sailed to the coasts of western India where in 711 they conquered and occupied the densely-populated" Hindu and Buddhist society of Sind, beginning the long encounter between Islamic and Indic civilisations (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 30, folio 18). He reports Andrew Watson's view that "the Arab conquest of Sind in 711, which established a direct and regular contact between India and the Fertile Crescent" threw open western Asia, Africa and Europe to the agricultural treasures of India, "effectively incorporating all these regions for the first time into a single agricultural universe" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 39, folio 27). Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi calls it "a myth" that the conquest began as a punitive expedition against "the ruling Raja Dahir": "In reality, the conquest of Transoxiana and Sind was initiated by Hajjaj, Walid's governor in Iraq, and was one aspect of his expansionist policy"; Muhammad bin Qasim al-Saqafi began his advance through Makran and Baluchistan in 710 and conquered Sind "between 711 and 712" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 127, folio 109). Shemeem Burney Abbas writes that Muhammad bin Qasim "came to Sind through the land route from Iran" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 50, folio not stated).

Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti, writing in Urdu in the nineteenth century, carries the story further back. Arab trade had begun in Gujarat and Sindh, and a commander whose name is uncertain in the printed text was sent to attack Sindh (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 802). Under Uthman the Arabs attacked the lands along the river of Sindh, and under Ali they conquered some part of the country of Sindh, which was given up after his death (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 802). Under Walid, between the years 705 and 715 of the Christian era, both numbers uncertain in the printed text, they conquered the country of Sindh and reached the Ganges (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 802–803). In Sabuktigin's time, Chishti says on the authority of the *Tarikh-e Firishta*, the Muslims knew no route into India except the one by way of Sindh, a word before it being illegible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 804).

Rizvi describes the society the conquest made. "Early Arab conquerors settled their families in large numbers in the various towns of Sind"; Brahmans in high office converted to keep their positions, and Buddhists, hostile to Brahman domination, converted as well; "Hindu and Buddhist scholars from Sind were sent to the 'Abbasid court", and in the eighth century a scholar of hadith, Abu Ma'shar Najih (d. 170/787), and the poet Abu'l-'Ata (died after 158/774), both names imperfectly printed, were leading literary figures from Sind (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 127, folio 109). Schimmel writes that the pious of these western provinces were in the early centuries "apparently interested mainly in the collection of hadith and in the transmission to the central Muslim countries of scientific information from India" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 375, folio 344). Eaton reports, after Y. Friedmann, that "in the eighth century, when Arabs replaced Brahmans as rulers of Sind, the new rulers merely continued the earlier practice of requiring the Jats to associate themselves with dogs" and in this way to affirm their lowly status (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 224, folio 212). "Arab rule in eighth century Sind having weakened and died, it was left to Persianized Turks to establish a permanent Muslim presence in India from the thirteenth century" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 47, folio 35).

The tenth century is the age of the Isma'ilis. For Schimmel the Carmathians "ruled not only Bahrain but also northern Sind and Multan" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 98, folio 67). Rizvi explains the lack of information about Sufis in Sind by two factors: the land routes "between Khurasan, Transoxiana and Sind were slow and arduous", and the rise of Sufism coincided with the Isma'ili Fatimid Caliphate, so that "Mansura, the capital of Sind, ruled by the Sumira dynasty had also accepted the over-lordship of the Egyptian Caliphs" and Sufism there was cut off from other areas (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 128, folio 110). "The Sumira dynasty continued to rule the Lower Sind until the middle of the fourteenth century. After the end of the twelfth century, the Upper Sind came under the domination of Sunni rule" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 128, folio 110).

The thirteenth century tied the province to the Sultans of Delhi and to the great Suhrawardi centre at Multan. Kanhaiya Lal calls Taj al-Din Yalduz king of Kech, Makran, Sindh and Multan (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 24, folio 19), and writes that Sultan Shihab al-Din Ghori gave him a wide realm that took in Kech, Makran and Sindh with one further named territory (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 25, folio 20). Rizvi has Iltutmish, seven years after November 1221, rout his rival Nasiru'd-Din Qubacha from Sind (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 152, folio 134). Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya "openly sided with Sultan Shamsu'd-Din Iltutmish when he wished to add Multan and Sind to the Delhi Sultanate" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 209, folio printed "19]" [OCR?] [191]), and "After the annexation of Multan and Sind by Iltutmish in 1228, relations between the Sultan of Delhi and the Shaikh became more intimate" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 210, folio 192). In the fourteenth century, Rizvi writes, the help of Makhdum-i Jahaniyan "was also sought by the governors of Sind" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 301, folio 279).

Fifteenth and sixteenth century Sind was ruled from the province itself and then from outside it. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori, writing in Persian in 1875, records that after Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus settled at Uch, Sultan Husain Mirza, the ruler of Sind, and Sultan Sikandar Lodhi, king of Hind, became his disciples; the text gives no year, and the editor's note on the page dates the saint's coming to Uch to 887 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 40, folio 28). Schimmel writes that Turkish influence "was prevalent in Sind from 1520 onward, when the Turkish dynasty of the Arghun, descending from Afghanistan, overcame the last indigenous Sindhi rulers; they themselves were followed by the Turkish family of the Tarkhan under whom Sind fell to the Mughal Empire", and finds a trace of it in a Turkish word, *otaq*, in Shah Abdul Latif's verse, its printed form uncertain (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 207, folio 207). "Sind was, since 1595, part of the Mughal Empire, and although representatives of the Delhi court came there, the indigenous rulers enjoyed much freedom" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 25, folio not stated). Kugle tells how one of Akbar's chief nobles, Abd al-Rahim Khan-i Khanan, "was traveling with an army toward Sindh" and stopped in Lahore to receive the blessing of Shah Hussayn; the Mughal armies then "conquered Thatta in Sindh, and Hussayn's reputation spread" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 241, folio 220). Zahur ul-Hasan Sharib writes that in Sarmad's day travellers from Iran came to Hindustan by way of Sindh, and that Sarmad came by the same road and reached Thatta (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 297, folio 293).

Schimmel's *Pain and Grace* gives the eighteenth century in most detail. "The first obvious sign of the struggle for power in the Lower Indus Valley becomes visible in 1717", in the conflict around the Sufi Shah Inayat; "The Kalhora clan from Upper Sind, particularly active in the struggle against Inayat, soon afterwards became independent in Sind", and the army sent against Jhok came "from various places in Sind and Hind" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 25, folio not stated). After Aurangzeb's death "the native Kalhoro dynasty took over the rule in Upper and later in Lower Sind, only to become the vassals of the invading Nadir Shah, who first conquered Sind and the Panjab before he reached Delhi in 1739"; Ahmad Shah Abdali's influence "on the scene in Sind is not as important as it was in the northern areas of present day Pakistan"; the Kalhoros were overthrown by "the former disciples of their saintly ancestor, Adam Shah Kalhoro", and "The Talpurs, who thus came to power in 1781, ruled the country until it was conquered by the British in 1843" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, pp. 30–31, folio not stated). Most of the books of the scholar Makhdum Abdur Rahim Girhori "were, however, destroyed during the Afghan raids in Upper Sind in 1781" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 28, folio not stated). In the religious life of the province, "A strong Naqshbandiyya influence in the beginning of the eighteenth century, however, changed the spiritual climate of southern Sind to a certain extent": Makhdum Muhammad Hashim of Thatta and his relatives attacked the Sufis who defended the emotional side of religion, and "this Naqshbandi group contributed to promoting a more sober mystical piety" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 428, folio 397).

Of the British period the books record the conquest of 1843, a new script and the politics of the pirs. Schimmel writes that "Sindhi continued to flourish after the British occupation in 1843. With the introduction of a new Arabic alphabet in 1852, a considerable literature grew up that included mystical poetry and prose" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396). After the uprising of 1857 the Sufi Imdadullah fled through the Punjab and Sindh visiting Sufi shrines, none of which Kugle names, and sailed from Karachi into exile in Mecca (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 280, folio 259). The political history of the *hurr* and the Pir Pagaro belongs under the state below.

## Saints and Pilgrimage

Schimmel's Sind is a land of mystics from the first. Hallaj passed through it: Sind is the country he "traversed shortly after 900 to call people to God" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 175, folio 157); "From Gujerat he wandered through Sind, the lower Indus valley, which had been a part of the Muslim Empire since 711. The seeds he sowed there grew in later centuries in the mystical poetry of this province" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 98, folio 67); "In 905, a mystic like Hallaj traveled extensively throughout Sind and probably discussed theological problems with the sages of this country" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 376, folio 345). Abbas writes that "The Sufi mystical traditions came to the subcontinent of Pakistan and India through the Sind province", and that after Muhammad bin Qasim's conquest "a long line of Sufi mystics came to the region through Baluchistan, Mekran, Multan, and Sind itself" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 50, folio not stated). Rizvi, by contrast, finds that the spread of Sufism seen in Transoxiana and Khurasan is not matched in the early province: "no such developments in Sind are recorded" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 127, folio 109).

The landscape of shrines took shape from the thirteenth century. In *Pain and Grace*, from the day that Lal Shahbaz Qalandar settled in Sehwan in the thirteenth century, and from Baha'uddin Zakariya's Suhrawardi centre "at the northernmost border of the country, in Multan", "Sind had produced an immense number of mystics, whose shrines are scattered all through the Indus Valley, and who invented short songs in their mother tongue to express the mysteries of love and union" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 25, folio not stated). Abbas writes, without giving a source, that "In the province of Sind alone there are said to be more than a hundred thousand Sufi shrines" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 50, folio not stated). Rizvi records the belief that "the Sumirahs of the lower Sind who were Isma'ilis, adopted Sunnism under the influence of the Saiyid", Makhdum-i Jahaniyan, and that "A Jat chief of Sind, later named 'Abdu'llah, is also said to have embraced Islam through a supernatural feat performed by Makhdum Jahaniyan" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 301, folio 279). In the sixteenth century, Schimmel writes, "a group of mystics migrated from Sind to Burhanpur in central India, where they founded a new branch of the Qadiriyya tariqa; they are reported to have used Sindhi verses" in their sessions of *sama'*, and "From that time on the indigenous Sindhi tradition contains innumerable stories of saints, mainly of those who are buried on Makli Hill near Thatta" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384).

The cult of ʿAbd al-Qadir Gilani runs through the province. "Old Sindhi songs" describe how his "spiritual realm extends from Istanbul to Delhi"; Richard Burton, writing about 1850, "says that there were about a hundred large trees in Sind, all called after Gilani. Each had a pole and a flag hung upon it to fulfill some vow made in adversity or sickness"; sweetmeats were distributed to the poor in the saint's honour, and "the fruit and leaves of the trees were not allowed to be touched even by cattle"; and "Proper names like Ghauth Bakhsh, 'gift of the help,' in Sind and Balochistan point to his popularity" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 279, folio 248). Schimmel finds hymns to Gilani in both Sind and the Punjab (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, pp. 416–417, folios 385–386), and "One of the first 'geographical' poems in Sindhi was composed in his honor" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 417, folio 386). Of the province's trees more generally, "still in our day the trees near Sindhi shrines are decorated with many pieces of cloth, preferably red, in the shape of little flags or tiny cradles, which are attached there for the sake of a vow, as it is usual almost everywhere in the Islamic world" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 168, folio 168).

The province's piety is also a matter of books and of women. "It is said that some mystics in Sind", and it may be true of other provinces as well, "kept only three books for spiritual nourishment": the Koran, the Mathnawi and the Diwan of Hafiz (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 358, folio 327). "In Sind there were many pious who, by their recitation of the Mathnawi, 'kept the marketplace of union warm', and Rumi's work was quoted by stern Naqshbandis as much as by members of the more emotional orders" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 165, folio 165). "The province of Sind, generally famous for its saint worship, is particularly rich in legends connected with women saints", and Schimmel quotes Burton: "To the credit of the Sindhi it must be said that they do not refuse to admit the religious merits of the softer sex"; Burton speaks of the *faqirani*, the female *faqir*, of whom "a few of them occasionally rise to the high rank of a murshid" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 464, folio 433). His remarks on Bibi Fatima Hajram, a *hafiza*, are corroborated by the many books Sindhi scholars have devoted to the religious life of their country, and in Sind, as elsewhere, there are whole groups of women saints, such as the Seven Chaste and the veiled women in the rank of the *abdal* (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 465, folio 434). Schimmel writes in a note that she has "learned more about Islamic piety from women in Anatolia and Sind than from most of my learned colleagues or from sophisticated books" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 281, folio 263), and that in Sind women often use endearing diminutives in addressing the saints, with poems in honour of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar of Sehwan and, in more recent times, verses devoted to the Pir Pagaro (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 287, folio 269).

Shared and older devotions belong to the same landscape. Omar Kasmani writes that "In most historical accounts, it is the figure of Udero Lal who is conflated with Khizr in Sindh" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 9), that "Offerings to the Indus River can still be documented in the wider region of Sindh" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 11), and that Raja Bharthari, who renounced his throne and made a hermitage at Sehwan, "is also known as Raja Virag by Hindus of Sindh" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 15). For Haroon Khalid, Iqbal Qaiser's *Historical Sikh Shrines of Pakistan* identifies a temple at Clifton as "the temple which Nanak had visited on his tour of Sindh" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 217, folio 191), and tradition has it that "Nanak visited Uch Sharif on his way to Sindh, towards the end of his travels, after he had already visited the East, South, North and West" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 278, folio 252).

Schimmel observes that in Shah Abdul Latif's *Risalo* none of the great Sufi orders is named, "nor any of the sanctuaries to which the inhabitants of Sind flocked at many occasions" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 190, folio 190). At his own tomb today dervishes perform his music on Thursday nights, "and the melodies are well known all over Sind" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 421, folio 390). Abbas's informant Naseer Mirza told her that the *lotevali* gypsy women sang only at the shrine of Shahbaz Qalandar, "certainly, that is the only place in the Sind now where the loṭevālīs come", and that he had seen no such tradition at Bhit Shah, at Sachal Sarmast's shrine, at Rikhail Shah's or at any other Sufi *dargah* in the Sind (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 61, folio not stated). Asked whether it is more a tradition of the Sind that women sing at Sufi shrines, the singer Reshma said that it was so at the shrines, less so in Rajasthan, but above all at the Shahbaz Qalandar shrine (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 60, folio not stated).

## Communities

The books see Sind as a province where traditions met. Schimmel writes that Hindu sharing in the Muslim orders is "more visible in Sind than in the Punjab": "Hindu writers wrote mystical poems in which they used the imagery of the Muslims; they composed poems in honor of the Prophet" and even wrote *taʿziya* for the Muharram mourning of the Shia community of Sind, or devoted ballads to the fate of famous Sufi martyrs (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 418, folio 387). In *Pain and Grace* she warns against taking Makhdum Abdur Rahim Girhori as typical of Sindhi Sufism: on the whole there was, in H. T. Sorley's words, "a common basis of understanding, by Muslim and Hindu alike, of the mystical message of Islam"; Sorley also found in eighteenth century Sind an "almost incredible veneration for sayyids as such and a great accretion of superstitious practices in the belief of the multitude"; the understanding between the communities "may perhaps have been facilitated by the activities of the Ismaili missionaries who came to Sind in the 14th and 15th centuries"; "neither Hindus nor Muslims are orthodox in Sind", and still in the 1920s there were "numerous Hindus and amongst them some of the best brains of Sind, old and new, who are Sufi by religion"; "a number of Hindus became devoted disciples of Muslim saints" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 28, folio not stated). Shah Abdul Latif, "as did his Panjabi contemporaries and his successors in Sind, accepted the Hindus who were striving on the way to perfection as true pilgrims in the eternal Light" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 267, folio 267).

Eaton gives the long view of how the province became Muslim. Sind is one of the western "wings of India that became Muslim-majority regions", where, from the fourteenth century through the Mughal period, "the growth of Muslim societies correlated with the adoption of sedentary agriculture", in a frontier society not yet deeply penetrated by Brahmanical values, and where "shrines of great Sufis attracted and integrated pre-agrarian and non-Muslim pastoral clans" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 48, folio 36). He follows Irfan Habib on the Jats, pastoralists who "had been moving up from Sind into the Multan area between the seventh and eleventh centuries": Hsuan Tsang wrote of river groups in Sind who "gave themselves exclusively to tending cattle and from this derive their livelihood" and "have no masters", and the Chach-nama "styled these groups 'Jatts', located them in the wastes of the Indus valley in Sind, and noted an absence among them of social hierarchy" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 224, folio 212). Eaton sums this up as the Jats' "migration north from Sind to Punjab" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 227, folio 215).

Abbas's ethnography names the province's peoples. "The Kolhis are the indigenous peasant communities of Sind who are sometimes migratory. Usually they are temporary farm laborers in the large feudal holdings. They are non-Muslims, and together with the Bhils they are the native inhabitants of the Sind-Rajasthan continuum" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 46, folio not stated); because Shah Abdul Latif wrote "in the Sindhi vernacular, the Kohli and Bhil male and female peasants can understand and relate to it" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 47, folio not stated). Her glossary calls the Bhils and the Kolhis aboriginal landless roving peasants of Sind (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 179, folio not stated; Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 184, folio not stated), and a note describes them as non-Muslim migratory peasants who work on large landholdings in Sind and Indian Gujarat (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 200, folio not stated). The Sidis "were initially brought as slaves from Africa to work in the subcontinent of Pakistan and India. Although they are now free, some of them are attached to the feudal households in the Sind province of Pakistan. Their native language is Sindhi" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 67–68, folios not stated). Her informant Qalandar Shah said that Africans are present in the Sind in large numbers because of the slave trade, were freed there too when slavery was abolished, and had been coming through the ages (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 69, folio not stated). Abbas writes that "the Sidis in Sind and Kacch claim descent from Hazrat Bilal" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 167, folio not stated); they are also known as Sheedi or Syedi in Sind and Gujarat (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 188, folio not stated), and their *mugarman* drum is held sacred by the Sidi communities of Sind, Baluchistan, Rajasthan and Gujarat, who use it in their shrines and rituals (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 185, folio not stated). Schimmel adds that "The Mohana traditionally belonged to the lowest social strata in Sind, and their women were noted for beauty and not too strict morals" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 175, folio 175). On a visit to the Sidis of Karachi Mori Abbas was served "fish cooked in curry, a traditional Sindhi lunch eaten with rice and flatbread" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 71, folio not stated).

Kasmani records how custom shapes women's place. Evoking the idea of the *niyani*, the little sister in Sindhi society, the husband of one of her companions explained that among locals the greatest conflicts can be resolved if one feuding party can get a *niyani* to intervene for it, so that "Women fakirs were therefore perfect instruments for intercession in his opinion" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 94, folio 77). She cites a study by Lubna Chaudhry finding that in parts of Sindh and Punjab unmarried girls in their teens and early twenties were "most vulnerable", facing "discriminatory food portions within the family and limited access to health care" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 191, folio 174, ch. 2 n. 24).

The province's Hindus and Sikhs today are the subject of Khalid's books. The majority of the remaining non-Muslims "are Hindus living in the 'faraway' 'peripheral' regions of Southern Punjab, inner Sindh and Baluchistan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 12, folio not stated); "according to the census of 1998, the Hindu minority is the largest minority in the country, with Christians in the second place, most of the Hindus are scattered in Sindh, Baluchistan, and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 87, folio not stated). "The majority of the Hindu community is based in Sindh and Southern Punjab, where they have a proportionate representation from the lower and the upper castes", and there they "are a part of the social fabric of the area", though Khalid says he could not go into the community's experience in these regions in detail (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 233, folio not stated). After the demolition of the Babri Mosque on 6 December 1992, "Hindu communities in Sindh and Quetta were attacked" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 22, folio not stated). Nanak Panthi Sikhs "belong to the areas of South Punjab and Sindh"; their temples in Sindh and Southern Punjab hold portraits of Guru Nanak beside Hindu deities, and "They are found in very large numbers in the province of Sindh" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 197, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* thousands of such Sikhs live in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Punjab and Sindh (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 98, folio 80); the communities who refused the Khalsa and called themselves followers of Guru Nanak "still exist in Sindh and in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. Baptised Sikhs look down upon them", and at Manora Khalid saw them merging into the larger groups of Sikhs (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 230, folio 204). An informant of Khalid's in *In Search of Shiva* speaks of "several shrines in the Multan region and in Sindh where people still practise Hinduism and give it the name of Islam" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 10, ch. 4, folio not stated).

## Language, Poetry and Music

The books agree that the Sufis made Sindhi a literary language. "Sind, as other countries, had, and still has, a big treasure of folk poetry, ballads, and working songs; folk tales are located in the different parts of the country, be it the hill slopes of Balochistan, the desert region of Thar Parkar, the fertile plains that were watered by the Indus, or the shifting creeks near the Indus mouth. It seems that in Sind, as in other parts of the Muslim world, the Sufis are responsible for the development of the spoken language into a literary one" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 161, folio 161). Schimmel dates native language mystical poetry in Sind, as in the Punjab, from the fifteenth century (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384). Mystical poets "in Sind and the Punjab" developed "the art of utilizing simple folk tales as media for mystical instruction" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 154, folio 136), and "In Sind, the range of folk tales utilized for mystical purposes is even wider": the first allusions appear, "however veiled, in Qadi Qadan's verse" in the first half of the sixteenth century, become more obvious in the short verses of Shah Abdul Karim of Bulri, and reach full bloom in the *Risalo* of his great-grandson Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit in the eighteenth; "Since the original tales were known to everyone, he usually begins his poetical renderings with the most dramatic moment" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 171, folio 153). In Sind, too, "the 18th century can be called the most important period for the formation of mystical poetry and prose" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, pp. 30–31, folio not stated). Schimmel finds the mystical literature of Sind and the Punjab closely alike, with the motif of spinning and weaving "in a cotton-growing country" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, pp. 416–417, folios 385–386). "Many later Sindhi Sufi poets, mainly those of Baloch origin, used also the northern dialect of Sindhi, Siraiki", and the imagery of the two linguistic areas is closely related, as one would expect among the people of predominantly rural provinces (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 154, folio 154).

The great tales of the province are love stories. The Lila Chanesar story "can be dated back to the time of Jam Chanesar, one of the Summa rulers in 14th century Sind", and the Mumal Rano tale "originated in Lower Sind some time in the 15th century" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 157, folio 157). "Sohni is one of the favorite folk tales in both Sind and the Panjab" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 156, folio 156), and Abbas writes that the Sohni myth "is sung in Sufi poetry in all the regions of the Sind, the Siraiki-speaking area of the Panjab" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 126–127, folios not stated). Of Sassi, the annotator of Usborne's Waris Shah writes: "Sassi-Punnoo is a story of old Sind." Sassi belonged to Bhambore, an old city, and Punnoo was a Baluch chief, and the romances of Sohni and Sassi have travelled beyond their first setting to become popular "both in the old Punjab and Sind" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 12, folio 11, note 5). For Abbas the Sassi myth is "told in the lands of Sind and Baluchistan. In the oral traditions of the region the legend is sung with all the emotion that manifests Sassi's grief" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 118, folio not stated), and performers love to sing it in the regional languages and dialects of Sind, Baluchistan and the Siraiki speaking regions (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 120, folio not stated). Schimmel recalls Sassi's run through the desert at sunset in Latif's verse as "a scene which can be appreciated by those who know the glowing evenings at the western border of Sind, when all of a sudden, darkness overtakes the wayfarer, and nothing is left but the sounds of a few animals" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 170, folio 170).

Latif's verse is, for Schimmel, a picture of the province. He "offers in his verse a vivid picture of the conditions of the Sind countryside during the early 18th century"; "The imagery of the swan must have been popular in Sind for a long time", and Lila's swinging bed recalls the "colorful, laquer-work beds" that "are still a specialty of Sind" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 168, folio 168). The spinning of cotton is "one of the most important occupations of women" and a frequent topic of the poetry of Sind and the Panjab, and "the crow, kang, is the typical messenger bird in Sind, as was the pigeon in high Persian poetry" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 160, folio 160). The complaint that no letter has come "is general among the Sindhis, so that most of the girls who never learned how a pen looks or knew even a single letter always complain that they do not receive letters from their friends", and it occurs often in wedding songs (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 172, folio 172); "In the folk poetry of Sind the heroines always complain that no letter from the beloved has reached them" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 455, folio 424). In the Golden Alphabets words with the same letters were combined in one verse, "since alliteration was a frequently used poetical device, particularly in Sind" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 454, folio 423), and the mystical bards of Sind and the Punjab knew the tradition of *Ahmad bila mim* (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 451, folio 420). An Arabic saying "was very popular in 18th century Sind": "Affliction is for saintliness the same as are flames for the gold" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 222, folio 222). Schimmel also writes that "Even the poetry of the eighteenth-century mystic" Shah Abdul Latif, in "the remote province of Sind", calls the reader to admire Iblis as the one true lover (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 226, folio 195).

The Prophet is a theme of the province's poets. Poets "in the plains of Sind and the Punjab wrote tender little poems or instructive Golden Alphabets to instil love for him" in the souls of the villagers (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 222, folio 204), and "The great mystical poets of Sind have devoted wonderful songs to the Prophet, who appears as the dawn between the night of created human life and the Divine day" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 223, folio 205). Abbas writes that *virahini* poetry, in which the devotee is a young bride awaiting her bridegroom, who is equated with the Prophet Muhammad, "is also sung in the Sindhi devotional contexts, such as the mauluds that celebrate the Prophet's birth" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 149, folio not stated).

Two passages of Latif's verse in Schimmel's translation speak of the province by name. In the lament of Marui he says that Marui is no more in Malir, nor Hamir; that "Punhun is no longer in Kech", nor is there in Sind an Amir; and that in the convents there is no Pir (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 235, folio 235). In Sur Sarang the cloud of mercy, rising over the desert of Eastern Sind, the Thar Parkar, is seen floating from Africa to China, and the poet, coming closer to his home, prays: "My Lord, be kind over dear Sind, / Friend! Sweet darling! You may make the whole world happy!" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 259, folio 259). The singer Alan Faqir recited to Abbas the line "Punnu is not in the Kec / Sind is not the amir", which her notes explain to mean that Sind does not lead the land politically, that it is not in the position of leadership or amirship; they add that these are all Alan Faqir's political interpretations of the female myths (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 156, folio not stated; Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 210–211, folios not stated).

The poetry lives in its singing. Schimmel writes that "It is moving to listen to illiterate folk musicians in remote corners of Sind" repeating the name of Hallaj in ecstatic songs, and to hear them "claim that he was the true lover who paid with his life for his love" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 175, folio 157). The visitor who goes on to Sind "will enjoy listening to the moving mystical songs" of Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit (1689 to 1752) or the ardent poems of Sachal Sarmast (d. 1826), which are almost as popular today as they were a hundred and fifty years ago (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 414, folio 383). The translator Naveed Alam writes that "Even to this day, most Punjabis and Sindhis are more likely to have heard a kafi than read one" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 15, folio 15). Abbas writes that Shah Abdul Latif and other Sufi poets of the Sind region composed in the vernacular languages and dialects, in which they sang as roving mendicants within an Islamic framework, and that "For fear of the clergy mystic poetry sung to music remained confined to the minstrel class" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 50, folio not stated). *Sufiana kalam* "is usually sung in the vernacular and is a feature of the shrines in Sind, especially those of Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar and Shah Abdul Latif at Bhit" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 85, folio not stated). "Among musician communities in the Sind, Shah's Risālo is treated with great respect because of its sacred Quranic context, and the book is not allowed to lie around on the floor" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 51, folio not stated); "Since large sections of the Quran are quoted in his Risālo, musicians in Sind hold Shah's written text in the utmost veneration" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 166, folio not stated).

Abbas describes the province's musicians and their forms. "In Sind there can be no ritual without the Manganhar, be it a birth, a wedding, or a death. They are the designated community musicians, and each community has its own Manganhars" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 77, folio not stated); "In Sind, the Sidis of African descent and the Manganhar musicians render the ceremonial singing of devotional narratives" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 169, folio not stated). Her glossary calls the Manganhar hereditary professional musicians of Sind and Rajasthan and gives *mangiari* and *mangti* as names for a female musician there (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 184, folio not stated). "There is evidence of roving minstrels in the Sind-Rajasthan area. Among them are the jogī snake charmers and flute players" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 77, folio not stated). "In Sind this form of choir singing is called sūng" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 40, folio not stated), and "The speech and song create ecstasy, which is a very special context of some shrines in Sind" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 55, folio not stated). Sikander Baloch told her that there is a school of thought in Sindhi Sufi poetry, the *sung*, in which the lover sings to win back the estranged beloved, and that this is called *manana* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 63, folio not stated). Baloch and Naseer Mirza claim that Abida Parvin's use of the *dohra* or *bait* within her narrative, a style she took from her male mentors, is unique: "They claim that no other woman musician in the Sind has this style, which is essentially a unique male style" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 55, folio not stated). Among the areas of women's participation Abbas lists "Women as singers/musicians/participants of Sufi songs, sometimes even called the faqīrīanī in the Sindhi shrines" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 21, folio not stated). The Sidi, Mohana and Manganhar women attached to feudal households in the Sind told her that they sing devotional Sufi poetry at their mentor's shrine only on special occasions such as his *urs*, that they perform there only before women, and that their female patrons pay them well (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 95, folio not stated); these musicians sing in the Syed households of the Sind to women only (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 112, folio not stated), and "Sidi women render ritual musical services in the households in the Sind" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 167, folio not stated). The singer Taj Mastani deals with television stations and with clients "from the affluent feudal households in Sind, who can reach her for ritual ceremonies" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 34, folio not stated). In the Siraiki belt around Multan, Bahawalpur, Mianwali and northern Sind, "the female voices are again prominent" in the Sufi poetry of the oral traditions (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 148, folio not stated).

Abbas places the province in a wider world of song. "The mystical lore of the region that includes Sind, Baluchistan, and the coastal belt of Iran shares myths, melodies, and linguistic codes", among them "the famous 'Mast Qalandar' dhun" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 47–48, folios not stated); the *dhun* is "a tune reported to be sung all along the Rajasthan, Sind, Baluchistan, and Iran continuum", and "Almost every folksinger in the region can sing it" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 57, folio not stated). She cites Michael Wood's film *In the Footsteps of Alexander*, which shows "the Indus along the Baluchistan coast back through Persia as a route that seems to have been used for centuries" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 210, folio not stated). Her glossary records the ceremonial music of Sind: *gic*, songs usually sung by women at births and weddings (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 182, folio not stated); *jamalo*, a folk tune and also a dance, and *kapati*, the songs of the cotton spinners (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 183, folio not stated); *olang*, a drum rhythm played at the bridegroom's home (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 186, folio not stated); *sadmana*, a melody for ceremonial occasions (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 187, folio not stated); *candar*, a ceremonial Sindhi melody for auspicious occasions, and the wooden castanets that keep rhythm in Sindhi folk music (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 180, folio not stated); *haswari*, a ceremonial tune played with drums and bagpipes, which Sindhi musicians claim to play for the pirs and murshids when they appear at festive ceremonies such as a wedding, and *laon*, a Sindhi nuptial song (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 182, folio not stated; Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 184, folio not stated). *Dhammal* is the vigorous ritual dance performed at Sufi shrines in Panjab and Sind (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 180, folio not stated).

The modern language has its own history. Taufiq Rafat's selection reports Asaf Khan's account of the many Sindhi dialects and of how the Hyderabadi dialect became "standard when the British in 1853 asked the Hydrabadi scholars to write the textbooks for the whole of Sind" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 236, folio 216). "The province continued to produce mystics who wrote either in Persian or in their mother tongue" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396). On the road between Bahawalpur and Uch, Khalid found that "the cultures of Punjab and Sindh merge": a Punjab Hotel beside a Sindh Hotel, Siraiki as an amalgamation of the two languages, and the Siraiki of Uch more like Sindhi (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 276, folio 250).

## Shrines and the State

Saints and rulers were bound together in Sind from early on: Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya's support for Iltutmish's annexation, the governors who sought Makhdum-i Jahaniyan's help, and the ruler of Sind who became Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus's disciple are told above. Divan Gidumal, "the minister of the Kalhoro princes", offered an invader two bags which he said "contain the most valuable of Sind's gifts; they contain the holy dust from the tombs of numerous saints and Pirs of Sind" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 25, folio not stated). In *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* Schimmel tells the same story as a mark of the country's pride in its mystical heritage: "Did not Diwan Gidumal, the minister, give the invading Nadir Shah (1739) the most precious treasure Sind had to offer, some of the dust of its saints?" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396).

Under British rule the pirs became landlords in the eyes of the state. Robert Rozehnal quotes Katherine Ewing: "British policy, especially in the Punjab and Sind, was to treat them in essentially the same way that landlords and tribal leaders were treated", the hereditary saints, the *sajjada nishin*, being meant (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 33, folio 23). Jamal Malik finds the cult of holy men "particularly evident in the countryside, where major landlords and large-scale land concentrations are crucial features, especially in Punjab and Sindh", where "the local pîr represents the local landlord or is at least closely related to him" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 199, folio 188). Charles Lindholm argues that under indirect rule in South Asia "Sufis were usually equally content to maintain the peace as long as they were allowed to continue their religious routines and keep their estates and authority" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 237, folio 226), and notes as exceptions "the two Hur rebellions against the British in the Sind by the followers of pir Pagara, a landlord, political leader and hereditary head of a Sufi brotherhood who still retains great authority in present-day Pakistan" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, pp. 241–242, folios 230–231). Schimmel writes that "The attitude of some mystical leaders in the lower Indus valley" proved "quite decisive in the political arena": the *hurr*, the Free, the loyal and absolutely obedient disciples of the Pir Pagaro, were gathered first to support Ahmad Brelwi's struggle against the Sikhs between 1829 and 1831 and later joined actively in the struggle against the British; "The hurr even played a minor role in the Indian-Pakistani war in 1971"; and "This was not the first time that mystical leaders in Sind, or in other parts of the Muslim world, were engaged in politics or in social reforms" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 428, folio 397).

The wealth of the shrines has drawn criticism and reform. Schimmel writes that "the majority of the masses continued, and partly still continue, in the veneration of their saints, which is echoed in hundreds of simple folk songs and popular verses. The exaggerated veneration of a saint's tomb has been made the target of some of the best modern Sindhi short stories. The discrepancy between the wealth of the shrines and their guardians and the poverty of the masses indeed provides an impressive theme for social and religious criticism. That holds true not only for Sind, but for other parts of the Muslim world as well" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 429, folio 398). "In Bengal as in Sind, the Naqshbandiyya and some more militant mystically inspired groups tried to curb the overflowing emotionalism expressed in the lovely folk songs" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 432, folio 401; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 433, folio 402). In Khalid's present the reformers come from outside the shrines: Tableeghi preachers "focus on southern Punjab and rural Sindh where syncretistic religion held sway" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 10, ch. 4, folio not stated), and, as Tahir Kamran puts it, in recent years "the focus of the Deoband has been on South Punjab and Sindh where there is an ubiquitous saint and shrine culture" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated). Khalid explains that "Saeen is a title used to refer to a feudal lord or a spiritual leader from lower Punjab and Sindh, where the shrine culture thrives" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated).

Abbas sees the same tie between land and shrine in the lives of musicians and fakirs. Her glossary defines the *vadera* as a feudal landlord in the Sind (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 189, folio not stated), and the singer Alan Faqir told her that the *vaderas* have disputes with one another and come to the *faqir* for a prayer (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 78, folio not stated).

The Pakistani state has shaped which saints the province celebrates. Kasmani writes that "Sindhi publications of the state hardly mention the saint or the shrine of Sehwan until the late 1960s", while "other saints of Sindh (especially Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai, Sachal Sarmast, and Shah Inayat) were regularly featured during this time, and their annual fairs were marked by special editions. Only from the 1960s onward do Urdu and Sindhi newspapers offer detailed reportage on the annual fair of Sehwan" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 70, folio 53). The publications she means are "two prominent state-run journals, Nayin Zindagi and Mehran, published by the Information Department, Ministry of Information and Broadcasting, Sindh, and the Sindhi Adabi Board, respectively" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 189, folio 172, ch. 1 n. 28), and today "State institutions like the Department of Culture or the Sindhi Adabi Board routinely publish books and brochures dedicated to the saint's life as well as to Sehwan's history and heritage" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 20). Rozehnal records the Sufi Shaykh Wahid Bakhsh, who died in 1995, warning against nationalist slogans, "Sindhu Desh", the ethnic Sindhis' country, among them: "They do not realize that four smaller countries, which they wish to create, will be swallowed by India overnight"; in Rozehnal's reading, "In Wahid Bakhsh's view, the homegrown divisions of ethnic rivalry are as destructive as any external enemy" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 127, folio 118).

## The Province in the Books

For Schimmel, Sind is above all a mystical country. In 711 the Arabs conquered it, "and this province never ceased to be a center of Islamic studies and a seat of Islamic mysticism" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 6, folio 2). "Sind has always been praised as the country with a particularly strong inclination towards mysticism", and she quotes a Sindhi author, J. Parsram: "Sind has been flattened and has been in a state of negation. This is the cause of the many disadvantages from which it suffers; but it has been also the cause of a great blessing", the blessing being "something of which India is in need", namely the mystical tradition (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, pp. 24–25, folio not stated). She closes *Pain and Grace* with the province's light: "the villages in Sind are covered with a veil of golden dust; the walls, built of unbaked clay, look like bronze, and the blue and white tiles which cover a lonely saint's tomb match the purity of the blue sky", and the approaches of Dard and Shah Abdul Latif are "as different as are the sunsets in their native provinces" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 263, folio 263). In *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* she writes that "The country had always been proud of its mystical heritage" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396).

Other voices in the same book are harsher. Schimmel quotes E. Trumpp, in his introduction to Sur Sorathi, on "how much even the strong monotheistic deism of Islam has become modified and corrupted in Sind, as in other parts of India, by its contact with Hinduism" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 158, folio 158), and Sorley on the province's veneration of sayyids and accretion of superstitious practices, quoted above (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 28, folio not stated). Schimmel herself notes that in discussions of Bayezid's master, said to be Abu ʿAli as-Sindi, "i.e., from Sind", the name may point to the lower Indus valley or to a village called Sind close to Bistam, and that even in the first case "not every man from Sind could be expected to know all the intricacies of Hindu monistic philosophy" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 78, folio 47).

For Abbas the province is part of a larger cultural region. She speaks of "the Sind-Rajasthan continuum" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 46, folio not stated), of the lore of "the region that includes Sind, Baluchistan, and the coastal belt of Iran" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 47–48, folios not stated), and of the Sassi myth as belonging to "the area currently referred to as a continuum in ethnomusicology" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 156, folio not stated). Her informant Alan Faqir spoke of Sind as a land invaded again and again, when the Mongols, the Tartars and the Sassanids came, looted and plundered and assaulted, which yet goes on and will not die; "Sindhri is beaten", he said, and yet Sindhri goes on (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 156–157, folios not stated). Her notes explain that Sindhri is a name used for Sind in the poetic traditions (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 210–211, folios not stated).

For Khalid, Sindh is a region he names but did not travel in for *In Search of Shiva*: "southern Punjab and Sindh are excluded, where there are many such shrines. These shrines also represent similar characteristics as discussed in this book" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 6, Introduction, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* he looks from Manora at "the Makran mountain range, the boundary line between Sindh and Baluchistan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 228, folio 202).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **To which invader Divan Gidumal gave the dust of Sind's saints.** In *Pain and Grace* the gift of two bags holding the holy dust of the tombs of Sind's saints and pirs is set at Ahmad Shah Abdali's invasion, which took place in Shah Abdul Latif's lifetime (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 25, folio not stated); in *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* the dust is given to "the invading Nadir Shah (1739)" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396).
- **The date of the Arab conquest.** Schimmel and Eaton give 711 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 61, folio 30; Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 30, folio 18); Rizvi has Muhammad bin Qasim begin his advance in 710 and conquer Sind "between 711 and 712" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 127, folio 109); Chishti has the country of Sindh conquered under Walid between 705 and 715 of the Christian era, both numbers uncertain in the printed text, after earlier attacks under Uthman and a partial conquest under Ali (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 802; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 802–803).
- **Whether Sufism came early to Sind.** Abbas writes that the Sufi traditions came to the subcontinent through the Sind province and that a long line of Sufi mystics followed Muhammad bin Qasim's conquest (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 50, folio not stated); Rizvi writes that "no such developments in Sind are recorded" in the early period and explains the lack of information about Sufis there by slow land routes and Fatimid overlordship (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 127, folio 109; Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 128, folio 110); Schimmel finds the pious of the region interested in the early centuries mainly in hadith and in transmitting Indian learning westward (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 375, folio 344).
- **Where the Sassi story belongs.** The annotator of Usborne's Waris Shah calls it "a story of old Sind" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 12, folio 11, note 5); Abbas writes that it "originated in Baluchistan and Sind" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 156, folio not stated).
- **What *sung* means.** Abbas calls *sung* the form of choir singing in Sind (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 40, folio not stated); her informants Sikander Baloch and Naseer Mirza use it for the practice in which the lover woos the estranged beloved through a serenade on the castanets (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 55, folio not stated); her glossary has a choir that sings at the Sufi shrines in Sind (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 188, folio not stated); and a note records that a native Sindhi informant corrected the term to mean a choir of mendicants who sing mystical poetry (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 198, folio not stated).
