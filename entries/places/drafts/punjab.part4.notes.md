## Weak material and how it was treated

- Qureshi's "Panjab" is unbounded and set against Uttar Pradesh; the block flags that a reviewer may prefer to move it to `not attached`. Kept, as a statement about the region's musical tradition, with the sentence that Qureshi does not say which side she means. Rauf may wish to drop it (see questions).
- Abbas p. 124 and p. 206: "Panjabi" is a cultural adjective (weddings, ritual), not a place name. Kept as about the province's people and custom, briefly.
- Sorley p. 270: the block says Punjab figures only as the source of a migration and a tale. Kept as exactly that.
- Abbas p. 161: the Bibi Pak Daman passage is tradition ("Supposedly", "It is reported that"). Given as tradition reported by Abbas, one sentence; the shrine has its own article.
- Rozehnal p. 90: the will names only Bahawalpur; the extension to the southern Punjab is Rozehnal's. The prose says so.
- Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 403: "the Sikh, who had occupied the whole of the Punjab": carried as printed, framed as Schimmel's memory of Shah Ismaʿil Shahid and Sayyid Ahmad.
- Boivin p. 43: hedged ("It is possible"); given as a possibility.

## Disagreements (all in ## Where the Sources Disagree)

1. Abbas on the *arain*: "gardeners or small cultivators who worked on their own land" (p. 108) against the glossary's "A caste of landowners in the Panjab" (p. 178).
2. When Punjabi mystical poetry began: Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415 (reliable tradition, fifteenth century) against *Pain and Grace*, p. 16 (may have emerged as early as Fariduddin Shakarganj, d. 1265).
3. Siraiki: Boivin's glossary (p. 220, one language of Northern Sindh and Southern Punjab) against the specialists he reports in his text (p. 171, a distinct language).

Not listed, for Rauf's judgement: Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 154 places the use of folk tales for mystical instruction "only in the centuries after" Badaʾuni's death, while *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415 has the sixteenth century Madho Lal Husayn "seem to inaugurate (or perhaps even continue from unknown beginnings)" the insertion of folk tales into mystical thought. Whether these conflict depends on Badaʾuni's dates, which the material does not give, so I did not set them side by side; both are in the prose.
