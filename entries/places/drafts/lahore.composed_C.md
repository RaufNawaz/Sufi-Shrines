## Shrines, Endowments and the State

Kanhaiya Lal's survey of Lahore's religious property in the 1880s begins with who holds a site, and authority is often stated as a legal formula, frequently a proprietary one. Of Basant Gar Sanyasi at the Thibi Wala shivala he writes that Basant Gar holds authority in the shivala in part and in whole, the word for part uncertain in the printed text; other sadhus, that word also uncertain, and chelas serve there, and their daily expenses too are Basant Gar's charge (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 124, folio 127). Of Bir Sidh Nath at the Bhairo ji *asthan*, the second part of his name uncertain in the printed text, Lal writes that the distribution and spending of the income from offerings lies in his authority alone, and that his four chelas draw their living from that income (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 129, folio 132). Ownership vests in the officiant, not in a trust. Madho Das Bairagi, the epithet uncertain in the printed text, holds the mandir, its wells, garden and land and keeps proprietary possession of them (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 201, folio 204); Bawa Ram Das Bairagi, the epithet again uncertain, is described as pujari and owner of the temple (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 200, folio 203); of Ganita Nand Gar, the first name uncertain in the printed text, Lal writes that he takes the shivala's income and is also the owner of its land (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 199, folio 202). At the Shivala Tarpoliya the grandson disciple Balak Nath, his name uncertain in the printed text, is called the temple's pujari and the holder of authority over it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 108, folio 111), and at the Gilani Sayyids' graveyard Pir Nizamuddin is simply its owner, *malik* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 308, folio 311). Against this, imam and *mutawalli* are kept distinct: Mufti Jalal-ud-Din officiates with the permission of Mufti Ghulam Sarwar, the mosque's *mutawalli* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 170, folio 173), and one named *mutawalli* does the repairs himself, Lal recording that Mirza Nur Ali, *mutawalli* and manager of the mosque, had the work done in lime (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 155, folio 158).

Custodians were paid by shares, by salaries from outside bodies, and by stipends. More than seventy *mujawirs* at Data Ganj Bakhsh divide the offerings in equal shares (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 291, folio 294). At Shah Abu Ishaq Qadri's tomb seven *bighas* of agricultural land adjoining the tomb, with a running well, belong to it, and the *mujawirs* live on their income, two words of the sentence uncertain in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 283, folio 286). Three custodians divide one enclosure at Mehr Das: Bawa Chandi Nath Jogi, the epithet uncertain in the printed text, performs the *puja* of both shivalas and takes their income; the thakurdwara's *puja* belongs to Moti Bairagi, the epithet again uncertain; and the Devi's mandir has no pujari appointed separately, the two serving it together (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 131–132, folios 134–135). At the Baoli the offerings are divided by kind between custodian and servers (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 114, folio 117).

Salaries also came from outside bodies. A Brahmin serving a *samadh* draws his salary from the *wakil* of the Jammu government; he comes every morning, offers flowers on the *samadhs* and on Shiv ji, the word that follows uncertain in the printed text, and goes (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 195–196, folios 198–199). A whole royal *samadh* complex is staffed by one boy on a state salary: only one person, a boy named Nikka Singh, seventeen years old, is appointed by the government, and he receives one hundred rupees a year from it, the figure uncertain in the printed text, with one Granth Sahib for reading and one mat (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 212, folio 215). Private patrons paid clergy too. At the Chhote Lal shiwala the pujari, a Brahmin whose printed name cannot be read with certainty, and the other servants draw their salary from Lala Chhote Lal's *kothi* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 448–449, folios 455–456); Rai Singh's Brahmin pujari, his name printed uncertainly, perhaps Mukhh Ram, is said to draw his salary from Rai Singh (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 455–456, folios 462–463); and Pandits Prem Nath and Shiv Nath pay a salary to the Brahmin Keshav Ram as well (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 115, folio 118).

State and princely stipends are recorded with their withdrawal. Bhai Nihal Singh held eight thousand rupees a year in *jagir* in return for service at the Baoli (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 113, folio 116). Maharaja Kharak Singh, for as long as he lived, kept up a mosque imam's allowance of ten rupees a month; he would sit in the mosque's courtyard inside his own haveli and tell the Muslims to give the call to prayer and to pray, and sweets were distributed afterwards, the word for them uncertain in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 343–344, folios 350–351). The Tali Sahib *mahant* had one rupee a day in Ranjit Singh's time, but it is now stopped, though the *mahant* is comfortably off because the income from offerings is good (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 228–229, folios 231–232). Mauj Darya's khanqah had forty rupees monthly under the Sikhs, and now receives nothing at all (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 280, folio 283). At Jahangir's tomb there were *jagirs* worth thousands of rupees and salaried *hafiz* Quran readers, men kept in employment to recite the Quran at the grave in turns, the number of men uncertain in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 315, folio 322).

Funding lapsed with the funder. Of one shivala Lal writes that Sardar Bhagwan Singh used to meet its expenses, but that he too has now died (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 192, folio 195). Raja Dina Nath came without fail to Fazl Shah Majzub's khanqah and gave thousands of rupees in *nazrana*; he kept a *munshi*, Jiwan Das, the name uncertain in the printed text, permanently in attendance to write down whatever came from the faqir's tongue and report it nightly, made the building and the grave *pakka* in the faqir's lifetime, and paid for the funeral in costly shawls (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 325, folio 332). After that, the *ʿurs* was kept with great pomp while Raja Dina Nath lived, and devotees went on holding it afterwards, but now there is only an annual *fatiha*; the family sold its houses and havelis and died destitute (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 325–326, folios 332–333).

Succession passed by discipleship, by heredity, and by teaching lineage, and Lal records the lines name by name. At Chor Mor Wala, Mahant Balram Das was followed by Jai Ram Das as *mahant*, and the *mahant* now is Bhagwan Das, the last name uncertain in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 124–125, folios 127–128). At Jwala Dai, Salig Ram Bairagi was followed by his chela Aya Ram (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 127, folio 130). At Thibi Wala, Basant Gar Sanyasi is the chela of Mangal Gar (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 124, folio 127). At Bhairo ji, Bir Sidh Nath is the chela of Bawa Alhar Nath Jogi, both names uncertain in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 129, folio 132). At Chhajju Bhagat's, Parsotam Das was followed by Bawa Godar, his name uncertain in the printed text, as successor and *mahant*, then by Dwarka Das, then by Bawa Har Bhajan, the name again uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 186, folio 189). At the dharamsal, when Goma Singh, the founder of the building, died, Daya Singh succeeded him, and the holder now is Nihang Singh (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 213, folio 216). At the shivala by the Badhu-wala well, the name of the well uncertain in the printed text, a *mahant* has held office for some generations (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 214, folio 217).

Muslim imamat ran the same way. At Wazir Khan's mosque there are three generations: Imam Ghulam Muhammad, who died in a year printed as 1234, the reading uncertain; his son Imam-ud-Din Bakhsh, who was the mosque's imam all his life and died in the year 1289, again uncertain in the printed text; and now Imam Muhammad, his son, who leads the prayers (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 163, folio 166). At Masjid Bazar Bi, the name uncertain in the printed text, two sons of Maulvi Nizam-ud-Din are still the mosque's imams (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 150, folio 153). An imamat could pass with a house, as a going concern: Aziz-ud-Din gave over his house together with the office of imam of the Baluchan mosque that went with it, and left on Hajj (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 53, folio 56).

Teaching lineage could serve as a title deed. Mian Ahmad-ud-Din, *sajjada nashin* of the khanqah of Mian Wadda, holds Hamid Qari's khanqah, the care of that building now resting with him (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 253, folio 256). He took possession of the emptied Qasab Khana mosque on a claim of descent from a teacher: when Sikh rule ended and the mosque fell empty, Mian Ahmad-ud-Din, *sajjada nashin* of the Mian Wadda *dars*, seized it on the claim that, while the mohalla was inhabited, Mian Jan Muhammad, maulvi and teacher, had taught in the mosque and had been Mian Wadda's pupil and disciple (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 259, folio 262). At his own khanqah succession is hereditary with a scholarly warrant: Ahmad-ud-Din has been its *sajjada nashin* since the time of the English, and like his father he is a *hafiz*, a scholar and a learned man; his predecessor, Mian Sharf-ud-Din, *mujawir* and *sajjada nashin* of the khanqah, was a most devout and orderly man (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 239, folio 242). A gift could turn into hereditary control of a mosque. After a sermon, Nura gave Maulvi Jan Muhammad, besides cash and a robe of honour, the haveli he lived in, turning his own household out with nothing but the clothes they wore; the haveli, with its goods, cash and jewellery worth thousands of rupees, passed to Maulvi Jan Muhammad, and his descendants still live in it and hold the mosque as well (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 175, folio 178).

The state could name the *sajjada nashin* of a Hindu *samadh*. In the time of the three rulers of the city of Lahore, Bawa Parsotam Das came from Jaipur and they jointly appointed him *sajjada nashin* of the temple, when only the old *chaubara* and a boundary wall stood; he then had the great temple with gold work built (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 186, folio 189). Lal names neither the three rulers nor the year. On the same page he records the rule of the *mahants* there: the temple's *mahants*, who live in a sect whose printed name is uncertain or as celibates, the word again uncertain, are forbidden to marry; the sect's *granth*, composed by Mahant Dau Maram, the name uncertain in the printed text, is a separate book written in a script printed as *shastri*, a reading that is also uncertain; and eating meat, drinking wine, lying and taking interest are forbidden, as is dependence on anyone (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 186, folio 189). A hereditary temple office was ranked against a colonial one. After Pandit Radha Kishan's death, Pandit Lakhi Kishan, the first name uncertain in the printed text, became the temple's patron and bears its expenses; the founder was a peerless pandit with the title Chief Pandit, his successor is his *janashin*, and the post is distinguished from an honorary magistracy; the passage is damaged in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 134, folio 137).

Murids were sometimes preferred to heirs, and heirs to murids, and Lal records both. Of Faqir Sain Qutb Shah Qadri he writes that although the saint has descendants, it is the murids who keep the greater charge of the place, the key word imperfectly printed (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 271, folio 274). Against this, Shah Rahmatullah Qureshi's family holds both lineage and custody: the chain of *piri muridi* still continues in his family, and the same people are the guardians of the building (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 251, folio 254). Ganita Nand Gar, who is Shiv Gar's grandson disciple, lives in his shivala with his wife and has made it his home, one word uncertain in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 199, folio 202). At Shalamar, a gift of land created a hereditary office: because Mehr Manga the gardener, the name uncertain in the printed text, owned the land, he gave it willingly as an offering to the emperor and would not take a price despite the emperor's insistence, and so the gardenership of the garden was entrusted to him, generation after generation, and his descendants are its gardeners to this day (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 347, folio 354). A transfer of *tauliyat* could fail for want of an endowment. In Sir Henry Lawrence's Residency, Khwaja Ahmad Kashmiri, of the progeny of the man buried there, took permission from the Sahib Bahadur and took possession of the place, spent much money, rebuilt the mazars, repaired the mosque, and gave the *tauliyat* to Muhammad Bakhsh Sahib Lahori, the last name uncertain in the printed text, before leaving for Kashmir (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 241–242, folios 244–245). Lal's diagnosis follows: because no means had been established for the building's necessary expenses, it fell desolate again as before (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 242, folio 245).

One establishment might administer several unrelated sites, and custodians might be absent. An unnamed thakurdwara belongs to the Banke Bihari mandir, whose *mahant* takes its offerings and has appointed its pujari on his own behalf, a man whose printed name, perhaps Thahra Das, is uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 442, folio 449). At Abdul Jalil Qutb-e Alam's tomb the guardian and caretaker now is Khurshid Alam, son of Ghulam Muhi-ud-Din Shah, who lives at the village of Ratta Piran in Sialkot district, with two rooms at the tomb to live in (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 247, folio 250). A silsila's seat might lie outside Lahore altogether: Shaikh Tahir's family at *qasba* Batala (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 292, folio 295), and Mauj Darya's collateral line likewise at Batala (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 281, folio 284).

The absent heir at Kalka Galla, the name uncertain in the printed text, is one of whom Lal writes that he takes no care of the temple at all (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 122, folio 125). Rani Jindan's thakurdwara stands roofless for want of care; it now has no guardian, and only a Brahmin named Murli serves it, living on whatever offerings are made (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 205, folio 208). Balkishan, the name uncertain in the printed text, sole servant of an unnamed temple with fourteen shops, spends their rent on his own maintenance while the building lies unrepaired and decayed (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 105, folio 108). Custodians, in Chishti's account, did not always share their histories. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar had long sought an account of the elders of Lahore's environs and could not obtain it, because the *sajjada nashins* would not give him word of their own history, the passage partly illegible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 31).

Custodians alienated what they held, and this is the custodianship fact Lal repeats most. The fullest case runs from founding patrons through a hereditary *mutawalli*, community witnesses, colonial *nazul* and a decree to a sale. In 1856, the year uncertain in the printed text, after the city's *nazul* had been recorded, a man printed as Sayyid Shamsi, the name uncertain, filed a claim in the district *kachahri* that his grandfather had been *mutawalli* of the mosque when the mohalla was inhabited, asking for the Khoja mosque so that he might restore it. Witnesses of the Khoja community declared that the claimant's grandfather had been their *murshid* and imam, that they had built the mosque and given it to him, and that it was now the claimant's right, and the Deputy Commissioner gave him the mosque. He ran it as a mosque for a year or two, then made it a *kothi*, put English tenants in it, took the rent, one word uncertain in the printed text, and then sold it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 84–85, folios 87–88).

Mujawirs sold shrine land. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar, a poet of Lahore, built a family burial enclosure inside Sayyid Jan Muhammad Huzuri's shrine enclosure at his own expense, buying the land from the *mujawirs* of the khanqah, its door opening from inside the tomb's enclosure; only three graves stand in it now, the number uncertain in the printed text, though the enclosure is wide (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 167, folio 170). Lal records the purchase without comment. At Shah Ismail's tomb the *mujawir* has now sold the land to the English, who have joined it to their *kothi*, and the old well no longer belongs to the mazar, the people of the *kothi* drawing their water from it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 298, folio 301). An imam sold mosque land: at Masjid Chinian Wali, the name uncertain in the printed text, Gauhar Singh, his name also uncertain, took out the slabs and lintels of the eastern gateway and the door collapsed; then some greedy imam of the mosque sold the land, where a barbers' shop and a *hammam* now stand (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 153, folio 156). A graveyard became rental property: when English rule came, Pir Nizamuddin, its owner, settled many people in it, among them water carriers and others of a group whose printed name is uncertain, and fixed a rent for them, the words for the rent also uncertain; and this was the same man, Sayyid Nizamuddin Gilani, known as Pir Bodiyan Wale, who had earlier tried hard to restore the place without its former splendour ever returning (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 308, folio 311).

Endowment land was mortgaged and absorbed. At Sitla Mata all the outer land is mortgaged from the *mahant*, whose printed name is uncertain, and a great deal of further land, several words uncertain in the printed text, which the *mahant* had attached to his own building, has now been settled as the property of the *asthan* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 187–188, folios 190–191). At Chhajju Bhagat's *chaubara* the *mahant* has now built shops on the adjoining land outside (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 186, folio 189). Rent was diverted to the holder's own livelihood. The Sunehri Masjid's *mutawalli* through the Sikh period spent the shop rents he received on the mosque's upkeep and on his own living (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 147, folio 150). At Wazir Khan's mosque what survives of the *waqf* is held in the names of the mosque's servants, imam, muezzin, sweeper, lamp lighter and the like, two of the words uncertain in the printed text, and even the man who fills the mosque's tank lives on the shop rents (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 158, folio 161). At Chhujhi Mal Khatri's thakurdwara the two endowed shops are now with the founder's brother, whose printed name is uncertain, and he takes their rent and gives not a *habba* towards the mandir's expenses (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 452, folio 459).

Possession was contested, and Lal records who won. At Begampura the contest ran fifty years under three regimes. Under Ranjit Singh an artillery officer named Khazana took possession and settled zamindars there; Sansar Chand of Kangra was granted it and lived there for months, the land assessed at a hundred rupees a year, then made it over to his Brahmins, who pulled down many graves, took away the stones and sold the bricks. Sardar Lehna Singh Majithia took it from them and compensated them with a *jagir* of a hundred rupees in the hills. Nawab Ghazni, the name uncertain in the printed text, whose genealogy truly matched that of the Khan Bahadur who founded the building, came from Kabul, took possession and dismissed the zamindars, but the case stood before Maharaja Ranjit Singh for some years and in the end the Nawab achieved nothing.

In Sambat 1895 Bikrami General Gulab Singh Powindia's cantonment was fixed near Salimpura, the General's epithet uncertain in the printed text, and the General made the mosque his dwelling, expelling one *lambardar* and keeping Kahiya *lambardar*, the name again uncertain, as his gardener. Under the English that same Kahiya *lambardar* claimed possession and became owner; the heirs brought suits, an order for a civil suit followed, and they got nowhere and fell silent. One heir won possession for some months in a summary suit and sold bricks worth hundreds of rupees before the zamindars' possession was restored on appeal (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 276–277, folios 279–280). Twice the decision went for possession against genealogical heirship.

Lal judges some custodians' dealings with the new power. Nihang Singh of the dharamsal, known as the picture baba, always keeps a portrait of McLeod Sahib Bahadur, former Lieutenant Governor of the Punjab, the name uncertain in the printed text, and produces it at Englishmen's bungalows: in short, Lal writes, he has made the picture a means of his own advancement (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 213, folio 216). In the Sikh period Dhani Nath Jogi at Than Bhairo cultivated the Sikh nobles of Lahore, bought out the old occupants at fifty rupees a year, and made himself possessor of the place (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 231, folio 234). At Than Bhairo a custodianship dispute reached a British Settlement court and the claimants lost, and here the Settlement department appears as the forum that dismissed a claim to custody of a shrine. The old occupants no longer hold the place; it is held by the jogi, having passed from the faqirs. Dhani Nath Jogi had been appointed by Jawala Nath in Sambat 1880 Bikrami, the year uncertain in the printed text; his successor Ram Nath paid the fifty rupees for a few years and then stopped, and the descendants of the holders of that time are still living in Lahore. Under the English the claim was filed in the Settlement department, the *band o bast*: the claimant Badri Das pursued the case for some months, and Pandit Man Phul, the Settlement officer, his name uncertain in the printed text, went to the spot, but the jogis' possession was not broken and the claim was dismissed (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 231, folio 234).

Other claims were settled by the neighbourhood, by the court, or by building. At Sufi Wali Masjid, when the founder's son put forward a proprietary claim to the mosque and the house attached to it, the people of the mohalla, one word uncertain in the printed text, had him evicted through the court (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 173, folio 176). A land seizure was litigated before the Sikh court and settled by a rent deed: one Dilawar Khan, superintendent of Kunwar Nau Nihal Singh's stables, forcibly took the land of a mosque courtyard from the imam and built his own haveli on it; the heirs of the mosque, Mufti Ghulam Rasul and Mufti Ghulam Muhammad, appealed to Raja Kharak Singh, and the Maharaja sternly rebuked Dilawar Khan and had a rent deed for the land written in the imam's favour; the mosque fell in last year's rains and was rebuilt by Nawab Abd al-Majid Khan, *rais* of Lahore (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 170, folio 173). Of Masjid Naqiban, under English administration, when it had lost its splendour and stood empty, a *naqib* whose printed name, perhaps Subah Shah, is uncertain claimed it and had it released (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 252, folio 255). The land outside the Wilbagh Rai shivala belonged to the old wide well, and the people of the mohalla were forever quarrelling over it, until Wilbagh Rai, the name uncertain in the printed text, settled the matter by building a shivala on it with his own money; the well is now regarded as belonging to the shivala (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 122, folio 125). These shrine disputes sit inside a wider run of property litigation in the same pages: a long eviction suit lost to Arain occupants whose possession for a long period defeated the owner (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 336, folio 343); a partition suit among five named heirs with an *amin* appointed (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 328, folio 335); the claim of Wazir Khan's descendants to the Pari Mahal refused because the building had stood confiscated for a long time in the Sikh period (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 333, folio 340); the many suits of Kanu, the name uncertain in the printed text, refused because the English government did not think it proper to alter the matter (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 338, folio 345); and a *katra* passing by a *shariʿa* decree and the *qazi*'s fatwa, enforced by Maharaja Ranjit Singh's order (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 335, folio 342).

Faqirs quartered on shrine premises were a standing arrangement. Lal records them at Ghore Shah (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 237, folio 240); at Shah Gada Lahori's and Shah Husain Zanjani's (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 274–275, folios 277–278); at Hamid Qari's khanqah, a masonry room for a faqir to live in, the word for the room uncertain in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 252, folio 255); at Taj Shah Majzub's (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 285, folio 288); at Khwaja Muhammad Saeed's (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 287, folio 290); at the Shia Imambara (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 302, folio 305); and at Shah Kanhh's, the name uncertain in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 304, folio 307). At Shah Sarwani's a restorer of the British period built a room and seated a faqir in it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 303, folio 306), and at Mauj Darya's the buildings are for the servants of the khanqah (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 280, folio 283). He finds them too in the Suthra courtyard (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 203–204, folios 206–207); in the enclosure of Bokan Khan's mosque (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 182, folio 185); at Moran's mosque, where rooms with lattice windows above the shops hold the mosque's dervishes (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 180, folio 183); and in the masonry rooms for faqirs at the dharamsala (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 103, folio 106). At the sixteen cells of Wazir Khan's mosque he itemises the occupants: in one an illuminator is at work, in another a scribe sits, and in others the *mujawirs* of the mazar are lodged (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 162, folio 165).

## Shrines, Endowments and the State: Endowment and Waqf

How an endowment's legal source decided its survival is shown at a shivala of Bakhshi Bhagat Ram, who had set apart a village worth four hundred rupees a year from his own *jagir* for the shivala's expenses; but because the assignment had been made on the *bakhshi*'s own authority and not the government's, the village was confiscated together with his *jagir* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 97, folio 100). Its replacement was house rent paid by the founder's son (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 97, folio 100). The fullest fiscal sequence behind that loss runs from audit to confiscation, to a life pension, to lapse. In 1847 John Lawrence demanded accounts from Bakhshi Bhagat Ram; he delayed, and his *jagir* was confiscated and he was suspended from office, five lakh rupees being found outstanding. After annexation no right was established; the Chief Commissioner fixed a pension of twelve hundred rupees for the rest of his life, and on his death in 1876, the year uncertain in the printed text, that pension too was confiscated (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 68, folio 71).

The same point is proved over four stages at the Haqiqat Rai *samadh*. In Ranjit Singh's time a well with twenty *bighas* of land belonging to the *samadh* was held free of revenue from the government, one word uncertain in the printed text, and two villages in Sialkot district, one printed as Salharia, the name uncertain, the other Mandi, were held in *jagir*, their revenue illegible in the printed text; but villages and well stood in the name of one Sarup Singh, *khadim* of the *samadh*. Under the English the *muafi* was fixed for Sarup Singh's lifetime only; on his death the whole was confiscated and his grandson Nihal Singh left without it; at last, after much effort, a yearly cash sum, its amount illegible in the printed text, was released in the name of the *samadh*, Nihal Singh was settled as *khadim* and pujari, and the stipend fixed in perpetuity (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 209, folio 212). Lal makes the complementary distinction elsewhere: at the shivala near the Badhu-wala well, the name uncertain in the printed text, fourteen *bighas* of revenue free agricultural land, the number also uncertain, belong to the office, the *mansab*, not to the man, and the *mahant* takes its income (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 214, folio 217).

Lal names some very large grants. A *jagir* of nine thousand nine hundred and fifty five rupees, written in words and again in figures, the figures uncertain in the printed text, was held by Pandit Madsudan, a Brahman ritual specialist, with one village, Qila Gujjar Singh, held *muafi* generation after generation; lakhs of rupees were given in charity to Brahmins through him, and he held the office by succeeding his father in a rite printed as the *pashu puja*; the perpetual *jagir* then passed by *hiba* to a youngest son by a second wife, against the claims of three elder sons (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 67, folio 70). Of Raja Teja Singh Lal writes that a *jagir* of ninety three thousand rupees has now been fixed, Sialkot being exchanged for Batala and Sheikhupura held in *jagir* by his successor (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 63, folio 66). Rani Gul Begum, whose *jagir* was the largest of all, received twelve hundred rupees a month under the English and built her own garden tomb, with a chronogram numeral printed as 1247, the reading uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 293, folio 296). The state created a corporate endowment, secular in form, for the Qizilbash Nawabs: an estate in Lahore district, printed as Rakh Khamina, the name uncertain, was granted by the government to the family in perpetuity in exchange for a cash pension, with proprietary right and the government revenue remitted, its succession written down and the maintenance of the whole family laid upon it; on it the grantee settled many villages and built splendid *kothis*, mosques and *imambaras* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 71, folio 74). A *jagir*, a purchased mohalla and a royal *farman* were attached to the office of *ifta*: Sitpur, its second word uncertain in the printed text, in pargana Qasur of Lahore district, was in the mufti's *jagir*; he bought land in mohalla Adil Khani Lohani and settled his own mohalla there, still known as Kotli Muftian; and under Ahmad Shah Durrani came the robe of *ifta* and a royal *farman* written in the month of Ramazan, the year illegible in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 52, folio 55).

Grants of land and its shares go back to the Mughals in Chishti's account. At Miani the land itself was granted and shared. In Akbar's time the ulama of the village shared a grant, the word uncertainly printed, and its headship was later shared between Abu Muhammad Qadiri and Mir Ali, whom the king appointed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 191). After the Sikhs looted the village, the resettled houses paid a rent of one rupee a year each (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 212).

Shops built under and over mosques were the standing Muslim form of endowment. At the Sunehri Masjid the prayer building was raised a storey and shops were built beneath it on every side so that their rent might be spent on the mosque's expenses, one word uncertain in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 145, folio 148). The same kind of income went to three different destinations. At Hafiz Chabuk Sawar's mosque the shop rents were made *waqf* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 178, folio 181). Mirza Muhammad endowed shops to the east and south so that their income might keep the mosque flourishing, and a *nashistgah* that is likewise *waqf* as the imam's dwelling (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 171, folio 174). At Nura Iman Wala's mosque there are shops beneath the mosque whose rent is spent on its expenses (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 174, folio 177). At Sufi Wali Masjid two shops beneath the mosque on the bazaar side pay their rent to Haji Mauli Dad, the founder himself (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 173, folio 176). At Moran's mosque shops flank the stair, and the mosque's imam takes their rent (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 180, folio 183).

A *waqf* of shops was made for a named trade, and Lal records the trade's collapse. Shops above Wazir Khan's mosque, under the domed vestibule, were endowed by Wazir Khan, the word uncertain in the printed text, for bookbinders to sit in, the word for bookbinders also uncertain. In Sikh times they flourished, and there were no bookbinding shops anywhere else in the city; now all the mosque's shops are in ruins, and the binders' descendants are in service in offices and printing presses (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 162, folio 165). Of the whole Wazir Khan *waqf*, the income of everything Nawab Wazir Khan built and endowed, from the mosque to the Delhi Gate on both sides of the bazaar, shops, a *sarai* and a *hammam*, belonged to the mosque; but now the *sarai* is government property and the *hammam* too belongs to the government, and of the rest much property has passed into other people's names (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 158, folio 161). Earlier Lal counts the *hammam* of Wazir Khan among the mosque's endowments (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 15, folio 10). A room could be built and made *waqf* with a grave already in it. Of Sayyid Sarbuland's mazar, Lal writes that Nawab Wazir Khan built this room too and endowed it in the mosque's name, the mazar having stood there first, one word uncertain in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 164, folio 167). The same precedence of grave over mosque is asserted for the tomb at Masjid Suf, the mazar having stood there before the mosque was built, which came afterwards (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 164, folio 167), and for Miran Badshah's grave, which Wazir Khan kept as it was and indeed regarded as the ornament and adornment of his mosque (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 156, 161; folios 159, 164).

*Waqf* could serve as legal shelter, and the state paid for it. Warned that Aurangzeb had ordered his dismissal and the confiscation of his property, Sarfaraz Khan at once made all his immovable property *waqf*, began pulling down the haveli he lived in, which stood on his own land, and gave out that he was building a mosque on the spot, the last word uncertain in the printed text. The emperor was much pleased to hear of it, ruled that so devout a man ought not to be dismissed, a word illegible in the printed text, ordered property given in exchange for what he had endowed, and restored him to office (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 154, folio 157).

Under British rule endowment was created anew, in a form that bore rent. After the army left the Badshahi Masjid, Khan Bahadur Muhammad Barkat Ali Khan, during his term as *tehsildar*, turned to the mosque's upkeep and by subscription assembled some property whose rent began to be spent on the mosque's expenses, and a running well and tank were set going and an imam and a muezzin appointed (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 141, folio 144). At the mosque of the released Kashmiri Bazaar shops, whose rent brings in five hundred rupees a year, a committee of notables administers the income; the mosque is now well attended, the imam and muezzin draw salaries, one word uncertain in the printed text, necessary repairs are made, and because the land has grown the shops have increased in number too (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 148, folio 151). At the low mosque, by contrast, there is no endowment at all: the care of the people of the mohalla keeps the mosque going and the mulla in his living (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 151, folio 154).

Lal uses *waqf* of Hindu temple endowments too, created by purchase. Of Bhauri Sarkar he writes that she had the mandir built in her lifetime, bought much agricultural land and endowed it for its expenses, one word uncertain in the printed text, and had a very fine garden planted, which still goes with the mandir (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 200, folio 203). Inside the city a woman founder, Musammat Jwala Dai, built the mandir and endowed it out of heartfelt devotion and inner sincerity (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 126, folio 129). Of Daya Anga's mosque, the founder, the word uncertain in the printed text, endowed much property with it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 167, folio 170). The fullest Hindu *waqf* was made for childlessness: Pandit Bishnath, being without children, endowed a *pakka* haveli adjoining his thakurdwara, four shops in a bazaar whose name is uncertain in the printed text, and a second *pakka* haveli, from whose rent the mandir's expenses run comfortably and the pujari has a reasonable living, with a will that their income should always go to the mandir (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 450–451, folios 457–458). At the Bhagwan Das thakurdwara the rents of the lower storey's shops all go to the mandir, and besides these, fourteen shops built on the east side of the road, the word for road uncertain in the printed text, are also *waqf* in the mandir's name (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 462, folio 469).

Shop and house rent was the working income of many Hindu establishments. There are shops under the Kotwali shiwala occupied by tenants, one word uncertain in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 94, folio 97); the Teja Singh thakurdwara's bazaar shops on the first storey, whose rent the heir collects and spends on the temple (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 103, folio 106); the fourteen shops of the unnamed temple (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 105, folio 108); and at the Shivala Tarpoliya the adjoining shops on the north and west of the bazaar, which also belong to the mandir and whose rent the *mahant* takes (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 108, folio 111). The Baoli had a hundred shops, the number uncertain in the printed text, and rented *kothris* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 110, 113; folios 113, 116). At the mandir of Sant Shah and Devi Dial, the second name uncertain in the printed text, Lal writes that there are shops below with tenants in them, whose rent the founders of the mandir take and spend on its expenses (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 432, folio 439; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 433, folio 440). The Nihal Chand mandir has four shops to the north and three to the south (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 436, folio 443), and an unnamed pair at the Mori Gate three shops let to wood sellers (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 441, folio 448).

The Chhote Lal shiwala has eight shops toward the bazaar whose rent is spent on the mandir's expenses (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 448–449, folios 455–456), and Rai Singh's thakurdwara has shops whose rent Rai Singh takes and spends on the mandir's expenses (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 455–456, folios 462–463). At the mandir of a Pandit Nath, the first part of whose name is uncertain in the printed text, there is a *pakka* haveli of three storeys in which tenants live, and the rent is spent on the mandir's expenses (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 450, folio 457). At the Dehona Das mandir temple land is let to trade: a wood seller keeps a shop on it and pays his rent to the *mahant* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 443, folio 450).

Lal gives rent figures and uses rent as an index of the city's fortunes. While the fort was inhabited, each shop and *baithak* in the bazaar on that side fetched twenty rupees a month in rent, and now they stand empty (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 143, folio 146). A son in law gave a cash subvention: one Har Narain gives five or seven rupees a month towards the mandir's expenses (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 127, folio 130). Rising street levels he records as a direct loss of rentable frontage. At Masjid Muhammad Salih Kambo the ground of the bazaar has risen and the shops have sunk half into the earth, only five steps of plinth remaining (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 152, folio 155). At Masjid Chinian Wali, the name uncertain in the printed text, the ground of the lane has risen so far that the mosque's plinth is gone, and the northern window that once stood a storey up now clears the lane by about two feet (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 153, folio 156).

Land endowments are quantified. At Shah Sharf's khanqah eight *bighas* of cultivated land and seven of uncultivated land are held with the khanqah free of revenue from the government (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 297, folio 300). At Hassu Teli's shrine there are six *bighas* of cultivated land with a well and a wooden Persian wheel, whose income meets the shrine's expenses and its keepers' living every year (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 169, folio 172). There are eight *ghumaon*, the number uncertain in the printed text, at the Shiv Gar shivala (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 199, folio 202); about ten *ghumaon*, again uncertain, at Rani Lachhmi's thakurdwara, cultivated every harvest (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 200, folio 203); forty *bighas* and a running well at the dharamsal, whose income Nihang Singh takes and with which he feeds travellers (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 213–214, folios 216–217); twelve *ghumaon*, the unit uncertain in the printed text, with a well at Than Bhairo, revenue free since Maharaja Ranjit Singh's time (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 232, folio 235); and eighteen *kanals* remaining at Tali Sahib after the railway took the rest (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 228, folio 231). At the shiwala of the Hamri Gyan Mandir, the first name uncertain in the printed text, built in the Sikh period by Ishri Prashad Kayath, the first name again uncertain, there is much land, with four rooms and a well (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 440, folio 447); at Shah Gada Lahori's mazar there is cultivated land, *zamin mazruʿa* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 274, folio 277). Land was also taken by encroachment: Baba Dehona Das took into the mandir much land that lay waste on all sides (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 442, folio 449).

Grain, the treasury and a *rakh* also endowed institutions. Mian Muhammad Sultan, the contractor, endowed much land from the *rakh* printed as Challu, the name uncertain, in the khanqah's name, and all the grain the land yields is spent on the madrasa's expenses; in his time the madrasa's prosperity doubled and its income rose; Lal gives no area and no revenue figure (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 239, folio 242). A *rakh*, printed as Jallu, the name uncertain, was granted by the Sarkar to the same contractor Muhammad Sultan (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 360, folio 367), and later passed into mortgage, being now in pledge to the Maharaja of Jammu (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 360–361, folios 367–368). Another madrasa was endowed on the treasury, its expense fixed on the treasury of Lahore by the emperor's order (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 363, folio 370).

A *jagir* for service could collapse into debt. After Bhai Nihal Singh's eight thousand rupee *jagir* at the Baoli, his successor took the monthly shop rents, and within a few years Bhai Jawahar Singh owed several thousand rupees, the figure uncertain in the printed text; when the debt was put under administration, the Bhai's *jagir* and all the income of his property were confiscated, the word uncertain; a maintenance allowance was fixed for him, and that income too sank in the debt (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 113, folio 116). Endowment could be defeated by debt. Muhammad Sultan, owing six or seven lakh rupees, took the money from the Maharaja of Jammu and mortgaged all his property to him, every *kothi*, Landa Bazar, a property whose name is illegible in the printed text, the Pari Mahal shops and the rest, keeping five hundred rupees a month for himself. The Maharaja of Jammu now collects Landa Bazar's shop rents, though the founder had earlier remitted them, and that remission, Lal writes, was itself a cause of the bazaar's prosperity, the word for the shopkeepers uncertain in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 358–359, folios 365–366; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 360–361, folios 367–368). On his death he had no true heir, no son of his own body; Sirajuddin son of Abdul Rahman was declared heir and also died, and now two or three women receive a stipend (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 361, folio 368).

## Shrines, Endowments and the State: Rulers, Courts and Schools

The courts of the Muslim kings are described as they were and as they ended. In their time Lahore had four muftis and one qazi; each mufti was reckoned the ruler of a quarter of the city, every case was completed in the mufti's court and then sent to the qazi, and the final order was issued by the qazi's department; of the old muftis, Kanhaiya Lal writes, only the name is left (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 51, folio 54). Office could be refused at a price. A king wished to make Pir Siraj-ud-Din the city's qazi; he refused the office, and for this disobedience was ordered beheaded in the crossroads bazaar, until the ulama who had brought him to court won his pardon by much pleading; from that day he closed the door of teaching and instruction, the last word uncertain, renounced the world and withdrew into seclusion (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 149, folio 152).

For Bhandari the state is the city's builder and keeper. Under Jahangir, the royal palace of the capital Lahore was built with many kinds of pleasant apartments, and eight lakh rupees were spent on it, the figure uncertain in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 135), and Bhandari heads the section on the buildings of Jahangirabad and Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 136). In the fourteenth regnal year, the year uncertain in the printed text, Jahangir ordered a tall minar at every kos of the highway from Agra to Lahore, a baked brick well at intervals, and two rows of trees, and his officers built the minars and wells and planted fruit trees (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 136). Under Aurangzeb the Ravi turned toward the city and damaged its buildings and gardens; in the fourth regnal year an embankment ten kos in length was ordered, which Bhandari calls the Alamgiri dam, like the dam of Alexander (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 524). The riverbank was laid out like a handsome tank, one word uncertain in the printed text, and the nobles built pavilions over the water, and the work has been kept up at the crown's cost ever since: from the fourth year to the present, more than forty years, it has been repaired and rebuilt every year by the imperial government, and a large sum is spent on its upkeep (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 524).

The state also disciplined the city. Jahangir's farmans forbade tobacco as a reprehensible innovation, a *bidʿa*, and those who smoked in defiance were paraded in disgrace through the city of Lahore, and some even had their lips cut; yet they took no warning and did not abstain, and the practice grew (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 133). In the case of Haqiqat Rai, the Qazi of the city wrote a fatwa for his death and sent it to the governor of Lahore for approval, and Zakariya Khan told him that if he became a Muslim his life could be saved and otherwise he would be beheaded; he refused and was beheaded. The book gives no date of any kind for it, and states his offence twice and differently (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 208, folio 211).

In Chishti's account the city's monuments were at the mercy of its rulers. The elders of Lahore invited Ranjit Singh to take the city when Lehna Singh, as ruler, began destroying the monuments of former kings (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 786). Yet in Ranjit Singh's own time, he writes, the red, black and marble stones of the country around Lahore were torn down (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 296). The buildings of the earlier age survived to the reign of Maharaja Ranjit Singh; they had no guardian, and what people did to them is lost in a damaged phrase; when the cantonments of Ranjit Singh's armies were built, the bricks of old tombs and houses were pulled out, so that thousands of tombs, domes, mosques, gardens and old havelis that were memorials of the amirs of the past were lost (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 431). In the Sikh period an oath on the Quran was court procedure: when investigation showed one such oath to be false, the Maharaja had both the man's hands cut off, and he was known thereafter as Hafiz Tunda, the name uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 178–179, folios 181–182).

Under colonial sanction hereditary claim gave way to qualification. Shams-ud-Din drew up a petition and had the seals of the people, elders, chiefs and gentry of Lahore set on it, but the officials preferred a qualified jurist and another man was made qazi, and the court sought its fatwas from the blind Hafiz Wali Allah (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 51, folio 54). Teaching passed from the mosques to the government. Under the Sikhs there was no government madrasa, but four madrasas in mosques and faqirs' lodges, one of them teaching thousands of Hindus and Muslims, and boys were taught in every mosque besides; the course was Persian, Arabic, grammar, syntax, logic, rhetoric, hadith and Quranic commentary, and the first lesson was the Quran (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 38–39, folios 41–42). Kanhaiya Lal then writes that the English government removed all those defects and opened government schools everywhere (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 39, folio 42). A single state college taught Hindu law, the *shastri*, under Pandit Gur Parshad, his name uncertain, and Muslim *sharia* questions under Maulvi Abd al-Hakim, beside English law (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 39, folio 42), and in one school boys were made to learn by heart *ghazals* in praise of Jesus Christ (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 40, folio 43).

At Moran's mosque the mechanism is stated plainly. By Maharaja Ranjit Singh's order the imamat of the mosque was given to Maulana Ghulam Rasul and Ghulam Allah, because there was then no maulvi or teacher in Lahore greater than they and they were the master teachers of the whole Punjab; with them a madrasa began, the mosque's fame spread through other lands and students came from far away; and when both died and government schools were opened under English rule, the chain of the madrasa broke (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 180, folio 183). Of Ghulam Allah's five sons, Imam al-Din is dead; Nizam al-Din teaches without payment, for the sake of God, in Bombay; Ahmad Bakhsh is a teacher in the government school at Jhelum; Khalifa Ghulam Muhyi al-Din teaches at Rahon in the Jalandhar district, the place name uncertain; and Khalifa Hamid al-Din is a teacher in the Lahore Government School and still holds the imamat of the mosque (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 180–181, folios 183–184). Three of the five sons were in state teaching posts within two generations.

The shrine and mosque schools that survived are listed beside this. The Wazir Khan mosque has cells on three sides for scholars and students to live in, sixteen great cells set round the mosque wall (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 156–157, folios 159–160). The Badshahi Masjid has cells for dervishes and students (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 140, folio 143), and a Madrasa Islamiya was started in it with a named imam and preacher (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 141, folio 144). A madrasa is attached to a Suhrawardi tomb (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 169, folio 172), and in the cell of one mosque Maulvi Muhammad Bakhsh teaches (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 183, folio 186). Mian Wada's madrasa teaches Quran, commentary, hadith and jurisprudence (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 238, folio 241); it was finished in the Akbari period in a year read as 1000 AH (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 237, folio 240), it maintains the blind and lame by the hundred, and it is endowed on the grain of a *rakh* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 239, folio 242). Craft and copying went on in the holy places too: Miran Bakhsh the illuminator, famed for illumination in printing ink and for gold work, worked in a room of a mosque, a scribe and an illuminator beside the *mujawirs*, and the bookbinders' endowment gave way to employment in the printing presses (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 162, folio 165).

The colonial state administered, licensed and suppressed. The one explicit act of state mela administration in Lal's book concerns the *chiraghan* mela held inside a garden: it used to last one day, and now the government has made it two, and merchandise comes there for sale; the book ties it to no shrine or saint (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 351, folio 358). Outside the Delhi Gate stood the house of the Rasul Shahi order, among whom, Kanhaiya Lal writes, the drinking of wine comes before every act of worship, and where a still ran day and night. On the recommendation of Raja Dina Nath the British licensed them to distil for their own drinking only; for fifteen or sixteen years the still was left to them and the house flourished as before, and then, on information from someone whose description is uncertain, the government grew suspicious and closed the still, and from that day no one came to the house (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 247–248, folios 250–251). At the bhang dharamsal, built expressly for Sikhs to lodge the night and drink bhang, bhang was ground day and night and its Sikh drinkers gathered with the government's permission, and no one hindered them (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 213, folio 216). The *sair* of the whole city, the duty on articles of trade, was held by Harbari Singh, his name uncertain, whose Lahore garden the government hospital replaced, and on the one well kept when that garden was cleared the same wooden Persian wheel was retained (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 377, folio 384).

The colonial state reached into shrine succession through the city's schools as well. Carl W. Ernst and Bruce B. Lawrence write that "a debate raged in Chishti circles in the 1930s over whether or not the British could compel a minor who had become diwan at Pakpattan to obtain education at a British college in Lahore" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 116, folio 103). The college is not named. Rozehnal places the provincial government in the city, "the governor of Punjab in Lahore" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 39, folio 29).

Kasmani ties Lahore to the state's project at Sehwan, under which "special trains like the Jhule Lal Express were initiated to facilitate the annual journey of pilgrims connecting Sehwan with Lahore, the capital of Punjab" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 70, folio 53). Private patrons in the city did the same: the Bukhari brothers "would go on to organize pilgrimages from Lahore to Sehwan, reserving entire trains for their annual journeys", and "Private patronage such as theirs greatly complemented the state's project in Sehwan as much as it was motivated by it." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 61, folio 44). Violence at a Lahore shrine changed how shrines were guarded across the country. "A terror attack at Lahore's most prominent shrine had led to security measures being beefed up at major Sufi sites across the country." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 104, folio 87). Kasmani names neither the shrine nor the date of the attack; the year 2011 in his account is when he saw the tightening at Sehwan.

In Khalid's present the state reaches into the shrines. "Recently the Punjab government banned the consumption of drugs at a famous Sufi shrine in Lahore to control the law and order situation there, but in doing so it permanently altered the culture of the shrine, developed over thousands of years"; Khalid does not name the shrine (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated). He once petitioned "with a group of prominent architects from the city to save the destruction of a historic Hindu temple in Lahore that was being encroached upon by the local populace", and one architect asked him "if it was un-Islamic to save a Hindu temple" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 10, ch. 4, folio not stated). Of the endowments of the minorities, he names the "Dyal Singh Memorial Trust, a pre-partition trust based in Lahore", which runs the book stall at Tambu Sahib (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 224, folio not stated).

Khalid also records the courts and the government acting on the city's moral and religious life. He notes that the Lahore High Court heard the Shaheed Ganj case under the British and again "Around 1957-58" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 77, folio 59; Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 78, folio 60), and that "In February 2012, the lawyers' community of the Lahore High Court un-officially banned the sale and consumption of Shezan drinks in the premises of the court" because the firm is owned by an Ahmadi family (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 211, folio not stated). Behind the Roshani gate lies "the Heera-Mandi bazaar, the area reserved for the prostitutes of the city before they dispersed all over Lahore, after a crackdown on them during the Islamist government of General Zia-ul-Haq" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 205, folio not stated). The fort, "which was once the emblem of their power and wealth, is now a tourist destination. For twenty Pakistani rupees, one can visit it and even tour the private chambers of the mighty Mughal Emperor, Jahangir" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 195, folio not stated).

## The Religious Economy

For the Hindu establishments inside the walled city the offering is the endowment, and Lal says so in a formula he repeats. Of an unnamed thakurdwara he writes that the temple's necessary expenses rest on the income from offerings alone, no *jagir* and no daily stipend being fixed (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 120, folio 123). Of Bishno Devi, the building's expenses are met from the income of offerings, and the pujari lives on the same (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 121, folio 124). At the Wilbagh Rai shivala the offering, two words uncertain in the printed text, suffices for the shivala's expenses and the pujari's living (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 122, folio 125). At the Thibi Wala shivala a great deal is offered, one word uncertain in the printed text, and on it the *mahant* and the pujaris live (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 124, folio 127). At Chor Mor Wala the *mahant* and pujaris live on the income from offerings alone, and the mandir's necessary expenses are met from it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 125, folio 128). At the Kali Mata mandir the income is measured against the clergy, Lal setting the daily income against the pujaris' maintenance, though his printed sentence leaves it unclear whether it suffices (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 133, folio 136).

The Chhajju Bhagat house lives the same way (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 136, folio 139), and at Bhadar Kali at Niaz Beg the offerings are enough for the pujaris' living (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 230, folio 233). At the Sitla Devi mandir the keepers live on the income from offerings (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 453, folio 460), and at Atar Singh's the pujaris live on the mandir's income (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 454, folio 461). For the temples on pages 120 to 132 Lal records no land, no revenue grant and no state stipend at all (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 120–132). One temple states the absence flatly: no stipend or *jagir* is fixed for it by the government; devout Hindus come daily, bow their foreheads and make their offerings, and on that the pujaris live, the word for them misprinted and the rest of the sentence illegible (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 94, folio 97). The Kotwali shiwala, by contrast, has three streams of income: some land and a stipend fixed by the government, and shop rent besides (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 96, folio 99). One pujari lives on collections in the market, the word for it uncertain in the printed text, and on begging (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 451, folio 458).

Elsewhere the custodian takes offerings alongside rent and land income. At Sitla Mata the *mahant* takes all the rent, and the daily income from offerings comes on top of it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 187, folio 190). At Bawa Mangal Das's thakurdwara cultivated land outside the enclosure yields income to the *mahant*, and the *mahant* takes the income from offerings too (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 189, folio 192). At the Suthra house the *mahant*, a most courteous and hospitable man, serves heart and soul every traveller who comes to the house, and he alone takes the offerings (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 204, folio 207). At Than Bhairo the pujaris also take the offering of what is printed as the eighth day, the reading uncertain, and live well on it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 232, folio 235). At Ganga Parbat the same man performs the *puja* of the mandirs and takes their income (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 215, folio 218). At Abul Muali's tomb his descendants live on *piri muridi* and on the income from the tomb's offerings (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 285, folio 288).

Lal explains the volume of offerings by location and population, not by sanctity. Beside Sadhu's well, because the mandir stands on the bazaar, people come in great numbers to worship and much is offered (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 138, folio 141). At the Bhairo ji *asthan* the *mahant* sits in the window and at once picks up whatever cash and goods are offered at the mandir, because its platform stands on the road (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 129, folio 132). At Mian Mir he attributes the five melas and the large income from offerings to the population of the cantonment (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 245, folio 248).

Some charges were fixed per transaction. In one Khatri brotherhood, its *got* named in a form uncertain in the printed text, every family holding a wedding offers eight annas at the shivala, four annas when the wedding party sets out and four when the bride is brought home, and the pujari takes the sum (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 97, folio 100). For a mosque's upkeep it was settled that four annas should be taken from the party to every *nikah* in the city as help towards the mosque's expenses, and the sum grew to a good one each year, levied alongside a committee of Muslim notables who kept watch over the mosque (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 141, folio 144). At the Dharm Chand shiwala the whole mohalla contributes to its expenses and five annas are fixed for each wedding, the word uncertain in the printed text, with the daily offerings besides (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 455, folio 462). By Mughal order, Jhingar Shah Suthra had one paisa a shop a year fixed for him in every city, by command of the emperor Alamgir (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 203, folio 206). At the cremation ground a man takes one anna a corpse and helps with the work of burning the dead, in the same *dalan* where the government's sepoy who guards the cremation ground lives (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 223, folio 226).

The gravediggers' income shows how a burial ground paid its people. When a body comes, Chishti writes, the owner of the dead gives the gravediggers their due for digging the grave, and beyond that gives the *sajjada nashin* something as he is able (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 213). A gravedigger keeps his share whatever other work he does, and a rupee is first divided into four parts, the figure uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 213); the detailed division that follows is mostly illegible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 214). He also names a well at Miani and the property of Ilahi Bakhsh the gravedigger, in a damaged passage (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 214).

*Nazr* ran upward to the holy, and was sometimes refused. Ranjit Singh gave Bhai Wasti Ram, the name uncertain in the printed text, lakhs of rupees in cash as *nazr*, and *jagirs* and stipends were fixed for him (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 201, folio 204). After his illness the Maharaja offered five hundred rupees, the figure uncertain, as *nazr* at the Miran Badshah mazar, bowed his forehead there and vowed that such an act would not happen again (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 161, folio 164). The sultans of the time gave lakhs of rupees in *nazr* to Shah Chiragh's family (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 281, folio 284), and Raja Dina Nath gave thousands in *nazrana* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 325, folio 332). Against these, Mastan Shah threw away the thousand rupee *nazrs* Ranjit Singh sent, refusing to take even one, the word uncertain, and flinging it away as he went, the last words illegible (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 295, folio 298). Haji Nur, of the Pahracha caste, lent Shah Jahan one crore rupees for the Qandahar campaign, the name of the place uncertain in the printed text: the emperor summoned him and said, Baba, lend me a crore of rupees, and he gave it at once; years later he refused to take it back, saying that the emperor had called him Baba and so had become his son (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 295, folio 298).

*Nazr* also ran downward, from patron to participants. The Qizilbash household's Muharram is described thus: for the ten days of Muharram the *taziya* is kept at their house, lavish meals are distributed day and night, and they are given to Sunni and Shia alike. On the seventh of Muharram, the day of the *mehndi*, all who bring out the *alam* come to the house, and the chief gives each of them *nazr* of one to ten rupees a head; in all, not less than ten thousand rupees a year is spent (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 71, folio 74). At Mubarak Haveli the seventh of Muharram draws the whole city's *mehndi* and *alam* processions, and Nawab Nawazish Ali gives every one of them a cash *nazr* out of his own generous purse (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 332, folio 339).

Subscription, *chanda*, was the alternative to endowment. Temples were built by subscription and run on the income from offerings (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 114, 116, 117; folios 117, 119, 120), among them a mandir built by subscription within the last ten years (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 114, folio 117). The Bishno Devi *murti* was brought from Amritsar and its *devi dwara* built by subscription (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 121, folio 124). A silver *jalhari* weighing four hundred rupees was remade by subscription after a theft (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 107, folio 110). Under Sikh rule the people of the bazaar raised a subscription and built a shivala beside the well as well, which still stands (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 136, folio 139). Residents bought a building together: the people of the mohalla bought the house, where a confectioners' shop had been, the word uncertain in the printed text, and had a thakurdwara built in it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 135–136, folios 138–139).

A mosque was completed by subscription after its founder's death, the people of the mohalla collecting money, with a large donation given for the sake of God by Sayyid Haidar Shah, an officer of the Canal Department, his rank uncertain in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 183, folio 186). A shiwala was raised on money collected by begging (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 445, folio 452). Under British officials the same instrument was used three times within a few pages (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 136, 141, 142; folios 139, 144, 145).

Private money did public work. At the Faqir brothers' house there were a *shifakhana* and a madrasa where people were taught free (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 71, folio 74). Nawab Abdul Majid Khan distributed English and native medicines worth hundreds of rupees from his own means, and built and repaired many mosques in Lahore, which won him the highest good name; the exchange is stated plainly, building and repair purchasing a good name, and Lal names no mosque and gives no date (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 75, folio 78). Hajj and *ziyarat* were elite expenditure: two Hajjs and the *ziyarat* of Karbala Mualla (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 71, folio 74); lakhs of rupees spent on the Hajj (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 73, folio 76); and a corpse sent to Karbala Mualla (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 70, folio 73). Charitable wells were even endowed out of the proceeds of a theft: some time after the thief was hanged, the shopkeeper used the same goods to build several wells in the city of Lahore, and this well, named after Sadhu the thief, is one of them (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 138, folio 141).

Money moved at the scale of the court. On the day of Nur Jahan's bath of recovery the emperor gave the physician a robe of honour worth one lakh and seven lakh in cash; the queen gave all the jewellery she wore and eleven attendant slave girls, the number uncertain, gave theirs as *tasadduq*, so that twenty two lakh came into the physician's hands in one day, the figure uncertain in the printed text; the physician already held the headship of the royal hospitals (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 160, folio 163). Jawahar Singh paid an army in ornaments and *jagirs*: he courted the Sikh troops, giving a gold *kantha* worth twenty five rupees to each soldier, and robes, gold bangles, pearl necklaces and *jagirs* to the officers, but even so his vizierate did not last (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 196, folio 199). Of Moran, the Maharaja struck coin in her name, which circulated through the whole Punjab, and Moranshahi rupees and paise, the name uncertain in the printed text, are still kept as keepsakes (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 179, folio 182). On the economy of the city's shrines Kasmani does not write himself but points elsewhere: "For an account of the distribution of shrine revenues and the economic dimension of a Sufi shrine in Lahore, see Strothmann (2016, chapters 5 and 6)." He does not say which shrine (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 189, folio 172, ch. 1 n. 26).

## Religious Property and the Colonial State

Kanhaiya Lal is himself a party to part of what he reports. He was the Executive Engineer through whom the state's repairs to Lahore's buildings ran, and his book records the charge: an annual repair grant for Shalamar Bagh administered by him (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 348, folio 355); the Tehsil building, whose supervision and annual repair, the book says, belong to its author (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 371, folio 378); Montgomery Hall, under his supervision, whose necessary annual repairs are carried out through him by the Municipal Committee of Lahore, and for which his replacement roof was ready at the end of 1865 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 402, folio 409); and the women's jail, whose repair and supervision are still his (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 409, folio 416). Where he reports a repair, a grant or a state building in his own division, the evidence is his own administrative record, and he petitions in print for more of it, writing of the Badshahi Masjid that if the administration would turn its attention to it once more, the mosque would be fully repaired (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 142, folio 145). His description of compensation as full and uncontested (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 38, folio 41), his framing of Sikh loss against English restitution (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 148, 150, 153, 158; folios 151, 153, 156, 161), and his relay of the administrations' own reasons for demolition (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 15, 21; folios 10, 16) come from the same position.

Under the British, *nazul* and auction were the mechanism by which religious property changed hands. The word is spelled several ways in the printed text, among them *nazdul* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 257, 260, 261; folios 260, 263, 264), *naqsha nazdul*, the *nazul* register (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 268, folio 271), *basigha nazul*, by way of *nazul* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 270, folio 273), *naqsha nazul* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 272, folio 275) and *nazmul* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 322, folio 329), but what it does is consistent: property is entered in a register and auctioned on application. Shaikh Muhtaram's *rauza* was auctioned on the application of an Englishman and made into a *kothi*, its graves demolished (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 257, folio 260). Abul Hasan Khan's tomb was auctioned and quarried (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 260, folio 263). Nusrat Khan's tomb passed to *nazul* and was then sold, going to Lala Mela Ram (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 261–262, folios 264–265). The Dai Anga mosque was entered in the *nazul* register (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 268, folio 271). The *deorhi* of Jani Khan's garden was bought from the government by a zamindar (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 270, folio 273).

Nawab Mian Khan's tomb garden, ownerless, was taken by Raja Suchet Singh and then bought by Nawab Ali Raza Khan Qizilbash for two thousand two hundred rupees (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 272, folio 275). The Pari Mahal was bought by Sultan the contractor, and its bricks built Landa Bazar and a *sarai* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 333, folio 340); it was bought from the Sarkar for a price and rooted out to its foundations, as was the *sarai*'s land, taken from the Sarkar because it held the chowk of Prince Dara Shikoh and its bricks (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 358, folio 365). The well and garden of the Kallo Bai haveli were auctioned to Diwan Ajudhia Prasad (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 334, folio 341). On the other side of the ledger, *nazul* land was bought to found a temple: the government auctioned the land by way of *nazul*, and Nihal Chand bought it and began to build the blessed mandir on it, on land that had lain vacant since the Sikh period (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 435, folio 442; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 436, folio 443), and state land had been bought for a temple earlier, the land first being taken from the government in Sambat 1910 Bikrami (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 445, folio 452). The city's *nazul* record was also what set the Khoja mosque case in motion (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 84–85, folios 87–88).

The state sold and demolished monuments itself. The walls of Mahtabi Bagh survived, decayed, to the English period, but the government sold them to Mela Ram, and the ground is now under mango trees and cultivation (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 350, folio 357). Of Maharaja Kharak Singh's haveli, Lal writes that the government pulled it down and sold its bricks and stones (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 343, folio 350); he reports it again as sold by the government, and adds that people pulled down that splendid haveli and carried off its materials, one word uncertain in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 435, folio 442; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 436, folio 443). The royal buildings of the fort were pulled down and barracks built in their place for the British soldiers, the word for barracks uncertain in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 339, folio 346). At the Badshahi Masjid the eastern cells were pulled down in the English period on some ground of expediency, a word illegible in the printed text, and the eastern wall too was razed (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 140, folio 143), and under the English the British troops were quartered in the mosque, windows were cut in the cells for the English, and new verandahs were built (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 141, folio 144). At Nur Jahan's tomb, in the British period, the ebony coffins were taken from their stone vault and the bodies buried in the ground; the tomb was repaired in brick and lime and the basement left open, so that people go in and foul it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 324, folio 331).

Tombs and mosques were demolished for fortification, clearance, roads, pipes and rails. For Ranjit Singh's moat and double gates an open field was needed before the Bhati Gate, and so both the mosque and the tomb were pulled down, one word uncertain in the printed text; Shah Sharaf's coffin came out and was placed, through Faqir Aziz-ud-Din, in the enclosure of Haji Muhammad Saeed's mazar at a spot whose printed name is uncertain, and by the Maharaja's order a small masonry platform was built over it, which still stands (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 80–81, folios 83–84). In 1850, the year uncertain in the printed text, the Assistant Commissioner began the present Kotwali, and when the widening of the bazaar was approved, land was taken from the shivala as well and its southern wall pulled down and set back; no compensation is mentioned (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 96, folio 99). At the Shivala Tarpoliya a sitting place on the bazaar side was demolished by order of the officials (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 107, folio 110).

The bazaar straightening and water pipe scheme came with settlements. It began, Lal writes, two years earlier, when the government resolved to straighten every large bazaar of the city along one side and lay water pipes. Sufi Wali Masjid lost part of its fabric to the road, for which the government paid five hundred rupees, to which Haji Maula Dad added much of his own (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 173, folio 176). At Nura's first mosque the staircase, both *hujras*, part of the courtyard and a tier of shops went into the road, and the government's money rebuilt the staircase and a *hujra* above it and widened the courtyard with red stone slabs (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 174–175, folios 177–178). Of the second Nur Muhammad mosque, about half its land has now been taken by the government for laying the water pipes, and the compensation paid for a smaller rebuilding under Fazl Haq (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 175, folio 178). When the government straightened the bazaar's western line for the water supply of Lahore, much of Rai Singh's mandir's land and its well passed into the bazaar, and he added his own money to what he received from the government (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 455, folio 462).

A hospital road crossed temple endowment land at Sitla Mata, paid for in cash and in land: under some agreement, two words uncertain in the printed text, the government took land from it and cut a straight road to the hospital, and in return gave cash and also granted land in exchange for land (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 188, folio 191). The railway was the largest taker. At the shivala near the Badhu-wala well, the name uncertain in the printed text, the grand western door now stays shut because the railway gentlemen bought the adjoining land on that side for a price and paid five hundred rupees in compensation, and for the present the northern way is in use (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 214, folio 217). At Tali Sahib the land has now come within the railway's yard, and only eighteen *kanals* on the north side within the boundary wall remain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 228, folio 231). The tree there, a *tahli* or *shisham*, the second word uncertain in the printed text, present since Sri Chand's time, from which the place takes its name and which used to be worshipped, the railway cut down for some reason; its root has greened again and it has taken the form of a tree (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 229, folio 232).

Gathered together, the railway's dealings with religious property run as follows. It took the Dai Anga mosque and paid its private occupant (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 168, folio 171; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 268, folio 271); it bought for five hundred rupees the land that closed a temple's western gate (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 214, folio 217); it took Tali Sahib's land and cut down its worshipped tree (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 228–229, folios 231–232); it kept its material in the Shahjahani sarai (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 320, folio 327); it lodged its men in a converted tomb (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 257, folio 260) and its workshop officer in a converted mosque (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 268, folio 271); and it brought mela crowds from Amritsar and distant cities (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 351, folio 358). In one sarai the shops between its northern gate and the bazaar were occupied by women of the *kasbi* trade, and a government police post stood inside it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 359, folio 366; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 360, folio 367).

One temple's claim against the colonial state ran its full course. A government water pipe in the lane let water into the foundations of the Bhagat Ram shiwala and cracked its walls; the founder's son petitioned, an inquiry was held, the Deputy Commissioner and other officers came to the spot, the pipe was dug up and the cause proved, and five hundred rupees in cash were paid for the shivala's repair, with which he repaired it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 100–101, folios 103–104). The telegraph office was built on the land of an old well that had been known in the Sikh period as the well of a group whose printed name is uncertain, and for which the zamindars of the village of Mozang had already received compensation from the government (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 388, folio 395), and a confiscated haveli was given to Raja Tej Singh in exchange for the *kothi* that became Government House, the alternatives offered being cash at valuation or another government house (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 396, folio 403).

Restitution came through named officers on named dates, and sometimes went beyond the mosque's own title. Of the Masti Darwaza mosque, after the *nazul* department had entered it, Qazi Faqih-ud-Din of that department recorded it as *nazul*, two words uncertain in the printed text; but Major MacGregor, Deputy Commissioner of Lahore, in 1850, the year uncertain in the printed text, released the mosque to the Muslims with great generosity, together with a *baithak* and shops that did not belong to it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 143, folio 146). At Dalip Singh's exile the army was taken out of the Badshahi Masjid and the government generously handed the mosque to the Muslims, and Sayyid Buzurg Shah Hakim, whose forebears had been the mosque's imams in the royal period, was made its *mutawalli*: a hereditary *mutawalli* was installed and no revival followed (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 141, folio 144). At the Nila Gumbad, after the mess left, Munshi Najm-ud-Din, a contractor, the word uncertain and a further word illegible in the printed text, petitioned the government and had the mosque and tomb released, and spent rupees of his own on the mosque's repair, the sum illegible in the printed text; the mosque was then revived, and it is now called Najm-ud-Din's mosque, with the imamat given to Maulvi Nur Ahmad (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 287, folio 290). Masjid Bazar Bi, the name uncertain in the printed text, empty some years, revived when Maulvi Ghulam Qadir petitioned the government and obtained an order to restore it to use, and Imam Bakhsh the butcher, who held the contract for supplying meat to the British troops, spent much money on its repair and whitewashing (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 150, folio 153). The Sunehri Masjid was seized by the Akalis, settled by a *mochalka*, continued under *nazul*, and released under Egerton after thirty two years, the number uncertain in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 147–148, folios 150–151).

Across the regimes Lal keeps a standing register of changed use: tombs and mosques become barracks, *kothis*, offices, kitchens, stores, a treasury, a mess, a *thana*, a mission school and an asylum. Shah Chiragh's mosque served as officers' quarters and then as the Accountant's office (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 281–282, folios 284–285). The Nila Gumbad was whitewashed into a mess (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 286, folio 289). Bahadur Khan's tomb was converted when the Mian Mir cantonment was made, men of the Railway department still live in it, and the grave was demolished without trace (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 257, folio 260). Ali Mardan Khan's *deorhi* became a Sikh colonel's dwelling (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 254, folio 257). Nusrat Khan's tomb was absorbed into General Court Sahib's *kothi* with its old mosque, and a floor laid over the grave (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 261–262, folios 264–265). Abul Hasan Khan's tomb was occupied by a French general (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 259, folio 262). The Kambo tomb was made a *kothi* by Mr Samhur Sahib Bahadur, the name uncertain in the printed text, and an adjoining dome has now been made into a kitchen (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 313, folio 316).

The conversions run on. Shaikh Muhtaram's *rauza* was given verandahs and made a *kothi*, its graves demolished (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 257, folio 260). A mosque at Asaf Jah's tomb was turned into a *kothi* by an employee of the railway (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 323, folio 330). Of Pir Hadi's shrine mosque and well, Lal writes that an Englishman has now made it his *kothi* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 297, folio 300). The Shahjahani *sarai* holds railway material, coal and timber (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 320, folio 327). The Moti Masjid, renamed the Moti Mandir by Ranjit Singh, served as the provincial treasury under both the sultanate and the English (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 339, folio 346).

The buildings of Fazl Shah and the Rang Mahal served first as a *thana* and then as a mission school (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 329, folio 336). A Sikh general's cantonment *sarai* became the state asylum without demolition: in the time of the English government this secure building, built as a *sarai*, was assigned to house the insane (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 417, folio 424). A Mughal *sarai* at Ichhra lost its travellers, was filled with cannonballs for twenty years, the number uncertain in the printed text, whence its name Goliyan Wali, became family property, and lodged government prisoners on loan before the Lahore jail was built (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 365–366, folios 372–373). Sayyid Chiragh Shah's mosque is in government possession (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 79, folio 82); Dai Anga's mosque is one which the English have turned into a *kothi* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 88, folio 91); and in Dara Shikoh's mosque an Englishman had made himself a *kothi* in the English period (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 92, folio 95).

The fullest single case is the Dai Anga mosque, with five successive occupiers and the money going to the occupant, not to the endowment. Endowed as *waqf* under the Mughals, it held powder and shot for years under Ranjit Singh, was vacated and put on the *nazul* register, and then Mr Cope, superintendent of the Lahore press, took government permission to make it his residence, his *kothi*. When the railway works were set up on the spot, the railway took the mosque too and paid Cope the price, the name uncertain in the printed text at that point, and an officer of the works now lives in it, Mr Bokey Sahib, officer of the ironworks, his name also uncertain. Lal's verdict is that the *kothi*, which ought to be called a mosque, is now in the railway's possession (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 168, folio 171; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 268, folio 271). On the same page he records the state preserving its ornament: of the arches in enamelled tile, *kansi kar*, bearing Quranic verses and *durood* in *naskh*, he writes that the English have kept them intact for their beauty, since such coloured and decorated work would be hard to make now (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 168, folio 171; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 268, folio 271). The furthest passage of a holy place into state hands is Gilani's tomb. Desolated by Sikh plunder, its dome was made a wrestlers' arena, and a jamadar demolished the grave and built an octagonal house round it; the house was occupied by British Deputy Commissioners and a Commissioner and then taken into government as the Lieutenant Governor's residence, and the old tomb remains as it was in the lower storey, with the doorway of one dome blocked (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 394–396, folios 401–403).

Popular veneration could defeat a security objection. Of the Suthra house Lal writes that the authorities have several times set about pulling it down, because anyone who wished could climb from its walls and the branches of its trees into the fort, but it was passed over each time, because thousands acknowledge the sanctity of the place; a following clause about a price or a sale of the house is too damaged in the printed text to read (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 203, folio 206). Clearance could endow a shrine rather than remove it, by the same mechanism a century apart. Under English rule, when the houses inside the chowk were demolished and the chowk opened out, Muhammad Sultan the contractor built a *rauza* over the mazar there; the dome increased the mazar's splendour and doubled the adornment of the chowk (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 163–164, folios 166–167). In Mir Mannu's time, when the Sunehri Masjid's founder straightened the bazaar to set off his mosque, a mazar came to stand in the road, and he rebuilt it as a masonry room with an arched doorway (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 165, folio 168). The public square itself passed through three regimes. Chowk Wazir Khan was open under the Chughtais; in the Sikh period shops and sitting rooms were built inside the chowk, but in the English period all those buildings were demolished and the ground of the chowk opened out as before, and a contractor built a khanqah inside it, beside a well built by Raja Dina Nath (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 158, folio 161).

Two fatwas governed mosque land in the chowk, and a colonial officer later did what one of them forbade. The Sunehri Masjid's founder asked the governor for the chowk's land; the governor could not order it on his own authority and asked the ulama for a fatwa, one word uncertain in the printed text, and they gave leave, holding that the land is God's property and that whatever of it is given for building a house of God is most fitting (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 146, folio 149). But on absorbing the small older mosque in the chowk, the ulama refused leave, writing that on a place where people have once prayed it is not lawful to build the stair, the door or the washroom of another mosque; the Nawab, helpless, gave the mosque a road on two sides and built stairs on both, which were most unsightly (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 146, folio 149). Then Captain Nisbet, Deputy Commissioner of Lahore, had the old small mosque, which lay unused, pulled down and the mosque's eastern door built, with the stair also planned on the east, and pulled down the old stairs on the two sides and added their ground to the mosque; and Lal frames the demolition as a fulfilment, writing that the late Nawab's wish was now granted and his soul made glad (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 146–147, folios 149–150).

Grants and houses were confiscated. Raja Suchet Singh's haveli came under confiscation and was reused by the Tehsil department (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 370, folio 377); Diwan Hakim Rai's haveli had been confiscated in the last Sikh disturbance (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 396, folio 403). With them go the *bakhshi*'s *jagir* and the temple village inside it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 97, folio 100; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 68, folio 71), the Bhai's *jagir* and property income lost to debt (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 113, folio 116), and the Haqiqat Rai *muafi* resumed at the grantee's death (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 209, folio 212).

The same record holds British repair and preservation. A monument is listed but not yet worked on: this tomb too, Lal writes, will now be repaired by the government, and its name has already been entered in the register (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 312, folio 315). Thousands of rupees went on a river embankment to save Jahangir's tomb: the English had it repaired from the first to the present day, and when the river came near they spent thousands of rupees on building an embankment to protect it, one word uncertain in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 315–316, folios 322–323). Municipal drainage works at government expense filled the great tank at Haveli Mian Khan, because its stagnant water spread a stench through the mohalla (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 327, folio 334). Of the Wazir Khan *baradari*, in very bad condition in the Sikh time, Lal writes that the English government repaired it so well that it was as if built anew (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 357, folio 364). The enclosure of the *marghat* was built by order of the English (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 223, folio 226), and the unfinished *samadh* of Kharak Singh and Nau Nihal Singh was completed by them (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 222–223, folios 225–226).

Descendants restored their forebears' tombs. The outer enclosure of Abdul Jalil Qutb-e Alam's tomb was built in a year printed as 1274, the reading uncertain, by Ghulam Muhi-ud-Din Shah of his progeny (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 247, folio 250). Khawand Mahmud's *maqbara* was built by Nawab Zakariya Khan Khan Bahadur, because he was of the saint's descendants, and the Nawab also settled the mohalla of Mughalpura on all four sides of the mazar (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 241, folio 244). At Shah Sarwani's tomb, after the Sikhs had prised up the whole building, its garden and half the bricks of the well, a descendant came to Lahore with the Superintendent of Lahore, the last word uncertain in the printed text, repaired and whitewashed the tomb, built a new verandah, repaired the well and set it working again, built a *kotha* and seated a faqir in it, and planted trees on the tomb's land (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 303, folio 306).

The repair of the Badshahi Masjid was raised by a Deputy Commissioner's *darbar*, with a government grant and subscription across the communities. Nawab Nawazish Ali Khan, Muhammad Barkat Ali Khan and Nawab Abdul Majid Khan petitioned Captain Nisbet, Deputy Commissioner of Lahore, the name uncertain in the printed text at this point, and he held a *darbar*, gathered Hindus and Muslims from inside and outside the city and urged them to subscribe, gave money from his own purse and obtained a good sum from the Government. The mosque was repaired, and Lal then lists what is still undone, the turrets of the four minarets, the courtyard floor, and many stone slabs still to be laid, and addresses the administration directly: if it will turn its attention to the mosque once more, its repair will be made complete (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 142, folio 145).
