## History: From the Founding of the City to Babur

The beginnings of Lahore are told in more than one way. In his description of the suba of Lahore, Sujan Rai Bhandari calls the city an ancient city on the bank of the Ravi, whose founding he attributes to Lo, adding of its name that some histories also write it Lahur and Lahawar (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525). Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti tells the founding of Lahore twice, and the two tellings differ. In his section on Shah Kamal, introducing it as something people say, he relates that Raja Ram Chand had three sons: one, whose printed name may be Lo, built Lahore; a second, whose name is lost in an illegible passage, settled Kasur, the reading of that name also uncertain; and the third, whose name may read Rao, founded Mauza Rawan; the passage closes with the formula that God knows best (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 661). In his dynastic history the raja, Ramchandar, has two sons, Kushu and Lohu, and it is Lohu who settled the city of Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 795). Haroon Khalid, in the present day, records the tradition that "Lahore and Kasur are said to be named after Luv and Kush", and gives Parsuni as the old name of the Ravi flowing next to the city (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 71, folio not stated).

Chishti's account of the city before the Mughals is a history of conquest. When Sabuktigin first invaded Hind, in a Hijri year printed only in part, as a lost number of hundreds and seventy seven, the reading uncertain, Raja Jaipal ruled at Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 804). Sabuktigin, having won the Afghans over, brought Lahore and Multan into his possession (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 804). Chishti tells how Jaipal, defeated, asked that a trusted man of Sabuktigin's come with him to Lahore to collect the tribute, and a Nawab Khair Allah Khan, the name uncertainly printed, went with him; at Jaipal's court the Chhatris stood on the left and the Brahmins and Khatris on the right, the Chhatris urging the raja to keep his word and the Brahmins urging him not to pay; Jaipal broke the agreement and imprisoned the envoy, and Sabuktigin marched on Lahore, where the rajas of Delhi, Ajmer and Kalinjar, the last name uncertainly printed, came to Jaipal's aid, and the Hindus were defeated (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 804). In Sultan Mahmud's time, he writes, a raja of the Tawar family, whose printed name may be Osa, ruled Lahore and Kannauj, both names uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 804). In Bhandari, Mahmud's ninth campaign, in the year 412 as the printed text gives it with an uncertain reading, failed before a fort in Kashmir, and he then set out toward Lahore and Bangar, took plentiful booty, and returned in victory and triumph (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 414). In Bhandari's telling the city had fallen into ruin over long ages and little trace of it was left, so that the city of Sialkot became the seat of government of this province; after Sultan Mahmud's conquest, Malik Ayaz rebuilt the city and built a pakka fort (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525).

Annemarie Schimmel places the beginning of Lahore's Muslim religious life at "The second wave of Muslim conquest in India, that of the Ghaznawids about the year 1000", which brought scholars into the Subcontinent and "theologians and poets as well" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 376, folio 345). In her account "Lahore became the first center of Persian-inspired Muslim culture in the Subcontinent", and it was in this town that Hujwiri composed his famous Persian treatise on Sufism (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 376, folio 345). She calls the city "Lahore, the capital of the Ghaznawids in India" and says that Hujwiri "died there in 1071" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 119, folio 88). In an earlier book she names the *Kashf al-maḥjūb* of Hujwiri, "who spent his last years in Lahore", as the first major treatise on Sufi theories in Persian (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 61, folio 51). Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi gives the same eleventh century city a less settled face. In his telling "a number of mystics and scholars who had settled in Lahore were strongly hostile to the views of its author", "Some Lahore sufis believed that gnosis emanated from inspiration", and he speaks of a ‘captive among uncongenial folk’ in Lahore (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 131, folio 113).

Lahore was a refuge and then a capital for the sultans who followed. Mas'ud, hard pressed by the Turkmans of Tughril Beg, was forced to set out toward Lahore; his army looted the treasury at the Indus and put the blinded Muhammad back on the throne (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 807). In Maudud's time the Hindu rajas besieged Lahore, which Chishti calls the capital of the people of Islam, for seven months, the numeral uncertainly printed, until the besieged drove them off (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 807). A passage of Bhandari's city description, damaged in the printed text, names Khusraw Malik and records that Lahore was made the capital, and that for thirty eight years it remained the seat of government of the Sultan's descendants (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 524–525). In Chishti, Khusrau Shah son of Bahram came to Lahore and, out of necessity, made it his capital, ruling there for seven years, the numeral again uncertain; his son Khusrau Malik succeeded him; the Ghurids took the country from Khusrau by a thousand tricks and deceptions, and Ghurid rule began here too (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 808–809). Bhandari's account of the last Ghaznavids likewise makes Lahore their refuge. Khusraw Shah son of Bahram Shah, driven from Ghazni by Ala al-Din Husain Ghuri, turned to Hindustan, took Lahore, and governed the Punjab until his death, which came in the year 555 in the territory of Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 405). His son Khusraw Malik succeeded him; Shihab al-Din raided Lahore again and again and wore him down, and his rule in Lahore lasted twenty eight years (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 405).

Bhandari follows Shihab al-Din's campaigns against the city one by one. On the third, in 575, Khusraw Malik, who held the government of Lahore, sent his son with an elephant as peshkash and made peace, and the Sultan fixed a yearly tribute (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 404). On the fifth, in 580, the Sultan besieged the city, Khusraw Malik shut himself in the fort, and the Sultan plundered the country round about Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 404); Bhandari's description of the suba says of the same year that Shihab al-Din came intending to take Lahore, laid siege to it and could not master it (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). On the sixth, in 582, Khusraw Malik submitted, was taken to Ghazni and died there, and the Sultan installed his own deputy in Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 404). In this list of campaigns the digits of all three years are uncertain in the printed text. Later, when the Khokhars rose in the country round Lahore, the Sultan came from Ghazni to the city, Aibak joined him there from Delhi, and after the punishment the Sultan left Lahore for Ghazni (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 401).

Under the first Delhi sultans the city changed hands repeatedly. Aibak came from Delhi to Lahore and became sovereign there in 603, a year uncertain in the printed text, and later fought Yildiz over the city (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 400–401). Aram Shah was enthroned in 607, again uncertain in the printed text, in the delightful territory of Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400). The translator of Madho Lal Hussain's verse writes that Qutbuddin Aibak "died playing polo in Lahore in AD 1210" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 185, folio 185). When Iltutmish came, Aram Shah moved out of Lahore toward Delhi and fled after a brief fight (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 399). Shemeem Burney Abbas carries the city's religious offices back to the Delhi sultans: her ancestors "were appointed as Muftis (religious scholars) of the city of Lahore in the reign of the Muslim slave king Shamsuddin Iltutmish" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 25, folio not stated). Sultan Jalal al-Din, routed by Chinggis Khan, came and besieged Lahore, and afterwards fled toward Sind and Siwistan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 399). Rizvi follows the city through these centuries as a stage on the roads of the Sufis: it lay on "one of the main routes from Multan to Lahore and Delhi" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 158, folio 140). By the period he describes "Lahore had become the capital", and his narrative records that "the Khwaja reached Delhi from Lahore" and, of another figure, that "His father, Ahmad, migrated from Lahore to Delhi" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 135, 140, 145; folios 117, 122, 127).

The Mongols came next. In Bahram Shah's reign, Bhandari writes, on Monday the sixteenth of Jumada al-Akhir in the year six hundred and thirty nine, 639 AH, the Mongol armies of Chinggis came and laid siege to Lahore, the word for Mongol uncertain in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 395). The governor, Malik Qaraqash, finding the people unwilling to stand together, slipped out of the city at midnight and went to Delhi, and the city of Lahore was ruined and laid waste by the tyranny of Chinggis Khan's people, and a great many of its people were taken captive (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 395). Rizvi records "the destruction of Lahore, by the Mongols in 1241", and notes that "A group of Lahore merchants visited Gujarat" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 241–242, folios 221–222). The wazir Nizam al-Mulk, sent toward Lahore to drive the Mongols off, turned the amirs against the Sultan on the bank of the Beas near Sultanpur, and they marched on Delhi instead (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 395). In Balban's reign the Mongols raided the country round Lahore and Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 385). In Kaiqubad's reign they reached the country round Lahore again, laid waste its towns and villages, killed its people, carried off women and children and burnt its settlements; the sultan's army met them there, defeated them, and brought the captives before him at Delhi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 380). Bhandari names only the environs here, and says nothing of the city's monuments or of any saint (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 380).

Through the fifteenth century Bhandari's Lahore is a prize fought over by the Khokhars, the Sayyid sultans and the Lodis. Timur's grant to Khizr Khan is headed by the government of Lahore, Multan and Dipalpur (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 331). Shaikh Ali reached Lahore, took its fort and left twelve thousand picked horsemen to hold it (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 325); later he plundered round Jalandhar and brought many captives into Lahore, and Jasrath came from Jalandhar and besieged the city, lifting the siege when the wazir Malik Sarwar reached Samana (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 326). After Mubarak Shah drove Shaikha Khokhar beyond the Chenab, he camped at Lahore, resettled with reassurance those whom Shaikha's oppression had ruined, and, giving his attention to the resettlement of the city, ordered the fort repaired and rebuilt (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 327). Shaikha besieged the city a second time without success, turned on Kalanaur, and killed Raja Bhim Jammuwal, who had come out to help the governor of Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 327). In the year of Nasir al-Din Mahmud Shah's accession an army was sent against Shaikha, who had seized Lahore; he was beaten twelve kos from the city and fled to the hills, and the country round Lahore was rid of the trouble (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 334). Letters were sent to the people of Multan and Lahore ordering the Firuz Shahi slaves to be killed, and massacres followed in most places (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 336).

The Lodis then made the city theirs. In Ala al-Din's reign Malik Bahlul acted independently from Lahore and Dipalpur as far as Panipat (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 323); Sultan Muhammad Shah gave him the title of Khan Khanan and the province of Lahore and Dipalpur (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 324), and under Hamid Khan's vizierate Sirhind, Sunam, Lahore, Dipalpur and Hisar Firuza were taken by force one by one (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 320). Bhandari's description of the city has Tatar Khan, one of the amirs of Sultan Bahlul Lodi, making it his seat of government after a time when no sultan of Hind lived there and the city declined (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 524). Citing the *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, Chishti writes that after a passage that cannot be read, Lahore lay deserted for a long time, until an amir of a sultan whose name is uncertainly printed settled it again; the name Kamran appears in the damaged continuation (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 430). Settlement then grew (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 431).

Harish Dhillon's retelling of the Janamsakhis sets Guru Nanak's second journey in this Lodi Lahore, which "had by now taken on the position of a pre-eminent" town of the empire north of Delhi, and whose governor, Daulat Khan Lodhi, "had risen in rank to become the governor of Lahore and the adjoining areas" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 124, folio 138), and was away in Delhi at the time (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 125, folio 139). In Dhillon's story of the needle, an evening gathering for the Guru drew a large turnout (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 125, folio 139); "Duni Chand, the amil (revenue collector) of Lahore", kept a haveli at the centre of the town, and "it was set in a large compound of its own" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 125–127, folios 139–141). Seven red pennants flew over it: "Each flag stands for a lakh of rupees" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 128, folio not stated). Nanak gave him a needle to return "when we meet next in the other world" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 129, folio 143), and Duni Chand and his wife became disciples and he gave away his "seven lakh rupees" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 134, folio 148). Dhillon's Nanak then went from Lahore to Sialkot (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 136, folio 151).

Daulat Khan Lodi turned against Sultan Ibrahim and went over from Lahore to Babur, in a sentence Bhandari's printed text leaves broken off (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 311). Babur's fourth expedition from Kabul, in 930 as the printed year is read with some doubt, reached as far as Lahore and Dipalpur; before his last campaign Daulat Khan and Ghazi Khan wrote from Lahore asking him to come, Daulat Khan himself set out from Lahore for Kabul, and once in revolt the two meant to fight the imperial amirs who had already reached Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 304). Khalid writes that Babur, attacking the Punjab, destroyed "the cities of Eminabad and Lahore on the way" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 15, folio not stated). Rizvi also records Babur's conquest of the city, which he dates 1523-24 (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 367, folio 345).

## History: Mughal Lahore, the Invasions and the Misls

After the defeat of Rana Sanga, Babur gave Prince Kamran Lahore and Multan in jagir, and Kamran came from Kabul to Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 298); once there he summoned Lashkar Khan from Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 294). Bhandari's description of the city has it flourish while Kamran Mirza, son of His Majesty Babur Badshah, lived there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 524). Muhammad Zaman Mirza came from Gujarat by the desert toward Lahore and stirred up trouble (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 290). After his second defeat Humayun came to Lahore and held a council with his brothers, each of whom spoke against his wishes, and Kamran left with Askari for Kabul (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 286). After Kanauj the defeated emperor could not stay at Agra or Delhi, rested a few days at Lahore and went on toward Multan and Thatta, while Sher Khan's men rode as far as Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 269).

Islam Shah, Bhandari writes, came to Lahore with guns dragged by men, a thousand or two thousand men to each gun (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 260–261). Once there he reasoned that because Lahore is a great city, the trappings of royalty, the equipment of armies, plentiful troops, gear, arms and the other means of war could be gathered there in a short time, and that it was the place where the Mughals came in from Kabul; he meant to lay the city waste and to make Mankot his capital, but this intention of his never took shape (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 260). Azam Humayun, called Haibat Khan, raised the standard of revolt at Lahore, though Bhandari does not state his office there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 263). On Humayun's return with Prince Muhammad Akbar and three thousand horse, the Afghans scattered and Lahore passed without a battle into the hands of the servants of the state (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 256). In Chishti's account, when Humayun returned to Hindustan, the governor of Lahore, Tatar Khan, came out against him, and Bairam Khan overcame him off his guard (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 823). At Humayun's death Sultan Sikandar came out of Mankot toward Lahore to stir up trouble, in a sentence with a gap in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 252). At the accession at Kalanaur, Shah Abu al-Maali was arrested and sent to Lahore, and escaped through the negligence of the kotwal (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 237).

Under the Mughals the city becomes a seat of power in Chishti's account. After Hemu took Delhi, Akbar gathered a force at Lahore and marched on Delhi (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 824). A badly garbled passage places Akbar's second enthronement, after Humayun's death, at Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 648). Akbar turned to the settlement of Lahore from the start of his reign: he built a city and a palace, his *daulat khana*, raised large buildings and stayed there for a time (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 431). Chishti calls Lahore the capital in Akbar's reign, when Malik Ali, kotwal in the time of Akbar Badshah, held the whole kotwali of the capital Lahore, in a passage whose surrounding words are uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 194); elsewhere Malik Ali is the kotwal of Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 193). A heavily damaged passage says that Akbar was staying in Lahore in the days when the ancestor of the Gilani family came there (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 525). The translator of Madho Lal Hussain writes that "Akbar had temporarily shifted his capital to Lahore" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 7, folio 7).

In Bhandari, Muhammad Hakim Mirza, in revolt at Kabul, crossed the Indus again and again, and once reached Lahore and besieged the fort for twenty two days, while Raja Bhagwant Das, the subadar of Lahore, held it firmly, and the Mirza withdrew without gaining his end after Kunwar Man Singh defeated him (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 203). While the court was at Lahore, Zain Khan Koka was sent against the hill zamindars (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 170) and Rustam Mirza was received a stage from the city (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 175); grain boats went from Lahore and Multan to the army before Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 174). Todar Mal stayed behind in Lahore when the court went to Kashmir and died there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 181). After the emperor's fall from an elephant, rumour spread, the revenue went unpaid and the roads grew unsafe, until the disturbance subsided when the imperial standards came to Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 181). Lahore also stands in the story of Nur Jahan's family. Khwaja Ayaz, her father, travelled with his wife and the infant in a caravan as far as Lahore, and there he took service and rose to be *mir munshi* under Akbar (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 827).

Kanhaiya Lal sets out his own programme for the city beyond the walls: to tell in which direction the settlement of Lahore grew outside the ancient *hisar* under the Chughtais, which were the famous quarters of that settlement, who founded each of them, and whether any trace of them still remains (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 76, folio 79). His thesis is that settlement outside the walls began in the reign of Humayun and moved south, south east and east, until a city twice the size of the original stood outside it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 76, folio 79). He traces one suburb through three regimes. Under the Mughals no deed of sale or mortgage there was counted valid without their seal and signature; under Ranjit Singh the Baloch were dominant; and the Arains arrived in the famine he calls the *Chalisi*, the Forty famine (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 77–78, folios 80–81). Chishti also traces the city's extent and fabric. Formerly, he writes, the city of Lahore was settled as far as Mozang (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 115), and of the site of Hashim Pura he says that Lahore once reached that far (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 298). A garbled passage mentions the embankment of the old city and its wall beside the courtyard of a samadh (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 572), and a maulvi bought a house inside the walls of the city of Lahore, in a mohalla whose printed name is uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 372).

The names of the city's gates carry their founders' bargains and orders in Lal's telling. The Bhats made it a condition with the ruler that a gate be called after them, and the ruler agreed and named it so (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 18, folio 13). Shah Alam had it proclaimed that the gate should henceforth be called Shah Alami (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 17, folio 12). Akbar set up a market for every kind of grain inside one gate and named it Akbari Mandi (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 16, folio 11). Inside Taksali Gate stood the royal mint, of which only the remains of the mint's mosque survive (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 19, folio 14). Roshnai Gate was lit every night by royal order; it was in origin a gate of the fort, but is counted among the gates of the city (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 19, folio 14). The city of Lahore, Chishti writes, has twelve gates, and he lists them: the Lahori Gate, the Shah Alami Gate, the Mochi Gate, the Akbari Gate, the Delhi Gate, a gate printed as the Dakki Gate and known by a second name that cannot be read with certainty, a gate printed as the Sarawala Gate which is also called the Kashmiri Gate, the Masti Gate, the Roshnai Gate, the Taksali Gate, the Bhati Gate and the Mori Gate (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 498). Of these twelve, the Delhi Gate, on the side he describes as the east in an uncertain reading, is famous (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 739).

Jahangir's reign opened with Khusrau's revolt. Khusrau and Hasan Beg Badakhshi plundered their way to Lahore, where the diwan Abd al-Rahim joined them (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 146); the subadar, Dilawar Khan, would not meet them and made the fort fast, and however hard the prince tried, the fort of Lahore did not fall to him (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 146). News of Khusrau's capture reached the emperor in Kamran Mirza's garden at Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 145). Hasan Beg was sewn into an ox hide and Abd al-Rahim into a donkey hide and paraded in the city, and from Kamran Mirza's garden to the gate of the palace Khusrau's companions were impaled in two rows while crowds came out of the city to watch; Khusrau was led between the stakes on an elephant and then imprisoned in the fort (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 145). Khalid writes that Khusro "rebelled against his father and moved towards Lahore" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 255, folio 229), that Guru Arjan "was summoned to Lahore by Emperor Jahangir" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 234, folio 208) and died there, and that Chandu Lal was "appointed as the Diwan of Lahore by the Mughal authorities" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 253, folio 227). Sikh tradition, he adds, traces the quarrel between Prithi Chand and Guru Arjan to the Guru's trip to Lahore (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 274, folio 248). Four lines on the coin legends of Nur al-Din Jahangir son of Akbar Badshah cannot be read in full; they appear to name Lahore and Ahmadabad among his mints, both readings uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 828).

The end of the reign also played out in Lahore. After Mahabat Khan's victory, Nur Jahan fled toward Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 829). Jahangir, ill on his way back from Kashmir, came to Lahore and died there; the date is printed as a day of November in a Christian year of which only 162 can be read, corresponding to a Hijri year of which only the final 36 can be read, both uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 830). At Jahangir's death, Bhandari writes, Shahryar was in Lahore and laid hands on the treasuries and workshops (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 107). When Dawar Bakhsh, set up by Asaf Khan, reached the country round Lahore, Shahryar's ranks broke at the first shock, and he took refuge in the fort, where he was brought out with his hands tied by his own waistband, imprisoned and, two days later, blinded (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 107). By Shah Jahan's orders Dawar Bakhsh, his brother Garshasp, Shahryar, Tahmuras and Hoshang were put to death in Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 107).

The dead emperor's body was sent to Lahore and buried by the Ravi at Shahdara (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 106–107). Chishti writes that he was buried north of the city of Lahore, on the bank of the Ravi, at Mauza Shahdara (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 830); he places the tomb on the bank of the Ravi four miles north of the city (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 855), and writes that from its minarets the whole city of Lahore can be seen, with the Badshahi mosque, Wazir Khan's mosque and its other buildings (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 860). Shah Jahan's coinage and khutba were then fixed in the hall of public and private audience of the exalted palace in the capital, Lahore, on a Sunday in Jumada al-Awwal of the year 1037, the day and year uncertain in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 106). Shah Jahan, too, is in Lahore in Chishti's pages: the anecdote of Haji Nur has him stopping in the city (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 200). Shah Jahan is said to have had many buildings in Lahore raised, and Nawab Wazir Khan was appointed nazim of Lahore, the title uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 738); Wazir Khan appears again as nazim of Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 749). Chishti calls the Shalamar garden in Lahore Shah Jahan's memorial, and writes that the eye of the age can seldom have seen such a garden (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 833).

Both chroniclers tie the city's great buildings to this age. Bhandari's description of the city credits Akbar with a strong fort, the city wall and the palace, the word for strong carrying an editor's footnote with a variant reading, Jahangir with lofty buildings which, he writes, still stand, the princes and amirs with mansions, Asaf Khan's above all, and Shah Jahan's reign with further growth (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 524). In Chishti, the great buildings stood until the reign of Alamgir; great amirs had raised large buildings of stone, and the buildings of Abu al-Hasan son of Asaf Khan were among the chief ornaments of Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 431). The city's splendour, Chishti writes, comes from its tiled buildings: in the city of Lahore proper, besides the Jami mosque of Nawab Wazir Khan, there are three mosques with tile work, the first being the mosque of Muhammad Salih Kambo (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 751). Its inscription speaks of the Valley of Ayman, of the sanctity of Lahore and of the grace of the Haram (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 753). Of one tomb he writes that no tomb in the district of Lahore is so lofty or has arches so large, in a partly illegible passage (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 414).

Scott Kugle's Lahore is the Mughal city. He calls it "the Mughal regional capital of Lahor (Lahore in contemporary Pakistan)" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 202, folio not stated), and elsewhere "a seasonal capital city and home of the Mughal court during its perennial rotation" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 233, folio 212). He describes how "a large public square stretches out before the high walls of the Red Fort in Lahore, into which crowds surge, eager to witness the public execution of criminals" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 203, folio 182), the condemned being "rebels against the Mughal emperor, highway robbers, and tax evaders" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 203, folio 182). The book calls the fort the Red Fort and does not otherwise identify it.

Kugle names the officers through whom the Mughal city governed religious and moral life. There was the *kotwal*, or police chief (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, pp. 204, 233, folios 183, 212), and the chief judge (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 233, folio 212). The *Shaykh al-Islam* was the "chief religious scholar and jurist of the realm" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 239, folio 218), and the *Qadi* set *taʿzir* penalties at his discretion (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 238, folio 217), with the emperor's court above them (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 240, folio 219). Kugle distinguishes *hadd*, "exemplary punishment of a fixed form dictated by scripture", from *taʿzir*, "discretionary punishment of a flexible form determined by the will of authorities" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 235, folio 214).

The war of succession brought Dara Shikoh to Lahore. In Bhandari he left Delhi for the city meaning to seize the imperial treasury, in which there was one crore of rupees, the word for crore uncertain in the printed text, with the arsenal and the artillery, to raise an army (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 77). He entered the imperial fort, took the treasury, arsenal, artillery and workshops that were in Lahore, and gave freely, and about twenty thousand horsemen gathered to him, the number uncertain in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 77). Men joined him at Lahore, among them Khanjar Khan, faujdar of Bhera and Khushab; Raja Rajrup left a son and agent there, who slipped away by night, and Guru Har Rai left the same way (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 76). Khalid writes that after his defeat Dara "decided to head west to Lahore and continue his fight", and that Guru Har Rai, "along with his forces, reached Lahore" and met him there (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 210–211, folios 184–185). In Chishti, after Aurangzeb's accession, Dara Shikoh returned to Lahore, raised a larger army there and crossed the Indus (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 836). When the imperial army approached, Bhandari writes, Dara could not stay, and left for Multan carrying all the stores and treasures of Lahore, in ashrafis and rupees and uncoined gold and silver, more than a crore of rupees, with the workshops' goods and most of the artillery (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 68). The man who betrayed Dara, having handed him over in Delhi, set off toward Lahore and was killed on the road (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 838).

Khalilullah Khan and Raja Jai Singh sent officers to secure what was left, to guard the city of Lahore, which was without a governor, and to reassure its inhabitants, the word for guarding uncertain in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 67–68). The two commanders camped outside the city on the day of Eid al-Adha and, ordered not to enter Lahore, moved to Ichhra (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 67). On his own return Aurangzeb camped at Ichhra, received there the amirs who were in Lahore, and on the next day rode an elephant toward the city (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 65); he paused at the gate of the fort to inspect it, dismounted at the Wazir Khan mosque and prayed the midday prayer there in congregation, and camped in the Shalamar garden, staying some days to settle the suba (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 64). During this stay Khalilullah Khan was made governor of Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 64). Chishti likewise writes that at the start of Alamgir's reign, Nawab Khalil Allah Khan held Lahore, a word uncertainly printed, and with a mansab of six thousand was appointed subadar of Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 497).

In the succession war after Aurangzeb, Muhammad Mu'izz al-Din, governor of Multan, came to Lahore with a large army and stores to his father Sultan Mu'azzam Bahadur Shah, and both went on to Akbarabad (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 845). Shah Alam died in the city of Lahore in 1712 CE, the numeral uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 846). Chishti follows the later Mughal governors closely. Abdus Samad Khan was given the title Saif al-Daula Dilir Jang Bahadur and appointed to Lahore by Shah Alam, in a passage whose words are heavily uncertain, and he arrived when his son Zakariya Khan was thirteen years old (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 714). Zakariya Khan was appointed nazim of Lahore through the support of the Sayyid brothers, his father moving to be nazim of Multan, and he continued as nazim of Lahore under Muhammad Shah; he had, Chishti writes, a great passion for building (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 716). In the reign of Muhammad Shah a governor of Lahore is named in connection with the land revenue, but the rest of the passage is damaged (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 372). Mir Momin Khan became nazim of Lahore in Muhammad Shah's time (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 172). Chishti writes that his administration of the subjects of Lahore was good, in words uncertainly printed; that he was not autonomous, the real nazim of Lahore being a Khan whose name cannot be read; that most of the buildings between the Mochi Gate and the Kashmiri Bazaar are his memorials; and that he was the last nazim of Lahore, after whom none was appointed or came (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 173).

In the year printed as 1155, the reading uncertain, the suba of Multan was joined to Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 717–718). Mir Mannu, Chishti writes, governed at Lahore in Muhammad Shah's reign; the Sikhs had grown in number, and there was a general order to seize and kill them (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 121). A Punjabi saying about Mir Mannu is recorded, but it cannot be read (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 121). Khalid writes that "the Governor of Lahore, Moin-ul-Mulk also known as Meer Manu" ordered the killing of Sikhs (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 77, folio 59). Under Nawab Abdullah Khan, nazim of Lahore from the year printed as 1161, a famine fell on Lahore and he squeezed the people; Chishti records a proverb that under Nawab Abdullah's rule neither the millstone nor the hearth remained, two of its words uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 717). Khwaja Abid, subadar of Lahore, attacked Charat Singh's fort, the *garhi*, in a year whose printed form cannot be read with certainty, and victory fell to the Sikhs (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 578). A Nawab whose name is damaged came from Kabul to Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 722), and the office of the highest governor of Lahore appears in the career of Rajab Ali Khan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 141).

The invasions that followed fell hard on the city. Nadir Shah plundered Lahore thoroughly after Shahnawaz Khan's defeat, taking cash and goods from Begumpura, while Shahnawaz Khan's petitions to Delhi went unanswered (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 717). Khalid calls Lahore "the prize possession of Punjab" in Ahmad Shah Abdali's wars: on "the 1751 attack by Ahmad Shah Abdali", the Afghan "had every intention of capturing Lahore", and "the forces of Ahmad Shah Abdali reached Lahore, and laid a siege on it. For months the citizens were trapped within the walls of their city" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). His forces raided the villages around the city for supplies, and "an informant from within the city" told him of the Sikh village of Maraka "a few kilometres from Lahore" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). At Ahmad Shah Abdali's assault, Chishti writes, the city was put to slaughter and most of its houses burned, the last words uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 88). In Chishti's telling, Ahmad Shah came to Lahore and Shah Kaku Chishti told him that this was his own city; Shah Kaku was sent as envoy, and the man he went to had him killed, although killing an envoy is forbidden; Ahmad Shah, drowned in grief and anger, crossed the Ravi, the river's name uncertainly printed, and ordered a general massacre (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 218). Thousands died, the number uncertainly printed; Ahmad Shah watched sitting at the gate of what may be the Nakhas, the reading uncertain; Shah Nawaz Khan answered that if he was a merchant he should sell him, if a tyrant imprison him, and if a king forgive him; he was spared, and Ahmad Shah conferred the crown and set off for Hindustan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 219).

Ahmad Shah came a second time and ordered a general massacre of Lahore, in which hundreds of thousands of people were killed and Begumpura was looted, in a heavily damaged passage (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 718). Hafiz Lutfullah lived until the time of Nawab Zakariya Khan, after which Ahmad Shah Abdali carried out a slaughter in Lahore and Begumpura was plundered; whenever Ahmad Shah came, the family moved into the city, and went back to Baghbanpura when he left (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 724). When great disorder fell upon the empire, Chishti writes in a damaged passage, Ahmad Shah Abdali rose and the Sikhs began to loot Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 372). Under Alamgir II the Afghans attacked the Punjab and took Multan and Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 851). Ahmad Shah Abdali had given the government of Lahore to a woman of Pathan descent; Ghazi al-Din arrested her, and Abdali invaded a third time, where the sentence breaks off (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 851). The Marathas, with help from the Sikhs, are said to have conquered Delhi, Agra, Multan and Lahore and driven the Afghans across the river at Attock; no date is given (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 852).

Kanhaiya Lal's account of the eighteenth century city is a record of ransom, tribute and self defence. When the Sikhs demanded fifty thousand rupees in cash, which the Khalsa Ji would take as a *nazrana*, the sum was settled at twenty thousand rupees; they took it and withdrew, and Ahmad Shah then imprisoned Karim Dad Khan for paying and sent him to Kabul (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 30, folio 33). On another occasion the noses and ears of several butchers were cut off, and the city paid a large *nazrana* to have the Sikhs leave (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 31, folio 34). The misls' demand, that the cow slaughtering butchers be killed or handed over to them, was settled by the same mutilation (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 90, folio 93). Lal also records tribute paid to an invader, a beheading for collusion, and plunder redistributed among the poor subjects of the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 27, folio 30).

The quarters of the city bought off their plunderers or defended themselves. Of the Khojas, Lal writes that each time they put the raiders off by paying them money, and the walls of their mosque served as their defence work (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 84, folio 87). Ten thousand rupees were paid as *nazrana* to Jai Singh Kanhiya, whose promise in return was in name only (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 87, folio 90). The Mughals of Mazang survived sometimes by flattery and entreaty, sometimes by bribery and sometimes by force of courage (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 77, folio 80). Dai Anga's mohalla had *pakka* gates and a rampart (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 88, folio 91), and inside the walls the lanes were barred against the Sikh army so that the army could not lay its hands on the city as it wished (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 32, folio 35).

The Sikh period begins, in Chishti's account, in fragmentation. After Mir Mannu's death, rule in Lahore fell apart, in words that cannot be read with certainty, and wherever any Sikh found the chance he set himself up as ruler (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 719). In the time of persecution by the Sikhs, the property of hundreds of people in the city is said to have been lost, in a very damaged passage (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 722). Lahore then had three rulers. In one place Chishti names them, at the arrival of Bawa Parsotam Das, as Gujjar Singh, Lehna Singh and Sobha Singh, the first and last names uncertainly printed, the chiefs of Lahore, and gives the year of that arrival only in part, as a Samvat year beginning 18 (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 251). In another he names only Gujjar Singh, the second and third names not printed, and has the fort built by Gujjar Singh, ruler of Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 326). Elsewhere Gujjar Singh rules a part of the city, in a damaged passage (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 111). Chishti dates other events from the time when the three rulers were appointed in Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 721), among them the resettlement of a village (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 724).

The city's ruin recurs as a theme. In the history of a *gaddi*, Chishti writes that after Jiju Bhagat merged into the spot where he sat, the city was ruined (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 251). Of the mohalla of the Tamarind *takia* he writes that it was deserted at the time of the city's desolation (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 523). Khwaja Inayatullah, the name uncertainly printed, is said to have sold thousands of houses in Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 719).

The settlements outside the walls did not survive. Lal writes that when the Mughal sultanate fell the outer mohallas emptied (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 300–301, folios 303–304), and of one mosque that still stood he gives the reason as its solid construction, not any respect shown it: when the Sikhs laid waste the settlement outside the city, this mosque escaped demolition because of its solidity (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 250, folio 253). Their ruins became a quarry: the foundations of the outer settlements were dug for forty years under the Sikhs and for a further number of years under the English, the figure being illegible in the printed text, and still the bricks did not run out, until the English gentlemen gave an order that no one should take bricks out of the ground (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 33, folio 36). The trade made fortunes for the Kashmiris struck by calamity, who, busy digging and selling bricks, became lakhpatis, and who often came upon buried treasure as they dug (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 33, folio 36).

Lal follows the same trade from site to site: at the walls of Langar Khan's and Daya Lado's quarters (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 76, 86; folios 79, 89); at the Lakhi mohalla, where treasures were found again and again, the word for treasures uncertain in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 81, folio 84); at Zakariya Khan's graveyard (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 91, folio 94); at the chowk of Dara Shikoh (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 92, folio 95); and at Mughalpura, which yielded so many bricks that thousands of mansions were built from them (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 90–91, folios 93–94). He names the agents. At Ali Mardan Khan's complex they were the Kashmiri brick sellers of Lahore, and Sardar Gulab Singh pulled down the remaining buildings for bricks for his cantonment (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 254, folio 257). Brick sellers took the fabric of Khawand Mahmud's *rauza* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 241, folio 244), and a Kashmiri brick seller that of Sayyid Abdul Wahab Qadri's tomb (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 265, folio 268). The foundations of Abul Hasan Khan's tomb, which had cost ten lakh, were dug out so that no trace was left (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 260, folio 263).

Sardar Gujjar Singh carried away the bricks of Begampura and founded *mauza* Qila Gujjar Singh with them, and a thousand rupees' worth of the bricks went into the building of the cantonment (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 276–278, folios 279–281). Colonel Gulab Singh Kabh Kabh[OCR?], of Court Sahib's platoons, took the bricks of the Qasab Khana mosque's tank for his own house (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 259, folio 262). Brick sellers took the walls of Bagh Nau Lakha (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 356, folio 363) and the bricks of the tank at Ichhra (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 366, folio 373), and the old settlement on which Bagh Lala Ratan Chand was later levelled had been pitted by brick sellers before it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 354, folio 361).

A mosque could be mined in the same way. The *lambardar* of Mazang claimed Shah Badr's mosque on the argument that it lay within the boundary of *mauza* Mazang, a word of that phrase uncertain in the printed text, so that the mosque was taken out of the *nazul* and passed to him; he then began to pull it down, treating it as a mine of bricks, sold thousands of rupees' worth, some of them to the government, and dug out its foundations; the government refused to let him dig out the bricks of the well because of the gunpowder buried in it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 83, folio 86). Of Muhammad Sultan the contractor Lal writes that he pulled down many old mosques and buildings that were memorials of the age of former kings, and did it only out of greed for bricks; and of a mosque he bought and pulled down Lal says that he martyred it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 358, folio 365). His contract turned a Mughal monument into the building of the Landa Bazaar and a sarai, the name Landa uncertain in the printed text, and he took over Dara Shikoh's mosque out of greed for bricks, pulling down the cells of its plinth and two of its domes before his death, so that now one dome of that mosque stands as before and the rest has fallen (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 92, folio 95).

## History: Ranjit Singh, the British and Modern Lahore

Lehna Singh is one of the sardars of Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 855) and its former ruler (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 858). When Lehna Singh, as ruler, began destroying the monuments of former kings, the elders of the city invited Ranjit Singh to come to Lahore and take possession of it (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 786). Shah Zaman gave Ranjit Singh leave to take Lahore if he could, and Ranjit Singh took it from a ruler whose name is lost (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 579). In 1799, Khalid writes, Ranjit Singh "was greeted enthusiastically by a population sick of the lawlessness of that time" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated). After Maharaja Ranjit Singh took Lahore, the Nawab's descendants scattered to Shahjahanabad and Kabul, and one of them was brought back and made the resident agent at the court of the Kabul government (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 721). A badly garbled passage on Mehr Muhkam tells of someone coming to Lahore by the Bhati Gate and opening it, and of ten thousand men, horse and foot, entering Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 646); the Maharaja then brought all the chaudhris and numbardars of the country round the city under his shadow (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 647).

Chishti's pages on Ranjit Singh's Lahore are full of works and courtiers. Nura, having gained access to Ranjit Singh's government, was appointed to the building of the moat of Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 423). A weapon made by Ahmad Shah was placed in Lahore at the baradari of Nawab Wazir Khan, where something whose printed name cannot be read with certainty now stands (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 575). The first of the misls, whose printed name is uncertain, was of the Jat people of the district of Lahore, with ten thousand horse, and the sardar of the third misl, his name uncertainly printed, became ruler of the eastern districts of Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 575). Part of one sardar's fourth share was confiscated during a disturbance in Lahore with the Sikh army, in an uncertainly printed passage (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 577). Khushal Singh received havelis and gardens in Lahore and Amritsar, and Dina Nath built a large *shivala* near a part of Lahore whose name cannot be read (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 583). Among the nobles and chiefs of Lahore who visited Mai Bhagi were Raja Dina Nath, the Sandhanwalia sardars and Raja Dhian Singh (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 790).

Under Sikh rule the city's mosques and tombs became powder magazines, arsenals and gun platforms, the pattern Lal records more often than any other. Abd al-Razzaq's *maqbara* and mosque were full of gunpowder, with iron shot made in the courtyard (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 80, folio 83). Shah Badr's mosque held powder and shot and a company of soldiers, gunpowder was manufactured in its *hujras*, and all the powder was later sunk in the mosque's well and earthed over (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 83, folio 86). The Khojas' mosque, the sole surviving building of their quarter, was kept filled with gunpowder (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 84, folio 87). Of Masjid Bazar Bi[OCR?] he writes that in the Sikh period the Maharaja's gunpowder was kept stored in it, the word for gunpowder uncertain in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 150, folio 153). The Masti Darwaza mosque was under government control and powder was made in it for years, until it was known through the city as the powder works mosque (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 143, folio 146).

In Maharaja Ranjit Singh's time, Lal writes, Daya Anga's mosque was kept full of powder and shot for years on end (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 168, folio 171). General Gulab Singh filled Khawand Mahmud's *rauza* with powder and kept it locked for years; under the British the powder was thrown into the river, the verb uncertain in the printed text, and the *rauza* was emptied (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 241, folio 244). Muhammad Salih Sindhi's mosque was used the same way by Ranjit Singh's order (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 250, folio 253). At the Nila Gumbad the tomb was filled with gunpowder, shot and bullets were stored in a part of the mosque whose printed name is uncertain, the verb also uncertain, and a separate building was put up for the smiths, in which they made guns (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 286, folio 289). The Kambo tomb was used likewise (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 313, folio 316), and the Pari Mahal held troops, shot and powder (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 333, folio 340).

Two of these stores exploded. The powder sunk in the well of Shah Badr's mosque went up when a neighbour lit a fire on the filled well: the powder blew up and an earthquake shook the ground, bricks flew far, the ground was pitted for fifty yards, and the zamindar or zamindars were killed (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 83–84, folios 86–87). At the Pattharan-wali haveli, run as a gunpowder factory, several thousand maunds of gunpowder blew up in an instant, the Mochi Gate quarter was demolished, and about two hundred lives were lost in the disaster, two words of the sentence uncertain in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 330, folio 337).

The Badshahi Masjid passed from royal splendour to military use. Its furnishings in the royal era, floor coverings, lanterns and the like, were worth lakhs of rupees; it then served sometimes as an artillery park and sometimes as a cantonment for infantry and cavalry, and the Sikhs prised up its stone slabs and carried them off, the word for slabs uncertain in the printed text, and the floor of the mosque was plundered (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 140, folio 143). Ranjit Singh had the turrets of its minarets pulled down: he had those three demolished as well, and all four minarets were left without their domes (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 140, folio 143). In Rani Chand Kaur's time Sher Singh mounted *zamburak*, small camel guns, on the minarets and shelled the fort, while the shot from the fort fell inside the mosque, and so it went on for three days; Raja Hira Singh did the same during the Sandhanwalia siege (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 140–141, folios 143–144).

For his own projects Ranjit Singh had the marble and stone of Lahore's tombs taken up, and Lal itemises it site by site. At Sayyid Abdul Wahab Qadri's tomb the floor inside and the floor of the grave were taken up by Maharaja Ranjit Singh's order and the grave was knocked down (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 265, folio 268). At Shahzada Parvez's tomb the marble floor was taken up by Maharaja Ranjit Singh's order (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 270, folio 273). At the graveyard of the Gilani Sayyids Maharaja Ranjit Singh had the stone taken off the graves, so that the graves fell in, and the grand marble dome over Sayyid Qasim's graves was pulled down; its stone was taken, and an enclosure wall was built (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 308, folio 311). At Zeb-un-Nisa's tomb, when the Maharaja conceived the wish to build a solid marble baradari in the Hazuri Bagh, he had all the stone of the tomb taken off, the garden's name uncertain in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 311, folio 314). Shah Rustam Ghazi's marble grave was knocked down by Maharaja Ranjit Singh, in a sentence that breaks off after naming its stone and a word uncertainly printed (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 312, folio 315).

The list runs on. Sayyid Ahmad's marble at Bibi Pak Daman was taken (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 305, folio 308). At Pir Hadi's tomb the marble lattices and red stone columns were taken down by Raja Dhian Singh and carried off to Jammu, the place name uncertain in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 296–297, folios 299–300). At Bahadur Khan's grave the loss rests on oral testimony only: elderly people say that this grave was of marble but that Maharaja Ranjit Singh had it stripped, two words of the passage uncertain in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 258, folio 261). Makhduma Begum's grave, Lal writes, was once of marble, and the Sikhs knocked it down (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 260, folio 263). At Nawab Mian Khan's tomb Raja Suchet Singh broke the marble *taʿwiz* and destroyed the grave: now only the platform remains, and the place where the *taʿwiz* of the grave was broken off can still be seen (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 273, folio 276).

Lal states the purpose twice. When at last Maharaja Ranjit Singh needed stone to send to Amritsar, and it was proposed to build a stone baradari in the Hazuri Bagh, stone was taken off many of the tombs of Lahore, the garden's name uncertain in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 322, folio 329). And, most explicitly, the Muslim tombs outside the city were stripped to build the Hazuri Bagh *baradari*. Since countless stone *rauzas* and tombs of Muslim nobles then stood outside the city, the order was given that their stone be taken off and set into the building of this baradari; first of all the stone of Zeb-un-Nisa's tomb was taken, then that of Shah Sharaf, whose tomb stood before a gate printed as Bhai, perhaps the Bhati Gate, and in the same way the stone of many tombs was taken off to finish the building. Even so the stone ran short, and the floors of the lower, second and third storeys were not made of stone but had to make do with lime (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 340, folio 347).

Stone also passed between traditions by written order. From Jahangir's tomb the order was to prise up the columns for lighting candles and the marble lattices on the outer part of the tomb, a word of that phrase uncertain in the printed text, take them to Amritsar and set them on the bridge and elsewhere at the Sri Darbar Sahib; countless stones were prised from this tomb and sent to Amritsar, and work in lime was put in their place (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 315, folio 322). The stone of Khwaja Muhammad Saeed's demolished mosque and tomb, marble and red stone and more, the last word uncertain in the printed text, was sent to Amritsar (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 287, folio 290). From Shalamar Bagh countless marble slabs were taken down for the building of the Darbar at Amritsar, and its red stone slabs were set into the Ram Bagh (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 348, folio 355). One exception to the destruction is recorded: Khushhal Singh's garden was levelled, but the *samadh* of his preceptor[OCR?] Ram Singh survived because Shiva Ji had also been installed in it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 353, folio 360).

Under Kunwar Nau Nihal Singh a belt half a mile wide was cleared round the walled city: the order was that for half a mile outside the wall of Lahore, on all four sides of the city, the ground be made an open plain, the buildings knocked down and the trees cut, two words of the order uncertain in the printed text. The work began outside the Delhi Gate under an Englishman whose printed name cannot be read with certainty, and in it the mosque at Shah Muhammad Ghaus's tomb fell too, and its enclosure walls, inner and outer, were demolished (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 249–250, folios 252–253). Lal records the protest and its failure: at this event the subjects of Lahore, Hindus and Muslims, raised a great outcry and wished this holy place to be saved from demolition, but no one listened, the word for outcry uncertain in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 249–250, folios 252–253). The same shrine later lost its western wall to a government garden laid out by filling the city ditch (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 249, folio 252).

After Ranjit Singh came the court's wars and the annexation. Maharaja Kharak Singh died in the Bikrami year 1897, the numeral uncertainly printed, and Maharaja Sher Singh was enthroned in the same year; Sher Singh came to Lahore after the deaths of Kharak Singh and Nau Nihal Singh (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 723). Raja Dhian Singh summoned Sher Singh to Lahore so that he might take the throne, and he came and pitched his camp near the cantonment of the Khalsa army (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 588). The Sandhanwalia party came into the fort and killed Raja Dhian Singh, and Sher Singh's killers were dispatched after the siege of the fort of Lahore and a great battle (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 589). Suchet Singh was killed in Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 582). In Lal's account a khanqah became the battlefield. Under Maharaja Dalip[OCR?] Singh, in Raja Hira Singh's wazirship, Raja Suchet Singh lodged in Mian Wada's madrasa, and the Sikh army attacked it on Hira Singh's order: Suchet Singh was killed, the walls of the building were brought down by cannon balls, the verb uncertain in the printed text, and many of the khanqah's dervishes were killed (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 239, folio 242). In the storming of Khanqah Mian Wadda[OCR?], foot and horse surrounded the khanqah and the guns began to fire, the verb uncertain in the printed text, and one hundred dervishes of the khanqah, the figure uncertain in the printed text, and about a hundred and fifty of Raja Suchet Singh's companions were killed in the affair; the passage does not say what became of the khanqah afterwards (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 194–195, folios 197–198).

Raja Hira Singh and Pandit Jalla were killed on the bank of the Ravi on the Id of sacrifice, and their heads and bodies were seen hanging, the heads staying there for some days, the number uncertainly printed, while the damaged passage names the bazaar and Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 590). After the defeat at Sobraon, the name uncertainly printed, the troops fled, looting, and entered Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 591). Under the treaty of 1846 territory passed from the government of Lahore, and at the request of the people of the state of Lahore a Resident was appointed in Lahore, while disorder continued (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 591). Raja Lal, the name uncertainly printed, was expelled from Lahore and sent to Agra, also uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 591). In December 1849, the day lost and the year uncertainly printed, Duleep Singh left Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 592).

Under the British, Kanhaiya Lal writes, Lahore was made the capital of the Punjab, and Delhi itself became subordinate to this province (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 33, folio 36). The Sadr Bazaar of Anarkali ran for about a mile, with trade in lakhs of rupees, as though a second city had been settled outside the first (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 34, folio 37), and the present population had grown to twice any earlier one (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 35, folio 38). The chief cause of the city's progress, in his judgement, was the new railway department (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 35, folio 38). Of the British city Chishti gives one testimony. Rajab Ali Khan, speaking in the first person, saw that Lahore had been adorned, its surroundings, its clean roads and its lofty buildings, and credited it to the Lieutenant Governor of the Punjab, the title uncertainly printed, finding the city thousands of times more splendid than he had seen it before (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 142).

Under the English the city's own defences were remade. Akbar's wall stood until the end of Sikh rule; then, in the English period, so high a wall was thought useless, and first about half of it was pulled down, then the order came to pull down the rest, and a low masonry wall was built in its place (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 15, folio 10). The bricks of the ditch were lifted, and the ditch was filled with earth, gardens were laid out on that same ground and a canal was set running (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 21, folio 16). Lal goes gate by gate: the Delhi Gate demolished and rebuilt in two storeys; the Akbari rebuilt anew on the old pattern; the Mochi demolished and its bricks sold; the Shah Alami and Lahori rebuilt on the old pattern; the Mori widened; the Bhati rebuilt in the English style, a word before that phrase illegible in the printed text; the Masti now pulled down by order of the English officials; the Kashmiri rebuilt in the new English style, on the pattern of the Bhati Gate; and the Zaki, or Makki, demolished, to be rebuilt soon (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 15–21, folios 10–16). Of the original Akbari fabric, only the Delhi Gate kept any (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 15–16, folios 10–11).

The railway decided which sarais prospered. At Muhammad Sultan's sarai outside Delhi Gate thousands of travellers rested every day, with a bazaar beside it whose printed name, Landa, is uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 35, folio 38). Outside Shah Alami Gate stood Diwan Ratan Chand's sarai and tank; the tank was always full, and thousands of men and women came from the city to bathe there (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 35–36, folios 38–39). He built a shivala there, and his son built a thakurdwara on the southern bank of the tank (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 65, folio 68). The complex had rooms on all four sides for travellers to lodge in, two masonry shops flanking the gate and shops along the bank where traders lived, and goods of every kind from outside, salt, ghee, oil and grain, were sold there, so that something like a great market gathered (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 224–225, folios 227–228). Kanhaiya Lal records that it was built in the period of English rule by a serving Honorary Magistrate of Lahore (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 224, folio 227). Mela Ram's sarai outside Bhati Gate, by contrast, failed because it lay too far from the railway station (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 36, folio 39).

The Municipal Committee sat under the Deputy Commissioner, and its native members, Kanhaiya Lal says, were appointed only with the consent of the city's people (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 62, folio 65). One gate held a court, a municipal chamber and a police station at once: police sepoys lived below and behind, and above were rooms for the sittings of the honorary magistrates and the members of the Municipal Committee (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 16, folio 11). A sanitation department was appointed and the bazaars were swept and sprinkled twice a day (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 36–37, folios 39–40); the book dates the first work to 1850 and the paving to 1860, both years uncertain in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 37, folio 40). The tank of a scheme for piped water shattered within an hour for want of concrete, and the scheme was suspended from that day (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 37, folio 40). Municipal drainage once worked in a shrine's favour, filling the stinking tank at Haveli Mian Khan at government expense (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 327, folio 334). Lal gives one series of wages. In the Sikh period a mason earned four annas a day and a labourer two; now a mason earns eight annas and a labourer four, and master masons are paid from one to two rupees a day (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 45, folio 48).

The new public buildings of the British city are dated and costed in the same pages. The kotwali was rebuilt at twice its former size under an Assistant Commissioner whose name, perhaps Hill, is uncertain, in a year printed as 1850 and also uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 369, folio 376). Lawrence Hall was finished in 1862 and Montgomery Hall in a year read as 1866, uncertainly (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 402, folio 409), and the museum dates from 1864 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 402, folio 409). The women's jail is dated 1868, uncertainly (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 409, folio 416), and the government hospital was completed after three years in a year read as 1870, also uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 377, folio 384). The New College was begun in 1872 and finished in five years (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 381, folio 388). The telegraph office was begun in a year read as 1880 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 388, folio 395), and the Art School was begun in 1881 and completed in 1882, both readings uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 384, folio 391). The Medical School was begun in 1881 and completed in a year read uncertainly as 1882 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 429, folio 436; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 430, folio 437).

The costs he prints are these. The Sadr Kachahri cost one lakh of rupees (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 375, folio 382). The Senate Hall was paid for with thirty four thousand rupees from the Nawab of Bahawalpur, a gift recorded in English on a carved stone (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 376, folio 383; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 377, folio 384). The hospital cost one lakh and a number of thousands that cannot be read with certainty (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 377, folio 384), and the New College three lakh twenty thousand rupees of government money (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 384, folio 391). For the Art School forty two thousand were sanctioned, thirty eight thousand spent on the school and four thousand on staff quarters (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 387, folio 394). The telegraph office cost thirty thousand and a further fifteen thousand, forty five thousand in all (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 389, folio 396).

The Medical School cost one lakh and thirty two thousand, the second figure uncertain, with its attached buildings reckoned separately (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 432, folio 439). The government spent thousands of rupees on the first Punjab exhibition (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 402, folio 409) and thousands every year on Government House (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 396, folio 403). Montgomery and Lawrence Halls were raised by subscription, the nobles and officials of the Punjab giving most willingly, and the subscribers' names are engraved twice inside the hall, once in English script and once in Persian (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 399, folio 406; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 400–401, folios 407–408).

He names where the materials came from. The slate of the New College came from the Dalhousie hills, and its upper columns are of black Chiniot stone (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 381, folio 388). Black Chiniot stone runs through all of Nawab Mian Khan's buildings, and he also built a mosque of the same stone at Chiniot (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 272, folio 275; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 329, folio 336). The telegraph office has double tiles of Allahabad, the place name uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 389, folio 396); four iron girders carry the ceiling of the Art School hall (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 386, folio 393) and iron girders stand in the Medical School (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 430, folio 437); and iron sheet covers the truss of Montgomery Hall (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 400, folio 407). The whiteness inside the Mian Mir church was made with marble ground from an old, unnamed building outside the city of Lahore (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 392, folio 399).

The prisons and hospitals of the two regimes are set side by side. Under the Sikhs the kotwali was given nothing by the government to feed its prisoners: every day in the third watch the chained prisoners were sent into the city to beg, one sepoy to every seven, and the sepoy took half of what they were given; punishment was mutilation at the post called Lal Lakkar, hands, ears, noses and feet cut off after five to ten days in the lockup, or else a fine (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 369, folio 376). Under the English the same labour was brought inside the walls. The Central Jail had large workshops for weaving shawls, carpets and a third fabric whose name is uncertain, and a printing press doing work worth lakhs of rupees; the benefit, Kanhaiya Lal writes, was that the prisoners learnt a trade, the government profited, and the prisoners never left the jail (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 410, folio 417). The women's jail took women prisoners from every district of the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 409, folio 416), held women newly arrived from the districts apart for forty days (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 408, folio 415), and had workrooms in which the imprisoned women worked (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 409, folio 416). In the solitary blocks one prisoner lived to a cell under hard labour, with both doors locked at all times, twenty four prisoners to a block (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 407, folio 414).

Race and class were written into the plans: there were buildings for European prisoners and for European women, separate lockups at the kachahri for natives and for European accused, separate buildings for European insane men and women, and railway station rooms graded first, second and third class, with separate seating for memsahibs and for Punjabi and Hindustani third class travellers (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 372, folio 379; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 408, folio 415; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 413, folio 420; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 419–420, folios 426–427; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 421–422, folios 428–429). At the asylum one garden was kept so that the insane could be brought there for recreation (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 420, folio 427). The hospital had a smallpox house, a building for examining the dead, a servants' latrine and a house for destroying bedbugs with an iron tank and a furnace, and it kept two wells, the ancient great well with a wooden Persian wheel and a new small one dug for drinking water (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 380, folio 387).

Buildings changed their uses as the regimes changed. When the army moved to Mian Mir, the empty barracks of the Anarkali cantonment became the district's courts, so that all the kachahris gathered in one place and the district's business was done together (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 371–372, folios 378–379); a hospital became the Munsifi court (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 371, folio 378); and a temporary exhibition became the permanent museum, which it has remained from that day (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 403, folio 410). One Mughal baradari in Wazir Khan's garden passed through European soldiers, the kachahri of the Settlement department, the Telegraph department, the museum's stores and a library, and is still held by the government (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 357, folio 364). The Mughal gardens passed into named hands: Angoori Bagh into the possession of Pandits after Ranjit Singh, the verb of what he did to it uncertain, and Inayat Bagh, its name uncertain, into that of the Sandhanwalia sardars (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 349, folio 356); Bagh Aluwalia to Sardar Fateh Singh Aluwalia, its gardeners now cultivating its land for their own living (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 352, folio 359); Bagh Lala Ratan Chand to Lala Bhagwan Das, with European gentlemen paying rent as tenants in its four towers (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 355, folio 362); and the gatehouse of Bagh Nau Lakha to Sardar Lehna Singh Majithia, who took it as his own residence, and which now houses high officials (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 356, folio 363). The Golian Wali sarai at Ichhra became family property when Jamadar Khushhal Singh petitioned Ranjit Singh for it, and its proprietary possession is now Raja Harbans Singh's (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 366, folio 373).

Khalid carries the story into the twentieth century. Of the colonial city he records that Bhagat Singh, Rajguru and Sukhdev "were hung at Lahore jail" in 1931 (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 48, folio 30). He writes that "Sir Zaffarullah Khan ... drafted the Lahore Resolution which the Muslim League presented in Lahore in March 1940" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 211–212, folio not stated). Before the partition riots, he records, "Tara Singh, a prominent Sikh leader at that time, took out his kirpan on the stairs of the Punjab Assembly in Lahore and said that the time to take revenge for the Gurus had come" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 199, folio not stated). He adds, in a passage on the district's records, that "Till 1972, Chunian district was part of Lahore" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated).
