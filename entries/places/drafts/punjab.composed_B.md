## Saints and Pilgrimage

For Lahori the province is first of all a land of saints' tombs. He includes a saint when his tomb lies in the soil of Punjab: having given the Naqshbandi chain down to Khwaja Baqi Billah of Delhi, he explains that since none of the elders before Khwaja Baqi Billah had a tomb in the land of Punjab, he has left out their detailed accounts and begun with Khwaja Baqi Billah (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 127, folio 115). The other books follow the saints into that land, their orders across it and the people who came to their shrines.

Rizvi makes the Ghaznavid conquest the beginning of the Sufi settlement of the province: "the annexation of the Panjab by Mahmud of Ghazna and its incorporation into his empire prompted many sufis to settle in the area" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112). Khattali, he writes, ordered his disciple Shaikh Husain Zinjani to move to the Punjab, and later sent Hujwiri after him; Hujwiri reached Lahore in about 1035, while Sultan Masud I reigned in Ghazna (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112). Lahori also records how Islam spread in Punjab, from a source his editor distrusts. In the time of Sultan Masud of Ghazni, he writes, citing *Tuhfat al-Wasilin*, Shaikh Muhammad Ismail Muhaddith Lahori, a Bukhari sayyid, settled in Lahore and taught *hadith* and *tafsir*; he was the first of the callers to Islam to come to Lahore, thousands became Muslims at his hands, and through him the religion of Islam spread in Punjab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 191, folio 179).

Two books tell traditions of the Bibis of Bibi Pak Daman. Shemeem Burney Abbas reports, as tradition and with her own hedges, that the Bibi Pak Daman, whose shrine is in Lahore, "involved the men in disseminating Islam among the lay non-Muslim populations in the Panjab", the women speaking to the male scholars and their audiences from behind the veil, and that "seven hundred male scholars came with the Bibi Pak Daman to Hind, as the land was known then" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 161, folio not stated). Kanhaiya Lal continues an account which, he says, the author of the *Hadiqat al-Awliya* gives on the authority of a *Tazkira* whose title is uncertainly printed, of the time when in the year 615 AH the infidel Mongols invaded the Punjab in pursuit of Sultan Jalal al-Din Khwarazmi, and the people of Lahore were put to death for resisting them (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 305, folio 308). That invasion is the setting of the tradition that the Bibis of Bibi Pak Daman prayed to be taken into the earth (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 305, folio 308).

The family of Baba Farid "came from Kabul, but migrated to the Panjab in the middle of the twelfth century" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 156, folio 138), and it was the Ghaznavid Sultan of the Punjab who made Farid's father Qazi of Kahtwal (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 157, folio 139). For Eaton, Baba Farid's settling at Ajudhan was "the religious dimension of a very slow transformation of the Punjab's cities from a Hindu to a Turkish-Islamic orientation" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 217, folio 205). Schimmel writes that in Nizamuddin's time "Sufism became a mass movement in northwestern India", and that "A number of Punjabi tribes still claim to have been converted by Fariduddin Ganj-i Shakar" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 381, folio 350). Of Baba Farid, Waris Shah writes in his invocation: "When Shakarganj made his abode at Pakpattan, the Punjab was delivered of all its troubles" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 2, folio 1). His annotator objects: "Much as one would like to agree with Waris Shah this is not historically correct. The Punjab has had plenty of troubles even after Baba Farid honoured it by making it his home and his place of burial!" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 2, folio 1).

Eaton follows the great shrines into the life of the countryside. From the fourteenth century through the Mughal period, he argues, "in Punjab and Sind, shrines of great Sufis attracted and integrated pre-agrarian and non-Muslim pastoral clans into their ritual, socio-economic, and political orbits"; the descendants of the Sufis married into the leading families of the clans, and the clans were "passing from a life of pastoral nomadism to one of settled wheat agriculture" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 48–49, folios 36–37). The practice of writing amulets for the people, he finds, "has not changed from the thirteenth century to the present; even now the successors of Baba Farid and his assistants, in common with those of other shrines in the Punjab, continue to write ta'widh for the masses" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 218, folio 206). He quotes Major Aubrey O'Brien's essay of 1911 on the Muslim saints of the western Punjab, which he holds to be equally true of medieval shrines: "The general idea of our riverain folk seems to be that the Deity is a busy person, and that his hall of audience is of limited capacity", and "Thus, all human beings require an intervener between them and God" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 216, folio 204). One of his chapters asks how the shrine "served to promote a conceptual conflation of Heaven and Earth among Muslims of rural Punjab" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 17, folio 5).

Rizvi follows the orders, and the wanderers who troubled them, through the province and beyond it. In the age of Mongol domination of Central Asia and Iran, wandering Qalandariyyas and Jawaliqis passed through Multan, where they "often came into conflict with Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya"; "From the Punjab, the displeased qalandars moved to Delhi creating scenes wherever they saw a khanqah. Some went as far east as Bengal" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 325, folio 303). In the time of Makhdum-i Jahaniyan, he writes, the Suhrawardiyya "spread from Uch to Gujarat, the Panjab and Kashmir", reaching even the Delhi of Sultan Firuz Shah Tughluq, whose name the printed text garbles (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 299, folio 277).

The Chishti order spread in Hind and Punjab from Khwaja Muin al-Din Hasan Sijzi of Ajmer, Lahori writes (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 85, folio 73). Boivin writes that the Chishtiyya "is dominant in Punjab and North India" while it was not widespread in Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 217, folio 204), and that it "is usually considered to be one of the most powerful Sufi orders in Northern India and Punjab" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 227, folio 214). Carl W. Ernst and Bruce B. Lawrence name the Punjab among five regions, with Gujarat, Bengal, Uttar Pradesh and the Deccan, where the early saints, and the Chishti masters above all, mark "a particular kind of South Asian spiritual authority that is not only political and religious but also cultural", and where they describe "a spectrum of spiritually charged spaces or sites" that are "devotional loci for Indian Muslims from several classes, and also for Hindus"; they say nothing there that is specific to the Punjab (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 110, folio 97).

Lahori traces how the Qadiri line reached the province. Of that line, whose ancestors had left Baghdad in the time of Hulagu Khan, the first to come to Punjab was Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus Uchchi Jili Gilani, who came by way of Khurasan to Multan and settled at Uch (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 40, folio 28). His modern editor adds, on the authority of Ali Asghar Gilani, that he entered Uch in 887 AH; the date is the editor's, not the author's (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 40, folio 28). By Guru Nanak's day, Rizvi holds, the Qadiris "had made the Panjab the main centre of their activities" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 420, folio 396). The Naushahi order flourished in Punjab through Shah Maruf Chishti and Qadiri, or through Sayyid Mubarak Haqqani, named just before him, for the text does not settle which of the two is meant (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 50, folio 38). The Naushahi way, whose faqirs in Punjab number in the thousands, began with Shaikh Haji Muhammad Qadiri, known as Hazrat Naushah Ganj Bakhsh (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 70, folio 58). The Punjab also drew learned men from afar: in Shah Jahan's reign Abul Khair came from Baghdad touring the countries, and because the climate of the Punjab pleased him he settled in Lahore, where his madrasa stood at Garhi Shahu (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 362–363, folios 369–370).

The Chishtis rose again in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. "The Nizami Chishtis came to prominence in the eighteenth century, principally in Delhi, the Deccan, and Punjab, through masters who were related to but in tension with local political leaders" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 119, folio 106). Shaikh Sulayman Taunsawi, who died in 1850, "consolidated the order by stressing learning through establishment of institutions", among them the academy at Taunsa on the upper Indus and "other religious schools throughout the Punjab"; Muhammad 'Aqil and Gul Muhammad Ahmadpuri, "both of whom founded religious schools in the southern Punjab that provided free instruction and lodging to hundreds of students", did the same (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, pp. 121–122, folios 108–109). In the footnote of Lahori's editor the Chishti Nizami Fakhri line spread most widely in Punjab through Khwaja Sulayman Taunsawi, born in 1184 AH and died in 1267 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 120, folio 108). The line of Shah Mirra, Ernst and Lawrence write, "resurfaces in the nineteenth century in the figure of Hafiz Sayyid Muhammad 'Ali Khayrabadi, marking the expansion of the Nizami branch of Chishti Sufism through disciples of Shaykh Sulayman Taunsawi in the upper Punjab" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 71, folio 57).

Smaller orders had small followings. In Chishti's day the Rasul Shahi order was not numerous: he writes that in all Punjab it had no house but this one, and that only four faqirs of the line were living at the time of writing, with no other house, *takia* or *khanqah*, though he then names one more house near Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 145). He dismisses the claim that the Jalali faqirs are Chihil Tani as belonging to the talk of the ignorant of Punjab (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 623). Of one boy, a *sajjada nashin* foretold that he would be the *subahdar* of *faqr* in Punjab (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 84).

The Punjab is also the home ground of Guru Nanak's journeys as Dhillon retells them. At Haridwar, in an episode dated 1504, Guru Nanak faces "west, in the direction of Punjab" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 79, folio 87), and in the same episode "Punjab is very far away" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 80, folio 88); at Prayag he is called "Nanak, the great Guru from Punjab" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 102, folio 113). By his return to Talwandi in 1506, a year imperfectly printed, "there were very few in the Punjab who did not know of him and his teachings" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 123, folio 137).

On his second journey Guru Nanak "crossed through Rajpurana and the south-west Punjab till he came to Multan" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 164, folio 180); "Nanak was by now a well-known figure in Punjab" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 167, folio 183), and the Sufis of Multan, in Dhillon's telling, had shown him a "patronizing tolerance" as long as he preached elsewhere in the Punjab (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 168, folio 184). In the more modern accounts his third journey, of 1514 to 1516, went as far as Tashkent "and then back to Punjab via the Kashmir Valley" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 55, folio 61). The fourth journey closed, around the time of Babur's third incursion into India, with a south-eastern route back to the Punjab (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 224, folio 246). In the Puratan Janamsakhi the fifth, of 1523 to 1524, "lasted about one year and covered the towns and regions within Punjab" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 55, folio 61).

The death of a saint could move the whole province. At Hazrat Husain's death, Chishti writes, there was hardly anyone in Punjab who did not grieve, and in the mourning for Husain a clamour like that of Karbala rose in Punjab; some words of the passage cannot be read (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 70). Shah Hussein, writes his translator Naveed Alam, "continues to command great reverence in Punjab" more than four hundred years after his death (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 6, folio 6).

The annotator of Waris Shah records the beliefs and devotions of the countryside. "According to popular superstition in the Punjab countryside" *pirs*, *fakirs* and *jogis* can, by the force of their piety, make the Moving Finger of Destiny cancel what it has written (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 31, folio 30). Lighting lamps in a mosque, even with ordinary oil, "is still a well-known way of expression of gratitude to God for His favours", and "Punjabi mothers still promise lamp-lighting in mosques when they pray for their children" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 41, folio 40). Bhang "is a favourite with the mystically inclined, particularly among Muslims and Sikhs", and is still taken "in the old Punjab area by wandering mendicants and many of those seeking spiritual exaltation or sexual pleasure" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 12, folio 11). The verse's dawn at the mosque, he writes, "is how a morning begins even now in a Punjab village" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 5, folio 4).

Not every holy place in the Punjab belongs to a named saint. Pnina Werbner and Helene Basu, introducing Lukas Werth's chapter, point to "the countless shrines to unknown saints found on the outskirts of Punjabi villages", whose anonymity is, in Werth's suggestion, a key feature of "their placement as mediators between the dangerous forces of nature and cultural continuity" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 23, folio 12). Werth himself notes that Khidr "is often called Zinda pir in Punjab, because he also is thought to have disappeared alive" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 101, folio 90). Abbas defines the *dhammal* as "The vigorous ritual dance performed at Sufi shrines in Panjab and Sind" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 180, folio not stated).

Robert Rozehnal shows how a Sufi master's domain could be drawn on the map of the province in the twentieth century. Shahidullah Faridi's last will and testament says that Wahid Bakhsh, whose *khilafat* was publicly confirmed in 1975, "has initiated halqa zikr and guidance in Bahawalpur" and "should spread the silsila in this place only"; Rozehnal reads this: "Here Shahidullah effectively delineates the southern Punjab as Wahid Bakhsh's personal spiritual domain (wilayat)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 90, folio 80). The will itself names only Bahawalpur; the southern Punjab is Rozehnal's reading of it (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 90, folio 80).

In the present the books place the heart of shrine devotion in the south of the province. Khalid "travelled to southern Punjab, believed to be the hub of shrine culture in the country" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated), and writes that "Saeen is a title used to refer to a feudal lord or a spiritual leader from lower Punjab and Sindh, where the shrine culture thrives" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated). "The southern region of Punjab, where Uch Sharif lies, has more traces of religious and cultural syncretism compared to the northern and central region, where there is a greater prevalence of education and hence religious intolerance" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 277, folio 251).

## Communities

The books weigh the meeting of the province's faiths in different ways. Dhillon describes the medieval province as divided: "For over three hundred years, Islam and Hinduism existed side by side in the Punjab region in a state of constant conflict" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 16, folio 18). Eaton observes that a map of South Asia's religious demography shows that "Islam took root much more readily in Bengal or the Punjab than in upper India, the area with the deepest traditions of Indo-Muslim rule and patronage" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 14, folio 2). In both Bengal and the western wing of Punjab and Sindh, he argues, "the growth of Muslim societies correlated with the adoption of sedentary agriculture", and "both regions were still frontier societies where Hindu religious values and the hierarchic social ideals of Brahmin priests had not yet deeply penetrated" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 48–49, folios 36–37). So "by the eighteenth century large communities of Muslim peasants had appeared in both Punjab and Bengal", whereas in India's heartland conversion was far less significant, and "Unlike Punjab or Bengal, Malabar lay beyond the orbit of Mughal imperial influence and the Persianized culture associated with it" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 49, folio 37).

The Jats are at the centre of Eaton's account. He asks "why and how it was that Jat tribesmen, as they abandoned pastoral nomadism for settled agriculture between the thirteenth and nineteenth centuries, gradually took on an Islamic identity" in the western Punjab (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 16, folio 4). Quoting a dissertation then in progress by Emily Hodges, he describes the towns of the western Punjab as living by intensive cultivation along the river flood plains, and beyond them "a vast tract of sparsely populated land, the barr country between the five rivers", "but little cultivated owing to the very scanty rainfall", which supported "pastoral nomadism based primarily on the herding of goats and camels"; the herders moved down to the rivers in the hot dry months and back to the barr after the rains, "never leaving the Punjab plains and covering at the most a distance of less than one hundred miles", in "a symbiotic relationship with the settled peoples of the riverine area" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 223, folio 211). The Jat pastoralists, "before their entry into the Punjab, had not yet been integrated into Hindu society"; "By the time of Baba Farid, however, in the 1260s, we find the first mention of Jats occupying the Punjab proper", Babur "described Jat pastoralist groups in the Sind Sagar doab and Sialkot regions", and "The northward movement of Jat clans and their settlement in the grazing tracts of the Punjab is thus well supported by contemporary evidence" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 224–225, folios 212–213). In Akbar's statistics, Eaton notes, of the 186 Punjabi parganas whose dominant zamindars are known, 82, nearly half, were held by Jat groups; "Only in the extreme western Punjab, in the Sind Sagar doab, where the Janjua Rajputs and Ghakkars dominated, were the Jats conspicuously absent as zamindars" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 225, folio 213). The Jats whom al-Biruni had called low Sudras in the eleventh century had become "the dominant agrarian caste in nearly half of the Punjab", and "by the 1650s, as today, the very word 'Jat' had become virtually synonymous with peasant agriculturalist"; Eaton gives Irfan Habib's explanation, "the introduction of the Persian wheel in the Punjab and its extensive use by Babur's time", which made "much of the arid western Punjab's naturally rich soil capable of supporting the cultivation of food crops" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 225, folio 213).

Conversion followed migration. The Jats' movement north from Sindh to Punjab, their rise from low Sudras to zamindars and their change from herders to farmers were accompanied, in Eaton's argument, by their conversion to Islam, and "of those Jat and Rajput groups that became Muslim in the medieval period, the vast majority of them claim to have been converted either by Baba Farid or by his contemporary, Baha' al-Haqq Zakariya (d. 1263), whose tomb is in Multan city" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 227, folio 215). The Punjabi Muslim clans that claim Baba Farid include the Bhatti, Chhina, Dhudhi, Dogar, Gondal, Hans, Jo'iya, Khokhar, Siyal, Tiwana, Watt, Kharral and Ara'in (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 227, folio 215). In court depositions of 1935 to 1938 before the Lahore High Court, "many of the major endogamous clans that are spread throughout the southwestern Punjab, mainly as peasant agriculturalists" claimed conversion by Baba Farid, among them the Wattu, the Siyal, the Dogar, the Kathia, the Bhatti and the Tiwana (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 241, folio 229). From the genealogies of the Siyals of Jhang District, which run from the late fourteenth to the early nineteenth century, Eaton concludes that "religious conversion among Punjabi clans was very slow indeed", "not only slow, but probably unconscious as well", in "a period long before either British census officials or zealous reformers began urging Indians to place themselves into sharply differentiated religious categories" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 234–235, folios 222–223). Kugle adds the weavers in an endnote: "New Muslims (naw-musalman) from weaver castes converted to join the Islamic community in Punjab slightly later than in Hindustan, where the mystic poet Kabir displays the same kind of Sufi-bhakti synthesis a century earlier" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 329, folio 308).

The province's peoples moved and settled across it. The Mongols who made peace with Jalal al-Din Khalji became Muslims and were settled at the place that came to be called Mughalpura (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 370). Under the Langah sultans, Bhandari writes, Malik Sohrab Dodai, father of Ismail Khan and Fath Khan, came with his tribe from the country of Kech and Makran, and Sultan Husain gave him a *jagir* from the fort of Kot Kardaz to Dhankot, the names uncertainly printed (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 295). Many more Baluch followed, the rest of the country along the Indus was assigned to the Baluch, and little by little the land from Sitpur to Dhankot came to them, so that from those days it has been in the hands of Malik Sohrab's descendants; Bhandari does not say where these places lie (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 295). In another passage he gives the Baluch who had come from Kech Makran a *jagir* from Karor Kot to Mankot (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525).

Famine moved people too. A few years before Kanhaiya Lal wrote, famine fell on Kashmir and thousands of famine stricken Kashmiris came into the Punjab, hundreds of them reaching Lahore itself (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 75, folio 78). The famine of the land of Punjab is remembered in Chishti's life of Mai Bhagi. It came shortly after her marriage at the age of seven, a famine known as the two and a half ser famine, in which the whole country was thrown into confusion and a mother would not look after her son, and because of it Mai Bhagi and her mother left their town, whose printed name is uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 789).

Other communities moved out of the province. Jürgen Schaflechner reports, after Cook, that "The Bhatia and the Khatris, for example, arrived in the eighteenth century from cities in the Punjab", Multan among them, "and were ultimately absorbed as Lohanas", and, after Thakur, that migrants from Rajputana and the Punjab who followed Lohana occupations acquired membership of the Lohana caste (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 80–81, folio not stated). Sorley, for his part, explains the Punjab tale of Suhini and Mehar in Sindh by a migration: "it must have come into Sind through the immigration from the Punjab of those Hindu races which form an important element in the population", as the Census of India volume for 1931 relating to the Bombay Presidency makes clear (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 270, folio 261).

Kanhaiya Lal's Punjab is a country of Hindus and Muslims serving the same state. Under the Mughal *suba* a Hindu amir served as *diwan* of the *suba* of the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 336, folio 343), and in the time of Nawab Zakariya Khan Bahadur, *suba* of Lahore, the brothers Diwan Lakhpat Rai and Jaspat Rai were *diwans* of the government of the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 337, folio 344). Of their wealth he tells that at his son's wedding Lakhpat Rai gave a gift to every Hindu and every Muslim in Lahore, a gift whose printed description, which seems to include a weight of sugar measured in seers, cannot be read with certainty (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 337, folio 344). Khalid writes that Meer Manu "cast the battle between the state and insurgents as a war between Muslims and Sikhs, even though there were several Muslims in the Sikh groups and many non-Muslim soldiers in the army of Meer Manu", and that "The Sikh groups did not control much land but because of their anti-status quo politics the lower rungs of the society supported and protected them" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).

The Muslims of the Punjab under Sikh rule appear through the plea of the Fakir sahibs. Pleading with Ranjit Singh for the mosque by the Baoli, they told the Maharaja that nowhere in the mosques of the whole Punjab did the mullas give the call to prayer in a loud voice, and they offered a bond that the mulla would not give the call (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 148, folio 151). Ernst and Lawrence see Chishti biography take sides in Shaikh Sulayman Taunsawi's time, as British colonialism began to take hold of India: Taunsawi "urged his followers to participate in resistance against Sikh aggression in the Punjab", so that "apparently for the first time in the annals of Chishti history, we have spiritual biographies drawn into partisanship on behalf of one political group"; yet "Notable, however, is the effort of Shah Sulayman to maintain cordial relations with Punjabi Hindus even while he opposed their Sikh neighbors" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 121, folio 108). The province's old ruling class kept its habits into English rule: silk drawstrings went out of use and everyone wears cotton, but the amirs of the Punjab are still accustomed to silk ones (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 47, folio 50).

Other books describe a Punjab in which the boundaries between communities were porous. Schimmel calls it a strange fact, "more visible in Sind than in the Punjab", that Hindus shared in the Muslim orders: "Some saints were claimed by both communities" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 418, folio 387). Shah Abdul Latif of Sindh, she writes, "as did his Panjabi contemporaries and his successors in Sind, accepted the Hindus who were striving on the way to perfection as true pilgrims in the eternal Light" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 267, folio 267). The annotator of Waris Shah describes a village life shared across faiths. Judging by their names some of Hir's friends are Hindus, and "This symbolises the atmosphere of the village in the old Punjab, where the village community as a whole was regarded as one social unit irrespective of caste or creed"; even a sweeper woman would be addressed as daughter, sister or mother (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 20, folio 19). "It may appear strange that a Muslim family should depend on Hindu Brahmans for fixing a marriage but this was by no means unusual in the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 26, folio 25).

Boivin follows the Sikh tradition outward from the Punjab and back. "The Nirguni tradition was more influential in Northern India, especially in Punjab, where the Sikh tradition historically derived from the Nirguni tradition of the Bhakti" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 89, folio 76). He suggests, as a possibility only, that "the Aroras imported this cult from Punjab during their migrations in Sindh in the eighteenth century", the cult being the Nanak Panth, "a form of Sikhism that does not recognize the authority of the Khalsa" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 43, folio 30). In the second half of the nineteenth century, he writes, came the first attempt to rationalise and make uniform the Sikh tradition, and "This process of normalizing the Sikh tradition was centred on Punjab, and it did not concern the Sindhi Nanakpanthis who were heterodox Sikhs" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 53, folio 40).

The land's people appear in the books by caste and clan, and the annotator of Waris Shah sets out the castes and tribes of "the old Punjab". "Ranjha is still a well-known caste of the Jats in the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 3, folio 2). "Khojas are a well-known tribe of Hindu converts to Islam in the old Punjab who are mainly engaged in trade" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 7, folio 6). "The Gujars are a famous tribe of the old Punjab who keep animals and sell their milk", and towns such as Gujranwala, Gujrat and Gujar Khan are named after them (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 19, folio 18). Shackle sets the legend of Hir and Ranjha "in the world of the Jat pastoral tribes of western Panjab" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 20, folio xix).

The annotator and Abbas say almost the same thing of the Siyal. The Siyal tribe, the annotator writes, "is one of the few tribes in the Punjab which allows women to inherit property under customary law" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 20, folio 19). Abbas writes that "The Jats of the Panjab are zamindars (landowners), known for practicing widow-marriage", and that the Siyal tribe "is one of the few tribes in the Panjab that allow women to inherit property under customary law" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 121, folio not stated). Her glossary calls the Siyal, Hir's family, "powerful landowners of the Panjab" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 188, folio not stated), names the Kheras as "A powerful clan of landowners in the Panjab mentioned in the Hir-Ranjha myth" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 183–184, folios not stated), and adds that "Some sections of Indian society, such as the Siyal Jats of the Panjab, permitted a widow to remarry, and she was not forced into satti" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 189, folio not stated). Of the Ara'in, she writes in her text that "The arāĩ in the Panjab were gardeners or small cultivators who worked on their own land" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 108, folio not stated), while her glossary calls them "A caste of landowners in the Panjab" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 178, folio not stated).

Marriage customs fill many of the annotator's notes. "Brides in the old Punjab wore coloured (and colourful) garments", while white clothes were by tradition worn by widows, particularly among the Hindus, and childless Muslim widows wore black or white; "The social pattern is now changed" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 28, folio 27). "A traditional Punjab marriage still retains the symbolism of ‘marriage by capture’", the bridegroom made to look like a general, and the bride's side now resisting only with abusive language (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 28, folio 27). Betrothed boys and girls of "the rural Punjab" do not see each other, and "After the ‘mangni’ or betrothal, a girl does not see her fiancé until marriage" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 48, folio 47). The Arsi "was one of the favourite ornaments of a bride in the old Punjab", and the newly married girl was not expected to do household work for a couple of months, as is "still the case in the less sophisticated parts of the Punjab countryside" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 26, folio 25). The ‘Mehndhi’, a fringe of intertwined strands of hair round the forehead, marks a virgin girl in the old Punjab and disappears at the wedding (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 43, folio 42).

Chishti records marriage customs of his own day. Following the custom of the Muslims of Punjab, the bride's family in the story of the marriage of Hazrat Naushah's daughter demanded a hundred rupees in customary dues (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 484). He writes that the killing of daughters was an old custom among the descendants of Guru Nanak through Lakhmi Chand, who counted themselves the noblest of creatures in Punjab and would give a daughter to no one, because, he explains, the custom of that country is that the girl's father becomes subordinate to the boy's family (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 685).

Of the household and the village the annotator gives many details. "Old houses in the Punjab countryside had a big courtyard with a number of living-rooms and bedrooms round it", and a joint family of brothers living with their parents was quite common (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 16, folio 15). "A spinning-wheel (charkha) used to be the inseparable companion of every woman, old or young, in the rural Punjab", and "The spinning-wheel was an important link in rural society. Women spun cotton yarn, and the village weaver made it into cloth" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 17, folio 16). Spinning parties "were a famous institution of the Punjab village", where young girls, generally unmarried, competed in spinning skill and scandalous conversation (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 4, folio 3, note 14). Printed red scarfs with white flowers were thrown at each other by young girls at play, "A favorite game with young girls in the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 21, folio 20).

Food and offerings follow the same pattern. "Lassi is a favourite drink in the Punjab countryside", and "Washing the hair with curds is still well known in the Punjab countryside" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 4, folio 3). Gur, raw sugar, "is well known as a ceremonial offering in the Punjab countryside", and an invitation accompanied by it cannot be trifled with (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 33, folio 32). Rice cooked in ghee and sprinkled with sugar is the common ceremonial dish, and "The institution is alive in the countryside of the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 23, folio 22). Millet "is even now considered inferior food in the Punjab countryside" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 44, folio 43). Basmati rice "is grown In the central districts of the old Panjab", the best in the subcontinent and now a valuable export from Pakistan (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 26, folio 25). "Sawan and Bhadon are the two rainy months of the year in the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 26, folio 25).

Other customs complete the annotator's picture. "Mauj Din is now a common name In the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 4, folio 3), and "Jugglers and their bears are still a familiar sight in the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 40, folio 39). "Fingers in their mouths is a typical female gesture common in the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 14, folio 13), and "A snub nose is not regarded as a point of beauty either in men or women in the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 39, folio 38). A mother's exaggerated threat "is by no means unfamiliar in the Punjab villages even now" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 14, folio 13). "Dandasa" bark served as the lipstick of "the rural Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 48, folio 47); shaving with soap "is a much later invention in the Punjab", and village barbers still commonly shave with water alone (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 55, folio 54); and the ‘pearha’ is "an article of furniture common in Punjabi homes" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 56, folio 55). The tufted turban "beloved of the rural aristocracy of the Punjab" is a recent development, said to have been invented by the *mirasis*, "or hereditary bards of the Punjab in the late nineteenth century" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 11, folio 10).

The introduction to Rafat's selection reads the province's history into its people. The period of duress after the Mughals "formed the character of the" inhabitants "of the region", and the crisis of character in the Punjabi of today "is traceable to this period of duress in the history of his land" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, pp. 26–27, folios 6–7). To illustrate this lapse it quotes a satirical questionnaire by the columnist Janus, "Are you a Punjabi?", in which the Punjabi response to learning that one's ancestors came from Central Asia is "What the hell! I am a Bokhari and superior to all the low stock inhabiting the Punjab" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 236, folio 216; Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, pp. 237–238, folios 217–218).

Reform movements have pressed on the shrine culture of the countryside. In the early twentieth century Muhammad Ilyas, founder of the Tableeghi Jamaat, "worked in undivided rural Punjab, exhorting Muslims to 'purify' their religious practices which he argued were corrupted due to the influence of the dominant Hindu culture", and Muslim reformists like him "founded reformist movements of their own, aggravating the communal tension in Punjab" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 10, ch. 4, folio not stated). Tableeghi preachers, Khalid writes, "focus on southern Punjab and rural Sindh where syncretistic religion held sway", and "The Tableeghi Jamaat has managed to permeate the social fabric of the major cities of Punjab", so that "shrines similar to the ones at Kamalia and Apal Muri are likely to face a major onslaught due to their 'impure' religious practices, in coming years" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 10, ch. 4, folio not stated). He cites Tahir Kamran: "In recent years, the focus of the Deoband has been on South Punjab and Sindh where there is an ubiquitous saint and shrine culture", and "the people of the South are attracted towards the Deoband school of thought, and feel victimized by the feudal nature of society intertwined with the saint and the shrine culture" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated). The Deobandi school, he adds, "gained popularity in the lower and middle echelons of the urban Punjabi Muslim populace, a feature that they have retained in contemporary Pakistan" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated).

In *Walking with Nanak* Khalid reports the view of Ayesha Siddiqa, whose name is imperfectly printed, that organisations of the Ahl-e-Hadith school, whose names are also uncertainly printed, "have seeped into the social fabric of southern Punjab, a region that has historically rejected such strands of Islam and adopted a syncretistic version of religion. Such Sufi shrines are a symbol of that strand of Islam", and that this is changing as they open seminaries in the rural areas and draw in unemployed young men (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 284, folio 258). A recent paper by Tahir Kamran on Deobandi Islam in the Punjab, he adds, finds such organisations popular with the young because "they offer an explanation for their current oppression", and their first step is "to target such shrines and their guardians, who have become immensely rich feeding off the oppressed classes" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 284, folio 258).

Partition changed who lived beside whom. Locals, Khalid finds, "generally tend to be more embracive of practices 'non-Muslims'" than the migrants who came after the division of the Punjab, and he has "seen several gurdwaras and temples being taken over and destroyed by the migrants", while where the population is largely indigenous "such 'non-Muslim' religious spaces are better looked after", with exceptions (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated). "Compared to Central Punjab, Southern Punjab has been historically tolerant towards other non-Muslim faiths, which is why a significant Hindu population continues to live here" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 31, folio not stated).

Khalid's account of the province's religious minorities today is drawn from his own travels. Of Pakistan's non-Muslims other than the Ahmadiyya, "the majority are Hindus living in the 'faraway' 'peripheral' regions of Southern Punjab, inner Sindh and Baluchistan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 12, folio not stated). In Khalid's words, "unlike the Hindus, the Christian community has a formidable presence in the Punjab", and Hindus "are a smaller community in the Punjab and can therefore be ignored" by political parties; most Hindus are scattered in other provinces, "with only a few in Punjab. The Christians on the other hand have an overwhelming majority in the Punjab (among the minorities)" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 87, folio not stated). "The religious distinctions between Hindus and Christians have become blurred in urban Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 28, folio not stated). "A lot of Hindus in Punjab do this, passing off as Muslims or Christians by taking up non-Hindu names. This is a survival technique in a hostile environment" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 39, folio not stated), and where minorities in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa are visible in society, that is "hardly the case in Punjab, which is much more monolithic" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated). After the demolition of the Babri Mosque on 6 December 1992, "Well-known Hindus living in the cities of Punjab had to hide for several months after the incident" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 22, folio not stated).

In most villages of the province, Khalid finds, Christians are confined to menial work. At Shanti Nagar, where Christians own the land, he finds "a unique case study in the social fabric of the Punjab. Whereas in most of the villages across the province, Muslims are the dominant landowners, while Christians are confined to menial jobs associated with the untouchable caste" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 123, folio not stated); in an ordinary village, he writes, they would be treated as untouchables (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 124, folio not stated). He lists the attacks at Sangla Hill in 2005 and at Gojra and Bahmniwala, Kasur, in 2009, "all of which happen to be located in Punjab", and adds that "The story of a few burnt pages from the Quran found in local mosques seems to happen often" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 125, folio not stated). An informant he calls Daria Boatwala, who moved to Lahore in the 1980s, tells him: "here in the Punjab, the situation is much different. There are hardly any Zoroastrians here and I have never come across any Hindu. The only Christians that you see are those who are cleaners and sweepers" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 142, folio not stated).

Khalid also follows the province's Sikhs and Nanak Panthis. Nanak Panthi Sikhs, in Khalid's account, "belong to the areas of South Punjab and Sindh", with temples "in Sindh and Southern Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 197, folio not stated), and the langar workers at Dera Sahib are "low-caste Nanak Panthis from Southern Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 201, folio not stated). Of the devotees of Guru Nanak who did not join Guru Gobind Singh's Khalsa, he writes: "There are thousands of such Sikhs living in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Punjab and Sindh in Pakistan, and they are referred to as Nanak Panthi Hindus. In their temples, along with pictures of Hindu deities, one also finds a picture of Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 98, folio 80). At the November 2011 festival at Nankana the kirtan singers "have covered their heads with a loose turban, a popular style in the villages of West Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 230, folio not stated).

Many of the Sikhs Khalid meets in the province are Pathans who came from the tribal areas: "Moving from tribal areas to the heart of Punjab, an overwhelming majority of them could not even speak the language. They remained confined to their little enclaves" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 203, folio not stated). The Sikhs of Nankana "remember this internal migration from KPK to Punjab", which gives "a good picture of the different socio-economic conditions in both of these provinces" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 235, folio not stated). Boota Singh of Nena Kot, who told him that his people were not from the Tribal Areas but Punjabis, was "the first Pakistani Punjabi Sikh I had interacted with. Earlier, I had only encountered Pathan Sikhs who had moved to Punjab in the past few years" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 83, folio 65).

The modern books look at the hardships of the countryside. Omar Kasmani writes that "Being a child in a poor rural family in central Punjab, one who is regarded as intersex means being given to particular bodily horizons, oriented to certain futures more than others", and that the most likely path for such a child would have led to a life among the *khwaja-sara* (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 73, folio 56). He cites Lubna Chaudhry's study of women and poverty in parts of Sindh and Punjab, which finds that "unmarried girls in their teens and early twenties are most vulnerable", pointing to "discriminatory food portions within the family and limited access to health care" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 191, folio 174).

The province's shared festivals belong to its farming year. "Punjab, still predominantly an agricultural society, upholds agrarian culture and traditions, of which the harvest season is the ultimate source of happiness", Khalid writes, but in the days after the creation of Pakistan, among the country's elite, "several cultural festivals like Vaisakhi, Lohri and Basant were given up" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). "Lohri like Vaisakhi or Basant, is an ancient festival of the Punjab that commemorates the change of season", kept "on the 13th of January, which is generally the last night of the month of Poh", with a village bonfire, dry fruit, folk songs, children collecting wood and kheer left to cool overnight (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 192–193, folio not stated). After the creation of Pakistan "this indigenous festival of the Punjab died down", and "the Sikh and the Hindu minorities of central Punjab also stopped this celebration" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 193, folio not stated). It was "earlier celebrated on a large scale throughout the province. It is ironical though that this Punjabi tradition shunned by Punjab is now being revived by a group of Pathan men residing in Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 194, folio not stated).
