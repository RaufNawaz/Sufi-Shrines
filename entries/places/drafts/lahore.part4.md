## History

In his description of the suba of Lahore, Sujan Rai Bhandari calls the city "لاهور مصریست متقدمین برکنار دریاے راوی": an ancient city on the Ravi, whose founding he attributes to "لو", adding of its name that "دربعضی تواریخ لهور و لهاور نیز مینویسند" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525). Haroon Khalid, in the present day, records the tradition that "Lahore and Kasur are said to be named after Luv and Kush", and gives Parsuni as the old name of the Ravi flowing next to the city (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 71, folio not stated).

In Bhandari's telling the city fell into ruin over long ages and little trace of it was left, so that "دار الحکومت این ولایت شهر سیالکوت گردید"; after Sultan Mahmud's conquest, Malik Ayaz rebuilt the city and built a pakka fort (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525). Mahmud's ninth campaign, in the year 412 as the printed text gives it with an uncertain reading, failed before a fort in Kashmir, and he then "بجانب لاهور وبانگر روان شد غنیمت فراوان بدست آورده بافتح وظفر بسعادت معاودت نموده" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 414). A passage of the city description, damaged in the printed text, names Khusraw Malik and records that the descendants of the Sultan "کرده لاهور را دار السلطنت کردند و تا سی و هشت سال دار الحکومت اولاد سلطان بود" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 524–525).

Bhandari's account of the last Ghaznavids makes Lahore their refuge. Khusraw Shah son of Bahram Shah, driven from Ghazni by Ala al-Din Husain Ghuri, turned to Hindustan, took Lahore, and governed the Punjab until his death, which came in the year 555 "درخطه لاهور" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 405). His son Khusraw Malik succeeded him; Shihab al-Din raided Lahore again and again and wore him down, and "مدت حکومت او درلاهور بست وهشت سال" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 405). On Shihab al-Din's third campaign, in 575, Khusraw Malik, who held the government of Lahore, sent his son with an elephant as peshkash and made peace, and the Sultan fixed a yearly tribute (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 404). On the fifth, in 580, the Sultan besieged the city, Khusraw Malik shut himself in the fort, and the Sultan plundered the country round about, "سلطان نواحی لاهور غارت کرده" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 404); Bhandari's description of the suba says of the same year that Shihab al-Din "بقصد تسخیر لاهور امده محاصره کرد و بران دست نیافت" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). On the sixth, in 582, Khusraw Malik submitted, was taken to Ghazni and died there, and the Sultan "ودرلاهور نایب خود نصب کرد" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 404). In this list of campaigns the digits of all three years are uncertain in the printed text. Later, when the Khokhars rose in the country round Lahore, the Sultan came from Ghazni to the city, Aibak joined him there from Delhi, and after the punishment the Sultan left Lahore for Ghazni (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 401).

Under the first Delhi sultans the city changed hands repeatedly. Aibak came from Delhi to Lahore and became sovereign there in 603, a year uncertain in the printed text, and later fought Yildiz over the city (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 400–401). Aram Shah was enthroned in 607, again uncertain in the printed text, "درخطه دل کشاۓ لاهور" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400). The translator of Madho Lal Hussain's verse writes that Qutbuddin Aibak "died playing polo in Lahore in AD 1210" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 185, folio 185). When Iltutmish came, Aram Shah moved out of Lahore toward Delhi and fled after a brief fight (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 399). Sultan Jalal al-Din, routed by Chinggis Khan, came and besieged Lahore, and afterwards fled toward Sind and Siwistan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 399).

The Mongols came next. In Bahram Shah's reign, Bhandari writes, "روز دوشنبه شانزدهم جمادی الآخر سنه تسع وثلثین وستمائه افواج مغول[OCR?] چنگیزی آمده لاهور را محاصره کردند" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 395). The governor, Malik Qaraqash, finding the people unwilling to stand together, slipped out of the city at midnight and went to Delhi, and "شهر لاهور از ستم چنگیز خانیان خراب ونابود شد وخلق کثیر اسیر وگرفتار گشت" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 395). The wazir Nizam al-Mulk, sent toward Lahore to drive the Mongols off, turned the amirs against the Sultan on the bank of the Beas near Sultanpur, and they marched on Delhi instead (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 395). In Balban's reign the Mongols raided the country round Lahore and Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 385). In Kaiqubad's reign they reached the country round Lahore again, laid waste its towns and villages, killed its people, carried off women and children and burnt its settlements; the sultan's army met them there, defeated them, and brought the captives before him at Delhi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 380). Bhandari names only the environs here, and says nothing of the city's monuments or of any saint (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 380).

Through the fifteenth century Bhandari's Lahore is a prize fought over by the Khokhars, the Sayyid sultans and the Lodis. Timur's grant to Khizr Khan is headed by "ایالت لاهور و ملتان و دیپالپور" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 331). Shaikh Ali reached Lahore, took its fort and left twelve thousand picked horsemen to hold it (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 325); later he plundered round Jalandhar and brought many captives into Lahore, and Jasrath came from Jalandhar and besieged the city, lifting the siege when the wazir Malik Sarwar reached Samana (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 326). After Mubarak Shah drove Shaikha Khokhar beyond the Chenab, he camped at Lahore, resettled with reassurance those whom Shaikha's oppression had ruined, and ordered the fort repaired: "در آبادی ان مصر توجه بر گماشته حکم ترمیم و تعمیر قلعه فرموده" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 327). Shaikha besieged the city a second time without success, turned on Kalanaur, and killed Raja Bhim Jammuwal, who had come out to help the governor of Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 327). In the year of Nasir al-Din Mahmud Shah's accession an army was sent against Shaikha, who had seized Lahore; he was beaten twelve kos from the city and fled to the hills, and the country round Lahore was rid of the trouble (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 334). Letters were sent "با اهل ملتان و لاهور" ordering the Firuz Shahi slaves to be killed, and massacres followed in most places (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 336). In Ala al-Din's reign Malik Bahlul acted independently from Lahore and Dipalpur as far as Panipat (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 323); Sultan Muhammad Shah gave him the title of Khan Khanan and "ولایت لاهور و دیپالپور" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 324), and under Hamid Khan's vizierate Sirhind, Sunam, Lahore, Dipalpur and Hisar Firuza were taken by force one by one (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 320). Bhandari's description of the city has "تاتارخان از امراے سلطان بهلول لودی دار الحکومت نمود" after a time when no sultan of Hind lived there and the city declined (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 524).

Harish Dhillon's retelling of the Janamsakhis sets Guru Nanak's second journey in this Lodi Lahore, which "had by now taken on the position of a pre-eminent" town of the empire north of Delhi, and whose governor, Daulat Khan Lodhi, "had risen in rank to become the governor of Lahore and the adjoining areas" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 124, folio 138), and was away in Delhi at the time (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 125, folio 139). In Dhillon's story of the needle, an evening gathering for the Guru drew a large turnout (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 125, folio 139); "Duni Chand, the amil (revenue collector) of Lahore", kept a haveli at the centre of the town, and "it was set in a large compound of its own" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 125–127, folios 139–141). Seven red pennants flew over it: "Each flag stands for a lakh of rupees" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 128, folio not stated). Nanak gave him a needle to return "when we meet next in the other world" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 129, folio 143), and Duni Chand and his wife became disciples and he gave away his "seven lakh rupees" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 134, folio 148). Dhillon's Nanak then went from Lahore to Sialkot (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 136, folio 151).

Daulat Khan Lodi turned against Sultan Ibrahim and went over from Lahore to Babur, in a sentence Bhandari's printed text leaves broken off (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 311). Babur's fourth expedition from Kabul, in 930 as the printed year is read with some doubt, reached as far as Lahore and Dipalpur; before his last campaign Daulat Khan and Ghazi Khan wrote from Lahore asking him to come, Daulat Khan himself set out from Lahore for Kabul, and once in revolt the two meant to fight the imperial amirs who had already reached Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 304). Khalid writes that Babur, attacking the Punjab, destroyed "the cities of Eminabad and Lahore on the way" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 15, folio not stated).

After the defeat of Rana Sanga, Babur gave Prince Kamran Lahore and Multan in jagir, and Kamran came from Kabul to Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 298); once there he summoned Lashkar Khan from Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 294). Bhandari's description of the city has it flourish while "کامران مرزا خلف حضرت بابر بادشاه" lived there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 524). Muhammad Zaman Mirza came from Gujarat by the desert toward Lahore and stirred up trouble (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 290). After his second defeat Humayun came to Lahore and held a council with his brothers, each of whom spoke against his wishes, and Kamran left with Askari for Kabul (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 286). After Kanauj the defeated emperor could not stay at Agra or Delhi, rested a few days at Lahore and went on toward Multan and Thatta, while Sher Khan's men rode as far as Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 269).

Islam Shah, Bhandari writes, came to Lahore with guns dragged by men, "هر توپ را هزار دو هزار آدم می کشیدند" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 260–261). Once there he reasoned "چون لاهور شهریست بزرگ در اندک فرصت تجملات بادشاهی و سامان لشکرها و سپاه فراوان و یراق و سلاح و دیگر اسباب جنگ بهم میرسد و محل در آمدن مغل از کابل است", and meant "آن شهر را خراب کرده" and to make Mankot his capital, "لیکن این اراده او صورت نه بست" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 260). Azam Humayun, called Haibat Khan, raised the standard of revolt at Lahore, though Bhandari does not state his office there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 263). On Humayun's return with Prince Muhammad Akbar and three thousand horse, the Afghans scattered and "لاهور بے جنگ تصرف اولیائے دولت در آمد" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 256). At Humayun's death Sultan Sikandar came out of Mankot toward Lahore to stir up trouble, in a sentence with a gap in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 252). At the accession at Kalanaur, Shah Abu al-Maali was arrested and sent to Lahore, and escaped "از غفلت کوتوال" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 237).

The translator of Madho Lal Hussain writes that "Akbar had temporarily shifted his capital to Lahore" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 7, folio 7). In Bhandari, Muhammad Hakim Mirza, in revolt at Kabul, crossed the Indus again and again, and once "بلاهور رسیده بست ودو روز قلعه را محاصره نموده", while Raja Bhagwant Das, the subadar of Lahore, held it firmly, and the Mirza withdrew without gaining his end after Kunwar Man Singh defeated him (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 203). While the court was at Lahore, Zain Khan Koka was sent against the hill zamindars (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 170) and Rustam Mirza was received a stage from the city (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 175); grain boats went "از لاهور و ملتان" to the army before Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 174). Todar Mal stayed behind in Lahore when the court went to Kashmir and died there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 181). After the emperor's fall from an elephant, rumour spread, the revenue went unpaid and the roads grew unsafe, until "چون لاہور مورد رایات عالی گشت این شورش فرو نشست" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 181). Bhandari's description of the city credits Akbar with "قلعه متین(۱) شهر پناه و دولتخانه", Jahangir with "عمارت عالیه که بالفعل موجود است", the princes and amirs with mansions, Asaf Khan's above all, and Shah Jahan's reign with further growth (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 524).

Jahangir's reign opened with Khusrau's revolt. Khusrau and Hasan Beg Badakhshi plundered their way to Lahore, where the diwan Abd al-Rahim joined them (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 146); the subadar, Dilawar Khan, would not meet them and made the fort fast, and "شاهزاده هر چند سعی کرد قلعه لاهور بدست نیامد" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 146). News of Khusrau's capture reached the emperor in Kamran Mirza's garden at Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 145). Hasan Beg was sewn into an ox hide and Abd al-Rahim into a donkey hide and paraded in the city, and from Kamran Mirza's garden to the gate of the palace Khusrau's companions were impaled in two rows while crowds came out of the city to watch; Khusrau was led between the stakes on an elephant and then imprisoned in the fort (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 145). Khalid writes that Khusro "rebelled against his father and moved towards Lahore" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 255, folio 229), that Guru Arjan "was summoned to Lahore by Emperor Jahangir" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 234, folio 208) and died there, and that Chandu Lal was "appointed as the Diwan of Lahore by the Mughal authorities" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 253, folio 227). Sikh tradition, he adds, traces the quarrel between Prithi Chand and Guru Arjan to the Guru's trip to Lahore (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 274, folio 248).

At Jahangir's death, Bhandari writes, Shahryar was in Lahore and laid hands on the treasuries and workshops (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 107). When Dawar Bakhsh, set up by Asaf Khan, reached the country round Lahore, Shahryar's ranks broke at the first shock, and he took refuge in the fort, where he was brought out with his hands tied by his own waistband, imprisoned and, two days later, blinded (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 107). By Shah Jahan's orders Dawar Bakhsh, his brother Garshasp, Shahryar, Tahmuras and Hoshang were put to death in Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 107). The dead emperor's body was sent to Lahore and buried by the Ravi at Shahdara (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 106–107). Shah Jahan's coinage and khutba were then fixed "در ایوان خاص و عام دولتخانه والا واقعه دارالسلطنت لاهور", on a Sunday in Jumada al-Awwal of the year 1037, the day and year uncertain in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 106).

The war of succession brought Dara Shikoh to Lahore. He left Delhi for the city meaning to seize "خزانه بادشاهی که یک کرور[OCR?] روپیه موجود است", with the arsenal and the artillery, to raise an army (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 77). He entered the imperial fort, took the treasury, arsenal, artillery and workshops "که در لاهور بود", and gave freely, and about twenty thousand horsemen gathered to him, the number uncertain in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 77). Men joined him at Lahore, among them Khanjar Khan, faujdar of Bhera and Khushab; Raja Rajrup left a son and agent there, who slipped away by night, and Guru Har Rai left the same way (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 76). Khalid writes that after his defeat Dara "decided to head west to Lahore and continue his fight", and that Guru Har Rai, "along with his forces, reached Lahore" and met him there (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 210–211, folios 184–185). When the imperial army approached, Dara could not stay, and left for Multan carrying "جمیع ذخایر و خزاین لاهور از اشرفی و روپیه و طلا و نقره غیر مسکوک", more than a crore of rupees, with the workshops' goods and most of the artillery (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 68). Khalilullah Khan and Raja Jai Singh sent officers to secure what was left and for the "نگاهبانی[OCR?] شهر لاهور که از حاکم خالی است و استمالت سکنه" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 67–68). The two commanders camped outside the city on the day of Eid al-Adha and, ordered not to enter Lahore, moved to Ichhra (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 67). On his own return Aurangzeb camped at Ichhra, received there the amirs who were in Lahore, and on the next day rode an elephant toward the city (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 65); he paused at the gate of the fort to inspect it, dismounted at the Wazir Khan mosque and prayed the midday prayer there in congregation, and camped in the Shalamar garden, staying some days to settle the suba (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 64). During this stay Khalilullah Khan was made governor of Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 64).

Khalid carries the story on into the eighteenth century. He calls Lahore "the prize possession of Punjab" in Ahmad Shah Abdali's wars: on "the 1751 attack by Ahmad Shah Abdali", the Afghan "had every intention of capturing Lahore", and "the forces of Ahmad Shah Abdali reached Lahore, and laid a siege on it. For months the citizens were trapped within the walls of their city" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). His forces raided the villages around the city for supplies, and "an informant from within the city" told him of the Sikh village of Maraka "a few kilometres from Lahore" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). Khalid writes that "the Governor of Lahore, Moin-ul-Mulk also known as Meer Manu" ordered the killing of Sikhs (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 77, folio 59). In 1799, he writes, Ranjit Singh "was greeted enthusiastically by a population sick of the lawlessness of that time" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated).

Of the colonial and modern city Khalid records that Bhagat Singh, Rajguru and Sukhdev "were hung at Lahore jail" in 1931 (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 48, folio 30); that "Sir Zaffarullah Khan ... drafted the Lahore Resolution which the Muslim League presented in Lahore in March 1940" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 211–212, folio not stated); and that before the partition riots "Tara Singh, a prominent Sikh leader at that time, took out his kirpan on the stairs of the Punjab Assembly in Lahore and said that the time to take revenge for the Gurus had come" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 199, folio not stated). He adds, in a passage on the district's records, that "Till 1972, Chunian district was part of Lahore" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated).

## Holy Places

Bhandari's city is thick with places of prayer: "درهر کوچه و بازار مساجد بسیار از بسیار است" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 524). Beside Hujwiri, he writes, "دیگر نزدیکان جناب صمدیت دران شهر آسوده اند", though he names none of them, and "درحواشی شهر فراوان باغ دل کشا و هزاران گلشن فرحت افزا است" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 523). In his description of the rivers, the Ravi "رونق افزاے دار السلطنت لاهور میشود و پایان عمارت بادشاهی شاهگذر واقع است", and the royal canal drawn from it runs to "باغ شالامار واقع لاهور" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 512). Aurangzeb, entering the city after the war of succession, prayed in congregation at the Wazir Khan mosque and camped in the Shalamar garden (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 64). Khalid adds that the Wazir Khan Mosque was "constructed in the seventeenth century by Wazir Khan, Mughal Emperor Shahjahan's governor of Lahore" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).

The fort and its gates stand at the centre of the Sikh memory of the city. Guru Arjan's torture took place "Outside the Alamgiri gate of the Lahore fort" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 195, folio not stated). Khalid writes that Guru Ram Das "was also born in present day Pakistan, in the city of Lahore", that "Guru Arjan spent a lot of time in the same city, where he was assassinated and his remains interred", and that Guru Hargobind "also spent a lot of time here" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated). The domes of the Badshahi Masjid face "the Alamgiri gate of the Lahore Fort, named after the imperial title of the Mughal Emperor Aurangzeb" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 205, folio not stated). "The Roshani gate, one of the thirteen gateways that lead into the ancient walled city of Lahore, is between the Alamgiri Gate and the Royal Mosque"; it "was once reserved for the prince and the princess, who would go from the Fort to the city through this entrance", and lamps "were lit all along the passageway", hence its name (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 205, folio not stated).

Khalid's city has temples as well as shrines. In 2008 he began to visit "an obscure monument around Lahore and write about it", and found "a Hindu temple, lying in the middle of a congested locality in the heart of Lahore", which he does not name (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 9, folio not stated). The Neela Gumbad Valmiki Mandir stands in "the historical market of Anarkali" and serves the city's Balmiki Hindus (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 66, folio not stated). He lists the Sitla Mandir at Shah Alami, the Jain Mandir at Anarkali and the Bhadra Kali Mandir at Thokar Niaz Baig among the city's prominent temples, with the Krishna Mandir on the Ravi road and the Valmiki Mandir at Neela Gumbad among its functional ones (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 21–22, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* he writes: "In Lahore, I found that Muslims and Christians, whose ancestors were once Hindus, continue to visit a Hindu temple their ancestors visited" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 32, folio 14).

The smaller sacred places of the modern city appear in Khalid's fieldwork. A shrine of cats, the "Billiyon Wala Mazaar", stands "next to Bhatti gate", one of the entry points of the walled city, and he writes that "The fact that the shrine is located in the heart of Lahore, a major metropolitan city, is probably why this practice has diminished much faster" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated). He describes "this historical graveyard situated in Sultan Pura, the heart of Lahore", seat of the shrine of Ghore Shah, where families gathered at graves on a Sunday morning while children played cricket, and where junkies "often end up finding a permanent abode in this land of the dead" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated). In Amar Sadhu, an old village now inside the Defence Housing Authority, stand Gurdwara Bibi Kaulan and the shrine of Baba Mastan Shah (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated). He opens a chapter on Abdali's siege with "a small shrine in the outskirts of Lahore raised by the villagers there" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).

The churches, the Baha'i centre and the graveyards of the city's other communities complete the landscape. On Mall Road, "the Lahore Cathedral built in the late nineteenth century is a model of the Gothic architectural tradition" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 100, folio not stated). Masjid Shuhada at the Regal Chowk junction is "a small white structure with an open courtyard", which on Fridays is "teeming with pilgrims", with mats spread on the road for "hundreds of men" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 100–101, folio not stated). The Baha'i assembly meets at the Baha'i Centre "near the Ganga Ram Hospital" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 149, folio not stated). There is no Tower of Silence in Lahore, and "the Parsi community buries its dead in a graveyard next to the Minar-e-Pakistan facing the ancient walled city of Lahore" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 144, folio not stated). The translator of Madho Lal Hussain places his saint's shrine in "Baghbanpura, Lahore" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 6, folio 6), and speaks of the shrines of the city as a whole: "I might not have taken up this project if it hadn't been for Ida Sofie Matzen who took me along to the shrines in Lahore while doing her doctoral research." (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 188, folio 188).

## Communities

Bhandari's Lahore is a gathering of peoples: craftsmen of every land live there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 524). Khalid's Lahore is a city of minorities. He counts "the five religious communities living in and around Lahore (Hindus, Christians, Sikhs, Baha'is and Zoroastrians)" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 9, folio not stated), and elsewhere "the four religious minorities living in Lahore (Hindu, Sikh, Christian and Baha'i)" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 10, folio not stated). His project on "religious minorities in Lahore" "officially began in September 2010" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 10, folio not stated).

Partition runs through the memories he gathers. An informant he calls Lal "was a teenager when partition riots spread in Lahore", and his grandfather "refused to leave his ancestral home" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 22, folio not stated). Bishna Ram recalls that friends and relatives told his family "that Lahore was no longer safe for us" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 67, folio not stated), while Shazia Waleed's father was "a prominent Hindu businessman from Lahore who continued living here despite partition" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 77, folio not stated). Mary, an eighty three year old widow, recalls that her family lived "near Shahjamal on the canal that now divides Lahore into two parts", that near partition "we would frequently sight mutilated bodies floating in the water", and that her mother made the children wear a cross to show "to anyone who would come asking for our religion" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 132, folio not stated). A Protestant poet tells him: "We Christians were living here before Lahore became a part of Pakistan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 128, folio not stated). Khalid notes that Alfred Woolner's statue outside the old campus of the University of the Punjab is "one of the few statues of pre-partition Lahore that has survived", "defying the iconoclastic streak of the country which emerged post 1947" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 100, folio not stated).

The Hindus of Lahore are few and careful. Prem Gupta, "a 40 year old Hindu activist from Lahore" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 46, folio not stated), lives in a Muslim neighbourhood where "theirs are the only Hindu houses. Unlike the majority of the Hindus, living in Lahore, they have refused to take up dual names" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 48, folio not stated). He tells Khalid: "If you want to see the lights of Diwali, go to Peshawar. You will find nothing in Lahore" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated). "A lot of prominent Hindus from Lahore went into hiding during the wars" of 1965 and 1971 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 74, folio not stated), and after the sixth of December 1992 the city's temples were attacked, and its functional temples on the Ravi road and at Neela Gumbad "were also burned down" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 22, folio not stated); Shami Lal, "Another Hindu from Lahore", shut his business for several months (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 22, folio not stated). The Neela Gumbad temple "serves a small community of Balmiki Hindus in Lahore, numbering only a few hundred, most of whom are Christians on record" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 66, folio not stated). Pilgrims went from the city to Katas in 2010 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 47, folio not stated), and in 2011 the Neela Gumbad priest took "a delegation of three hundred people from Lahore and neighbouring regions" to Ram Thamman (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 63–64, folio not stated).

Christians in Khalid's Lahore meet an old prejudice. "Even in prominent cities like Lahore, several Muslims refuse to eat with Christians and consider utensils used by them to be impure", and "A lot of low end hotels and restaurants not only in rural areas but even in metropolitan cities like Lahore have separate utensils for Christians" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 90, folio not stated). Colonel Chaudary, "a high-caste Christian who resides in Lahore", found that Muslim labourers in his house refused his cook's food "because of the impurity associated with eating at a Christian's house" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 91, folio not stated). Khalid reports that "a school principal in Lahore was accused of blasphemy" over a multiple choice question that listed his own name beside the Prophet's (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 104, folio not stated). In *In Search of Shiva* Khalid reports that "an angry mob of Muslims in Lahore had burned down 170 Christian houses", the rage "ignited by an argument between two friends, a Christian and a Muslim, while they were enjoying their evening drinks", while "the 'blasphemous' Christian was already in police custody" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated). The missionary colleges belong to this story: "the Forman Christian College Lahore, one of the oldest colleges set up by Christian missionaries" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 48, folio not stated), "one of the first English-medium institutes to be set up here, during the latter half of the nineteenth century" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 102, folio not stated).

The Sikhs, once the city's rulers, are now rare in it: "it is now rare that one runs into a Sikh in this city, a city which was once ruled by a Sikh and the seat of their political strength" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 196, folio not stated). Ranjit Singh, Khalid writes, built "a mosque at a mohalla called Gul Begum in Lahore", and Fakir Azizudin's "family still lives in Lahore in their ancestral house, located inside the walled city" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated). An informant he calls Kartar was refused service at a restaurant in Model Town because "they did not have 'our' utensils" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 186, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* Khalid lists Lahore among the cities whose Sikh communities have "Only recently" been able "to exert themselves" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 88, folio 70), and meets Ghulab Singh, "the first Sikh police officer in the revamped traffic police of Lahore" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 35, folio 17).

The Parsis are "microscopic" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 136, folio not stated). Farah Desai, eighty two, recalls that "At the time of partition, there were quite a few Parsis ... living in Lahore and Amristar", "all well to do", most of whom later emigrated; "There are only a handful of us left here" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 136, folio not stated), and she counts "only thirty five Parsis left in Lahore" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 141, folio not stated). Sadaf, born after partition, grew up in "a Lahore that was different from the multi-religious city that Farah grew up in", where the sari "had become the symbol of an Indian woman and wasn't encouraged in Lahore any more" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 138–139, folio not stated). Daria Boatwala, who moved to the city in the eighties, finds that "Lahore is much more monolithic and I would say religiously conservative" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 142, folio not stated). "Because of no priest there is no way to perform a traditional Parsi marriage in Lahore. The same applies for the rites after death" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 144, folio not stated).

"The LSA at Lahore was established before partition" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 149, folio not stated), and about "forty odd people" attend its meetings "out of the one hundred and fifty Baha'is registered with the LSA" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 147, folio not stated). Usman Nawaz, a Baha'i of Shahdara, was "eventually forced to resign because of the hostile attitude of his colleagues" at the Sohrab Cycle Factory after a Muslim worker injured him with a stone, and the management "were afraid to take any action" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 152–153, folio not stated). "On 28th May 2010, two houses of worship of the Ahmadiyya community in Lahore were attacked, resulting in the death of 94 people and all political leaders refrained from visiting the site or sympathizing with the community" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 152, folio not stated).

Among the Muslim majority Khalid observes the rise of a public piety. In 1935, he writes, "The Deobands, who by now had established themselves as a formidable religious force in the city", opposed the first Milad procession (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). In his own day, the walkers in "a quintessential middle-class park, located in a middle-class locality, in the heart of Lahore" carry "rosary beads", and some play "an Arabic recitation of the Holy Book, along with its Urdu translation, a public display of their religiosity" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 7, ch. 1, folio not stated). A friend from Mumbai, by contrast, said: "I was surprised to see that hardly any women in Lahore were wearing a burqa" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated). At the Lahore University of Management Sciences "there have been rumours about the religious right slowly dominating the administration of the university" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated). On the national day of protest against a film, at the old village of Hanjarwal, "Temporary gallows had been erected on the road", and "An effigy, dressed in a shirt with the US flag on it, was being dragged to the gallows" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 7, ch. 1, folio not stated); days earlier, "a group of lawyers along with members of religious parties had attacked the US consulate in Lahore, taking down its flag and burning it" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 7, ch. 1, folio not stated). Raiwind is "a small village in the outskirts of Lahore", and during its annual congregation the cars heading there cause a traffic jam at a junction Khalid regularly uses (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 10, ch. 4, folio not stated).

## Shrines, Endowments and the State

For Bhandari the state is the city's builder and keeper. Under Aurangzeb the Ravi turned toward the city and damaged its buildings and gardens; in the fourth regnal year an embankment was ordered, "بدرازی ده کروه", which he calls "سد عالمگیری بسان سد سکندری" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 524). The riverbank was laid out "مانند تالاب زیبه[OCR?]" and the nobles built pavilions over the water, and the work has been kept up at the crown's cost ever since: "از ابتداے سال چهارم لغایت حال که زیاده از چهل سال میگذرد و درهر سال ترمیم و تعمیر از سرکار بادشاهی میشود و براے بند و بست مبلغی کلیه بخرج میرود" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 524). Under Jahangir, "دولتخانه سرکار والا در دارالسلطنت لاهور" was built with many kinds of pleasant apartments "و هشت لک[OCR?] روپیه خرچ گردید" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 135), and Bhandari heads the section "عمارات جهانگیرآباد و لاهور" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 136). In the fourteenth regnal year, the year uncertain in the printed text, Jahangir ordered a tall minar at every kos of the highway from Agra to Lahore, a baked brick well at intervals, and two rows of trees, and his officers built the minars and wells and planted fruit trees (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 136).

The state also disciplined the city. Jahangir's farmans forbade the "بدعت" of tobacco, and those who smoked in defiance "در شهر لاهور تشهیر فرمودند بلکه بعضے را لب بریدند"; yet they "عبرت پذیر نشدند و اجتناب نورزیدند", and the practice grew (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 133).

In Khalid's present the state reaches into the shrines. "Recently the Punjab government banned the consumption of drugs at a famous Sufi shrine in Lahore to control the law and order situation there, but in doing so it permanently altered the culture of the shrine, developed over thousands of years"; Khalid does not name the shrine (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated). He once petitioned "with a group of prominent architects from the city to save the destruction of a historic Hindu temple in Lahore that was being encroached upon by the local populace", and one architect asked him "if it was un-Islamic to save a Hindu temple" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 10, ch. 4, folio not stated). The fort, "which was once the emblem of their power and wealth, is now a tourist destination. For twenty Pakistani rupees, one can visit it and even tour the private chambers of the mighty Mughal Emperor, Jahangir" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 195, folio not stated). Khalid records that the Lahore High Court heard the Shaheed Ganj case under the British and again "Around 1957-58" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 77, folio 59; Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 78, folio 60), and that "In February 2012, the lawyers' community of the Lahore High Court un-officially banned the sale and consumption of Shezan drinks in the premises of the court" because the firm is owned by an Ahmadi family (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 211, folio not stated). Behind the Roshani gate lies "the Heera-Mandi bazaar, the area reserved for the prostitutes of the city before they dispersed all over Lahore, after a crackdown on them during the Islamist government of General Zia-ul-Haq" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 205, folio not stated). Of the endowments of the minorities, he names the "Dyal Singh Memorial Trust, a pre-partition trust based in Lahore", which runs the book stall at Tambu Sahib (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 224, folio not stated).

## Festivals and Practice

Khalid traces the Milad procession to a single year and a single mosque. "In 1935, a group of about a hundred people left the premises of the historical Wazir Khan Mosque", singing praises of the Prophet, and walked "around the alleys of the walled city" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). The mosque's prayer leader, Maulana Syed Dedar Shah, had argued: "If the Hindus can arrange festivals and hold processions for their fake gods then can't we ... do the same for our Prophet [PBUH]", though "Most of his followers rejected his idea" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). "Soon after a man called Karam Elahi, also a resident of Lahore, organized his own little procession", and "Inayatullah Qadri, another religious scholar, started holding an annual Mehafal-e-Millad"; the event "crossed the boundaries of the walled city and spread to neighbouring villages and towns" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). In time the procession "had to be moved outside Delhi Gate, where a permanent platform was constructed", and "The junction where the procession converged every year got the name of Millad Chowk" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).

The minorities keep their own calendar in the city. Khalid's chapters follow Shri Valmiki's birthday, Krishna Janamashtami, Palm Sunday and Good Friday at the Sacred Heart Cathedral, Easter at the Cathedral Church of Resurrection, Navroz and Hazrat Bab's birthday in Lahore (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 5, folio not stated), and the commemorations of Guru Arjan's martyrdom at Gurdwara Dera Sahib and of Maharaja Ranjit Singh's death anniversary (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 6, folio not stated). "On religious festivals like Holi, Diwali and Shri Valmiki's birthday, the Neela Gumbad Mandir becomes an island of religious syncretism in Lahore" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 70, folio not stated). Before 9/11, a Catholic recalls, "a huge procession used to leave from the Don Bosco School" on Railway Road for the Sacred Heart Cathedral, and "All the Catholic Churches in the city would merge into that one procession"; the city's yearly religious conferences of about three thousand people were stopped at the government's request "Following the threats of rising terrorism" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 114–115, folio not stated). Easter breakfast at the Cathedral Church of Resurrection is "halwa puri and channa, typical Lahori nashta" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 134, folio not stated). At Navroz, Daria Boatwala says, "Nothing really happens here as you can see" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 142, folio not stated), and tambola "is the only game they play in Lahore now" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 143, folio not stated). The city also sends pilgrims out: a Lahore woman of Shahdara is a regular at the Maryabad festival (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 97, folio not stated), where on the second day "the Bishop Sahib comes from Lahore" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 86, folio not stated).

Older practices linger at the edges. "In Lahore, I met a man who claimed to have spent forty days inside a grave", Khalid writes in a passage on the forty day retreat (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated). On a Sunday on Mall Road, cricket in all the grounds is "a usual Sunday feature" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 100, folio not stated). In Bhandari's chronicle the army of Khalilullah Khan and Raja Jai Singh camped outside the city on "روز عید الضحی" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 67).

## In Verse

Lahore was a city of Persian poets under the Ghaznavids. Mas'ud-i-Sa'd-i-Salman's family came from Hamadan in Persia, but, as Browne's abridgement of Mirza Muhammad puts it, "he himself was born at Lahore, of which, in several passages in his poems, he speaks as his native place" (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 135, folio 117). He was the panegyrist of five Ghaznavid kings, Ibrahim, Mas'ud III, Shirzad, Arslan and Bahramshah (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 135, folio 117), and his special patron was Sultan Ibrahim's son Mahmud, "who was made governor of India in A.H. 469 (A.D. 1076-7)" (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 135, folio 117). "He was probably born about A.H. 440 (A.D. 1048-9) and died about A.H. 515 (A.D. 1121-2)" (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 135, folio 117). He shared his master's disgrace, was imprisoned, was freed, and "returned to his father's estate in India"; when Mas'ud III gave the government of India to his son Shirzad, the commander in chief made the poet "governor of Jalandar, a dependency of Lahore" (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 135, folio 117). Imprisoned again, he was released about A.H. 500 and, being then sixty, "he retired from public life and spent the remainder of his days in seclusion" (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 135, folio 117). Sana'i, "who first collected and edited his poetry", is among those who bear witness to his pre-eminence (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 135, folio 117), and the poet Rashidi was "his contemporary and rival" (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 134, folio 116). Another poet of the age, Abu'l-Faraj-i-Runi, is tied to the city by his name: Runa, "from which he took his nisba, was a place near Lahore", and not, as other works assert, in Khurasan or near Nishapur (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 135, folio 117).

Bhandari records a Persian couplet that Akbar recalled on the river below the fort. Setting out for Kashmir in the rains, "در وقت عبور از دریاے راوی پایان قلعہ لاہور" the emperor asked about whom this verse had been written (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 179):

کلاه خسروی و تاج شاہی
بہر گل کی رسد حاشا و کلا

Bhandari gives no rendering of the couplet; he tells it as part of the second journey of Akbar's court from Lahore to Kashmir, in the thirty seventh regnal year (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 179). In the same chronicle the blinded Shahryar, a prisoner in the fort of Lahore, spoke a quatrain that ends "بگو کور شد دیده آفتاب" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 107), and Jahangir's palace at Lahore is followed by a ruba'i in its praise (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 135).

Madho Lal Hussain is Lahore's own poet in Naveed Alam's translation: "drunkenly he roams the streets of Lahore in the company of fellow carousers" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 6, folio 6). His verse was first printed in the city: an unknown Sindhi editor's Gurmukhi collection was "published in Lahore around 1900" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 17, folio 17), and the Punjab University library holds a manuscript, number 374, of 1804 (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 17, folio 17; Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 186, folio 186). Alam also names Najm Hosain Syed's play "Takht L'hore (Throne of Lahore)" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, pp. 13–14, folios 13–14; Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 179, folio 179).

## The Place in the Books

For Bhandari, Lahore is "شهریست بزرگ و مصریست سترگ", where craftsmen of every land live and "اجناس هفت کشور و اشیاے بحر و بر بتجرید[OCR?] فروخت میرود" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 524). He calls it "دار السلطنت لاهور" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 512) and "دارالممالک لاہور" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 177), and Aram Shah's Lahore is "درخطه دل کشاۓ لاهور" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400). His city is a capital of rulers, a store of treasure and arms, and a place that Islam Shah feared for its very greatness (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 260). His only description of the city itself, as distinct from its battles, is the passage in his account of the suba (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 523; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 524; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525).

For Mirza Muhammad's notes as Browne abridges them, Lahore is the birthplace and the native place of Ghaznavid poets, and the seat on which Jalandar depended (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 135, folio 117).

For Khalid, Lahore is home and field. "Based as I am in Lahore, much of my fieldwork took place in the city itself and its vicinity. This is bound to have had its limitations." (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 233, folio not stated). He reads the colonial city on a Sunday drive: "Mall Road, one of the busiest boulevards in the city is deserted", and "A drive along the road is a journey through the colonial era", past the Lahore High Court, "combining European and Eastern architectural techniques" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 100, folio not stated). The National College of Arts was "established in Lahore in 1857" and is "considered to be the most prestigious art college in the country" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 47, folio not stated). He watches the city change: the Anarkali market "is named after the legendary lover of the Mughal Emperor Jahangir" and "over the years has become the largest tyre market in the city", and "The Pak Tea House, just a few shops away, once famed for its gatherings of the leading intelligentsia of the city, is now on its way to becoming yet another tyre shop" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 66, folio not stated). Rettigan Road is, for one informant, "a prominent locality in colonial Lahore, now taken over by small businesses and shops and no longer the clean spacious area that it once was" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 140, folio not stated). Behind the Roshani gate, "the musicians remain. This is still one of the largest markets for traditional instruments in the city" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 205, folio not stated). The Ravi, which once flowed next to the fort, "has changed its course, moving a few kilometres away" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 195, folio not stated), and today "the mighty Ravi of history flows like a stream in Lahore" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 281, folio 255).

In *In Search of Shiva* the metropolis swallows its villages. The Defence Housing Authority, "the neatest and most expensive residential area of Lahore", "sprawls across a vast tract of land, eating into the small historic villages in the area", whose people, having sold "their abundant land for peanuts to the housing authority", are left "Congested and inflicted with poverty" behind a wall (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated). Hanjarwal is "a village which had been subsumed by the ever-growing metropolis of Lahore" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 7, ch. 1, folio not stated). His guide Iqbal Qaiser "was working on an encyclopedia of Lahore, the first of its kind", in five parts of about a thousand pages each, drawn from land revenue records and government archives, until "administrative changes at the Punjab Cultural Institute put an untimely end to his project" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated).

In *Walking with Nanak* Lahore is a crossroads and a city of crafts. The Grand Trunk Road "connected with Lahore and then Delhi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115, folio 97), and the caravanserai route ran through "Lahore, Satgaraha, Deepalpur, Harrapa, Multan and Uch Sharif" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 119, folio 101); "The Bedian road is the old route to Amritsar" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 38, folio 20). "The Kashmiri binders who used to reside in Lahore were known for their meticulous binding" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 39, folio 21), and Bhai Ram Singh was Principal of "Mayo School of Arts in Lahore" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 117, folio 99). Rohtas, Khalid writes, "was not the kind of fort that was found in Lahore, where only royalty stayed" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 249, folio 223). Iqbal Qaiser's lecture on Sikh architecture in Lahore argued that "like the Sikh religion, which picks up from both Hinduism and Islam, Sikh architecture draws inspiration from both Hindu and Muslim architecture", and that domes once part of Muslim buildings, made smaller by the Sikhs, are such that "Now such domes are also used in Muslim buildings in Pakistan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 309–310, folio not stated / folio 284). He sees new apartments "built recently to cater to the growing demands of urban Lahore" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 36, folio 18), and indigenous trees "now being replaced by imported ones in the developed areas of Lahore" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 46, folio 28). And he calls it "a city like Lahore, where everyone wears their religion on their" sleeve, the last word lost in the printed copy (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 174, folio 148).

Naveed Alam translates Madho Lal Hussain from the city itself: his introduction is signed at its foot "Lahore / August 2015" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 19, folio 19). He names Nur Ahmad Chishti's history as "Tehqiqat-i-Chisti: Tarikh-i-Lahore ka Encyclopedia (Researches of Chisti: Encyclopedia of Lahore's History)" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 8, folio 8; Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 178, folio 178).

Jürgen Schaflechner's study of Hinglaj names Lahore only in passing: as a Punjabi city in its survey of Pakistani Hinduism (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 28, folio not stated), and as the home of an informant of the Nath sect (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 138, folio not stated). It attaches no shrine, saint or festival to the city.

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **Did Shihab al-Din Ghori take Lahore?** Bhandari has him fail before the city in 580 (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517), and then, on his sixth campaign in 582, receive Khusraw Malik's submission and set his own deputy in Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 404; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 405). Dhillon writes that Ghori "was not able to conquer Lahore" when he captured the Punjab in 1192 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 136, folio 151).
- **The gates of the walled city.** *A White Trail* calls the Roshani gate "one of the thirteen gateways that lead into the ancient walled city of Lahore" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 205, folio not stated). *In Search of Shiva* calls Bhatti gate "one of the twelve entry points" of the walled city (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).
- **How many minority communities.** Khalid counts five religious communities in and around Lahore, Hindus, Christians, Sikhs, Baha'is and Zoroastrians (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 9, folio not stated), and on the next page four religious minorities in the city, Hindu, Sikh, Christian and Baha'i (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 10, folio not stated).
- **When Abu'l-Faraj-i-Runi died.** Taqiyyu'd-Din-i-Kashi places his death in A.H. 489 (A.D. 1096); Mirza Muhammad, as Browne abridges him, holds that Runi survived into the reign of Mas'ud III, A.H. 492 to 508, and that Taqiyyu'd-Din "is evidently mistaken" (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 134, folio 116; Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 135, folio 117).
- **How long Mas'ud-i-Sa'd-i-Salman of Lahore was imprisoned.** Mirza Muhammad's summary gives "ten years" and then "eight or nine years" (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 135, folio 117). Nizami's own anecdote gives "twelve years" and "eight years more", and Browne's footnote there gives Mirza Muhammad's "ten years" and "seven or eight years" (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 69, folio 51).
