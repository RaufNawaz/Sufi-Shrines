## Language, Poetry and Music

For Boivin poetry is the heart of the province's religion: "Sufi poetry is the backbone of Sufism in Sindh, including for the Hindu Sufis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 26, folio 13). The books agree that the Sufis made Sindhi a literary language. Schimmel writes: "Sind, as other countries, had, and still has, a big treasure of folk poetry, ballads, and working songs; folk tales are located in the different parts of the country, be it the hill slopes of Balochistan, the desert region of Thar Parkar, the fertile plains that were watered by the Indus, or the shifting creeks near the Indus mouth. It seems that in Sind, as in other parts of the Muslim world, the Sufis are responsible for the development of the spoken language into a literary one" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 161, folio 161). She dates native language mystical poetry in Sindh, as in the Punjab, from the fifteenth century (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384), and writes that in Sindh, too, "the 18th century can be called the most important period for the formation of mystical poetry and prose" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, pp. 30–31, folio not stated). Shackle writes that there was already an established culture of vernacular Sufi poetry in Sindh before Shah Abdul Latif, with poets such as Shah Abdul Karim, who died in 1623, and Shah Inat Rizvi, who died in 1711, though it tends to be overshadowed in the literary histories by Shah Abdul Latif's unique reputation (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 9, folio not stated).

Boivin knew before he began that the Hindus of Sindh "had gone to Sufi shrines, and that they shared their passion for Sufi poetry with Muslim Sindhis and others, especially through Shah Abd al-Latif's Shah jo Risalo" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 14, folio not stated). The works of the Sufis of Sindh, known as *kalams*, "constituted the foundations of Sufi culture" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 19–20, folios 6–7), and "In Sindh, save the Naqshbandis, Sufism is dominated by poetry expressing the concept of wahdat-e wujud" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 162, folio 149). The Sufis to whom the Hindus look mostly belong to Upper Sindh, where Siraiki is used for Sufi poetry besides Sindhi, and all belong to the *wujudi* school of the Sufism of Sindh, which leaves out the Naqshbandis (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 108, folio 95). This trend, he writes, "seems to have developed in the nineteenth century, during colonial domination" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 109, folio 96).

The province's religious culture, Boivin argues, was made in its own language. "Manuscripts and early lithographed books provide evidence that the religious culture of Sindh had been vernacularized", and the first phase was usually a translation from Arabic and Persian into Sindhi (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 23, folio 10). In the early eighteenth century Naqshbandi scholars from Lowari Sharif wrote treatises in Sindh showing that the Muslim Sindhis did not know the Quran, wrote paraphrases and then a Sindhi translation of it, and condemned as un-Islamic some rituals they called Hindu or Shia (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 22–23, folios 9–10). Their later treatises led to the conclusion that the Sindhis, even when officially Muslim, did not practise orthodox Islam (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 228, folio 215). "Devotional literature in Sindhi thus formed the basis of a culture that transcended social, religious and ethnic divisions", and it was carried not by the *ulema* or the *pandits* but to the villages by the caste musicians, the Manganhar or *mirasi* (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 52–53, folios 39–40). The Shia devotional poetry of the *marsiya* "is cherished by both Muslims and Hindus, the latter being performers as well as authors" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 42, folio 29).

The province's learned languages were not its spoken one. The five native histories of Sindh, Sorley writes, are all in Persian, "which was the Court language of the ruling houses of the country up to the days of the British conquest" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 24, folio 15). The learned men of Mughal and Kalhora Sindh, though not few, do not make a company imposing in achievement, and they wrote "in Persian and Arabic and not in the language of the countryside" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 217, folio 208). Most of them were Sayyids, Kazis and Mullas, or men concerned with teaching the Quran, and the writers belonged largely to families that had immigrated into Sindh; "They were not Sindhis by birth" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 218–219, folios 209–210). In the days of the Afghans before the Mughals, and earlier, "Sind proved a favourite home for learned men", drawn by the reputation Sindh had gained for its Sufi philosophy, and the age of the Arghuns and Tarkhans was specially notable for the entry of "this kind of intellectual aristocracy", which had "but little effect upon the unlettered cultivator class"; "In this respect Sind was merely typical of Muslim India generally" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 219, folio 210).

Arabic and Urdu left little mark on the province's verse. Arabic, Sorley writes, has always been known and spoken in Sindh by a few learned men, and the Quran "is known by heart by large numbers of devout Muslims in the province", but the effect of Arabic on the poetical literature of Sindh is negligible (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 248, folio 239). "Urdu has never been a language which has taken kindly to Sind soil", and "the literary currents which played in Sind came from Persian and Arabic sources" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 258, folio 249). The Sindhi language itself, in his view, suits "a peace-loving agricultural and pastoral culture elaborated by an industrious and non-martial people" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 120, folio 111). Schimmel, for her part, writes that "The province continued to produce mystics who wrote either in Persian or in their mother tongue" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396).

Learning reached few. Sindh has always had a number of persons called *hafiz*, which does not mean, Sorley writes, that they know Arabic as scholars (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 219, folio 210). There is no contemporary account of the working of its educational system in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, the best description being Burton's, gathered orally (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 220, folio 211). Sindh in Mughal days, he concludes, showed merely the working of the Islamic educational system, and "The great mass of the people remained illiterate" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 222, folio 213). Sindh, he writes, has never been distinguished for learning and could have had few chances in medieval times of keeping anything like a Muslim university (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 224, folio 215). The teaching profession, he writes, does not seem ever to have been held in great respect in Sindh, teachers seldom being paid more than half a rupee a month by each pupil; in the time of the Talpurs Sindh had six madrasas, at Sehwan, at Trippat, at a place north of Sehwan whose printed name cannot be read with certainty, at Matiari, at Mohar or Walhari near Umarkot, and at Chothiyari on the Nara (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 225, folio 216). The Sindhi language "was much neglected as a medium of instruction except by Hindus, who used a non-Arabic script", and conditions were unlikely to have been better under the Mughals and the Kalhora (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 225–226, folios 216–217).

Northern Sindh had a second language of verse. Siraiki, in Boivin's account, is "the Sindhi dialect mostly spoken in Northern Sindh, and especially among the Baluchi population", and many Sindhi Sufi poets, especially from Northern Sindh, composed in several languages (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 171, folio 158); the glossary calls it a language of Northern Sindh and Southern Punjab (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 220, folio 207). Schimmel writes in the same sense that "Many later Sindhi Sufi poets, mainly those of Baloch origin, used also the northern dialect of Sindhi, Siraiki", and that the imagery of the two linguistic areas is closely related, as one would expect among the people of predominantly rural provinces (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 154, folio 154). She finds the mystical literature of Sindh and the Punjab closely alike, with the motif of spinning and weaving "in a cotton-growing country" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, pp. 416–417, folios 385–386). On the road between Bahawalpur and Uch, Khalid found that "the cultures of Punjab and Sindh merge": a Punjab Hotel beside a Sindh Hotel, Siraiki as an amalgamation of the two languages, and the Siraiki of Uch more like Sindhi (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 276, folio 250).

The province's Hindus shared in this poetry. Ajwani held that the Bhakti reached Sindh only in the sixteenth century, because of its isolation, but Boivin finds no evidence in him of the influence of the *sants* on the province's spiritual life (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 90, folio 77). Jotwani, cited in the book as Motwani, called Shah Abdul Latif and Sachal "the late participaters in the Bhakti movement which had a major impact in Sindh through the spread of Guru Nanak's teaching" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 91, folio 78), and the *slokas* of Sami were probably widespread among the Hindus of Northern Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 104, folio 91). Sachal Sarmast had many followers among the Hindu trade castes of Northern Sindh, and some of them took their love of singing his poetry to India when they migrated after Partition (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 150, folio 137). For the Sindhis who left, singing Sindhi Sufi poetry keeps "a link, through language and music, with Sindh as a fatherland" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 205, folio 192).

Above this tradition stands one book. For Sorley, Shah Abdul Latif's *Risalo* "is the only classic which the language of Sind has yet produced in the realm of deeply imaginative literature", its poems held in universal and popular esteem and known in some form to all classes (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 7, folio not stated). Shackle writes that the *Risalo* "is universally acknowledged to be the greatest classic of Sindhi literature in both Sindh itself and other parts of Pakistan, as well as among the Sindhi émigré population in India and the wider diaspora" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 8, folio not stated). The poetry "exercises over Hindus in Sind a spell almost as complete as that exercised by it over Muslims" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 175, folio 166), and its appeal lies "deep in the hearts of all classes of Sind's population, Muslim and Hindu, lettered and unlettered" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 178–179, folios 169–170). The *Risalo*, Sorley says, is understood and loved in Sindh by Hindus as much as by Muslims (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 225, folio 216). The poet's achievement came in days when Sindh was still medieval in outlook, and "the probabilities are all against Sind's producing a second Shah Abdul Latif", though his poems "still bind Sind with a powerful spell of love and admiration" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 215, folio 206). His poetry is "the only gem cast up from the sea of Sind's vernacular literature" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 216, folio 207).

Sorley explains the poem's place by the province's circumstances. The cultural heritage of Sindh, he writes, came to fruition in Shah Abdul Latif's poetry at a time when the Mughal Empire was showing clear signs of its dissolution; the province's peculiar circumstances were the late conversion of most of its people to Islam and the absence of any considerable body of native literature in Sindhi, because its intelligentsia was drawn mostly from the learned and privileged classes, many of them immigrants rather than original inhabitants (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 245, folio 236). During the Muslim domination of Sindh from the fourteenth century onwards, he writes, "the thoughts and religion of the Hindu population received but scant acknowledgement in the literary forms of the day" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 246, folio 237), while the Sufi strain of thought has in Sindh always appealed to Hindus (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 247, folio 238). The poem's stories, in his reading, "are all Hindu in origin. It is plain that most of them relate to the Sind of pre-Islam days" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 269, folio 260); apart from the Punjab and Kutch tales they are stories of Lower Sindh current among the Hindu peoples on the borders of the Thar before their general conversion to Islam, so that the Hindu peoples of Sindh in his day feel that their culture, no less than that of Islam, went into the making of the poem (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 271, folio 262).

Shackle, for his part, writes that the heroines' stories "are loosely set in the pre-Mughal period when Sindh was ruled by the Muslim Rajput dynasties of Sumiros and Samos, but they are not related in detail" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 17, folio not stated), and that other surs are based on male characters "from the heroic Rajput period of Sindhi history" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 21, folio not stated). These legends give the province a heroic past. Shackle glosses Lakho Phulani as "a legendary bandit from the heroic age of Sindh, who is remembered for his fearless attacks on the ruling Jarejo Samos", and Jakhiro as "a Rajput ruler of medieval Sindh famous for his bravery and generosity", whose verses are to be read as an extended panegyric to the Prophet; in a famous episode of these legends, the invasion of the Muslim emperor of Delhi, Sultan Alauddin, who died in 1316, was resisted by Jakhiro, who took under his protection the women of his fellow Rajputs, the Sumiros (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 342, folio not stated).

The great tales of the province are love stories, and its poets made them teach. Mystical poets "in Sind and the Punjab" developed "the art of utilizing simple folk tales as media for mystical instruction" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 154, folio 136), and "In Sind, the range of folk tales utilized for mystical purposes is even wider": the first allusions appear, "however veiled, in Qadi Qadan's verse" in the first half of the sixteenth century, become more obvious in the short verses of Shah Abdul Karim of Bulri, and reach full bloom in the *Risalo* of his great-grandson Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit in the eighteenth; "Since the original tales were known to everyone, he usually begins his poetical renderings with the most dramatic moment" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 171, folio 153). The Lila Chanesar story "can be dated back to the time of Jam Chanesar, one of the Summa rulers in 14th century Sind", and the Mumal Rano tale "originated in Lower Sind some time in the 15th century" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 157, folio 157). "Sohni is one of the favorite folk tales in both Sind and the Panjab" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 156, folio 156), and Abbas writes that the Sohni myth "is sung in Sufi poetry in all the regions of the Sind, the Siraiki-speaking area of the Panjab" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 126–127, folios not stated).

Of Sassi, the annotator of Usborne's Waris Shah writes: "Sassi-Punnoo is a story of old Sind." Sassi belonged to Bhambore, an old city, and Punnoo was a Baluch chief, and the romances of Sohni and Sassi have travelled beyond their first setting to become popular "both in the old Punjab and Sind" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 12, folio 11, note 5). For Abbas the Sassi myth is "told in the lands of Sind and Baluchistan. In the oral traditions of the region the legend is sung with all the emotion that manifests Sassi's grief" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 118, folio not stated), and performers love to sing it in the regional languages and dialects of Sindh, Baluchistan and the Siraiki speaking regions (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 120, folio not stated). Schimmel recalls Sassi's run through the desert at sunset in Shah Abdul Latif's verse as "a scene which can be appreciated by those who know the glowing evenings at the western border of Sind, when all of a sudden, darkness overtakes the wayfarer, and nothing is left but the sounds of a few animals" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 170, folio 170).

The *Risalo* makes a world out of Sindh. It "draws upon a wide-ranging set of interlocking references to the scenery, society, and legends of Sindh to create a whole new imaginative world", Shackle writes (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 15, folio not stated). In sur 22, *Sarang*, "the transformation of the landscape in Sindh and far beyond is interpreted as a manifestation of the universal extent of divine grace" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 21, folio not stated), the monsoon rains being conceived as a manifestation of divine power both in Sindh, where the normally dry countryside is transformed by them, and across the rest of the world (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, pp. 337–338, folio not stated). After a catalogue of the places the rains reach, ending "Some have passed over Umarkot and have made the ground green", the verse, in Shackle's translation, asks God to make Sindh flourish forever, and closes: "Dear friend and sweet beloved, make the whole world prosperous" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 265, folio not stated). That final invocation of God's blessings on Sindh, Shackle writes, is one of the most often quoted verses of the *Risalo* (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 338, folio not stated). Schimmel reads the same sur: the cloud of mercy, rising over the desert of Eastern Sindh, the Thar Parkar, is seen floating from Africa to China, and the poet, coming closer to his home, prays: "My Lord, be kind over dear Sind, / Friend! Sweet darling! You may make the whole world happy!" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 259, folio 259).

The countryside fills the poem. In sur 18, *Ramakali*, the yogi's horn is praised: "There is nothing like it in the north, in Sindh, or in Hind" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 244, folio not stated), and Shackle explains that "Hind denotes the Gangetic region, as opposed to Sindh and the Indus valley" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 336, folio not stated). "The crane (S. kūṉja) comes to Sindh as a winter migrant", he writes in a note to sur 28, *Dahar*, its plaintive cry heard as the pain of separation from the flock (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 341, folio not stated). Sorley too finds the countryside in the poem: "few countries are as rich as Sind is in bird life", and in Sindh the crow is "a harbinger of good tidings" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 266, folio 257). For Schimmel, likewise, "the crow, kang, is the typical messenger bird in Sind, as was the pigeon in high Persian poetry", and the spinning of cotton is "one of the most important occupations of women" and a frequent topic of the poetry of Sindh and the Punjab (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 160, folio 160). Shah Abdul Latif, she writes, "offers in his verse a vivid picture of the conditions of the Sind countryside during the early 18th century"; "The imagery of the swan must have been popular in Sind for a long time", and Lila's swinging bed recalls the "colorful, laquer-work beds" that "are still a specialty of Sind" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 168, folio 168).

Schimmel traces other habits of the province's verse. The complaint that no letter has come "is general among the Sindhis, so that most of the girls who never learned how a pen looks or knew even a single letter always complain that they do not receive letters from their friends", and it occurs often in wedding songs (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 172, folio 172); "In the folk poetry of Sind the heroines always complain that no letter from the beloved has reached them" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 455, folio 424). In the Golden Alphabets words with the same letters were combined in one verse, "since alliteration was a frequently used poetical device, particularly in Sind" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 454, folio 423), and the mystical bards of Sindh and the Punjab knew the tradition of *Ahmad bila mim* (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 451, folio 420). An Arabic saying "was very popular in 18th century Sind": "Affliction is for saintliness the same as are flames for the gold" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 222, folio 222). Schimmel also writes that "Even the poetry of the eighteenth-century mystic" Shah Abdul Latif, in "the remote province of Sind", calls the reader to admire Iblis as the one true lover (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 226, folio 195).

The Prophet is a theme of the province's poets. Poets "in the plains of Sind and the Punjab wrote tender little poems or instructive Golden Alphabets to instil love for him" in the souls of the villagers (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 222, folio 204), and "The great mystical poets of Sind have devoted wonderful songs to the Prophet, who appears as the dawn between the night of created human life and the Divine day" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 223, folio 205). Abbas writes that *virahini* poetry, in which the devotee is a young bride awaiting her bridegroom, who is equated with the Prophet Muhammad, "is also sung in the Sindhi devotional contexts, such as the mauluds that celebrate the Prophet's birth" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 149, folio not stated).

The poet names the province in lament as well as in prayer. In the lament of Marui, in Schimmel's translation, he says that Marui is no more in Malir, nor Hamir; that "Punhun is no longer in Kech", nor is there in Sindh an Amir; and that in the convents there is no Pir (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 235, folio 235). The singer Alan Faqir recited to Abbas the line "Punnu is not in the Kec / Sind is not the amir", which her notes explain to mean that Sindh does not lead the land politically, that it is not in the position of leadership or amirship; they add that these are all Alan Faqir's political interpretations of the female myths (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 156, folio not stated; Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 210–211, folios not stated).

Under British rule, the poem acquired a scholarship and an examination syllabus. The poems, Sorley writes, lived as sung verse in the days of the Kalhora and the Talpurs, but "it was only after the settled days of the British occupation of the country that there was born that type of literary curiosity which calls forth books of comment and criticism"; the literature on the poet owes its origin largely to English education in the eighty years before he wrote, Trumpp having worked in the days when Bartle Frere was Commissioner in Sindh, and the vast growth of general education in Sindh under British rule has spurred the study of the poems (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 233, folio 224). Shackle writes that after 1843 Persian was quite rapidly replaced as the language of education, administration and elite literature by English and by Sindhi, "whose development was actively encouraged by the colonial authorities in Bombay", and that the *Risalo*, a classic that had always appealed to all sections of the Sindhi population, "was pressed into service for the examinations prescribed by the new education syllabus" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, pp. 12–13, folio not stated). Because Sindh was administered from Bombay rather than Calcutta, he adds, Urdu was not installed as the immediate successor of Persian (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 25, folio not stated). Taufiq Rafat's selection reports Asaf Khan's account of the many Sindhi dialects and of how the Hyderabadi dialect became "standard when the British in 1853 asked the Hydrabadi scholars to write the textbooks for the whole of Sind" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 236, folio 216). In colonial Sindh there was also an attempt to impose a script of its own, the Khudawadi (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 53–54, folios 40–41).

Print and the colonial state remade this heritage. "In Sindh, a printing press was brought to Karachi in the 1850s, but the main event in relation to Sufism came with the printing in 1866 of the Shah jo Risalo", which was followed by a rationalisation of the poetry begun by Hindu literati in the 1890s (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 47–48, folios 34–35). Hindu Sindhis played a leading part in publishing Sufi books in late nineteenth century Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 173, folio 160), and Boivin stresses "the key role played by the Hindus of Sindh in the transmission of the Sufi legacy up through partition" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 170, folio 157). For most Sindhi intellectuals before Partition, Hindu or Muslim, the Theosophical Society gave the framework in which the Sufi legacy of Sindh was read anew (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 157, folio 144), and in the late nineteenth century that legacy was built on the *wujudi* ideology, recast in Theosophical terms through its likeness to *advaita vedanta* (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 204, folio 191). After the lectures of the Reverend Bambridge in Karachi, "In Sindh, the lectures led to a revival of religious and theological studies" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 46, folio 33), and Annie Besant was known in Sindh as Basanti Devi (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 47, folio 34).

Shah Abdul Latif, whom Schaflechner dates 1690 to 1751 (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 95), stands at the centre of the province's modern self image. Verkaaik, as Schaflechner reports him, organises Sindhi nationalist narratives around the "Sindhi language, the poetry of [national poet] Shah Abdul Latif, the Indus, the peasant or hari, and above all, Sufism and mysticism", with the link to Mohenjo-Daro besides (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 88, folio not stated). According to Verkaaik it was G. M. Syed who was chiefly responsible for making Shah Abdul Latif the most important poet of Sindh (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 101, folio not stated, endnote 52). Abdul Qadir Mangi writes: "Every Sindhi, of whatever faith he may be, believes in the greatness of Bhitai. From a Kohli Bheel to a Syed, every one considers Bhitai his spiritual guide" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 95, folio not stated). Sindhi nationalists see Shah Abdul Latif as the ideal Sindhi, the perfect example of the tolerant and syncretic history of the province, while more orthodox writers seek to explain away his journey as youthful recklessness (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 95, folio not stated).

Music was everywhere, and it came early. The *Ain-i-Akbari*, Sorley writes, describes the music of Sindh as *kami*, amatory (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 229, folio 220). Whatever the reason for the Chishtiyya's absence, "music appeared very early in the Sufism of Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 227–228, folios 214–215), and Shah Abdul Latif's poetry shows "the importance of music both to him personally and, historically, to the region" and the role of the musician in the society of the region (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 92, folio 79). "Most of the musicians in Sind have been Hindus and not Mussulmans", one reason, Sorley thinks, why the poetry "exercises so strong a spell over the non-Muslim inhabitants of the land" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 231, folio 222). "There is nothing distinctive in the music of Sind", whose instruments are chiefly the ektar, the sitar, the sarangi, the tambur and the pipes and drums used elsewhere in India, yet "Sind has long had a reputation for proficiency in musical execution prevailing amongst all classes" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 232, folio 223). He quotes Crowe: "The Sindians are excessively fond of singing" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 146, folio 137). The Naqshbandis stand apart: "the majority of the Naqshbandis in Sindh are strongly opposed to music and to the singing of devotional songs" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 228, folio 215).

The poetry lives in its singing. Schimmel writes that "It is moving to listen to illiterate folk musicians in remote corners of Sind" repeating the name of Hallaj in ecstatic songs, and to hear them "claim that he was the true lover who paid with his life for his love" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 175, folio 157). The visitor who goes on to Sindh "will enjoy listening to the moving mystical songs" of Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit (1689 to 1752) or the ardent poems of Sachal Sarmast (d. 1826), which are almost as popular today as they were a hundred and fifty years ago (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 414, folio 383). The translator Naveed Alam writes that "Even to this day, most Punjabis and Sindhis are more likely to have heard a kafi than read one" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 15, folio 15). Abbas writes that Shah Abdul Latif and other Sufi poets of the Sindh region composed in the vernacular languages and dialects, in which they sang as roving mendicants within an Islamic framework, and that "For fear of the clergy mystic poetry sung to music remained confined to the minstrel class" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 50, folio not stated). *Sufiana kalam* "is usually sung in the vernacular and is a feature of the shrines in Sind, especially those of Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar and Shah Abdul Latif at Bhit" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 85, folio not stated). "Among musician communities in the Sind, Shah's Risālo is treated with great respect because of its sacred Quranic context, and the book is not allowed to lie around on the floor" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 51, folio not stated); "Since large sections of the Quran are quoted in his Risālo, musicians in Sind hold Shah's written text in the utmost veneration" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 166, folio not stated).

Abbas describes the province's musicians. "In Sind there can be no ritual without the Manganhar, be it a birth, a wedding, or a death. They are the designated community musicians, and each community has its own Manganhars" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 77, folio not stated); "In Sind, the Sidis of African descent and the Manganhar musicians render the ceremonial singing of devotional narratives" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 169, folio not stated). Her glossary calls the Manganhar hereditary professional musicians of Sindh and Rajasthan and gives *mangiari* and *mangti* as names for a female musician there (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 184, folio not stated). "There is evidence of roving minstrels in the Sind-Rajasthan area. Among them are the jogī snake charmers and flute players" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 77, folio not stated).

She records the forms they sing in. "In Sind this form of choir singing is called sūng" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 40, folio not stated), and "The speech and song create ecstasy, which is a very special context of some shrines in Sind" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 55, folio not stated). Sikander Baloch told her that there is a school of thought in Sindhi Sufi poetry, the *sung*, in which the lover sings to win back the estranged beloved, and that this is called *manana* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 63, folio not stated). Baloch and Naseer Mirza claim that Abida Parvin's use of the *dohra* or *bait* within her narrative, a style she took from her male mentors, is unique: "They claim that no other woman musician in the Sind has this style, which is essentially a unique male style" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 55, folio not stated).

Women sing too, often for women. Among the areas of women's participation Abbas lists "Women as singers/musicians/participants of Sufi songs, sometimes even called the faqīrīanī in the Sindhi shrines" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 21, folio not stated). The Sidi, Mohana and Manganhar women attached to feudal households in Sindh told her that they sing devotional Sufi poetry at their mentor's shrine only on special occasions such as his *urs*, that they perform there only before women, and that their female patrons pay them well (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 95, folio not stated). These musicians sing in the Sayyid households of Sindh to women only (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 112, folio not stated), and "Sidi women render ritual musical services in the households in the Sind" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 167, folio not stated). The singer Taj Mastani deals with television stations and with clients "from the affluent feudal households in Sind, who can reach her for ritual ceremonies" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 34, folio not stated). In the Siraiki belt around Multan, Bahawalpur, Mianwali and northern Sindh, "the female voices are again prominent" in the Sufi poetry of the oral traditions (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 148, folio not stated).

Abbas places the province in a wider world of song. "The mystical lore of the region that includes Sind, Baluchistan, and the coastal belt of Iran shares myths, melodies, and linguistic codes", among them "the famous 'Mast Qalandar' dhun" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 47–48, folios not stated); the *dhun* is "a tune reported to be sung all along the Rajasthan, Sind, Baluchistan, and Iran continuum", and "Almost every folksinger in the region can sing it" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 57, folio not stated). She cites Michael Wood's film *In the Footsteps of Alexander*, which shows "the Indus along the Baluchistan coast back through Persia as a route that seems to have been used for centuries" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 210, folio not stated).

Her glossary records the ceremonial music of Sindh. It names *gic*, songs usually sung by women at births and weddings (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 182, folio not stated); *jamalo*, a folk tune and also a dance, and *kapati*, the songs of the cotton spinners (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 183, folio not stated); *olang*, a drum rhythm played at the bridegroom's home (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 186, folio not stated); and *sadmana*, a melody for ceremonial occasions (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 187, folio not stated). It gives *candar* both as a ceremonial Sindhi melody for auspicious occasions and as the wooden castanets that keep rhythm in Sindhi folk music (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 180, folio not stated). It has *haswari*, a ceremonial tune played with drums and bagpipes, which Sindhi musicians claim to play for the pirs and murshids when they appear at festive ceremonies such as a wedding, and *laon*, a Sindhi nuptial song (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 182, folio not stated; Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 184, folio not stated). *Dhammal* is the vigorous ritual dance performed at Sufi shrines in Punjab and Sindh (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 180, folio not stated).

## Shrines and the State

In Sorley's account religion and government were bound together in Sindh for centuries. Since the time of the Sumiros in the thirteenth century, he writes, "the land has been predominantly Muslim in population and the government had a strong theocratic basis with much virtual power in the hands of the priestly class of Sayids" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 23, folio 14). Saints and rulers were bound together in Sindh from early on: Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya's support for Iltutmish's annexation, the governors who sought Makhdum-i Jahaniyan's help, and the ruler of Sindh who became Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus's disciple are told in the history above. The Kalhora's deference to the priestly class "led to the extraordinary reverence for pirs, Sayids and holy men, which may be said to have been the predominant characteristic of eighteenth century Sind" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 163, folio 154). MacMurdo put ecclesiastical expense at one-third of the gross revenue (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 167, folio 158). Of the order under the Kalhora, in which the true Sindhis were exploited and the position of the Hindus grew worse, Sorley writes that a quasi-theocratic state could have taken no other form (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 171, folio 162).

The saints were a treasure of the state and an instrument of it. Diwan Gidumal, "the minister of the Kalhoro princes", offered an invader two bags which he said "contain the most valuable of Sind's gifts; they contain the holy dust from the tombs of numerous saints and Pirs of Sind" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 25, folio not stated). In *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* Schimmel tells the same story as a mark of the country's pride in its mystical heritage: "Did not Diwan Gidumal, the minister, give the invading Nadir Shah (1739) the most precious treasure Sind had to offer, some of the dust of its saints?" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396). Burton, as Sorley quotes him, saw Sufism in Sindh as a power in politics: *tasawwuf* "under the native governments was as formidable a political engine as most of the secret confraternities recorded in history"; "Even among the mild Sindhis a noted Pir" might formerly safely order one of his disciples to murder an enemy; "Yet the native princes encouraged it partly from superstition and partly because the price of every Pir was well known to them" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 299, folio 290).

The law was the law of a Muslim state. "Sind was a Muslim state and in theory justice was administered according to the tenets of the Koran", Sorley writes, though in Mughal and Kalhora days every man was to some extent the defender of his own rights and the police were lamentably insufficient (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 159, folio 150). Collective village responsibility, as nineteenth century officers described it, might apply word for word to the Sindh of Mughal and Kalhora days, and crime, he writes, was comparatively little rife in Sindh, apart from cattle stealing and the wildness of the Baluchi tribes, though the country held many expert thieves and equally expert trackers (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 160–161, folios 151–152). Trial by ordeal was practised, and under the Talpurs there were no regular civil courts (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 161, folio 152). Burnes wrote that as the state religion of Sindh was taken from the Quran, so was its system of jurisprudence; Hindus settled disputes "by panchayats", and the Baluchis seemed generally to take the law into their own hands (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 162, folio 153). Learning, too, was supported through religious property. Sorley finds no evidence that a single educational institution in Sindh owed its origin to any act of an emperor of Delhi (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 221, folio 212), and writes that the chief support of the literary groups came from revenues granted as *inams* to mosques and to the *maktabs* attached to them (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 222, folio 213).

Under British rule the pirs became landlords in the eyes of the state. Robert Rozehnal quotes Katherine Ewing: "British policy, especially in the Punjab and Sind, was to treat them in essentially the same way that landlords and tribal leaders were treated", the hereditary saints, the *sajjada nishin*, being meant (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 33, folio 23). Jamal Malik finds the cult of holy men "particularly evident in the countryside, where major landlords and large-scale land concentrations are crucial features, especially in Punjab and Sindh", where "the local pîr represents the local landlord or is at least closely related to him" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 199, folio 188). Charles Lindholm argues that under indirect rule in South Asia "Sufis were usually equally content to maintain the peace as long as they were allowed to continue their religious routines and keep their estates and authority" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 237, folio 226), and notes as exceptions "the two Hur rebellions against the British in the Sind by the followers of pir Pagara, a landlord, political leader and hereditary head of a Sufi brotherhood who still retains great authority in present-day Pakistan" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, pp. 241–242, folios 230–231). Schimmel writes that "The attitude of some mystical leaders in the lower Indus valley" proved "quite decisive in the political arena": the *hurr*, the Free, the loyal and absolutely obedient disciples of the Pir Pagaro, were gathered first to support Ahmad Brelwi's struggle against the Sikhs between 1829 and 1831 and later joined actively in the struggle against the British; "The hurr even played a minor role in the Indian-Pakistani war in 1971"; and "This was not the first time that mystical leaders in Sind, or in other parts of the Muslim world, were engaged in politics or in social reforms" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 428, folio 397).

The tie between land and shrine reaches the lives of musicians and fakirs. Abbas's glossary defines the *vadera* as a feudal landlord in Sindh (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 189, folio not stated), and the singer Alan Faqir told her that the *vaderas* have disputes with one another and come to the *faqir* for a prayer (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 78, folio not stated). Khalid explains that "Saeen is a title used to refer to a feudal lord or a spiritual leader from lower Punjab and Sindh, where the shrine culture thrives" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated).

The wealth of the shrines has drawn criticism and reform. Schimmel writes that "the majority of the masses continued, and partly still continue, in the veneration of their saints, which is echoed in hundreds of simple folk songs and popular verses. The exaggerated veneration of a saint's tomb has been made the target of some of the best modern Sindhi short stories. The discrepancy between the wealth of the shrines and their guardians and the poverty of the masses indeed provides an impressive theme for social and religious criticism. That holds true not only for Sind, but for other parts of the Muslim world as well" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 429, folio 398). "In Bengal as in Sind, the Naqshbandiyya and some more militant mystically inspired groups tried to curb the overflowing emotionalism expressed in the lovely folk songs" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 432, folio 401; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 433, folio 402). In Khalid's present the reformers come from outside the shrines: Tableeghi preachers "focus on southern Punjab and rural Sindh where syncretistic religion held sway" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 10, ch. 4, folio not stated), and, as Tahir Kamran puts it, in recent years "the focus of the Deoband has been on South Punjab and Sindh where there is an ubiquitous saint and shrine culture" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated).

Partition divided the custodians from many of their followers. After Partition, Boivin writes, "The darbars in Sindh were most of the time preserved with different arrangements regarding their management", often with *gaddi nashins* or followers in India paying the keepers, and "despite the fluctuant diplomatic relations between Pakistan and India, it was always possible to travel across the border" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 68, folio 55). Yet one of his subjects, Molchand, worried after Partition that it had become difficult for Hindu Sindhi Sufis to visit the Sufi places in Sindh, now in Pakistan (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 124, folio 111). In Sindh, he notes, the groups of Sufis and Nanakpanthis had no central organisation ruling over a whole body of followers (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 202, folio 189).

The Pakistani state has shaped which saints the province celebrates. Kasmani writes that "Sindhi publications of the state hardly mention the saint or the shrine of Sehwan until the late 1960s", while "other saints of Sindh (especially Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai, Sachal Sarmast, and Shah Inayat) were regularly featured during this time, and their annual fairs were marked by special editions. Only from the 1960s onward do Urdu and Sindhi newspapers offer detailed reportage on the annual fair of Sehwan" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 70, folio 53). The publications she means are "two prominent state-run journals, Nayin Zindagi and Mehran, published by the Information Department, Ministry of Information and Broadcasting, Sindh, and the Sindhi Adabi Board, respectively" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 189, folio 172, ch. 1 n. 28), and today "State institutions like the Department of Culture or the Sindhi Adabi Board routinely publish books and brochures dedicated to the saint's life as well as to Sehwan's history and heritage" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 20). Rozehnal records the Sufi Shaykh Wahid Bakhsh, who died in 1995, warning against nationalist slogans, "Sindhu Desh", the ethnic Sindhis' country, among them: "They do not realize that four smaller countries, which they wish to create, will be swallowed by India overnight"; in Rozehnal's reading, "In Wahid Bakhsh's view, the homegrown divisions of ethnic rivalry are as destructive as any external enemy" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 127, folio 118).

The modern state appears in Schaflechner's fieldwork through representation and money. The Sindh province, he notes, "has 9 minority seats out of 168" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 28, folio not stated, endnote 5). Government funding for the places of minority communities shows in a wave of temple restorations across Sindh within the past decade: "While in Sindh, I visited many temples that were renovated within the past decade, coinciding with changes in the political landscape" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 16, folio not stated). He also records how violence against a temple was settled outside the state: in one of the rumours about the attack on a temple at Larkana in March 2014, told him by NGO friends in Hyderabad, "According to tradition in Sindh, the culprits took the Quran and asked the Hindu family for remission" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 17, folio not stated). The instability of the country and "the feudal environment in Sindh", he suggests, have kept scholars away from the province (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 13, folio not stated).

Boivin sees a growing threat. "A possible explanation is that the Hindu population of Pakistan, including Sindh, is more and more under the threat of the radical Muslims of the country" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 127, folio 114). "Despite the islamization, though, and despite the spread of Islamist terrorism in Sindh as in other parts of Pakistan, I argue that the Sufi culture still pervades Sindhi society", and he aims to balance the view that the integrating role of Sufism is weakening in Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 177, folio 164). He is plain that Sufism does not of itself mean tolerance: "the most active people working for the conversion of young Hindu girls to Islam are well-known Sufi pirs" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 177, folio 164), and "the Sufi dargahs are now targets of Islamist terrorism" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 186, folio 173).

## The Province in the Books

Bhandari's description of the *suba* of Thatta, written in the author's own time, is the fullest account of the province as a Mughal unit. It measures two hundred and fifty *kos* in length, from Bhakkar to Kech Makran, and one hundred *kos* in breadth, from the town of Badin to the port of Lahari; it is bounded on the east by Gujarat Ahmadabad, on the west by Kech Makran, on the north by Bhakkar and on the south by the salt sea (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529). Four *sarkars*, Thatta, Siwistan, Nasirpur and Umarkot, make up the *suba*, with fifty seven *mahals* and five ports, and a revenue given as ten crore, forty nine lakh and seventy thousand *dams*, the first two words of the sum uncertain in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 528–529). In this country, he writes, a third share is taken from the cultivator (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530). Bhandari places the province at the edge of Hindustan. In his own day, he writes, this realm adjoins Bengal to the east and the Deccan to the south, and on the west Thatta reaches the salt sea (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 579). In his account of Gujarat, Kutch is a separate country, two hundred and fifty *kos* in length and breadth, with Sindh to its west (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 531). Among his sources he names the *Tarikh-i Bahadurshahi*, on the sultans of Gujarat Ahmadabad and of the *wilayat* of Sind known as Thatta, lands that at various times had passed out of the hands of the throne holders of Delhi and come each under an independent ruler of its own (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 581).

The province, in his eyes, lives on its river. The greatest river, in his words, is the Sind; merchants from Multan and Bhakkar ship their goods down it to Thatta, travellers and even heavy armies go by water, and large armies rarely go by land because of the jungle and the want of water (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529). Most things turn on the boat, and there must be forty thousand boats, large and small (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530). Salt and iron mines are plentiful, and at a distance of six *kos*, the figure uncertain in the printed text, is a quarry of yellow stone that is cut to any size for building (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530). There are wild ass, hare, hog deer, pig and fish; the people live on rice, curds and fish; dried fish is shipped to the ports and fish oil, the word uncertain in the printed text, is used on the boats; and a fish called the *palla*, its name also uncertain in the printed text, comes up from the salt sea into the Sind, is caught in nets, and is found in no other country (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530). Curds keep for four months, there are flowers and many fruits, the mango above all, and small melons grow in the desert (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530).

Bhandari also sets down what was said of the province's liver-eaters, the *jigar khwara*, more famous here than anywhere round about. By a spell, he relates, they take the livers of people, especially of children and especially at mealtimes; some say a trance comes over them; they share the liver with their fellows and the victim dies (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530). They make others like themselves by feeding them a piece and teaching them the spell, and they ride the hyena and bring news from far away (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530). When one is caught, those who know the art cut open the calf of the leg and take out a seed like a pomegranate seed, which is fed to the victim, who recovers (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 529–530). A liver-eater, he reports, will not sink with a millstone tied to the neck, nor burn in fire; to cure one, the experts brand both temples, fill the eyes with salt, hang the person up in a house for forty days, give food without salt and recite spells (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529). Most of them are women, some men; they are found in every land, but more in the lands of Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529).

Sorley dedicated his book "TO THE PEOPLE OF SIND" with a verse of his own on a peace that followed an age of war: "The drums of peacefulness are struck", runs one line, and the next, "Your land a full abundance yields" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 9, folio not stated). For him, eighteenth century Sindh was a survival of an older world. In the days when the verses were composed, he writes, "Islam in Sind preserved the true native form of its characteristic power"; the ideas that then permeated popular thinking, he adds, penetrated the Muslim consciousness much more deeply than in the Sindh of his own day, when that territorial dominion had been lost; and the Muslim India of the Middle Ages, "of which eighteenth century Sind was a relic", recalled in some features medieval Christian Europe (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 213, folio 204). The Sindh from which the poetry could spring, he writes, was more like a "Merrie England" of village self-sufficiency: "It was a society of landholders, petty cultivators, herdsmen, craftsmen and traders, knowing little of the outside world except what could be seen of it at markets and fairs, in the gatherings of strangers at tombs on saints' days or in the company of fellow pilgrims" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 213–214, folios 204–205). Summing up his history, he writes that Sindh in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries "was a land of cultivators and herdsmen, keepers of camels, buffaloes, sheep, goats and oxen, of tombs and Sayids, of dust and heat, of the annual rise and fall of the waters of the Indus but inefficiently controlled by a crude system of irrigation" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 265, folio 256). Of the poem's rustic scenes, he writes, "No one familiar with Sind will dispute how true a portrayal this is of a simple rustic civilization of husbandmen and cattle herds" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 267–268, folios 258–259), and the poems are loved by all classes in Sindh (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 268, folio 259).

Sorley argues with the nineteenth century writers. He holds that "many of the criticisms of Victorian writers are unjust" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 30, folio 21). Burton, he writes, "despised Sind for what he considered its lax Muhammadanism" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 120, folio 111), and Burton's view that Sindh came into British hands in an almost exhausted state cannot be accepted "without considerable qualification" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 143, folio 134). Against such critics he quotes Assistant-Surgeon Heddle in May 1836: "The large masses of the indigenous population are particularly industrious", the merchants of Sindh are active and intelligent, and "The people are orderly and obedient and the laws are respected" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 116–117, folios 107–108). He sets his own judgement that "The peoples of Sind, whether indigenous or immigrant, have never shown any military genius", and that "The people as a whole have always been peaceful and industrious" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 23, folio 14). Lieutenant James, reporting in 1847 on the Chandookah pargana, wrote of Sindh as one continued battlefield, the scene of usurpation, tyranny and bloodshed, "till finally the British bayonet gave the worn-out country peace" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 36–37, folios 27–28).

The word itself is not always the province. Baluch poetry, Sorley writes, has many references to Sindh, but the word so used means not the modern political division of that name but the valley of the Indus, though some places named are actually Sindhi places in the country south of Multan that reaches far down the river towards the sea (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 256, folio 247). One Baluchi bard, as Longworth Dames records him, sings that drunkards "are the young men of the Sindh country", since "there is much water and bhang is cheap" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 256–257, folios 247–248). Schimmel notes that in discussions of Bayezid's master, said to be Abu ʿAli as-Sindi, "i.e., from Sind", the name may point to the lower Indus valley or to a village called Sind close to Bistam, and that even in the first case "not every man from Sind could be expected to know all the intricacies of Hindu monistic philosophy" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 78, folio 47).

For Schimmel, Sindh is above all a mystical country. In 711 the Arabs conquered it, "and this province never ceased to be a center of Islamic studies and a seat of Islamic mysticism" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 6, folio 2). "Sind has always been praised as the country with a particularly strong inclination towards mysticism", and she quotes a Sindhi author, J. Parsram: "Sind has been flattened and has been in a state of negation. This is the cause of the many disadvantages from which it suffers; but it has been also the cause of a great blessing", the blessing being "something of which India is in need", namely the mystical tradition (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, pp. 24–25, folio not stated). In *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* she writes that "The country had always been proud of its mystical heritage" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396). She closes *Pain and Grace* with the province's light: "the villages in Sind are covered with a veil of golden dust; the walls, built of unbaked clay, look like bronze, and the blue and white tiles which cover a lonely saint's tomb match the purity of the blue sky", and the approaches of Dard and Shah Abdul Latif are "as different as are the sunsets in their native provinces" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 263, folio 263). Other voices in the same book are harsher. Schimmel quotes E. Trumpp, in his introduction to Sur Sorathi, on "how much even the strong monotheistic deism of Islam has become modified and corrupted in Sind, as in other parts of India, by its contact with Hinduism" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 158, folio 158), and Sorley on the province's veneration of sayyids and accretion of superstitious practices (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 28, folio not stated).

Later authors of the province described it as a meeting of civilisations. Jethmal Parsram, in *Sind and its Sufis* of 1924, summarised the many religions that had flourished in Sindh, Buddhism, Jainism and Hinduism, to which in more recent times Sikhism and Sufism had been added, and wrote that "there is hardly a country in the whole of Asia, including India, in which the mystic thought of two great civilizations, the Indian and the Arabic-Iranian, is seen as in so beautiful a union as in Sind" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 49, folio 36). Ram Panjwani was already convinced, while still in Sindh, "that Sufism was the core of Sindhi culture, a culture shared by all creeds and social milieus" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 157, folio 144). His preface set out to acquaint the Sindhis "with their past and the glorious cultural heritage of Sind which was inhabited by their forefathers" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 159, folio 146), and his vision of Sindh was that of "a land of the mystic poets, who are humanists as well" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 160, folio 147). When he spoke of the religious culture of Sindh, he meant the spirit of Sufism (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 161, folio 148).

Boivin's own view is that "Sindh hosted a specific shared religious culture resulting from a process of vernacularization, which is not contradictory of the fact that some of its features can also be found in neighbouring provinces" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 37–38, folios 24–25). "The Sufi culture of Sindh was nonetheless the most shared product, but this does not prevent the society from being highly fragmented" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 19, folio 6). He reports Kothari's explanation, which finds the cause in Sindh's place as a frontier, with a constant influx of migrants, far from the main imperial centres (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 28, folio 15), and adds: "It is a fact that Sindh, because of its geographical location both as an isolated and a buffer zone, has hosted many unorthodox versions of normative religions, especially Hinduism and Islam" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 29, folio 16). The impact of British rule was twofold: it objectified Sufism, "enthroning it as the fundamental of Sindhi culture at large", and it renewed religion through a composite middle class (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 64, folio 51). Sindh, like most regions of India, underwent this objectification of the Sufi legacy (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 43–44, folios 30–31), but in his judgement it "had drawn" for centuries "a kind of specific religiosity based on devotion" which placed faith beyond any formal belonging to an institutional religion (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 66, folio not stated). He notes that "In the field of Sindh studies, however, there is no published research related to Sufism and the Hindu Sufis in Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 177–178, folios 164–165).

Schaflechner reads the same idea as a nationalist one. In nationalist writing found "on both sides of the border", Sindh is presented "as a distinct entity lacking any cultural links to other provinces or the state of Pakistan generally" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 87, folio not stated). He quotes K. R. Malkani, a Sindhi Hindu who left Hyderabad after Partition for New Delhi: "Sindh has always been there, Pakistan is a passing show", and "A Sufi Sindh and an Islamic Pakistan cannot coexist"; he calls the book full of rants against Pakistan (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 87, folio not stated). Nationalist writers also blame British rule, which they see as having victimised Sindh; Hussain Soomro writes that the province was once so prosperous that it was said to abound in milk and honey, and in these narratives Pakistan and the British become "the culprits in the case of lost harmony and affluence in Sindh" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 87–88, folio not stated). Sindhi nationalist discourse ties Hinglaj itself to a real Sindh that prospered before outsiders began to suppress the province (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 97, folio not stated), and his chapter plan calls the shrine a hallmark of Sindh's golden past for modern Sindhi nationalism (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 51, folio not stated).

The nationalist case, as Schaflechner presents it, joins the province to Sufism. G. M. Syed, a politician who spent many years in prison for his separatist views, "directly linked the province of Sindh with a tradition of Sufism", found Sufism in its purest form in the province, and took as proof the shrines of Sufi saints that are "to this day, visited by Hindus and Muslims alike" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 88–89, folio not stated). Verkaaik writes that "both Sindhi Hindus and Muslims were influenced by mysticism and religious tolerance. This was the Sindhi essence that both Hindus and Muslims shared" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 89, folio not stated). Ishtiaq Ansari ties Mohenjo-Daro directly to today's Sindhis, claims that the Veda was composed on the shores of the Indus, and holds that Shiva and the Mother Goddess "were already known and honored in the ancient civilization of Sindh" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 93, folio not stated). Badar Abro defines the real Sindh, *asal sindh*, as the valleys between Rohri and eastern Baluchistan where the Sindhis once lived in prosperity, and argues that because Sindh remained under the influence of Sufi mysticism the area is, in his English word, secular, so that its religious places belong to the shared heritage of that real Sindh (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 94, folio not stated). Khadim Soomro writes: "A Sindhi has a special place in human civilization because he stands for good and liberal attitude and behavior" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 96, folio not stated).

Two of the books set limits to this picture. Schaflechner's own verdict is that the common notion among Sindhis today "that in the history of Sindh, there is no religious tension between the two communities" is "merely a nationalist utopian ideal, as Aggarwal (2013) has shown" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 90–91, folio not stated). Shackle's Sindh, for its part, is a land read through its poet, and he warns against reading the poet too narrowly as its voice. Sur 15, *Marui*, he writes, "also allows for a nativist interpretation that exalts the authenticity of the values and landscape of Sindh" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 332, folio not stated), but his introduction cautions against seeing Shah Abdul Latif "too narrowly" as "an authentic spokesman of the Sindhi folk tradition or as an advocate of Sindhi nationalism" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 13, folio not stated).

For Abbas the province is part of a larger cultural region. She speaks of "the Sind-Rajasthan continuum" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 46, folio not stated), of the lore of "the region that includes Sind, Baluchistan, and the coastal belt of Iran" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 47–48, folios not stated), and of the Sassi myth as belonging to "the area currently referred to as a continuum in ethnomusicology" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 156, folio not stated). Her informant Alan Faqir spoke of Sindh as a land invaded again and again, when the Mongols, the Tartars and the Sassanids came, looted and plundered and assaulted, which yet goes on and will not die; "Sindhri is beaten", he said, and yet Sindhri goes on (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 156–157, folios not stated). Her notes explain that Sindhri is a name used for Sindh in the poetic traditions (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 210–211, folios not stated).

For Khalid, Sindh is a region he names but did not travel in for *In Search of Shiva*: "southern Punjab and Sindh are excluded, where there are many such shrines. These shrines also represent similar characteristics as discussed in this book" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 6, Introduction, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* he looks from Manora at "the Makran mountain range, the boundary line between Sindh and Baluchistan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 228, folio 202).
