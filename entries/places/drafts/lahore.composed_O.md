## Overview

In the books Lahore is first of all a great city full of places of prayer. Sujan Rai Bhandari writes that craftsmen of every land live there and that in every street and bazaar there are mosques in great abundance (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 524). Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti wrote his account of the city on the orders of the commissioner, who asked for a detailed account of the buildings, mazars, tombs and mosques of the environs of Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 28), and he calls his book at root a history of the saints (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 29). Kanhaiya Lal sets out to tell in which direction the settlement of Lahore grew beyond its ancient walls, which were its famous quarters and who founded each of them (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 76, folio 79). For the modern scholars it is home and field: Robert Rozehnal was based in what he calls "the cultural capital of Lahore" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 26, folio 15), and Haroon Khalid, based in the city, did much of his fieldwork in it and its vicinity (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 233, folio not stated).

The books tell the founding of the city in more than one way, giving it to Lo (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525), to Lohu, son of Ramchandar (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 795), and, in a tradition Khalid records, to Luv and Kush (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 71, folio not stated). In Bhandari's telling the old city had fallen into ruin, and after Sultan Mahmud's conquest Malik Ayaz rebuilt it and built a fort (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525). Annemarie Schimmel makes it the first centre of Persian inspired Muslim culture in the Subcontinent and the town where Hujwiri composed his Persian treatise on Sufism (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 376, folio 345). It was the refuge of the last Ghaznavids (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 405), and in Bahram Shah's reign the Mongols besieged it and left it ruined, with many of its people taken captive (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 395). Akbar built a city and a palace there and stayed for a time (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 431), and Jahangir was buried north of the city on the bank of the Ravi at Shahdara (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 830). Nadir Shah plundered Lahore thoroughly (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 717), Ahmad Shah Abdali ordered a general massacre (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 218), and after Mir Mannu's death rule in the city fell apart (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 719). Ranjit Singh then took Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 579). Under the British, Kanhaiya Lal writes, it was made the capital of the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 33, folio 36), and the chief cause of its progress was the new railway (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 35, folio 38). Khalid carries the story on to the Lahore Resolution presented in the city in March 1940 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 211–212, folio not stated) and to the partition riots that spread through it (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 22, folio not stated).

At the heart of its sacred landscape stands Hujwiri's shrine, Data Ganj Bakhsh, still a popular place of pilgrimage in Schimmel's account (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 119, folio 88), and for Omar Kasmani the city's most prominent shrine complex (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 65, folio 48). Scott Kugle calls ʿAli Hujwiri the patron saint of Lahore (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 206, folio 185), and Schimmel finds the Qadiriyya represented in the Mughal city by Mian Mir and his sister Bibi Jamal (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 392, folio 361). In Kanhaiya Lal's account saints founded the quarters of the outer city, among them Mauj Darya Bukhari and Sayyid Chiragh Shah Gilani (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 79, folio 82), and the village of Mian Mir stands inside the saint's own enclosure (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 242, folio 245). Chishti gives the great burial ground of Miani a section of its own (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 186). The landscape is not only Muslim. Kanhaiya Lal warns that in the Sikh period even temples of old foundation were built anew (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 93, folio 96), and Khalid names the Sitla Mandir at Shah Alami, the Jain Mandir at Anarkali and the Bhadra Kali Mandir at Thokar Niaz Baig among the city's prominent temples (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 21–22, folio not stated). For Sikh memory the fort is the centre: Guru Arjan was tortured outside its Alamgiri gate (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 195, folio not stated), and Guru Ram Das was born in the city (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated).

Its communities lived side by side, sharing some holy places and fighting over others. Kanhaiya Lal divides the city's Muslims into Sunnis, Shia, Wahhabis and Sufis, the last bearing enmity to no one (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 44–45, folios 47–48). On Sundays Hindus and Muslims alike brought their sick children to bathe at Shah Dargahi's well (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 82, folio 85; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 299, folio 302), and at the Guru Ram Das gurdwara *qawwals* sang *kafis* of divine unity (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 101–102, folios 104–105). Hindus and Muslims together protested the demolition of the mosque at Shah Muhammad Ghaus's mazar (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 249–250, folios 252–253), and Hindus, Muslims and Christians all joined the subscription to repair the Badshahi Masjid (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 142, folio 145). The books record conflict as well: under Mir Mannu there was a general order to seize and kill the Sikhs (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 121), in 1849 Shia and Sunni came to blows near Shah Alami Gate (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 302, folio 305), and Ranjit Singh renamed the Moti Masjid the Moti Mandir (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 339, folio 346). In Khalid's present the city's religious minorities are Hindus, Christians, Sikhs, Baha'is and Zoroastrians (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 9, folio not stated); it is now rare to meet a Sikh in a city the Sikhs once ruled (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 196, folio not stated), and only a handful of Parsis remain (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 136, folio not stated).

The holy places had an economy and a politics of their own. More than seventy *mujawirs* at Data Ganj Bakhsh divide the offerings in equal shares (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 291, folio 294), while of temples inside the walls Kanhaiya Lal writes that their expenses rest on offerings alone, with no *jagir* and no stipend fixed (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 120, folio 123). At the Sunehri Masjid shops were built beneath the prayer building so that their rent might pay the mosque's expenses (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 145, folio 148). Every regime remade this property. Under the Sikhs the Masti Darwaza mosque became known through the city as the powder works mosque (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 143, folio 146), and the stone of Muslim tombs outside the city was taken to build the Hazuri Bagh *baradari* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 340, folio 347). Under the British a tomb could be auctioned on an Englishman's application and made into a *kothi* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 257, folio 260), and the railway took temple land into its yard (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 228, folio 231). Kanhaiya Lal was himself the Executive Engineer through whom the state's repairs to Lahore's buildings ran (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 348, folio 355). In Khalid's day the Punjab government banned the consumption of drugs at a famous Sufi shrine in the city (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated).

The city's calendar is crowded. The greatest mela of Lahore is Bhadar Kali's at Niaz Beg in the month of Jeth, and Muslims too mostly attend it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 229, folio 232). Mian Mir has five melas a year (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 245, folio 248), and Data Ganj Bakhsh has a Friday mela and its *ʿurs* in Safar (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 291, folio 294). In Muharram the Qizilbash household fed Sunni and Shia alike (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 71, folio 74), and Madho Lal's anniversary, the Fair of Lamps, is kept near the Shalimar Garden (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384). Khalid traces the Milad procession to a group that left the Wazir Khan Mosque in 1935 (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated), and follows the minorities' own feasts, from Shri Valmiki's birthday to Easter and Navroz (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 5, folio not stated).

Lahore is a city of poets too. In Mirza Muhammad's account, as Browne abridges him, it is the birthplace and native place of Ghaznavid poets (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 135, folio 117). In Naveed Alam's translation Madho Lal Hussain roams the streets of Lahore with fellow carousers (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 6, folio 6), and his verse was first published in the city around 1900 (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 17, folio 17). Kanhaiya Lal copies the verse inscriptions of its mosques (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 152, folio 155), one of them the work of Farid, a poet of Lahore (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 182, folio 185), and Schimmel lists the Lahore houses that printed mystical poetry (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, pp. 318, 321, 325, 326, 329, 331, 333, 340, 341).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The founding of Lahore.** Bhandari attributes the founding of the ancient city on the Ravi to Lo (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525). Chishti tells it twice: in one telling Raja Ram Chand had three sons, of whom one, perhaps Lo, built Lahore, a second, whose name cannot be read, settled Kasur and the third, perhaps Rao, founded Mauza Rawan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 661); in another Raja Ramchandar had two sons, Kushu and Lohu, and Lohu settled the city (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 795). Khalid records the tradition that Lahore and Kasur are named after Luv and Kush (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 71, folio not stated).
- **How long the Ghaznavids ruled at Lahore.** Bhandari's description of the city, in a damaged passage, has the Sultan's descendants make Lahore their capital and hold it as their seat of government for thirty eight years (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 524–525), and gives Khusraw Malik's own rule at Lahore as twenty eight years (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 405). Chishti has Khusrau Shah son of Bahram rule there for seven years, the numeral uncertainly printed, before his son Khusrau Malik succeeded him (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 808–809).
- **Did Shihab al-Din Ghori take Lahore?** Bhandari has him fail before the city in 580 (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517), and then, on his sixth campaign in 582, receive Khusraw Malik's submission and set his own deputy in Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 404; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 405). Dhillon writes that Ghori "was not able to conquer Lahore" when he captured the Punjab in 1192 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 136, folio 151).
- **When Abu'l-Faraj-i-Runi died.** Taqiyyu'd-Din-i-Kashi places his death in A.H. 489 (A.D. 1096); Mirza Muhammad, as Browne abridges him, holds that Runi survived into the reign of Mas'ud III, A.H. 492 to 508, and that Taqiyyu'd-Din "is evidently mistaken" (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 134, folio 116; Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 135, folio 117).
- **How long Mas'ud-i-Sa'd-i-Salman of Lahore was imprisoned.** Mirza Muhammad's summary gives "ten years" and then "eight or nine years" (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 135, folio 117). Nizami's own anecdote gives "twelve years" and "eight years more", and Browne's footnote there gives Mirza Muhammad's "ten years" and "seven or eight years" (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 69, folio 51).
- **Humayun's return.** Bhandari writes that the Afghans scattered and Lahore passed into the hands of the emperor's men without a fight (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 256); Chishti writes that the governor of Lahore, Tatar Khan, came out against Humayun and that Bairam Khan overcame him off his guard (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 823).
- **Lahore's standing under the Mughals.** Chishti calls it the capital in Akbar's reign (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 194); the translator of Madho Lal Hussain writes that "Akbar had temporarily shifted his capital to Lahore" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 7, folio 7); Kugle calls Lahore "the Mughal regional capital of Lahor" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 202, folio not stated) and also "a seasonal capital city and home of the Mughal court during its perennial rotation" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 233, folio 212).
- **Jahangir's tomb.** The book gives 1034 AH, while the inscription on the grave reads 1037, both uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 314, 316; folios 321, 323).
- **The last nazim of Lahore.** Chishti calls Mir Momin Khan the last nazim of Lahore, after whom none was appointed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 173); he also names Nawab Abdullah Khan as nazim of Lahore from the year printed as 1161 (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 717), Khwaja Abid as subadar of Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 578), and an unnamed ruler of Lahore at the time Ranjit Singh took the city (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 579).
- **Who damaged the monuments at the Sikh conquest.** Chishti writes that when Lehna Singh, as ruler, began destroying the monuments of former kings, the elders of the city invited Ranjit Singh to come to Lahore and take possession of it (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 786), though he also writes that in Ranjit Singh's own time the red, black and marble stones of the country around Lahore were torn down (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 296). Khalid writes that in 1799 Ranjit Singh "was greeted enthusiastically by a population sick of the lawlessness of that time" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated). Kanhaiya Lal lists tombs whose floors, marble and stone were taken by Ranjit Singh's order, among them Sayyid Abdul Wahab Qadri's (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 265, folio 268), Shahzada Parvez's (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 270, folio 273), the graves of the Gilani Sayyids (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 308, folio 311) and Zeb-un-Nisa's (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 311, folio 314).
- **The heads of Hira Singh and Pandit Jalla.** In one account their heads and bodies were seen hanging after they were killed on the bank of the Ravi, and the heads stayed there for some days, in a place the damaged passage connects with a bazaar (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 590); in the afterword the heads were hung from a gutter spout opposite the haveli where Jawahar Singh was held, and a Sikh then kicked Pandit Jalla's head through the city of Lahore for several days, which Chishti says he saw himself (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 868).
- **Schooling under the Sikhs.** Kanhaiya Lal records thousands taught and the respect of the whole Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 38, folio 41), and then that the English government removed all its defects (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 39, folio 42).
- **The gates of the walled city.** *A White Trail* calls the Roshani gate "one of the thirteen gateways that lead into the ancient walled city of Lahore" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 205, folio not stated). *In Search of Shiva* calls Bhatti gate "one of the twelve entry points" of the walled city (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated). Chishti writes that the city has twelve gates, and lists them (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 498).
- **Who is Lahore's patron saint.** Kugle calls ʿAli Hujwiri "Lahore's patron saint" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 206, folio 185), and calls Shah Hussayn "one of Lahore's patron saints" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 204, folio 183) and "one of the patron saints of Lahore" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 241, folio 220).
- **How Miani was emptied and became a graveyard.** One account has the learned village deserted after a split over a point of the *shariat*, then resettled under Mir Ali (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 191); the gravediggers' history has the graveyard itself deserted under Muhammad Shah and growing again under Ranjit Singh (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 204); another passage has the Sikhs loot Mauza Miani and scatter its people, after which a settlement of houses grew up (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 212); and another says that when Miani was deserted it became this graveyard (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 213).
- **The name of Miani.** Chishti derives it from the ulama, whom Punjabis call *mian*, and adds Panj Dher from the five elders' graves (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 191); the gravediggers gave their own reason for a name printed as Siani, but its key word cannot be read (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 211).
- **The gravediggers' genealogy.** In one version Khwaja's two sons are Karim Bakhsh and Ilahi Bakhsh, each with four sons, among them Nabi Bakhsh, Miran Bakhsh and Rahmat (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 209); in another a son Nur Muhammad also dug graves, his sons were Khwaja and Taj, Khwaja's sons Karam Bakhsh and Ilahi Bakhsh, and Karam Bakhsh had five sons, the number uncertainly printed, among them Muhammad Bakhsh, Ahmad Bakhsh and Rahmat (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 213).
- **The gravediggers then working.** Chishti's list on the word of Sayyid Mansur, the *sajjada nashin*, runs to fourteen names, including Ranjha, Kalu and Ludhu, the last uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 209); his second list runs to ten, including Ghulam Husain and a name printed as Ilah Bakhsh, who are not in the first (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 213).
- **The gravediggers' income and the sajjada nashin.** One statement divides the income half and half between two lines and has part of it go to the *sajjada nashin* as wages for digging graves, in uncertainly printed words (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 209); the other has the family of the dead pay the gravediggers their due and give the *sajjada nashin* something besides, the *sajjada nashin* having no connection with the gravediggers' income (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 213).
- **Amin Chand's account of the Parsi graves.** Chishti writes that what is to be found in the Parsi graveyard does not match Lala Amin Chand's description of open pit graves (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 733), and he disputes Amin Chand's account of the city more generally (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 733).
- **How many minority communities.** Khalid counts five religious communities in and around Lahore, Hindus, Christians, Sikhs, Baha'is and Zoroastrians (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 9, folio not stated), and on the next page four religious minorities in the city, Hindu, Sikh, Christian and Baha'i (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 10, folio not stated).
- **Akbar's grant to Mauj Darya Bukhari.** Kanhaiya Lal gives it once as a *jagir* of two lakh rupees for the expenses of the khanqah's dervishes, containing the township of Batala (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 79, folio 82), and once as a *jagir* of one lakh twenty five thousand at Batala, the place name uncertain in the printed text, under Akbar (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 279, folio 282).
- **Who reburied Shah Sharaf's coffin, and which tomb was demolished.** In one passage a mosque and tomb before the Bhati Gate were pulled down for Ranjit Singh's moat, and the coffin was placed through Faqir Aziz-ud-Din in Haji Muhammad Saeed's enclosure at Dola Wari, with a small masonry platform by the Maharaja's order (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 80–81, folios 83–84). In another Khwaja Muhammad Saeed's tomb and mosque were ordered demolished for standing too near the gate, and Shah Sharf's coffin was reburied through Faqir Nur-ud-Din under a very small platform and enclosure wall (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 287–288, folios 290–291).
- **Who receives what at Shah Dargahi's well.** In one passage the zamindar of the well takes the rotis together with one paisa *charaghi*, the lamp offering (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 82, folio 85); in the other the rotis are distributed to the people and one paisa goes to the well's zamindars (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 299, folio 302).
- **Shah Abu al-Maali's festivals.** In one place the melas of both Eids and an annual urs attended in thousands (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 87, folio 90); in another three melas a year, with illumination and dancing courtesans, *qawwali*, and both Eids (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 285, folio 288).
- **Major MacGregor's office.** He is the Deputy Commissioner of Lahore when he releases the Masti Darwaza mosque in a year read as 1850, the year uncertain in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 143, folio 146), and the Assistant Agent to the Resident, the title uncertain, when the dispossessed sanyasi petitions him (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 300–301, folios 303–304).
- **What Mr Cope made of the Dai Anga mosque, and his name.** In one passage he made the mosque what is printed as a *kachhi*, a word whose sense is unclear, and his name is uncertain in the printed text; in the other he made it a *kothi*, a residence; his name is spelled both Cope and Coop, and he is once given the office of superintendent of the Lahore press (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 168, folio 171; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 268, folio 271).
- **How Raja Suchet Singh came by Nawab Mian Khan's tomb garden.** One passage has the garden ownerless and taken by Suchet Singh before its sale for 2,200 rupees (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 272, folio 275); another has the same property confiscated, *zabt*, by Ranjit Singh and given to Suchet Singh (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 329, folio 336).
- **The mahant of the Dehona Das mandir.** Lal gives him as Ram Das, grandson or chela of Dehona Das, and does not choose (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 443, folio 450).
- **The six bighas at Hassu Teli's shrine.** One passage says the land's income serves the shrine's expenses and its keepers' living every year (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 169, folio 172); the other, in wording that differs by a few letters, that it is set free for the expenses (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 269, folio 272).
- **Where Mufti Ghulam Sarwar's purchase and Daya Anga's endowment are printed.** Each is reported at two places in the book, the purchase from the khanqah's mujawirs at (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 167, folio 170) and (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 267, folio 270), and the statement that much property was endowed with the mosque by its founder, the word for the founder uncertain in the printed text, at (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 167, folio 170) and (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 267, folio 270), the two printings differing slightly in spelling.
- **One rakh or two, and its name.** The land Muhammad Sultan endowed to the khanqah is from a *rakh* printed as Chalu (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 239, folio 242), and the *rakh* granted to him and later mortgaged is printed as Jalu (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 360, folio 367; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 360–361, folios 367–368), both names uncertain; the book does not say whether they are the same.
- **The physician's fees on Nur Jahan's recovery.** A robe of one lakh and seven lakh in cash, with the queen's and her attendants' jewellery, are said to come to twenty two lakh rupees in the day, the figure uncertain in the printed text; the parts as printed do not add up to the total (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 160, folio 163).
- **The Wazir Khan hammam.** Lal counts it among the mosque's endowments, where the word may be read as *auqaf* but is printed *auqat* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 15, folio 10), while elsewhere he says the hammam is now government property (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 158, folio 161).
- **Masjid Chinian Wali.** The outer gateway's chronogram reads 1082 AH and the inner mihrab 1080, both uncertain; Kanhaiya Lal reconciles them rather than choosing, concluding that two years passed between the interior and the outer gateway (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 153, folio 156).
- **Mulla Majid's mosque.** One inscription carries both 1235, uncertain, for the earlier building and 1294 for the present one (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 183, folio 186).
- **The Mian Mir church.** The book says it was built between 1850 and 1857, both years uncertain, and in the same sentence that this was a period of about six years (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 390, folio 397).
- **Wasdev's renovation.** The book dates it to the present time, Samvat 1937, while his own brick reads Samvat 1936, both uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 130, folio 133).
- **Two ages that cannot share one moment of writing.** Masjid Muhammad Salih Kambo is said to be two hundred and twenty six years old, the figure uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 152, folio 155), and the Wazir Khan mosque two hundred and fifty one (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 155, folio 158); reckoned from their founding years, the two figures do not point to the same year of writing.
- **Haqiqat Rai's offence.** The book states it twice, and differently (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 208, folio 211).
- **The name of the tilework.** It is *kansi kar* at the Dai Anga mosque and Jani Khan's dome (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 268, folio 271; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 270, folio 273), and *kashi kari* at the Begampura mosque, the Nila Gumbad, Makhduma Begum's dome, the Zakariya Khan mihrab, Miskin Shah's walls and Sharaf un-Nisa's tomb (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 277, folio 280; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 286, folio 289; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 260, folio 263; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 262, folio 265; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 263, folio 266; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 264, folio 267).
- **The name of Nau Lakha.** The book explains it as the garden whose annual income was nine lakh rupees, the reading uncertain, and, on the word of some, as the garden on which nine lakh were spent (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 355, folio 362; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 356, folio 363).
- **The festival of lamps, its name and its ground.** Schimmel names it *Melā Chirāghān* and places it "near the Shalimar Garden" on the last Saturday in March (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384); Abbas names it the *Melā Chirāg* and places it at the Shalimar Gardens, with no date (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 95, 185, folios not stated).
