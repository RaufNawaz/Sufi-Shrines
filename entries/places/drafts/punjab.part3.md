## The Province in History

The modern authors give the Punjab of their books a long history before Islam. Haroon Khalid compares the Indus Valley, "an agriculture-intensive society", to present day Punjab, Pakistan, where in his reading the fertility cult has particular significance (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated). For Harish Dhillon, retelling the Janamsakhis, the Punjab of the centuries before Guru Nanak was the land through which every invader came, from the Ghaznavis, Ghoris and Tughlaqs after the beginning of the eleventh century to Taimur in 1398: "Since Punjab in the north was the gateway to India, it bore the brunt of all these invasions" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 15, folio 17). Once the conquerors had settled down in the Punjab region, he writes, they turned to converting the non-believers or destroying them (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 16, folio 18).

Richard M. Eaton begins the pattern of conquest he traces in 986, "when the Ghaznavid Sultan Sabuktigin (r. 977-97) attacked and defeated the Hindu Shahi raja who controlled the region between Kabul and northwest Punjab" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 110, folio 98). In Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi's history, Mahmud "established Ghaznawid rule over Khurasan, Afghanistan and the Panjab" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 83, folio 67), and Khalid places Al-Beruni's coming to India in "the first half of the 11th century, after the Muslim invader Shah Mahmood Ghaznvi attacked Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 44, folio not stated). From the middle of the eleventh century, in Eaton's account, Mahmud's successors "became progressively more provincial, their kingdom focused around their capital of Ghazni in eastern Afghanistan with extensions into the Punjab", and their raids "appear to have been less destructive and more sporadic than those of Sabuktigin and Mahmud" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 111, folio 99). The Punjab became the dynasty's refuge: after two defeats by 'Ala'u'd-Din of Ghur, Rizvi writes, "Bahram Shah was forced to flee to the Panjab" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 83, folio 67). The destruction of Ghazna by "'Ala'u'd-Din Husain of Ghur in 1150-51, followed by the Ghuzz invasion six years later" in turn "drove a large number of people from that region to the Panjab for safety", and Rizvi speaks of "The Ghaznawid Sultan of the Panjab" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 157, folio 139).

The Ghurids followed. "A hundred and fifty years later in 1175 Mu'izzu'd-Din Muhammad bin Sam seized Multan, establishing firm control and Sunni rule over the southern part of the Panjab", in Rizvi's words (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 128, folio 110), while Dhillon writes that "Shahabuddin Muhammad Ghori captured Punjab in 1192" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 136, folio 151). After Jalalu'd-Din's defeat in November 1221 and Chingiz Khan's march back to the west, Rizvi writes, the Mongol's absence let Iltutmish (the name is misprinted in the text) consolidate power in the Panjab, and seven years later Iltutmish routed his rival Nasiru'd-Din Qubacha from Sind (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 152, folio 134).

The Mongols shaped the province for two centuries. Eaton describes how, from the late tenth century onward and culminating in the thirteenth, Mongol pressures "forced waves of Turkish settlers into the subcontinent, many of them settling permanently in the urban centres of the Punjab, such as Lahore, Dipalpur, and Multan" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 217, folio 205). In the middle of the thirteenth century Balban, "in order to build a defensive bulwark against Mongol incursions, pursued the policy of strengthening and populating certain cities in the Punjab with large garrisons of Turkish elements", which added to Muslim settlement in the province's towns not only soldiers "but also thousands of refugees fleeing before the advancing Mongols"; Eaton's footnote names the garrisoned cities as "Lahore, Multan, Sirhind, Bhatinda, and Dipalpur" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 222–223, folios 210–211). Before Muhammad bin Tughluq's scheme of 1327, Rizvi writes, the Sultan raised "the slogan of jihad against the Mongols, who in previous years had devastated the Panjab and the region around Delhi" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 193, folio 175), and "Timur's invasion in 1398-99 devastated both the Panjab and Delhi" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 290, folio 270). Recovery came under the Afghans: "In the fifteenth century, the Panjab enjoyed a peaceful period due to Afghan rule. The Mongol invasions between the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries had devastated portions of the Panjab and the Afghans had successfully contributed to its re-settlement and urbanization by founding many new towns and promoting trade and commerce" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 408, folio 384).

Babur ended that peace. Guru Nanak, Rizvi writes, came back from eastern India about 1520 "only to find the Panjab plunged into a number of significant political crises"; Babur "started unsuccessful negotiations with Ibrahim to annex west Panjab to Kabul", and "After two minor raids around the region between the Indus and Jhelum, Babur invaded a third time over the Panjab in 926/1520 reaching as far as Sialkot" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 409, folio 385). A footnote on the same page says that Daulat Khan "Gradually he also became the governor of the Panjab" and then invited Babur to invade India, in a year the printed text gives as 1523 with an uncertain reading (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 409, folio 385). Khalid writes that Nanak's Babur Bani is "a long poem that Nanak wrote at the time the Mughal marauder Babur attacked Punjab, destroying the cities of Eminabad and Lahore on the way" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 15, folio not stated).

Under the Mughals the Punjab appears in Eaton as two of Akbar's provinces: he reads Abu al-Fazl's statistics for the Lahore and Multan subas, compiled about 1595, though the opening of his sentence is damaged in the printed text and the scholar's name is misprinted there (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 225, folio 213). Eaton calls the reign of Jahangir a time "when the Mughals enjoyed effective authority in the Punjab" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 231, folio 219). Khalid reports "some claims" that Guru Hargobind took service with Jahangir, serving him in the Punjab, the Guru's name being misprinted (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 234, folio 208), and writes that by the time the Guruship passed to Harkrishan "the Sikh community had expanded beyond the confines of the Punjab" into places as far as Kabul and a city whose printed name, perhaps Ghazni, is uncertain, which made central control by the Guru "increasingly difficult" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 181, folio 155).

Khalid tells the eighteenth century as a time of breakdown. "Ever since the fall of the Mughal Empire conditions had deteriorated in Punjab", and Ahmad Shah Abdali, the Afghan king, "had already completed two raids of Punjab and beyond" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). Moin-ul-Mulk, "popularly known as Meer Manu", "was the governor of Punjab" and was "constantly pestered by the Sikh insurgent groups Misl, which had sprung up all over Punjab"; Abdali had "agreed an annual tax stipulation which the governor was responsible of paying to him, for the right of governing Punjab" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). "In 1797, when the grandson of Ahmad Shah Abdali, Shah Zaman Durrani, following the footsteps of his grandfather, attacked Punjab", a Sikh band "met him on the banks of the Chenab" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). In the days of Nanak's parents, Khalid writes in his reconstruction, "The jungles of Punjab were infested with thugs and dacoits who attacked innocent travellers" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 22, folio 4).

Maharaja Ranjit Singh is for Khalid "Maharaja Ranjit Singh (1780-1839), the erstwhile ruler of Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 42, folio not stated), "the Sikh ruler of Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 159, 196, folio not stated) and "the Sikh king who ruled Punjab before the British took over" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 206, folio not stated), "crowned as the Ruler of Punjab" in 1801 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated). In a land ruled, he writes, for over a millennium by Afghans, Mughals and other non-Punjabis, "Ranjit Singh was the first Punjabi to rule Punjab", and he brought stability "in Punjab which lasted a good forty years" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 208, folio not stated). "Punjab became a British colony almost immediately after his death eventually leading to its division at the time of Partition", and Dalip Singh was "the last Maharaja of Punjab, before the British took over" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 209, folio not stated).

The British came late to the Punjab. Khalid dates their "arrival, which in Punjab was in the middle of the nineteenth century", and calls the nineteenth century the one "which is also known as the Sikh period" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 57, folio not stated); "Punjab was colonized almost a century after the colonization of Bengal by the British, as a result of which English language is not as institutionalized here" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 105, folio not stated). The colonial state remade the land. In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, Khalid writes, "the British connected the five rivers of Punjab with each other through canals, turning several acres of jungle land into agricultural land"; "thousands of new villages, referred to as chaks were established, and farming families from all over Punjab were invited to settle"; the former inhabitants, who were mostly nomads, could not prove their ownership of the land, and "Their land was snatched away and allotted to these migrants" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated). Jungli, "which literally means 'people of the jungle', is a popular term used to refer to the indigenous Punjabis, as opposed to those who settled here after the construction of the canal colonies by the British during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated). After the uprising of 1857, in Scott Kugle's brief mention, Imdadullah "fled through the Punjab and Sindh, visiting Sufi shrines and eventually sailing from Karachi into exile in Mecca" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 280, folio 259).

The province's history in these books ends with its division. Khalid speaks of "the wave of the second migration that took place after the division of Punjab between India and Pakistan" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated). In the south, he writes, "violence here during the partition never scaled the heights it did in the other regions, therefore the resentments between communities that seeped in after 1947 were not that serious" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 31, folio not stated).

## Saints and Pilgrimage

Rizvi makes the Ghaznavid conquest the beginning of the Sufi settlement of the province: "the annexation of the Panjab by Mahmud of Ghazna and its incorporation into his empire prompted many sufis to settle in the area" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112). Khattali, he writes, ordered his disciple Shaikh Husain Zinjani to move to the Panjab, and later sent Hujwiri after him; Hujwiri reached Lahore in about 1035, while Sultan Mas'ud I reigned in Ghazna (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112). The family of Baba Farid "came from Kabul, but migrated to the Panjab in the middle of the twelfth century" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 156, folio 138), and it was the Ghaznavid Sultan of the Panjab who made Farid's father Qazi of Kahtwal (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 157, folio 139).

For Eaton, Baba Farid's settling at Ajudhan was "the religious dimension of a very slow transformation of the Punjab's cities from a Hindu to a Turkish-Islamic orientation" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 217, folio 205). From the fourteenth century through the Mughal period, he argues, "in Punjab and Sind, shrines of great Sufis attracted and integrated pre-agrarian and non-Muslim pastoral clans into their ritual, socio-economic, and political orbits"; the descendants of the Sufis married into the leading families of the clans, and the clans were "passing from a life of pastoral nomadism to one of settled wheat agriculture" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 48–49, folios 36–37). The practice of writing amulets for the people, he finds, "has not changed from the thirteenth century to the present; even now the successors of Baba Farid and his assistants, in common with those of other shrines in the Punjab, continue to write ta'widh for the masses" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 218, folio 206). He quotes Major Aubrey O'Brien's essay of 1911 on the Muslim saints of the western Punjab, which he holds to be equally true of medieval shrines: "The general idea of our riverain folk seems to be that the Deity is a busy person, and that his hall of audience is of limited capacity", and "Thus, all human beings require an intervener between them and God" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 216, folio 204). One of his chapters asks how the shrine "served to promote a conceptual conflation of Heaven and Earth among Muslims of rural Punjab" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 17, folio 5).

The orders spread through the province and beyond it. In the time of Makhdum-i Jahaniyan, Rizvi writes, the Suhrawardiyya "spread from Uch to Gujarat, the Panjab and Kashmir", reaching even the Delhi of Sultan Firuz Shah Tughluq, whose name the printed text garbles (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 299, folio 277). In the age of Mongol domination of Central Asia and Iran, wandering Qalandariyyas and Jawaliqis passed through Multan, where they "often came into conflict with Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya"; "From the Punjab, the displeased qalandars moved to Delhi creating scenes wherever they saw a khanqah. Some went as far east as Bengal" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 325, folio 303). By Guru Nanak's day, Rizvi holds, the Qadiris "had made the Panjab the main centre of their activities" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 420, folio 396).

Carl W. Ernst and Bruce B. Lawrence name the Punjab among five regions, with Gujarat, Bengal, Uttar Pradesh and the Deccan, where the early saints, and the Chishti masters above all, mark "a particular kind of South Asian spiritual authority that is not only political and religious but also cultural", and where they describe "a spectrum of spiritually charged spaces or sites" that are "devotional loci for Indian Muslims from several classes, and also for Hindus"; they say nothing there that is specific to the Punjab (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 110, folio 97). "The Nizami Chishtis came to prominence in the eighteenth century, principally in Delhi, the Deccan, and Punjab, through masters who were related to but in tension with local political leaders" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 119, folio 106). Shaykh Sulayman Taunsawi, who died in 1850, "consolidated the order by stressing learning through establishment of institutions", among them the academy at Taunsa on the upper Indus and "other religious schools throughout the Punjab"; Muhammad 'Aqil and Gul Muhammad Ahmadpuri, "both of whom founded religious schools in the southern Punjab that provided free instruction and lodging to hundreds of students", did the same (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, pp. 121–122, folios 108–109). The line of Shah Mirra, they write, "resurfaces in the nineteenth century in the figure of Hafiz Sayyid Muhammad 'Ali Khayrabadi, marking the expansion of the Nizami branch of Chishti Sufism through disciples of Shaykh Sulayman Taunsawi in the upper Punjab" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 71, folio 57).

The Punjab is also the home ground of Guru Nanak's journeys as Dhillon retells them. In the more modern accounts his third journey, of 1514 to 1516, went as far as Tashkent "and then back to Punjab via the Kashmir Valley", and in the Puratan Janamsakhi the fifth, of 1523 to 1524, "lasted about one year and covered the towns and regions within Punjab" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 55, folio 61). At Haridwar, in an episode dated 1504, Nanak faces "west, in the direction of Punjab" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 79, folio 87), and in the same episode "Punjab is very far away" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 80, folio 88); at Prayag he is called "Nanak, the great Guru from Punjab" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 102, folio 113). By his return to Talwandi in 1506, a year imperfectly printed, "there were very few in the Punjab who did not know of him and his teachings" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 123, folio 137). On his second journey he "crossed through Rajpurana and the south-west Punjab till he came to Multan" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 164, folio 180); "Nanak was by now a well-known figure in Punjab" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 167, folio 183), and the Sufis of Multan, in Dhillon's telling, had shown him a "patronizing tolerance" as long as he preached elsewhere in the Punjab (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 168, folio 184). The fourth journey closed, around the time of Babur's third incursion into India, with a south-eastern route back to the Punjab (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 224, folio 246).

In the present the books place the heart of shrine devotion in the south of the province. Khalid "travelled to southern Punjab, believed to be the hub of shrine culture in the country" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated), and writes that "Saeen is a title used to refer to a feudal lord or a spiritual leader from lower Punjab and Sindh, where the shrine culture thrives" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated). "The southern region of Punjab, where Uch Sharif lies, has more traces of religious and cultural syncretism compared to the northern and central region, where there is a greater prevalence of education and hence religious intolerance" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 277, folio 251).

## Communities

Dhillon describes the medieval province as divided: "For over three hundred years, Islam and Hinduism existed side by side in the Punjab region in a state of constant conflict" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 16, folio 18). Eaton observes that a map of South Asia's religious demography shows that "Islam took root much more readily in Bengal or the Punjab than in upper India, the area with the deepest traditions of Indo-Muslim rule and patronage" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 14, folio 2). In both Bengal and the western wing of Punjab and Sind, he argues, "the growth of Muslim societies correlated with the adoption of sedentary agriculture", and "both regions were still frontier societies where Hindu religious values and the hierarchic social ideals of Brahmin priests had not yet deeply penetrated" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 48–49, folios 36–37). So "by the eighteenth century large communities of Muslim peasants had appeared in both Punjab and Bengal", whereas in India's heartland conversion was far less significant, and "Unlike Punjab or Bengal, Malabar lay beyond the orbit of Mughal imperial influence and the Persianized culture associated with it" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 49, folio 37).

The Jats are at the centre of Eaton's account. He asks "why and how it was that Jat tribesmen, as they abandoned pastoral nomadism for settled agriculture between the thirteenth and nineteenth centuries, gradually took on an Islamic identity" in the western Punjab (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 16, folio 4). Quoting a dissertation then in progress by Emily Hodges, he describes the towns of the western Punjab as living by intensive cultivation along the river flood plains, and beyond them "a vast tract of sparsely populated land, the barr country between the five rivers", "but little cultivated owing to the very scanty rainfall", which supported "pastoral nomadism based primarily on the herding of goats and camels"; the herders moved down to the rivers in the hot dry months and back to the barr after the rains, "never leaving the Punjab plains and covering at the most a distance of less than one hundred miles", in "a symbiotic relationship with the settled peoples of the riverine area" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 223, folio 211). The Jat pastoralists, "before their entry into the Punjab, had not yet been integrated into Hindu society"; "By the time of Baba Farid, however, in the 1260s, we find the first mention of Jats occupying the Punjab proper", Babur "described Jat pastoralist groups in the Sind Sagar doab and Sialkot regions", and "The northward movement of Jat clans and their settlement in the grazing tracts of the Punjab is thus well supported by contemporary evidence" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 224–225, folios 212–213). In Akbar's statistics, of the 186 Punjabi parganas whose dominant zamindars are known, 82, nearly half, were held by Jat groups; "Only in the extreme western Punjab, in the Sind Sagar doab, where the Janjua Rajputs and Ghakkars dominated, were the Jats conspicuously absent as zamindars" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 225, folio 213). The Jats whom al-Biruni had called low Sudras in the eleventh century had become "the dominant agrarian caste in nearly half of the Punjab", and "by the 1650s, as today, the very word 'Jat' had become virtually synonymous with peasant agriculturalist"; Eaton gives Irfan Habib's explanation, "the introduction of the Persian wheel in the Punjab and its extensive use by Babur's time", which made "much of the arid western Punjab's naturally rich soil capable of supporting the cultivation of food crops" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 225, folio 213).

Conversion followed migration. The Jats' movement north from Sind to Punjab, their rise from low Sudras to zamindars and their change from herders to farmers were accompanied, in Eaton's argument, by their conversion to Islam, and "of those Jat and Rajput groups that became Muslim in the medieval period, the vast majority of them claim to have been converted either by Baba Farid or by his contemporary, Baha' al-Haqq Zakariya (d. 1263), whose tomb is in Multan city" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 227, folio 215). The Punjabi Muslim clans that claim Baba Farid include the Bhatti, Chhina, Dhudhi, Dogar, Gondal, Hans, Jo'iya, Khokhar, Siyal, Tiwana, Watt, Kharral and Ara'in (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 227, folio 215). In court depositions of 1935 to 1938 before the Lahore High Court, "many of the major endogamous clans that are spread throughout the southwestern Punjab, mainly as peasant agriculturalists" claimed conversion by Baba Farid, among them the Wattu, the Siyal, the Dogar, the Kathia, the Bhatti and the Tiwana (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 241, folio 229). From the genealogies of the Siyals of Jhang District, which run from the late fourteenth to the early nineteenth century, Eaton concludes that "religious conversion among Punjabi clans was very slow indeed", "not only slow, but probably unconscious as well", in "a period long before either British census officials or zealous reformers began urging Indians to place themselves into sharply differentiated religious categories" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 234–235, folios 222–223). Kugle adds the weavers in an endnote: "New Muslims (naw-musalman) from weaver castes converted to join the Islamic community in Punjab slightly later than in Hindustan, where the mystic poet Kabir displays the same kind of Sufi-bhakti synthesis a century earlier" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 329, folio 308).

Other communities moved out of the province. Jürgen Schaflechner reports, after Cook, that "The Bhatia and the Khatris, for example, arrived in the eighteenth century from cities in the Punjab", Multan among them, "and were ultimately absorbed as Lohanas", and, after Thakur, that migrants from Rajputana and the Punjab who followed Lohana occupations acquired membership of the Lohana caste (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 80–81, folio not stated).

Ernst and Lawrence see Chishti biography take sides in Shaykh Sulayman Taunsawi's time, as British colonialism began to take hold of India: Taunsawi "urged his followers to participate in resistance against Sikh aggression in the Punjab", so that "apparently for the first time in the annals of Chishti history, we have spiritual biographies drawn into partisanship on behalf of one political group"; yet "Notable, however, is the effort of Shah Sulayman to maintain cordial relations with Punjabi Hindus even while he opposed their Sikh neighbors" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 121, folio 108). Khalid writes that Meer Manu "cast the battle between the state and insurgents as a war between Muslims and Sikhs, even though there were several Muslims in the Sikh groups and many non-Muslim soldiers in the army of Meer Manu", and that "The Sikh groups did not control much land but because of their anti-status quo politics the lower rungs of the society supported and protected them" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). In the early twentieth century Muhammad Ilyas, founder of the Tableeghi Jamaat, "worked in undivided rural Punjab, exhorting Muslims to 'purify' their religious practices which he argued were corrupted due to the influence of the dominant Hindu culture", and Muslim reformists like him "founded reformist movements of their own, aggravating the communal tension in Punjab" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 10, ch. 4, folio not stated).

Partition changed who lived beside whom. Locals, Khalid finds, "generally tend to be more embracive of practices 'non-Muslims'" than the migrants who came after the division of the Punjab, and he has "seen several gurdwaras and temples being taken over and destroyed by the migrants", while where the population is largely indigenous "such 'non-Muslim' religious spaces are better looked after", with exceptions (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated). "Compared to Central Punjab, Southern Punjab has been historically tolerant towards other non-Muslim faiths, which is why a significant Hindu population continues to live here" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 31, folio not stated).

Khalid's account of the province's religious minorities today is drawn from his own travels. Of Pakistan's non-Muslims other than the Ahmadiyya, "the majority are Hindus living in the 'faraway' 'peripheral' regions of Southern Punjab, inner Sindh and Baluchistan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 12, folio not stated). In Khalid's words, "unlike the Hindus, the Christian community has a formidable presence in the Punjab", and Hindus "are a smaller community in the Punjab and can therefore be ignored" by political parties; most Hindus are scattered in other provinces, "with only a few in Punjab. The Christians on the other hand have an overwhelming majority in the Punjab (among the minorities)" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 87, folio not stated). "The religious distinctions between Hindus and Christians have become blurred in urban Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 28, folio not stated). "A lot of Hindus in Punjab do this, passing off as Muslims or Christians by taking up non-Hindu names. This is a survival technique in a hostile environment" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 39, folio not stated), and where minorities in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa are visible in society, that is "hardly the case in Punjab, which is much more monolithic" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated). After the demolition of the Babri Mosque on 6 December 1992, "Well-known Hindus living in the cities of Punjab had to hide for several months after the incident" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 22, folio not stated).

In most villages of the province, Khalid finds, Christians are confined to menial work. At Shanti Nagar, where Christians own the land, he finds "a unique case study in the social fabric of the Punjab. Whereas in most of the villages across the province, Muslims are the dominant landowners, while Christians are confined to menial jobs associated with the untouchable caste" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 123, folio not stated); in an ordinary village, he writes, they would be treated as untouchables (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 124, folio not stated). He lists the attacks at Sangla Hill in 2005 and at Gojra and Bahmniwala, Kasur, in 2009, "all of which happen to be located in Punjab", and adds that "The story of a few burnt pages from the Quran found in local mosques seems to happen often" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 125, folio not stated). An informant he calls Daria Boatwala, who moved to Lahore in the 1980s, tells him: "here in the Punjab, the situation is much different. There are hardly any Zoroastrians here and I have never come across any Hindu. The only Christians that you see are those who are cleaners and sweepers" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 142, folio not stated).

Khalid also follows the province's Sikhs and Nanak Panthis. Nanak Panthi Sikhs, in Khalid's account, "belong to the areas of South Punjab and Sindh", with temples "in Sindh and Southern Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 197, folio not stated), and the langar workers at Dera Sahib are "low-caste Nanak Panthis from Southern Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 201, folio not stated). Of the devotees of Nanak who did not join Guru Gobind Singh's Khalsa, he writes: "There are thousands of such Sikhs living in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Punjab and Sindh in Pakistan, and they are referred to as Nanak Panthi Hindus. In their temples, along with pictures of Hindu deities, one also finds a picture of Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 98, folio 80). Many of the Sikhs he meets in the province are Pathans who came from the tribal areas: "Moving from tribal areas to the heart of Punjab, an overwhelming majority of them could not even speak the language. They remained confined to their little enclaves" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 203, folio not stated), and the Sikhs of Nankana "remember this internal migration from KPK to Punjab", which gives "a good picture of the different socio-economic conditions in both of these provinces" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 235, folio not stated). Boota Singh of Nena Kot, who told him that his people were not from the Tribal Areas but Punjabis, was "the first Pakistani Punjabi Sikh I had interacted with. Earlier, I had only encountered Pathan Sikhs who had moved to Punjab in the past few years" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 83, folio 65). At the November 2011 festival at Nankana the kirtan singers "have covered their heads with a loose turban, a popular style in the villages of West Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 230, folio not stated).

Reform movements now press on the shrine culture of the countryside. Tableeghi preachers, Khalid writes, "focus on southern Punjab and rural Sindh where syncretistic religion held sway", and "The Tableeghi Jamaat has managed to permeate the social fabric of the major cities of Punjab", so that "shrines similar to the ones at Kamalia and Apal Muri are likely to face a major onslaught due to their 'impure' religious practices, in coming years" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 10, ch. 4, folio not stated). He cites Tahir Kamran: "In recent years, the focus of the Deoband has been on South Punjab and Sindh where there is an ubiquitous saint and shrine culture", and "the people of the South are attracted towards the Deoband school of thought, and feel victimized by the feudal nature of society intertwined with the saint and the shrine culture" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated). The Deobandi school, he adds, "gained popularity in the lower and middle echelons of the urban Punjabi Muslim populace, a feature that they have retained in contemporary Pakistan" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* he reports the view of Ayesha Siddiqa, whose name is imperfectly printed, that organisations of the Ahl-e-Hadith school, whose names are also uncertainly printed, "have seeped into the social fabric of southern Punjab, a region that has historically rejected such strands of Islam and adopted a syncretistic version of religion. Such Sufi shrines are a symbol of that strand of Islam", and that this is changing as they open seminaries in the rural areas and draw in unemployed young men (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 284, folio 258). A recent paper by Tahir Kamran on Deobandi Islam in the Punjab, he adds, finds such organisations popular with the young because "they offer an explanation for their current oppression", and their first step is "to target such shrines and their guardians, who have become immensely rich feeding off the oppressed classes" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 284, folio 258).

The province's shared festivals belong to its farming year. "Punjab, still predominantly an agricultural society, upholds agrarian culture and traditions, of which the harvest season is the ultimate source of happiness", Khalid writes, but in the days after the creation of Pakistan, among the country's elite, "several cultural festivals like Vaisakhi, Lohri and Basant were given up" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). "Lohri like Vaisakhi or Basant, is an ancient festival of the Punjab that commemorates the change of season", kept "on the 13th of January, which is generally the last night of the month of Poh", with a village bonfire, dry fruit, folk songs, children collecting wood and kheer left to cool overnight (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 192–193, folio not stated). After the creation of Pakistan "this indigenous festival of the Punjab died down", and "the Sikh and the Hindu minorities of central Punjab also stopped this celebration" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 193, folio not stated). It was "earlier celebrated on a large scale throughout the province. It is ironical though that this Punjabi tradition shunned by Punjab is now being revived by a group of Pathan men residing in Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 194, folio not stated).

## Language, Poetry and Music

The province's language has a low standing among its own educated people, in Khalid's experience. In Pakistani Punjab, he writes, Punjabi "is looked down upon. It is considered to be a language of the boorish and the uneducated"; his elite private school fined any student who spoke Punjabi, even in the playground, and "the educated elite of Punjab ends up disowning, in fact, ridiculing anything Punjabi", the poetry of Baba Farid, Guru Nanak, Shah Hussain, Waris Shah and Bulleh Shah among it (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 128, folio 110). In the south the language shades into its neighbour. "The boundary between Sindh and Punjab becomes a blur as we drive through Bahawalpur"; "This is where the cultures of Punjab and Sindh merge. Some of the roadside hotels are called Punjab Hotel, while others are Sindh Hotel"; Siraiki is "an amalgamation of the two languages", and his guide, the historian Iqbal Qaiser, tells him that "The Siraiki spoken in Multan is closer to Punjabi, while here it is more like Sindhi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 276, folio 250).

Persian poetry was common ground in Nanak's time, in Rizvi's argument: "all literate Panjabis, both Hindus and Muslims, at the time of Guru Nanak were conversant with" 'Attar, Rumi and Sa'di (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 420, folio 396). The poetry of the saints lived on in song. In Khalid's reconstruction of the time of Sheikh Ibrahim, Baba Farid's twelfth successor, Farid's poetry "was still sung by qawwals at the shrine and by fakirs in the cities of Punjab" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 142, folio 124). Ernst and Lawrence write that "The name Qalandar continues to be intoned in popular qawwali lyrics of the Punjab", without saying on which side of the border (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 36, folio 22).

The province's minstrels carried the songs of every faith. In Nanak and Mardana's time, Khalid writes, the Mirasis went from city to city singing of local chieftains, and landlords showered them with gifts; Mardana "knew by heart several folk stories of Hindu deities and Muslim saints that he would sing during different festivals of Punjab" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 121, folio 103). As a younger man "he would roam the cities and villages of Punjab, singing folk songs and legends", the most popular of them Puran Bhagat's (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 184, folio 158).

The love legends are the province's own. "The Chenab, in the folk culture of Punjab, is known as the river of love", the river of Sohni and Mahiwal and of Ranjha's Takht Hazara (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 9, ch. 3, folio not stated). In conversation Khalid asks: "Isn't Heer Ranjha part of Punjabi culture? It is the most celebrated folk story here. For centuries it has been sung and dramatized"; Mirza Sahiban is "another famous folk love legend of Punjab", yet "the story of Mirza Sahiban is such a blemish on the legacy of spiritual love stories of Punjab, that it was said that these two brought an end to the era of love legends" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated). Kugle sets the legend of Hir and Ranjha against the devotional world further east: "Punjabi poets and their eager audiences poured into Hir and Ranjha the same erotic emotion that was invested in Radha and Krishna in regions closer to the Ganges. The story of Hir and Ranjha became a favorite Sufi device for conveying the value of erotic passion in spiritual life", and Shah Hussayn's poems explore divine love "in terms Islamic, Hindu, and indigenous Punjabi" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 228, folio 207).

## Shrines and the State

Rulers tied themselves to the province's shrines. Eaton writes that "the Delhi sultans and Mughal emperors granted huge tracts of rich land" for the ritual ceremonies of the great Sufi shrines of Punjab and Sind, while irrigation "permitted a rapid growth in wheat production" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 48–49, folios 36–37). For Ernst and Lawrence, Akbar used the Chishti saints, Shaykh Salim and "Shaykh Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar in the Punjab", to rule his provinces: "Through a twofold, redoubled Chishti loyalty, Akbar could anchor his imperial legitimacy in two provinces adjacent to Uttar Pradesh, the Punjab and Rajasthan, both of which also happened to be crucial to the political-military ambitions of his reign" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, pp. 112–113, folios 99–100). The book does not state the extent of that Mughal province.

The Sikh state endowed the gurdwaras. "When Maharaja Ranjit Singh became the sovereign of Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century he spent large amounts of money on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras", and "attached vast tracts of land to these gurdwaras, so that the revenues from the lands could facilitate their functioning" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16); he renovated "many Gurdwaras all over Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 208, folio not stated). Under the British their custody changed hands: "As a result of sectarian divisions between the Sikh communities and the takeover of Punjab by the British, a large number of important Sikh Gurdwaras were being administered by Hindu mahants" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 189, folio not stated). Ernst and Lawrence observe that "If we were to follow only the dictates of political history and the criteria of social science, the test case for the Chishti Nizami Sulaymaniyya lineage in the colonial period might seem to be the battles over shrines in the Punjab", with Taunsa as the example, and that "Political power struggles internal to the Sulaymaniyya can be traced to British manipulation and also the rivalry of local Sikhs, Hindus, and Muslim reformers"; they then set this reading aside, since "political contestation is only part of the narrative" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 124, folio printed "Ill" [text-layer?] [111]).

After Partition many non-Muslim holy places lost their purpose. Without the family that cares for it, Khalid writes, the shrine of Ram Thamman "would have also been taken over by the refugees and converted into a household, like the hundreds of temples and Gurdwaras all over Pakistani Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 58, folio not stated). At Makhdoom Pur Pahuran he finds the gurdwara inside a school, its gate locked, with a banner over it describing the Punjab government's new policy for improving education in public schools (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 128, folio 110).

The provincial state appears in the books in many guises. Schaflechner notes that "the Punjab has 8 reserved minority seats out of 371" in its assembly, against Sindh's 9 of 168 (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 28, folio not stated, endnote 5 to p. 15). He also cites a class V Urdu textbook of the Punjab Textbooks Board, published in Lahore in 2002, which teaches that the Hindu has always been an enemy of Islam (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 28, folio not stated, endnote 7), and draws from it, after Nayyar and Salim, that in Pakistani schoolbooks "the Hindu" is not only a danger to national security but "has always been an enemy of Islam" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 20, folio not stated). The road through Changa Manga, Khalid writes, "is much safer now, since the Punjab government introduced the motorway police" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated). He reports that "human rights activists, opposition party leaders and segments of the media have alleged that the Punjab government, dominated by the right-winged Pakistan Muslim League Noon, has been supporting these radical outfits who are based in Jhang", and that "The Muslim League Noon, on the other hand, rubbishes the claims" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).

The killing of the province's governor runs through Khalid's books. "On 4 January 2011 the Governor of Punjab Salman Taseer was shot down by one of his bodyguards, Mumtaz Qadri, for supporting the cause of a Christian woman, Aasia Bibi, accused of blasphemy"; Qadri "became an instant hero, and was hailed by the lawyers' community", and a retired chief justice of the Lahore High Court, Khwaja Sharif, offered to fight his case free of charge, calling him a hero of Islam (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 19, Notes, note 44, folio not stated). In *A White Trail* the governor "was gunned down" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 48, folio not stated), and the Aasia Bibi case "received international coverage after the former Governor of Punjab, Salman Taseer came out in support of this woman and was later killed for his pains" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 90, folio not stated).

## The Province in the Books

Each author's Punjab is drawn to the scale of his travels. Khalid limited the research for *In Search of Shiva* to the country around Lahore: "Since this was mostly a self-funded project I could only limit my research around my hometown of Lahore. Therefore only parts of central Punjab are included and southern Punjab and Sindh are excluded, where there are many such shrines" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 6, Introduction, folio not stated). In the afterword to *A White Trail* he explains that he worked in "Central Punjab where I conducted my research", where Hindus live in ghettos, adding: "My perception is that the conditions under which members of the Hindu community live in Central Punjab are the harshest compared to the other regions" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 233, folio not stated). "The majority of the Hindu community is based in Sindh and Southern Punjab, where they have a proportionate representation from the lower and the upper castes" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 233, folio not stated), whereas almost all his interviews were with low caste Hindus "because there are hardly any high-caste Hindus in this region", so that his observations from Central Punjab should not be taken to reflect Hindu communities where the population is large (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 233–234, folio not stated). On the other hand, "The majority of the Christian minority is based in Punjab", so that his Christian material can stand for the community in the country as a whole (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 234, folio not stated).

Khalid's Punjab is also personal. Khalid was "born and brought up in Pakistani Punjab" and knew of Guru Nanak as the founder of Sikhism, a poet and a spiritual leader, "Yet given the Muslim-dominant culture of Pakistani Punjab, there were no avenues available for me within the culture to explore his personality" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 15, folio not stated). On the road he asks whether "is it the bubble that we occupy in the urban centres that is out of context in the culture of Punjab", and remarks that "One rarely finds such a lonely road in the heartland of Punjab, the most prosperous province of Pakistan" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated). At the banyan of Apal Muri, where visitors had left their mobile numbers, he guesses that they were "probably hoping to initiate a secret love affair in this highly monitored social life of rural Punjab" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 9, ch. 3, folio not stated). He notes that "yellow was commonly used for buildings in Punjab during the older days" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 197, folio not stated), and on an abandoned mound he finds karir, "another ancient Punjabi species of plant that is no longer found anywhere except in abandoned places" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 92, folio 74).

In the novelised chapter of *Walking with Nanak* on the Sikh capture of Hassan Abdal under Hari Singh, Khalid imagines the province from its western frontier. The Sikh soldiers "had arrived from the Punjab"; invaders had for almost a thousand years "crossed into the Punjab from the West", and so, in the Punjabi imagination as the damaged sentence can be read, "the West was a fearful place where giants resided" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 258, folio 232); Hari Singh wanted the tribesmen to know "that now it was the time of Punjab to create havoc in the Khyber" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 262, folio 236).

For the historians the Punjab is defined by comparison. Eaton sets it against Bengal, where the Mughal officials who arrived around 1620 found that "The Bengali diet of fish and rice, for example, contrasted sharply with the wheat and meat diet of the Punjab and seems to have posed a special stumbling block for immigrants" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 263, folio 251). Rizvi writes against W. H. McLeod's picture of the Punjab of Guru Nanak's time: McLeod, he says, "seems to be unaware of Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Quddus Gangohi who lived in Shahabad, his disciple, Shaikh Jalal of Thaneswar, and the Qadiris who had made the Panjab the main centre of their activities" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 420, folio 396).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- When Ranjit Singh became ruler of the Punjab: *Walking with Nanak* has him become "the sovereign of Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16), and elsewhere calls him "the Sikh ruler of Punjab, Maharaja Ranjit Singh in the nineteenth century" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 87, folio 69); *A White Trail* has him "crowned as the Ruler of Punjab" in 1801 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated).
- How many Hindus live in the Punjab: in one passage of *A White Trail* most Hindus are scattered in other provinces, "with only a few in Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 87, folio not stated); in others a significant Hindu population continues to live in Southern Punjab (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 31, folio not stated), and "The majority of the Hindu community is based in Sindh and Southern Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 233, folio not stated).
- Whether Baba Farid converted the Punjab's clans: most Jat and Rajput groups that became Muslim in the medieval period claim conversion by Baba Farid or by Baha' al-Haqq Zakariya (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 227, folio 215), and the clans of the southwestern Punjab said so in court in the 1930s (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 241, folio 229); but the earliest sources on Baba Farid "make no mention of his having converted anyone", and many of these clans, "in the course of their northward migrations up the rivers of the Punjab, had not yet reached the Ajudhan area during Baba Farid's lifetime" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 227, folio 215).
- The Sufism of the Punjab in Guru Nanak's time: W. H. McLeod, as Rizvi quotes him, holds that "the Panjabi sufism of Guru Nanak's period had evidently departed radically from the classical pattern of Arab and Persian Sufism", and that most Muslims Nanak met represented "the blend of modified orthodoxy and debased Sufism" then dominant in the Muslim community of the Panjab (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 419–420, folios 395–396); Rizvi answers that "all literate Panjabis, both Hindus and Muslims, at the time of Guru Nanak were conversant with" 'Attar, Rumi and Sa'di, and that the Qadiris had made the Panjab the main centre of their activities (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 420, folio 396).
