## History

Annemarie Schimmel places the beginning of Lahore's Muslim religious life at "The second wave of Muslim conquest in India, that of the Ghaznawids about the year 1000", which brought scholars into the Subcontinent and "theologians and poets as well" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 376, folio 345). In her account "Lahore became the first center of Persian-inspired Muslim culture in the Subcontinent", and it was in this town that Hujwiri composed his famous Persian treatise on Sufism (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 376, folio 345). She calls the city "Lahore, the capital of the Ghaznawids in India" and says that Hujwiri "died there in 1071" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 119, folio 88). In an earlier book she names the *Kashf al-maḥjūb* of Hujwiri, "who spent his last years in Lahore", as the first major treatise on Sufi theories in Persian (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 61, folio 51).

Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi gives the same eleventh century city a less settled face. In his telling "a number of mystics and scholars who had settled in Lahore were strongly hostile to the views of its author", "Some Lahore sufis believed that gnosis emanated from inspiration", and he speaks of a ‘captive among uncongenial folk’ in Lahore (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 131, folio 113).

Rizvi follows the city through the next centuries as a stage on the roads of the Sufis. It lay on "one of the main routes from Multan to Lahore and Delhi" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 158, folio 140). By the period he describes "Lahore had become the capital", and his narrative records that "the Khwaja reached Delhi from Lahore" and, of another figure, that "His father, Ahmad, migrated from Lahore to Delhi" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 135, 140, 145; folios 117, 122, 127). He records "the destruction of Lahore, by the Mongols in 1241", and notes that "A group of Lahore merchants visited Gujarat" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 241–242, folios 221–222). He also records Babur's conquest of the city, which he dates 1523-24 (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 367, folio 345).

Shemeem Burney Abbas carries the city's religious offices back to the Delhi sultans: her ancestors "were appointed as Muftis (religious scholars) of the city of Lahore in the reign of the Muslim slave king Shamsuddin Iltutmish" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 25, folio not stated).

Scott Kugle's Lahore is the Mughal city. He calls it "the Mughal regional capital of Lahor (Lahore in contemporary Pakistan)" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 202, folio not stated), and elsewhere "a seasonal capital city and home of the Mughal court during its perennial rotation" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 233, folio 212). He describes how "a large public square stretches out before the high walls of the Red Fort in Lahore, into which crowds surge, eager to witness the public execution of criminals" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 203, folio 182), the condemned being "rebels against the Mughal emperor, highway robbers, and tax evaders" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 203, folio 182). The book calls the fort the Red Fort and does not otherwise identify it.

Kugle names the officers through whom the Mughal city governed religious and moral life. There was the *kotwal*, or police chief (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, pp. 204, 233, folios 183, 212), and the chief judge (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 233, folio 212). The *Shaykh al-Islam* was the "chief religious scholar and jurist of the realm" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 239, folio 218), and the *Qadi* set *taʿzir* penalties at his discretion (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 238, folio 217), with the emperor's court above them (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 240, folio 219). Kugle distinguishes *hadd*, "exemplary punishment of a fixed form dictated by scripture", from *taʿzir*, "discretionary punishment of a flexible form determined by the will of authorities" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 235, folio 214).

## A City of Saints

For Schimmel the oldest point in this landscape is Hujwiri's grave: "His shrine, called that of Data Ganj Bakhsh, is still a popular place of pilgrimage in Lahore." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 119, folio 88). She adds that "his tomb still provides a place of pilgrimage for the Panjabis" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 376, folio 345). Omar Kasmani calls Data Darbar "Lahore's most prominent shrine complex" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 65, folio 48), and Kugle calls ʿAli Hujwiri "Lahore's patron saint" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 206, folio 185).

Kugle gives the same title, in a plural form, to Shah Hussayn, "one of Lahore's patron saints" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 204, folio 183) and "one of the patron saints of Lahore" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 241, folio 220). Schimmel writes that Madho Lal Husain "is buried together with his Hindu beloved in a modest tomb near the Shalimar gardens in Lahore" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, pp. 170–171, folios 152–153).

In the Mughal period, Schimmel says, the Qadiriyya was "represented in Lahore by Mian Mir, who had come there from Sind, together with his mystically inclined sister Bibi Jamal" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 392, folio 361). She describes the *Sakinat al-auliya*, a work she associates with the Mughal heir apparent, as "a fine biography of Mian Mir and the Qadiriyya in Lahore", and gives it no date (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 392, folio 361).

Rizvi records an outlying burial that the city has since absorbed: "Zinjani was buried in Chah Miran, which is now a suburb of Lahore" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112).

Kugle gives the city's Sufi geography in a few strokes: "the empty lands outside the city walls" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 206, folio 185); "Baghabanpur on the outskirts of Lahore (the neighborhood is now within the expanding city just next to the Shalimar gardens)" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 215, folio 194); the Ravi River (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 221, folio 200); and, beyond the city, "the village of Babupur, far from Lahore" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 212, folio 191). Abbas describes the Shalimar as "Gardens built by Shah Jehan, the Mughal emperor", and gives no date for their building (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 188, folio not stated).

Kugle finds the city's antinomian Sufis still attached to its great Qadiri saints: "In Lahore today, the Qalandars (who completely abandon legal norms and social conventions and even gender distinctions) are still associated with the great" Qadiri "saints of the city, like Shah Hussayn, Shah Jamal, and Miyan Mir" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 326, folio 305, ch. 4 n. 7). Robert Rozehnal records the opposite caution from inside a living Sufi order: "There are many mazars of unknown shaykhs in Lahore. Some of them are qalandars", who "can only give their visitors what they have", so that "they make them qalandars as well"; "In our silsila we avoid this." (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 207, folio 199). The statement names no shrine and speaks of the city's lesser tombs as a class.

Kasmani's ethnography adds a women's shrine of the Shiʿa: he describes "Bibiyan-pak-daman, a shrine in Lahore dedicated to women saints and particularly revered by the Shiʿa" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 125, folio 108).

The city is also the resting place of modern figures. Schimmel notes that Muhammad Iqbal "is buried in Lahore", and that a few years before she wrote a *maqam* in his honour was erected in the garden of Jalaluddin Rumi's tomb in Konya (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 270, folio 239). Michel Boivin records that "Muhammad Iqbal was a poet born in Lahore, who was granted a PhD by the University of Munich." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 212, folio 199, Ch. 3 n. 14). Schimmel says that Bullhe Shah (1680-1752) "lived near Lahore", without naming his burial place (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 419, folio 388). Rozehnal relates that Faruq Ahmad "died in Lahore on February 13, 1945 after a prolonged bout of pneumonia", and that "Throughout the time of the funeral, a light rain fell from that cloud of mercy only above that spot in Lahore" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 75, folio 65).

The one named building in the city that Rizvi mentions which is not a shrine is "the Padishahi mosque at Lahore", in a footnote on Goldziher's discussion of the veneration of relics (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 339, folio 317, footnote 3).

## Communities

Kugle's Lahore has a Brahmin community with standing in the city: "It is not clear how the two managed to overcome this conflict with Madho's family and the Brahmins of Lahore." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 212, folio 191). The city's Muslim elite judged its saints too: "Shah Hussayn earned himself disrepute among the Muslim notables of Lahore, who saw him as a dissolute drunk" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 208, folio 187 [`l&J`, text-layer?]), and "this open passion ruined his reputation throughout Lahore" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 210, folio 189).

Abbas makes an argument about women and shrines for Pakistan as a whole that frames the city's shrines too. "In the major mosques in Pakistan, for instance, there is a small space where women can go and pray on Friday or on religious festivals such as the Eid." In the local neighbourhood mosques, she writes, a woman cannot offer her ritual prayers: "Thus, the important spheres of religious and spiritual participation for women are the Sufi shrines" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 18, folio not stated).

Abbas also places her own family among the city's old religious households. She writes of "the old walled city of Lahore from where I trace the ancestry of my maternal family" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 24, folio not stated), and of "the Muftis and Gardezis who belonged to Lahore and Multan" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 26, folio not stated). "Mufti Ghulam Sarwar, one of my ancestors, is mentioned together with my paternal grandfather, Mufti Muzaffar Din, in Kanhyalal's history of Lahore." She adds that Mufti Ghulam Sarwar wrote a history of the Panjab, *Tarikh-e Makhazan Panjab* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 193, folio not stated).

In Rozehnal's account of the Sufi order he studied, Lahore is where its devotion is most dense. A murid "now runs an informal religious school (madrasa) for women in Lahore" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 44, folio 34). Two weekly circles of remembrance meet in private homes: "a senior male murid hosts a weekly halqa-i zikr in his home", and "A female disciple of Shaykh Wahid Bakhsh Rabbani does the same, leading a separate session comprised mostly of women" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 201, folio 193).

Kasmani follows fakirs who move between Sindh and Lahore. One of them, Murad, "relocated to Lahore" after leaving a lodge at Sehwan and was reported at Bibiyan Pak Daman (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 125, folio 108). The young woman he married was "a young pilgrim visiting from Lahore" whom he had consulted at the shrine of Bodlo (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 125, folio 108).

## Shrines, Endowments and the State

Carl W. Ernst and Bruce B. Lawrence show the colonial state reaching into shrine succession through the city's schools: "a debate raged in Chishti circles in the 1930s over whether or not the British could compel a minor who had become diwan at Pakpattan to obtain education at a British college in Lahore" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 116, folio 103). The college is not named.

Kasmani ties Lahore to the state's project at Sehwan, under which "special trains like the Jhule Lal Express were initiated to facilitate the annual journey of pilgrims connecting Sehwan with Lahore, the capital of Punjab" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 70, folio 53). Private patrons in the city did the same: the Bukhari brothers "would go on to organize pilgrimages from Lahore to Sehwan, reserving entire trains for their annual journeys", and "Private patronage such as theirs greatly complemented the state's project in Sehwan as much as it was motivated by it." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 61, folio 44).

Violence at a Lahore shrine changed how shrines were guarded across the country. "A terror attack at Lahore's most prominent shrine had led to security measures being beefed up at major Sufi sites across the country." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 104, folio 87). Kasmani names neither the shrine nor the date of the attack; the year 2011 in his account is when he saw the tightening at Sehwan.

On the economy of the city's shrines Kasmani does not write himself but points elsewhere: "For an account of the distribution of shrine revenues and the economic dimension of a Sufi shrine in Lahore, see Strothmann (2016, chapters 5 and 6)." He does not say which shrine (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 189, folio 172, ch. 1 n. 26).

Rozehnal places the provincial government in the city, "the governor of Punjab in Lahore" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 39, folio 29).

## Festivals and Practice

Madho Lal's anniversary, Schimmel writes, is "still celebrated in Lahore near the Shalimar Garden on the last Saturday in March" and is famous as *Melā Chirāghān*, the 'Fair of Lamps' (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384). Abbas records the *Melā Chirāg* as held annually at the Shalimar Gardens (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 95, 185, folios not stated).

Kasmani describes the city at the time of a saint's festival from the side of a fakir who arrived at Data Darbar: "It was the time of the saint's festival, and countless other fakirs had gathered in the city." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 65, folio 48). He also met a "visiting fakir from Lahore who in the autumn of 2011 was in Sehwan to perform his duty at the shrine of Lal", who kept his retreat by the river there (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 9).

Music at the city's shrines has a long history in the sources Abbas uses. She cites Tahir Lahori, who "also mentions women musicians who performed hazrī at the Sufi shrines in Lahore, especially at the time of the urs", and who uses the term *hazrī* of qawwals who sang at the Sufi shrines in Lahore both before the partition and after it; the year of the partition is lost in the printed text (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 90, folio not stated). Lahori's essay carries the practice in its title, *Aulīyā ke Urs*, published in *Sohna Shehr Lahore* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 227, folio not stated). Abbas herself interviewed a grassroots musician, Zarina Khatoon, in Lahore (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 218, folio not stated).

Regula Burckhardt Qureshi's discography of other recordings of qawwal parties lists four from Lahore: Kausar Jabin, Mubarak Ali and Fateh Ali, Muhammad Siddiq and Alaf Din Pathan, with no shrine or assembly attached to any of them (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 282, folio 262).

Ernst and Lawrence record a sixteenth century anecdote in which "a man of Lahore came and said" that in his time there was no one worthy of listening to music, *samaʿ*: "In this time there is no one worthy" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 27, folio 13).

Rozehnal describes the quieter practice of a modern order in the city. His fieldwork included "frequent attendance at the zikr circles in Lahore" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 141, folio 133), and the full repertoire of remembrance he prints was recorded "at a gathering on a Thursday evening in October 2000" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 202, folio 194). Hospitality is refused at these gatherings: "That is why serving any food or even a cup of tea is strictly forbidden, because some people then might come just to socialize" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 203, folio 195).

The calendar of the saints is itself a Lahore product. Ernst and Lawrence write that "today one can still acquire current Urdu almanacs printed in Bombay and Lahore that prominently feature the death anniversaries of Sufi saints of the Indian subcontinent" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 105, folio 92). Of al-Busiri's *Burda*, the devotional ode, Schimmel writes: "Copies are still being sold in the shops of Lahore and elsewhere." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 256, folio 225).

## The Place in the Books

The scholars of this group saw Lahore from very different distances. For Rozehnal it was home in the field: he spent the academic year of 1995 and 1996 studying Urdu in the city (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 21, folio 10), and writes, "In Pakistan, I was based in the cultural capital of Lahore" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 26, folio 15). A photograph of the dead shaykh circulated among "several friends in Lahore" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 21, folio 10).

Rozehnal also shows the city as the publishing centre of the order he studied. A Sufi Foundation founded by a senior murid "published a series of translations and original writings by Wahid Bakhsh during the 1980s"; Bazm-i Ittehad al-Muslimin was "founded in 1984 by the shaykh's nephew"; and "Beginning in 1996, Talifat-i Shaheedi, established by another murid of Shahidullah Faridi, has published four additional texts, three of them in English" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 130, folio 121). Commercial houses carry the rest, among them Ziya al-Qur'an, Qausin Publishers and al-Faisal Publishers, and "At present, some fifty different texts are produced and distributed from a number of publishing houses" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 130, folio 121). The order's books reach readers through "the Urdu bookstores of Karachi and Lahore" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 111, folio 102). He cites the city's press too, "the Lahore-based English language weekly, The Friday Times" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 37, folio 27), and measures another great shrine from it: Pakpattan is "120 miles southwest of Lahore" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 35, folio 25).

The same city of print appears in the older scholarship as a place of publication, and that is all it is there. Schimmel's bibliography lists Lahore houses that printed mystical poetry, among them Ashraf, the University of the Punjab, Urdu Dunya and Allah wale ki qaumi dukan, whose numbered chapbook series carried Panjabi mystical verse (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, pp. 318, 321, 325, 326, 329, 331, 333, 340, 341). That series included a Panjabi version of the *Burda*, *Qasida-i Burda Panjabi* (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 331, folio 313), a commentary on Bullhe Shah (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 333, folio 315), and a collection of Panjabi mystical verse with no author named (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 329, folio 311). She notes two Panjabi verse translations published at Lahore, by Pir Imam Shah and by Maulana Muhammad Shahuddin, the first in a year whose reading is uncertain in the printed text and the second in 1939 (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 254, folio 236). Qureshi names Lahore publishers of the literature on qawwali, among them Sheikh Muhammad Ashraf and the Institute of Islamic Culture (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, pp. 269, 272, 273, 275; folios 249, 252, 253, 255).

Lahore is also a city of manuscripts and collectors. The Punjab University Library holds the "unpublished original manuscript" of *Haqiqat al-Fuqara'* (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 218, folio 197, ch. 4 n. 27), under the shelfmark 3253/248 Farsi (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 338, folio not stated); Kugle searched "libraries in Lahore" for a printing said to date from 1966 and found none (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 328, folio 307, ch. 4 n. 27). Ernst and Lawrence owe one of their manuscripts to "Mr. Khalil al-Rahman Dawoodi of Lahore" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 159, folio 147), the same man who gave them a manuscript copied at Taunsa (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 243, folio 233). They also record that the twentieth century Chishti Zauqi Shah worked as a journalist in Peshawar, Lahore, Hyderabad in Sind and Karachi (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 139, folio 126).

For Abbas the city's saints are a genre of local writing, and she cites it: M. Latif Malik's *Aulīyā-e Lahore* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 211, 228, folios not stated), Samina Qureshi's *Lahore: The City Within* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 231, folio not stated), and Kanhyalal's *Tarikh-e Lahore* in a Lahore reprint (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 193, 211, 226, folios not stated). The years of all three are lost in the printed text. Lahore is also the base from which she works: "Kasur is seventy miles from Lahore" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 86, folio not stated); she "started from Lahore on a beautiful spring afternoon" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 105, folio not stated); and "Khalida and Jaleel Naqvi participated in the fieldwork in Lahore and Kasur" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 30, folio not stated). The Naqvis were custodians of the shrine of Hazrat Pir Sabir at Paran Kalyar, interviewed in Lahore (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 217, folio not stated). She places Jhang Siyal "to the southwest of Lahore in Pakistan" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 121–122, folios not stated), and notes that landlords' sons now "live in large urban centers like Karachi, Lahore, or Islamabad" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 81, folio not stated).

In Pnina Werbner and Helene Basu's volume, Lahore marks the far edge of the area from which volunteers come to serve at the *ʿurs* at Ghamkol Sharif (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 122, folio 111). It is also the city to which one woman, Rabi‘a, returns, and the frame for its neighbourhood of Samnabad (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 180, folio 169; Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 188, folio 177).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- Who is Lahore's patron saint: Kugle calls ʿAli Hujwiri "Lahore's patron saint" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 206, folio 185), and calls Shah Hussayn "one of Lahore's patron saints" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 204, folio 183) and "one of the patron saints of Lahore" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 241, folio 220).
- The Mughal city's standing: Kugle calls Lahore "the Mughal regional capital of Lahor" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 202, folio not stated) and also "a seasonal capital city and home of the Mughal court during its perennial rotation" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 233, folio 212).
- The festival of lamps, its name and its ground: Schimmel names it *Melā Chirāghān* and places it "near the Shalimar Garden" on the last Saturday in March (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384); Abbas names it the *Melā Chirāg* and places it at the Shalimar Gardens, with no date (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 95, 185, folios not stated).
