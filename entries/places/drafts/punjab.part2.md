## The Province in History

The books give the name Punjab to more than one unit. For Sujan Rai Bhandari, describing his own time, about 1107 AH, it is the Mughal *suba* of Lahore, beside the *suba* of Multan, and in his narrative of earlier reigns it is the *wilayat* of Punjab, or simply Punjab under a governor (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 510; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 405; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 228). For Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori, writing in 1875, Punjab is all the country from Delhi to Peshawar that was then attached to it (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 37, folio 25). For Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti it is the land of Punjab that the British conquered and governed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 137). For the modern translators and annotators it is "the Punjab" or "the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 3, folio 2; Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 16, folio xvi). The history below is told in each author's own unit.

Chishti reaches furthest back. Alexander's historians, he writes, name Porus and a second king whose name is uncertainly printed, both of the Chandar Bansi line, of whom one lived at Hastinapur and the other in Punjab; he does not say which lived where (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 799). Bhandari relates that after Yudhishthira gave up his throne, at the coming of the Kaljug age, the five Pandava brothers and Draupadi travelled through the lands of Thatta, as the name is probably to be read, Multan and Punjab on their way to the mountain of Badrinath; he says nothing more of these lands (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 479).

Of Raja Jaipal, Bhandari notes that the historians of Hind do not count him among the rulers of Delhi, but that since his seat of government was a fort whose name is uncertainly printed, with another reading in the editor's footnote, it appears that the lands of Samana and Punjab and others, apart from Delhi, were in his hands (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 427). He dates Sultan Nasir al-Din's accession to 367 AH, the year uncertainly printed, and explains that the raids of that time succeeded because Hindustan had then fallen into petty kingdoms and no one ruler held independent power (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 426–427).

In Bhandari's history of the *wilayat* of Multan, which he says he found in certain histories, Islam began there in 99 AH with Muhammad Qasim, in the time of Hajjaj bin Yusuf (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 298). Sultan Mahmud of Ghazni took the *wilayat* from the Qarmatian heretics and made Islam current there, and when Shihab al-Din Ghori conquered Hindustan, Multan too came into his possession (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 298). From the beginning of the year 600 AH to 855 AH, both numerals uncertainly printed, the *wilayat* remained in the hands of the sultans of Delhi, until in the reign of Sultan Muhammad Shah, of Khizr Khan's line, Hindustan fell into petty kingdoms and the governor of Multan declared his independence (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 298).

Lahori tells of Punjab under Sultan Mahmud's successors. When Masud came to the throne after Sultan Mahmud's death, his young son Majdud was given Punjab as his governorship, with Ayaz appointed as his tutor (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 193, folio 181). After Masud died and Maudud took the throne, Majdud rebelled and set up a kingdom of his own in Hindustan; Maudud marched against him, Majdud died suddenly on the day of Eid during the siege of Lahore, and Maudud established his hold over Punjab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 193, folio 181). Retelling *Tuhfat al-Wasilin* under the Shahid Ganj entry, Lahori writes that when the Ghaznavid sultans came to rule Punjab and Lahore became the capital, their rule held well in Punjab for a long time, until in Bahram Shah's time their quarrels among themselves left the government of Punjab altogether weak; Raja Anangpal then took Lahore, and there was Hindu rule there for a time, its length uncertainly printed, until an army came again from Ghazni (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 264, folio 252). His modern editor calls *Tuhfat al-Wasilin* a fabricated tazkira (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 191–192, folios 179–180). Bhandari writes that Khusraw Shah, having taken Lahore, governed the *wilayat* of Punjab until the end of his life, dying in 555, and that his son Khusraw Malik succeeded him in the government of the *wilayat*, his rule ending when he went to Ghazni in 582 AH (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 405).

Lahori records, from a book he calls *Tazkira-e Hamidiya*, that Chingiz Khan's army entered Punjab in pursuit of the prince Jalal al-Din Khwarazmi and plundered the whole land of Punjab, and that the people of Lahore fought them for two months; he adds that the account is not well known and perhaps no one will believe it, but that it would be no wonder if it had happened (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 248, folio 236).

The raids of the Mughals from the north run through the books' account of the Delhi sultans. Urged to conquer Gujarat and Malwa, Balban answered, in Bhandari's account, that since the Mughals were always raiding the *wilayat* of Punjab it was not right to go from Delhi to distant lands, and that to keep one's own kingdom strong and at peace was better than to raid the kingdoms of others (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 388). Chishti writes that Balban's nephew Sher Khan, posted to guard the Indus, saved Punjab from the Mughals' attacks and drove them out of Ghazni (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 810). In Jalal al-Din Khalji's reign, in a year printed as 693 and uncertain, the Chingizid Mughals set out for Punjab with a great army; the sultan marched against them, they made peace, and their leader, a kinsman of Hulagu Khan, came in and became a Muslim with some other amirs; the sultan called him son, gave him a daughter and settled them at Ghiyaspur, which came to be called Mughalpura (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 370). Bhandari does not say where in Punjab the Mughals arrived (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 370). At the end of the Khaljis, Chishti writes, Ghazi Beg Tughluq, governor of Multan, gathered a great army in Punjab against Khusrau Malik (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 814).

In Timur's time, Bhandari records, Jasrath Khokhar, the name uncertainly printed, escaped from Timur's captivity and reached Punjab (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 191). Under the Lodis, Tatar Khan was governor of Punjab, appointed by Sultan Bahlul Lodi; for fear of him the son of the raja of Jammu, his name uncertainly printed, left Jammu for Kashmir, and Sultan Muhammad Shah and then Sultan Fath Shah of Kashmir each sought his help (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 190). Before Tatar Khan's governorship, it is said, a flood surged through Punjab so that all the land from the Sutlej to the Chenab lay under water and towns and villages were destroyed, and the Mughals of Balkh and Kabul raided Punjab every year, so that the country lay waste and its produce was small (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 523). When Tatar Khan became governor of Lahore in the time of Sultan Bahlul Lodi, Rai Ram Deo Bhatti took the whole of Punjab from him in farm for five lakh *tankas*, the first numeral uncertainly printed (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 522–523).

Meanwhile Multan went its own way. It is said, Bhandari writes, that in the reign of Sultan Ala al-Din the Second of Delhi the *wilayat* of Multan passed out of his control to the Langah people (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525). Sultan Husain Langah took the fort of Shor from Ghazi Khan, and Chiniot, and in a short time held the country as far as Karor Kot and Dhankot, the names uncertainly printed (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 296). Tatar Khan, governor of Punjab, was sent against him by Sultan Bahlul Lodi together with Bahlul's son (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 296). After Bahlul, Sultan Sikandar, ruler of Delhi, made peace with Sultan Husain on the terms that each side would rest content with its own *wilayat* and not raid the other's border (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 295). In a damaged passage Bhandari writes that Mirza Shah Husain Arghun ruled the *wilayat* for four years, and that for a period of eighty years up to 936 AH, the year uncertainly printed, it had been out of the hands of Delhi's rulers, after which it entered the dominion of Delhi and Prince Kamran Mirza took possession of it (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 294). In Babur's reign, he writes, the *wilayat* of Multan, long lost to the sultans of Delhi, was conquered by Kamran Mirza's good management and sword (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 298). Babur, after reaching Punjab, had granted Multan to Arghun with the charge that he strive for the prosperity of the country and the well being of its subjects (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 295). Before the battle with Rana Sanga, Babur's ministers advised him to strengthen a few forts and withdraw to stay in the *wilayat* of Punjab; he refused (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 299).

The struggles between the Mughals and the Sur passed through Punjab. Kamran's *jagir* was Lahore and Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 294), and Sher Khan by deceit gave Kamran hope that the *wilayat* of Lahore would be confirmed to him, so that Kamran withdrew from aiding Humayun (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 286). Later Kamran went to Salim Khan, son of Sher Khan, who had taken the throne of Hindustan after his father's death and was at that time in Punjab, the word for his presence there uncertainly printed; Kamran arrived at a town whose name is uncertain, and Salim Khan sent his son and Maulana Abdullah Sultanpuri to meet him (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 275). In Islam Shah's reign, whose accession Bhandari gives as 952, uncertainly printed (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 265), the Niazis revolted: Azam Humayun defeated Khwaja Wais and pursued him, then laid hands on the towns and countryside of Punjab, plundering the cattle and goods of its inhabitants, so that a great disturbance arose in Punjab and a strange state befell those who lived there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 262). Islam Shah marched from Delhi with a heavy army and much artillery, and the Niazis held out in Dhankot until they were defeated (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 262).

On Humayun's return to Hindustan, which Bhandari dates to 962, uncertainly printed (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 256), Shah Abul Maali was posted towards Lahore to manage the affairs of the *wilayat* of Punjab (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 255). Sultan Sikandar came out of the hills, seized the *parganas* of Punjab and began to collect their revenue as far as two *parganas* whose names are uncertainly printed; Shah Abul Maali, who treated his own soldiers badly, failed to repel him, and Akbar and Bairam Khan were sent (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 255). Akbar was on his way to Punjab against Sikandar when the news of Humayun's death reached him at Kalanaur, and Bhandari dates the accession to 963 AH (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 252).

The early years of the new reign were spent securing the province. Sultan Sikandar came out of the hills and began to collect revenue in the *wilayat* of Punjab, and disorder broke out there, so the emperor marched from Delhi towards Punjab (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 248). In the second year of the reign Mankot fell and Sikandar was driven out, and security returned to the *wilayat* of Punjab (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 247). Mir Muhammad Khan Kalan, brother of Atga Khan, and the other amirs posted in Punjab were sent against Sultan Adam the Gakhar (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 238). At his fall, which Bhandari places in the sixth year of the reign, Bairam Khan took the road to Punjab and, after Bikaner, turned towards Punjab and reached it by a route whose names are uncertainly printed (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 245; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 246). After Bairam Khan's fall Muzaffar Khan became revenue officer of the *pargana* of Parsarur, which Bhandari calls a dependency of Punjab (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 205).

Bhandari speaks of a governor of Punjab in these years. Ibrahim Husain Mirza and Masud Husain Mirza turned towards Punjab by way of Dipalpur, the name uncertainly printed, and Khan Jahan, governor of Punjab, who was close to finishing his campaign at Nagarkot, made peace with the raja there and marched against them (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 228). When the imperial standards moved towards Punjab, the Mirzas came out of Sambhal, plundered and besieged the fort of Delhi, and the emperor went from Punjab towards Delhi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 231). Muhammad Hakim Mirza, Bhandari writes, crossed the Indus many times to trouble and harm the people of Punjab (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 203). Emboldened by the revolt of the amirs of Bengal, he came from Kabul with an army to Punjab, stirred up disorder and harmed the people of that country, but on the imperial advance he could not stay in Punjab and returned to Kabul (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 202). The emperor set out towards Punjab to put an end to Muhammad Hakim Mirza's disturbance (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 203), and Bhandari dates these years by the reign, among them the twenty sixth and twenty eighth regnal years (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 201; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 203). Zahur ul-Hasan Sharib records that the emperor Akbar, on his way to Punjab to put down a revolt, halted at Thanesar on Saturday 2 Muharram 980 AH; the name of the rebel is printed in a form that cannot be read with certainty (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 243, folio 239). His footnote gives the Persian *Anwar al-Arifin* as his source (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 245, folio 241).

Around 985, the year uncertainly printed, Yusuf Shah of Kashmir fled by way of Jammu to Mirza Yusuf Khan, governor of Punjab, and with him and Raja Man Singh reached Fatehpur (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 186). During the emperor's residence at Lahore, Zain Khan Koka, sent to chastise the *zamindars* of the hills, brought all their rajas and rais to obedience by the force of the sword and the strength of good counsel and brought several of the rajas to court (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 170). Raja Basu, a *zamindar* of a place uncertainly printed that was dependent on Punjab, later rebelled (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 170). Bhandari speaks of these mountains as the hills of Punjab, but nowhere says where they lie (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 146; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 170). In Akbar's reign, he writes, Raja Todar Mal, the chief *diwan*, confirmed the *wilayat* of Multan to the Baluch, making it a strong vanguard between Khurasan and Hindustan and setting a firm barrier on the borders of both countries (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525).

In the narrative that follows, eight months after an accession that Bhandari dates to 1014 AH, uncertainly printed, Prince Khusrau rebelled (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 147; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 148). Dilawar Khan, governor of Lahore, held the fort against him, and Raja Basu, one of the chief *zamindars* of the hills of Punjab, who had just joined the emperor, was sent after the prince (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 146). The prince was captured at the end of Muharram 1015, the year uncertainly printed (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 145).

The war of succession, in years printed as 1068 and 1069, both uncertainly, was fought partly across the province (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 64–66). Dara Shikoh had agreed with Shuja that while he himself prepared the means of revolt in Punjab, Shuja would march from Bengal as far as Allahabad, and that after success they would divide the kingdom and its wealth equally (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 76). Dara told Sulaiman Shikoh to reach Punjab by a road whose name is uncertainly printed, and the Barha sayyids advised going to Punjab by way of Saharanpur (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 74). At Lahore Dara sent letters of conciliation, full of kindness and promises of favour, through all the surrounding parts of the *suba*, urging the soldiers of that land, of every place, people and tribe, to enlist with him (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 77). The emperor's march towards the *wilayat* of Punjab came in the rains: crossing the Sutlej and the Beas without boats or fords was judged all but impossible, and most of the nobles did not advise moving in that season, yet he set out on the seventh of Dhu'l Qa'da, which Bhandari makes the seventeenth of Amardad, both numbers uncertainly printed (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 70). While the standards were in Punjab in pursuit of Dara, Shuja took his chance and set out for Allahabad (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 61). The halt at Lahore was made to put the affairs of the *suba* of Punjab in order and to give the army rest and comfort (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 64). There Khalilullah Khan was appointed governor of Lahore with a reward of one crore *dams*, the figure uncertainly printed, and Lashkar Khan was moved from the *suba* of Kashmir to be governor of Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 64; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 65). The *faujdari* of the hills of Punjab went first to Khanjar Khan, *faujdar* of Bhera, the name uncertainly printed, after he left Dara (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 67), and then, at the Lahore halt, to Mir Miran, the name and his descent uncertainly printed, who had received the title of Mir Khan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 64). At the weighing festival of those months the governor of Lahore was honoured with robes of honour, the words of the gift uncertainly printed, and the page break leaves his name apart from his title (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 62; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 63).

The eighteenth century is, in every one of these books, a time of ruin. Taufiq Rafat's translator's note places Bulleh Shah's life in "a time of great upheaval. The Mughal Empire was breaking up", when "the Punjab was being persistently rent by petty chiefs and marauders" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 16, folio xvi). The introduction to the selection writes that "After Aurangzeb, the halcyon days that the Punjab had enjoyed quickly came to an end", and that the badly defended North Western region of the empire became increasingly subject to foreign invasions and inner quarrels (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 26, folio 6). It dates "the beginning of the misfortunes of the Punjab" to the period of Muhammad Shah, when the decay of Mughal administration "gave rise to small local tyrannies which lasted into the 19th century" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, pp. 26–27, folios 6–7). After Nadir Shah's invasion, it says, "Marauding Sikh bands formed themselves into robber baronies and imposed their own system of extortion on the Punjab farmer", and Ahmad Shah Abdali's "treatment of the Punjab weakened the government in Lahore and delivered the various doabs into the hands of the Sikh" confederacy of the misls (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 27, folio 7). Bulleh Shah, it continues, "was fated to see a second invasion of the Punjab by Ahmad Shah Abdali in 1748 and then a third in 1752" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 27, folio 7). "In the years to come, when plunder became routine in the Punjab, Bulleh Shah had no choice of factions", and the Sikh marauders under Banda Bairagi "were to lay waste the whole of the province excluding Lahore and Qasur", taxing the common peasant to raise armies on both sides (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, pp. 42–43, folios 22–23). "As Bulleh Shah lay dying in 1758 at the age of 78, the rape of the Punjab was proceeding apace" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 28, folio 8).

The annotator of Charles Frederick Usborne's translation of Waris Shah writes that "The Punjab has often been the battleground of invading forces from the North", that the campaigns of Mahmud of Ghazna are famous, and that the poet may be thinking of Ahmad Shah Abdali, whose capital was Kandahar and who in 1748 began a series of invasions of India through the Punjab (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 8, folio 7). In his epilogue, which he dates to the year 1180 Hijri, Waris Shah writes: "Of all the wretched Punjab I am most concerned for Kasur" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 56, folio 55). The annotator glosses the lament: "The latter half of the eighteenth century, to which Waris Shah is referring here, marked the decline of the Mughal Empire in India, and was a period of great confusion and disturbance" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 57, folio 56).

The Persian and Urdu books tell the same years through the lives of saints and scholars. Chishti writes that in the reign of Alamgir the Second the Afghans attacked Punjab and took Multan and Lahore, while the strength of the Sikhs began to grow, and that Ghazi al-Din then resolved to conquer Punjab again (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 851). Lahori's modern editor writes that Ahmad Shah Abdali began his various attacks on Punjab in 1161 AH, 1748, and died in 1187 AH, 1773 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 277, folio 265). When Ahmad Shah Durrani first came to take Lahore, Lahori relates, and after plundering Punjab the king had gone back to Kabul, a man of Lahore told the saint Haji Muhammad Said Lahori that the Afghans had looted his house and carried his daughter off to Kabul (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 214, folio 202). Ghulam Murtaza Qasuri, the editor writes, wearied of the Sikh disorders in Punjab and migrated to Peshawar, and when Ahmad Shah Abdali came into Punjab he put a legal question to its scholars, Ghulam Murtaza among those who answered (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 152, folio 140). When the plundering Sikhs stirred up their pillage in Punjab, Shaikh Karam Shah Qureshi, who had lived in Lahore, went away to Lucknow, and on his way back he was killed by bandits near Shahjahanpur (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 190, folio 178).

The books remember Ranjit Singh's Punjab through its saints. It was well known throughout Punjab, Chishti writes of the saint Subah Shah, that the outward king was Maharaja Ranjit Singh (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 85). Lahori calls him Maharaja Ranjit Singh, ruler of Punjab, and writes that he had complete faith in the *majzub* Mastan Shah and offered him thousands of rupees, which Mastan Shah refused (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 246, folio 234). Chishti writes of Jaswant Rao Holkar and Amir Khan and Punjab in 1805, the year uncertainly printed, but the verb is garbled in the printed text, so it is not certain that he says they came into Punjab (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 580). All the people of Lahore, and indeed of Punjab, knew the quarter of the Faqir family by a name that the printed text gives uncertainly (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 585). On Ranjit Singh's death Kharak Singh took the seat of the government of Punjab (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 586), and Dhian Singh told Sher Singh that, God willing, he would soon be ruler of Punjab (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 588). On the day Raja Ranjit Singh died, Lahori relates, the *majzub* Taj Shah foretold the ruin of the kingdom of the Sikhs: it would last nine years more, and then the Firangis would be masters of Punjab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 244, folio 232).

Chishti tells the end of the Sikh state under Maharaja Dalip Singh. In 1848, the year uncertainly printed, a disturbance broke out in Punjab once more, and he follows it through Multan, the country of Hazara and four hard battles; on 29 March 1849, the date uncertainly printed, the whole country entered the British dominion (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 592). Rajab Ali Khan was Mir Munshi at the time of the conquest of the land of Punjab by the British government, with the title Mir Munshi of the dominions of Punjab, and he presented himself to the Lieutenant Governor of Punjab (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 137, 141; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 142). With British rule a legal settlement was established in Punjab (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 777).

## Saints and Pilgrimage

For Lahori the province is first of all a land of saints' tombs. He includes a saint when his tomb lies in the soil of Punjab: having given the Naqshbandi chain down to Khwaja Baqi Billah of Delhi, he explains that since none of the elders before Khwaja Baqi Billah had a tomb in the land of Punjab, he has left out their detailed accounts and begun with Khwaja Baqi Billah (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 127, folio 115).

Lahori traces how the orders reached the province. Of the Qadiri line, whose ancestors had left Baghdad in the time of Hulagu Khan, the first to come to Punjab was Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus Uchchi Jili Gilani, who came by way of Khurasan to Multan and settled at Uch (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 40, folio 28). His modern editor adds, on the authority of Ali Asghar Gilani, that he entered Uch in 887 AH; the date is the editor's, not the author's (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 40, folio 28). The Naushahi order flourished in Punjab through Shah Maruf Chishti and Qadiri, or through Sayyid Mubarak Haqqani, named just before him, for the text does not settle which of the two is meant (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 50, folio 38). The Naushahi way, whose faqirs in Punjab number in the thousands, began with Shaikh Haji Muhammad Qadiri, known as Hazrat Naushah Ganj Bakhsh (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 70, folio 58). The Chishti order spread in Hind and Punjab from Khwaja Muin al-Din Hasan Sijzi of Ajmer (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 85, folio 73), and in his editor's footnote the Chishti Nizami Fakhri line spread most widely in Punjab through Khwaja Sulaiman Taunsvi, born in 1184 AH and died in 1267 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 120, folio 108).

Lahori also records how Islam spread in Punjab, from a source his editor distrusts. In the time of Sultan Masud of Ghazni, he writes, citing *Tuhfat al-Wasilin*, Shaikh Muhammad Ismail Muhaddith Lahori, a Bukhari sayyid, settled in Lahore and taught *hadith* and *tafsir*; he was the first of the callers to Islam to come to Lahore, thousands became Muslims at his hands, and through him the religion of Islam spread in Punjab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 191, folio 179).

Smaller orders had small followings. In Chishti's day the Rasul Shahi order was not numerous: he writes that in all Punjab it had no house but this one, and that only four faqirs of the line were living at the time of writing, with no other house, *takia* or *khanqah*, though he then names one more house near Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 145). He dismisses the claim that the Jalali faqirs are Chihil Tani as belonging to the talk of the ignorant of Punjab (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 623). Of one boy, a *sajjada nashin* foretold that he would be the *subahdar* of *faqr* in Punjab (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 84).

The death of a saint could move the whole province. At Hazrat Husain's death, Chishti writes, there was hardly anyone in Punjab who did not grieve, and in the mourning for Husain a clamour like that of Karbala rose in Punjab; some words of the passage cannot be read (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 70).

Of Baba Farid, Waris Shah writes in his invocation: "When Shakarganj made his abode at Pakpattan, the Punjab was delivered of all its troubles" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 2, folio 1). His annotator objects: "Much as one would like to agree with Waris Shah this is not historically correct. The Punjab has had plenty of troubles even after Baba Farid honoured it by making it his home and his place of burial!" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 2, folio 1). Shah Hussein, writes his translator Naveed Alam, "continues to command great reverence in Punjab" more than four hundred years after his death (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 6, folio 6).

The annotator of Waris Shah records the beliefs and devotions of the countryside. "According to popular superstition in the Punjab countryside" *pirs*, *fakirs* and *jogis* can, by the force of their piety, make the Moving Finger of Destiny cancel what it has written (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 31, folio 30). Lighting lamps in a mosque, even with ordinary oil, "is still a well-known way of expression of gratitude to God for His favours", and "Punjabi mothers still promise lamp-lighting in mosques when they pray for their children" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 41, folio 40). Bhang "is a favourite with the mystically inclined, particularly among Muslims and Sikhs", and is still taken "in the old Punjab area by wandering mendicants and many of those seeking spiritual exaltation or sexual pleasure" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 12, folio 11). The verse's dawn at the mosque, he writes, "is how a morning begins even now in a Punjab village" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 5, folio 4).

## Communities

The province's peoples moved and settled across it. Under the Langah sultans, Bhandari writes, Malik Sohrab Dodai, father of Ismail Khan and Fath Khan, came with his tribe from the country of Kech and Makran, and Sultan Husain gave him a *jagir* from the fort of Kot Kardaz to Dhankot, the names uncertainly printed (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 295). Many more Baluch followed, the rest of the country along the Indus was assigned to the Baluch, and little by little the land from Sitpur to Dhankot came to them, so that from those days it has been in the hands of Malik Sohrab's descendants; Bhandari does not say where these places lie (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 295). In another passage he gives the Baluch who had come from Kech Makran a *jagir* from Karor Kot to Mankot (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525). The Mughals who made peace with Jalal al-Din Khalji became Muslims and were settled at the place that came to be called Mughalpura (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 370).

The annotator of Waris Shah sets out the castes and tribes of "the old Punjab". "Ranjha is still a well-known caste of the Jats in the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 3, folio 2). "Khojas are a well-known tribe of Hindu converts to Islam in the old Punjab who are mainly engaged in trade" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 7, folio 6). "The Gujars are a famous tribe of the old Punjab who keep animals and sell their milk", and towns such as Gujranwala, Gujrat and Gujar Khan are named after them (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 19, folio 18). The Sial tribe "is one of the few tribes in the Punjab which allows women to inherit property under customary law" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 20, folio 19).

The same annotator describes a village life shared across faiths. Judging by their names some of Hir's friends are Hindus, and "This symbolises the atmosphere of the village in the old Punjab, where the village community as a whole was regarded as one social unit irrespective of caste or creed"; even a sweeper woman would be addressed as daughter, sister or mother (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 20, folio 19). "It may appear strange that a Muslim family should depend on Hindu Brahmans for fixing a marriage but this was by no means unusual in the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 26, folio 25). "Brides in the old Punjab wore coloured (and colourful) garments", while white clothes were by tradition worn by widows, particularly among the Hindus, and childless Muslim widows wore black or white; "The social pattern is now changed" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 28, folio 27). "A traditional Punjab marriage still retains the symbolism of ‘marriage by capture’", the bridegroom made to look like a general, and the bride's side now resisting only with abusive language (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 28, folio 27).

Chishti records marriage customs of his own day. Following the custom of the Muslims of Punjab, the bride's family in the story of the marriage of Hazrat Naushah's daughter demanded a hundred rupees in customary dues (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 484). He writes that the killing of daughters was an old custom among the descendants of Baba Nanak through Lakhmi Chand, who counted themselves the noblest of creatures in Punjab and would give a daughter to no one, because, he explains, the custom of that country is that the girl's father becomes subordinate to the boy's family (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 685).

Of the household and the village the annotator gives many details. "Old houses in the Punjab countryside had a big courtyard with a number of living-rooms and bedrooms round it", and a joint family of brothers living with their parents was quite common (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 16, folio 15). "A spinning-wheel (charkha) used to be the inseparable companion of every woman, old or young, in the rural Punjab", and "The spinning-wheel was an important link in rural society. Women spun cotton yarn, and the village weaver made it into cloth" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 17, folio 16). Spinning parties "were a famous institution of the Punjab village", where young girls, generally unmarried, competed in spinning skill and scandalous conversation (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 4, folio 3, note 14). Betrothed boys and girls of "the rural Punjab" do not see each other, and "After the ‘mangni’ or betrothal, a girl does not see her fiancé until marriage" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 48, folio 47). The Arsi "was one of the favourite ornaments of a bride in the old Punjab", and the newly married girl was not expected to do household work for a couple of months, as is "still the case in the less sophisticated parts of the Punjab countryside" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 26, folio 25). The ‘Mehndhi’, a fringe of intertwined strands of hair round the forehead, marks a virgin girl in the old Punjab and disappears at the wedding (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 43, folio 42). Printed red scarfs with white flowers were thrown at each other by young girls at play, "A favorite game with young girls in the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 21, folio 20).

Food and offerings follow the same pattern. "Lassi is a favourite drink in the Punjab countryside", and "Washing the hair with curds is still well known in the Punjab countryside" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 4, folio 3). Gur, raw sugar, "is well known as a ceremonial offering in the Punjab countryside", and an invitation accompanied by it cannot be trifled with (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 33, folio 32). Rice cooked in ghee and sprinkled with sugar is the common ceremonial dish, and "The institution is alive in the countryside of the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 23, folio 22). Millet "is even now considered inferior food in the Punjab countryside" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 44, folio 43). Basmati rice "is grown In the central districts of the old Panjab", the best in the subcontinent and now a valuable export from Pakistan (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 26, folio 25). "Sawan and Bhadon are the two rainy months of the year in the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 26, folio 25).

Other customs complete the picture: "Mauj Din is now a common name In the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 4, folio 3); "Jugglers and their bears are still a familiar sight in the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 40, folio 39); "Fingers in their mouths is a typical female gesture common in the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 14, folio 13); "A snub nose is not regarded as a point of beauty either in men or women in the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 39, folio 38); a mother's exaggerated threat "is by no means unfamiliar in the Punjab villages even now" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 14, folio 13); "Dandasa" bark served as the lipstick of "the rural Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 48, folio 47); shaving with soap "is a much later invention in the Punjab", and village barbers still commonly shave with water alone (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 55, folio 54); and the ‘pearha’ is "an article of furniture common in Punjabi homes" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 56, folio 55). The tufted turban "beloved of the rural aristocracy of the Punjab" is a recent development, said to have been invented by the *mirasis*, "or hereditary bards of the Punjab in the late nineteenth century" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 11, folio 10).

The famine of the land of Punjab is remembered in Chishti's life of Mai Bhagi. It came shortly after her marriage at the age of seven, a famine known as the two and a half ser famine, in which the whole country was thrown into confusion and a mother would not look after her son, and because of it Mai Bhagi and her mother left their town, whose printed name is uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 789).

The introduction to Rafat's selection reads the province's history into its people. The period of duress after the Mughals "formed the character of the" inhabitants "of the region", and the crisis of character in the Punjabi of today "is traceable to this period of duress in the history of his land" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, pp. 26–27, folios 6–7). To illustrate this lapse it quotes a satirical questionnaire by the columnist Janus, "Are you a Punjabi?", in which the Punjabi response to learning that one's ancestors came from Central Asia is "What the hell! I am a Bokhari and superior to all the low stock inhabiting the Punjab" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 236, folio 216; Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, pp. 237–238, folios 217–218).

## Language, Poetry and Music

The love of Hir and Ranjha is the province's own story in Bhandari's account. The people of Punjab, he writes, compose wonderful verses and designs on the love of Hir and Ranjha and sing them (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 512–511). Waris Shah's annotator adds that Sohni and Mahiwal "has its setting In The old Punjab", Sohni belonging to Gujrat on the Chenab, and that this tale and Sassi and Punnoo have travelled beyond their original setting to be popular in both the old Punjab and Sind (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 12, folio 11). Two musical instruments, one of them the *nad*, the other's name uncertainly printed, were "popular in the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 39, folio 38).

The *kafi* carried the mystics' teaching. Naveed Alam writes that "Kafis, the stanzaic poems couched in folkloric themes and techniques, were easy to understand and memorize, and thus served as the ideal means to disseminate the mystical teachings among the illiterate masses dependent on the oral transmission of knowledge", and that "Even to this day, most Punjabis and Sindhis are more likely to have heard a kafi than read one" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 15, folio 15). He sets Shah Hussein in the Punjab of Akbar, recalling a rebellion in Punjab led by Abdullah Bhatti, known as Dullah Bhatti, "who was captured and hanged in public on Akbar’s orders" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 14, folio 14). He notes that Najm Hosain Syed's play *Takht L’hore (Throne of Lahore)* "extrapolates the socio-economic conditions of Punjab" in Shah Hussein's time (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 14, folio 14).

The poets made Punjab itself a figure in their verse. Usborne's Waris Shah uses the invasions of the province as similes: "as if armies from Kandahar had swept over the Punjab", "looted him as armies have looted the Punjab", and "today the Punjab has fallen into the hands of Kandaharis", which the annotator takes to refer to Ahmad Shah Abdali (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 7, folio 6; Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 44, folio 43; Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 47, folio 46; Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 48, folio 47). When the poet writes "as if the armies of the Punjab had fallen upon Hindustan", the annotator calls it "an imaginary situation", since the armies of the Punjab are known to have defended Hindustan rather than fallen upon it; even the later Sikh power was contained on the Sutlej by the Anglo Sikh treaty of 1809, and "The poet’s flight of fancy may have been inspired by a mistaken sense of local patriotism" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 7, folio 6; Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 8, folio 7).

Bulleh Shah's lament for the province is the best known of these lines. Rafat's translator's note says that "The full bitterness of Bulleh Shah’s ‘Punjab has gone to the dogs’ will only come through against this backdrop" of upheaval (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 16, folio xvi). In Rafat's translation of the poem Come See Me Once in a While the lines run:

*Now hell has opened its bogs. / Punjab has gone to the dogs. / This hell is the nethermost hell.* (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 149, folio 129)

Lahori's book closes with a chronogram for the year 1292 AH by his son, Mufti Ghulam Safdar Lahori, which speaks of the province's saints:

جو خدا کے دوست تھے پنجاب میں
حال ان کا اس سے ہوتا ہے عیان

*A chronogram for the book, on the friends of God in Punjab whose lives it makes known.* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 282, folio 270)

Chishti ends his account of the annexation with a *qita* on Dalip Singh's departure from Punjab, of which he gives two hemistichs likening Dalip Singh to the candle of Punjab and to a new flower from the garden of Punjab; one word of the first hemistich is uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 592).

Of the language itself the modern books are gloomy. The introduction to Rafat's selection writes that "Punjabi literature in Pakistan is in a state of neglect. The language has become a purely spoken medium", and that the Punjabis can no longer read it in written form (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 50, folio 30). "In terms of written sources, the Punjab and indeed the whole of the Indus basin, has always" seemed "a wasteland to scholars" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, pp. 46–47, folios 26–27). Writing of how Baba Farid's and Shah Husain's poetry survived through Sikh collectors, it blames a lack of intellectual tradition among the Muslims of the Punjab for indifference to the classics, adding that "Bulleh Shah was treated no differently" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 47, folio 27). "After this the Punjab awaits a proper" scholarly "assessment of its greatest poet, a tryst that may never take place given the regional antecedents in the realm of letters" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 49, folio 29). The Janus questionnaire it quotes observes that "All regional personalities are moulded by a deep love of their idiom", and that while a Sindhi can name the greatest living Sindhi poet, and so can the Pathan and the Baluch, the Punjabi cannot; Punjabi alone, it says, is not taught in schools and has no dictionary (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 236, folio 216; Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, pp. 237–238, folios 217–218).

## Shrines and the State

Rulers in the books deal with the province's holy men and with its revenue. Bhandari writes that while the imperial armies were quartered in Punjab grain was dear and so the assessed revenue of the *parganas* was raised; when the armies left Lahore for the Deccan, grain became cheap, the peasants could not pay, and a reduction was ordered at a rate uncertainly printed; Bhandari gives this as the petition of Guru Arjan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 164).

Ranjit Singh's faith in the *majzub* Mastan Shah, and Mastan Shah's refusal of his thousands of rupees, are recorded by Lahori (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 246, folio 234). Under British rule Chishti argues for the khanqahs before the new government: attention from the government to the khanqah, he writes, would win the goodwill of the subjects of Punjab (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 95). When British rule and its legal settlement were established in Punjab, Jhuggi Shah returned to Dori Shah's site and set about restoring it (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 777).

The Mughal state built across the province and paid for its builders' tombs. Ali Mardan Khan, in Shah Jahan's time, built great and splendid buildings in Hind and Punjab, and his gardens remain his memorial in many places, Chishti writes, following *Mirat al-Hind* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 415). In Shah Jahan's reign the builder of the garden Chishti calls Sanhla Bagh was appointed to an office over the buildings of Punjab, its title damaged in the printed text, and on his death money was granted from the treasury for his tomb (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 727).

The Sikh years, in Lahori's editor's estimate, were hard on the shrines. Among the merits of the book he counts that the Mufti recorded, as an eyewitness, the disgrace of the notables of Punjab at the hands of the Sikhs and the destruction of the tombs of the shaikhs, his own family having been a target of the Sikh disorders (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 33, folio 21).

## The Province in the Books

Bhandari, writing about 1107 AH, describes the Mughal *suba* of Lahore as a country of five *doabs*, setting out the famous places of the five *doabs* and then the six rivers of the *suba* that divide them (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 513). He names the *doabs* as Bist Jalandhar between the Sutlej and the Beas, Bari Manjha between the Beas and the Ravi, the *doab* between the Ravi and the Chenab, which he calls Rachna where he places Sialkot in it, the *doab* between the Chenab and the Bihat, which he calls Chunhat where he places Gujrat in it, and Sind Sagar between the Bihat and the Indus, some of the names uncertainly printed (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 511; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 512; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 513; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 515; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). The five rivers of Punjab join the Indus beyond Multan in the Baloch country, and from there the whole stream is called the Sind and becomes a vast sea (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 510).

To Bhandari the *suba* of Lahore is a country of good water and air. Its summers are very hot and its winters cold; its melons and grapes are like those of Iran and Turan and its mangoes like Hindustan's; its rice is better than Bengal's and its sugarcane better than the Deccan's (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 510). Most of its irrigation depends on wells, worked by a wheel of three hundred and sixty pieces of wood and more than a hundred small pots, turned by a pair of oxen, each turn lifting several hundred maunds of water, while the autumn and spring harvests and cheap grain depend on the rain (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 510). Gold is washed from the sand, especially of the Beas and the Bihat, and the northern hills hold mines of silver, copper and other metals whose names are uncertainly printed; the miners profit and pay the duty to the imperial treasury (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 510). The *suba* measures one hundred and eighty *kos* from the Sutlej to the Indus, and eighty seven *kos* across from Bhera to a place whose name is uncertainly printed; it is bounded by Sirhind to the east, Multan to the west, Kashmir to the north and, to the south, a place whose name is also uncertain (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 510). The five *doabs* comprise three hundred and sixteen *mahals*, yielding eighty nine crore, thirty three lakh and seventy thousand *dams* (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 509).

Of the *suba* of Multan, which Bhandari calls the abode of safety, he writes that it runs four hundred *kos* from Firozpur to Siwistan and one hundred and twenty five *kos* across from Khatpur to Jaisalmer, and six hundred and sixty *kos* in length from Sadgahra to Kech Makran; it adjoins the *sarkar* of Sirhind to the east, Kech Makran to the west, Shorkot to the north and the *suba* of Ajmer to the south (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525). Its three *sarkars*, Multan, Dipalpur and Bhakkar, comprise ninety six *mahals* and yield twenty four crore, forty six lakh and fifty five thousand *dams* (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525). The *sarkar* of Dipalpur is a dependency of this *suba* (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 526), and Bhandari bounds the *suba* of Ajmer on the west by Dipalpur, dependent on Multan, without saying whether he means the *suba*, the *sarkar* or the city (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 534). The *suba* of Shahjahanabad has the *suba* of Lahore on its west, but he draws no line between them (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 550). Turning from the capital to the rest of the *suba* of Lahore, he sets out to write of some of its towns (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 523).

Bhandari's Punjab is a frontier made safe by union with Kabul. In former times, when Kabul lay outside the rule of the sovereign of Hind, the Turanians raided its borders and the *wilayat* of Punjab lay ruined and waste; since Hind and Kabul came under one ruler, this has been the cause of the prosperity of Punjab and the security of all Hind (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 501). Among his sources he lists a history of the sultans of the *wilayat* of Multan, together with those of Malwa, Daulatabad, the Deccan, Jaunpur, Bengal and Orissa, lands that at various times passed out of the control of Delhi and each had a separate ruler (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 581).

Lahori, writing in 1875, defines his own province. Friends asked him, he says, for another short collection in the Urdu language holding the lives of the saints of the land of Punjab, that is, of all the territory from Delhi to Peshawar then attached to Punjab, where the tombs of famous saints lie (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 37, folio 25). His fifth chaman holds the elders who were learned and renowned scholars in Punjab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 191, folio 179), his seventh the pious women who lived in Punjab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 247, folio 235), and a later section the elders of Punjab whose dates of death could not be established (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 261, folio 249). In his closing he explains that his Persian *Khazinat al-Asfiya* was so large that those interested in the land of Punjab could not learn from it how many masters of the path had lived in the land of Punjab, a difficulty this short Urdu book removes (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 280, folio 268).

Lahori's modern editor sees the book as filling a gap. Punjab, he writes, is in this respect the most unfortunate of regions, for no regular and researched history of it has yet been written, and those who wrote on its history gave only formal chapters without touching the Sufi tazkiras; the need is to fill the gaps in the political, cultural and spiritual history of Punjab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 23, folio 11). He has compiled a list of the tazkiras, *malfuzat* and letters of the Sufis of Punjab, and no comprehensive tazkira of them has come to his notice; without the Mufti's general tazkiras, he writes, we would see no scholar or gnostic in Punjab today (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 24, folio 12). The book holds the lives of 244 Sufis of Punjab, the numeral uncertainly printed, in seven chamans, from Sultan Mahmud of Ghazni to 1292 AH, 1875 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 31, folio 19), and also takes in some well known Sufis who had no connection with Punjab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 32, folio 20). Some Punjab shaikhs are found only in this book (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 32, folio 20), and by preserving his contemporaries the Mufti did Punjab a great service; yet, the editor adds, he wrote up whichever elders of Punjab came to mind at a single sitting (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 33, folio 21). The modern edition's subtitle calls it an authoritative tazkira of the great Sufis of Punjab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 9, folio not stated). The editor also points to Maulana Muhammad Aqil Lahori's *Tuhfat al-Muslimin*, which records the sayings of Shaikh Hamid Qari and the lives of the great scholars of Punjab from Shaikh Hamid to Shaikh Bahauddin Zakariya Multani, and whose rare manuscript is in his own library (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 278, folio 266). Of another modern work he writes that the author of *Nuzhat al-Khawatir* took *Khazinat al-Asfiya* as his largest source for Punjab and generally included its reports without any research (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 191–192, folios 179–180).

For the annotator of Usborne's Waris Shah the poem's world is "the old Punjab", with its *bars* such as the Nili Bar "in the Montgomery area (now renamed Sahiwal)", a *bar* being a kind of jungle (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 10, folio 9). Naveed Alam reads Shah Hussein's Punjab against W. H. Moreland's account of the Mughal Golden Age, though that account speaks of India taken as a unit rather than of Punjab (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 14, folio 14).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **When Shaikh Ismail Muhaddith Lahori died.** Lahori, following *Tuhfat al-Wasilin*, places him in the time of Sultan Masud of Ghazni and gives his death as 448 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 191, folio 179). His modern editor calls that source a fabricated tazkira and gives the correct year of his death as 980 AH, not 448 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 191–192, folios 179–180).
- **Ahmad Shah Abdali's dates.** Lahori gives a year read as 1120 AH, uncertainly printed, for Ahmad Shah Durrani's *farman* to Mufti Muhammad Mukarram; his editor calls the year wrong, writing that Abdali began his attacks on Punjab in 1161 AH, 1748, and died in 1187 AH, 1773 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 277, folio 265).
- **Abdali's invasion of 1748.** Rafat's introduction calls the invasion of 1748 Ahmad Shah Abdali's second invasion of the Punjab, followed by a third in 1752 (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 27, folio 7). The annotator of Waris Shah writes that Abdali "in 1748, began a series of invasions of India through the Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 8, folio 7), and Lahori's editor dates Abdali's first attack on Lahore to 1161 AH, 1748 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 214, folio 202).
- **Chingiz Khan in Punjab.** Lahori, from *Tazkira-e Hamidiya*, has Chingiz Khan's army plunder the whole land of Punjab and the people of Lahore fight it for two months, while himself doubting whether anyone will believe it (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 248, folio 236). His editor's addenda note that Shihab al-Din Nasawi does not mention an attack by Chingiz Khan on Lahore at all (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 352, folio 340).
- **The Ghaznavid account of *Tuhfat al-Wasilin*.** Lahori retells from it the Ghaznavid rule of Punjab, its weakening under Bahram Shah and Raja Anangpal's taking of Lahore (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 264, folio 252); his editor calls *Tuhfat al-Wasilin* a fabricated tazkira (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 192, folio 180).
- **The year of Shaikh Karam Shah's death.** Lahori gives it in words as one thousand two hundred, and in the line of his chronogram as 1201 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 190, folio 178).
- **Which Lodi sultan made Tatar Khan governor.** Bhandari has Tatar Khan sent to govern Punjab by Sultan Bahlul Lodi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 190; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 296), and places his governorship of Lahore in Bahlul Lodi's time, where the editor's footnote gives the variant Sikandar Lodi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 522–523).
- **The year the Mughals came to Punjab under Jalal al-Din Khalji.** Bhandari's text gives 693, uncertainly printed; the editor's footnote gives 691 from Firishta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 370).
- **The seat of Raja Jaipal.** Bhandari's text names the fort that was Jaipal's seat in one form, uncertainly printed, and the editor's footnote gives a different reading (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 427).
- **The bounds of the Baluch *jagir*.** In one passage Bhandari has Sultan Husain Langah give Malik Sohrab a *jagir* from the fort of Kot Kardaz to Dhankot, the Baluch later holding the land from Sitpur to Dhankot (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 295); in his description of the *suba* of Multan the grant to Malik Sohrab and the other Baluch runs from Karor Kot to Mankot (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525).
- **The Rasul Shahi houses.** Chishti writes that the order had no house in all Punjab but this one, and no other house, *takia* or *khanqah*, and then names one more house near Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 145).
- **Baba Farid and the troubles of Punjab.** Waris Shah writes that when Shakarganj settled at Pakpattan "the Punjab was delivered of all its troubles"; his annotator replies that this "is not historically correct" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 2, folio 1).
- **The armies of the Punjab.** Waris Shah's verse speaks of the armies of the Punjab falling upon Hindustan; his annotator answers that the armies of the Punjab are known to have defended Hindustan, never to have fallen upon it (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 7, folio 6; Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 8, folio 7).
