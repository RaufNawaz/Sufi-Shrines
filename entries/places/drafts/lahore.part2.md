## History

Kanhaiya Lal sets out his own programme for the city beyond the walls: to tell in which direction the settlement of Lahore grew outside the ancient *hisar* under the Chughtais, which were the famous quarters of that settlement, who founded each of them, and whether any trace of them still remains (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 76, folio 79). His thesis is that settlement outside the walls began in the reign of Humayun and moved south, south east and east, until a city twice the size of the original stood outside it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 76, folio 79).

He traces one suburb through three regimes. Under the Mughals no deed of sale or mortgage there was counted valid without their seal and signature; under Ranjit Singh the Baloch were dominant; and the Arains arrived in the famine he calls the *Chalisi*, the Forty famine (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 77–78, folios 80–81).

The names of the city's gates carry their founders' bargains and orders in his telling. The Bhats made it a condition with the ruler that a gate be called after them, and the ruler agreed and named it so (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 18, folio 13). Shah Alam had it proclaimed that the gate should henceforth be called Shah Alami (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 17, folio 12). Akbar set up a market for every kind of grain inside one gate and named it Akbari Mandi (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 16, folio 11). Inside Taksali Gate stood the royal mint, of which only the remains of the mint's mosque survive (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 19, folio 14). Roshnai Gate was lit every night by royal order; it was in origin a gate of the fort, but is counted among the gates of the city (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 19, folio 14).

Under the British, Kanhaiya Lal writes, Lahore was made the capital of the Punjab, and Delhi itself became subordinate to this province (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 33, folio 36). The Sadr Bazaar of Anarkali ran for about a mile, with trade in lakhs of rupees, as though a second city had been settled outside the first (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 34, folio 37), and the present population had grown to twice any earlier one (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 35, folio 38). The chief cause of the city's progress, in his judgement, was the new railway department (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 35, folio 38).

The railway decided which sarais prospered. At Muhammad Sultan's sarai outside Delhi Gate thousands of travellers rested every day, with a bazaar beside it whose printed name, Landa, is uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 35, folio 38). Outside Shah Alami Gate stood Diwan Ratan Chand's sarai and tank; the tank was always full, and thousands of men and women came from the city to bathe there (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 35–36, folios 38–39). He built a shivala there, and his son built a thakurdwara on the southern bank of the tank (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 65, folio 68). The complex had rooms on all four sides for travellers to lodge in, two masonry shops flanking the gate and shops along the bank where traders lived, and goods of every kind from outside, salt, ghee, oil and grain, were sold there, so that something like a great market gathered (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 224–225, folios 227–228). Kanhaiya Lal records that it was built in the period of English rule by a serving Honorary Magistrate of Lahore (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 224, folio 227). Mela Ram's sarai outside Bhati Gate, by contrast, failed because it lay too far from the railway station (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 36, folio 39).

The Municipal Committee sat under the Deputy Commissioner, and its native members, Kanhaiya Lal says, were appointed only with the consent of the city's people (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 62, folio 65). One gate held a court, a municipal chamber and a police station at once: police sepoys lived below and behind, and above were rooms for the sittings of the honorary magistrates and the members of the Municipal Committee (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 16, folio 11). A sanitation department was appointed and the bazaars were swept and sprinkled twice a day (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 36–37, folios 39–40); the book dates the first work to 1850 and the paving to 1860, both years uncertain in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 37, folio 40). The tank of a scheme for piped water shattered within an hour for want of concrete, and the scheme was suspended from that day (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 37, folio 40). Municipal drainage once worked in a shrine's favour, filling the stinking tank at Haveli Mian Khan at government expense (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 327, folio 334).

He gives one series of wages. In the Sikh period a mason earned four annas a day and a labourer two; now a mason earns eight annas and a labourer four, and master masons are paid from one to two rupees a day (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 45, folio 48).

The new public buildings of the British city are dated and costed in the same pages. The kotwali was rebuilt at twice its former size under an Assistant Commissioner whose name, perhaps Hill, is uncertain, in a year printed as 1850 and also uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 369, folio 376). Lawrence Hall was finished in 1862 and Montgomery Hall in a year read as 1866, uncertainly (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 402, folio 409), and the museum dates from 1864 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 402, folio 409). The women's jail is dated 1868, uncertainly (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 409, folio 416), and the government hospital was completed after three years in a year read as 1870, also uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 377, folio 384). The New College was begun in 1872 and finished in five years (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 381, folio 388). The telegraph office was begun in a year read as 1880 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 388, folio 395), and the Art School was begun in 1881 and completed in 1882, both readings uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 384, folio 391). The Medical School was begun in 1881 and completed in a year read uncertainly as 1882 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 429, folio 436; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 430, folio 437).

The costs he prints are these. The Sadr Kachahri cost one lakh of rupees (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 375, folio 382). The Senate Hall was paid for with thirty four thousand rupees from the Nawab of Bahawalpur, a gift recorded in English on a carved stone (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 376, folio 383; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 377, folio 384). The hospital cost one lakh and a number of thousands that cannot be read with certainty (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 377, folio 384), and the New College three lakh twenty thousand rupees of government money (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 384, folio 391). For the Art School forty two thousand were sanctioned, thirty eight thousand spent on the school and four thousand on staff quarters (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 387, folio 394). The telegraph office cost thirty thousand and a further fifteen thousand, forty five thousand in all (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 389, folio 396). The Medical School cost one lakh and thirty two thousand, the second figure uncertain, with its attached buildings reckoned separately (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 432, folio 439). The government spent thousands of rupees on the first Punjab exhibition (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 402, folio 409) and thousands every year on Government House (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 396, folio 403). Montgomery and Lawrence Halls were raised by subscription, the nobles and officials of the Punjab giving most willingly, and the subscribers' names are engraved twice inside the hall, once in English script and once in Persian (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 399, folio 406; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 400–401, folios 407–408).

He names where the materials came from. The slate of the New College came from the Dalhousie hills, and its upper columns are of black Chiniot stone (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 381, folio 388). Black Chiniot stone runs through all of Nawab Mian Khan's buildings, and he also built a mosque of the same stone at Chiniot (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 272, folio 275; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 329, folio 336). The telegraph office has double tiles of Allahabad, the place name uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 389, folio 396); four iron girders carry the ceiling of the Art School hall (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 386, folio 393) and iron girders stand in the Medical School (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 430, folio 437); and iron sheet covers the truss of Montgomery Hall (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 400, folio 407). The whiteness inside the Mian Mir church was made with marble ground from an old, unnamed building outside the city of Lahore (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 392, folio 399).

The prisons and hospitals of the two regimes are set side by side. Under the Sikhs the kotwali was given nothing by the government to feed its prisoners: every day in the third watch the chained prisoners were sent into the city to beg, one sepoy to every seven, and the sepoy took half of what they were given; punishment was mutilation at the post called Lal Lakkar, hands, ears, noses and feet cut off after five to ten days in the lockup, or else a fine (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 369, folio 376). Under the English the same labour was brought inside the walls. The Central Jail had large workshops for weaving shawls, carpets and a third fabric whose name is uncertain, and a printing press doing work worth lakhs of rupees; the benefit, Kanhaiya Lal writes, was that the prisoners learnt a trade, the government profited, and the prisoners never left the jail (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 410, folio 417). The women's jail took women prisoners from every district of the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 409, folio 416), held women newly arrived from the districts apart for forty days (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 408, folio 415), and had workrooms in which the imprisoned women worked (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 409, folio 416). In the solitary blocks one prisoner lived to a cell under hard labour, with both doors locked at all times, twenty four prisoners to a block (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 407, folio 414). Race and class were written into the plans: there were buildings for European prisoners and for European women, separate lockups at the kachahri for natives and for European accused, separate buildings for European insane men and women, and railway station rooms graded first, second and third class, with separate seating for memsahibs and for Punjabi and Hindustani third class travellers (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 372, folio 379; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 408, folio 415; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 413, folio 420; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 419–420, folios 426–427; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 421–422, folios 428–429). At the asylum one garden was kept so that the insane could be brought there for recreation (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 420, folio 427). The hospital had a smallpox house, a building for examining the dead, a servants' latrine and a house for destroying bedbugs with an iron tank and a furnace, and it kept two wells, the ancient great well with a wooden Persian wheel and a new small one dug for drinking water (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 380, folio 387).

Buildings changed their uses as the regimes changed. When the army moved to Mian Mir, the empty barracks of the Anarkali cantonment became the district's courts, so that all the kachahris gathered in one place and the district's business was done together (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 371–372, folios 378–379); a hospital became the Munsifi court (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 371, folio 378); and a temporary exhibition became the permanent museum, which it has remained from that day (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 403, folio 410). One Mughal baradari in Wazir Khan's garden passed through European soldiers, the kachahri of the Settlement department, the Telegraph department, the museum's stores and a library, and is still held by the government (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 357, folio 364). The Mughal gardens passed into named hands: Angoori Bagh into the possession of Pandits after Ranjit Singh, the verb of what he did to it uncertain, and Inayat Bagh, its name uncertain, into that of the Sandhanwalia sardars (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 349, folio 356); Bagh Aluwalia to Sardar Fateh Singh Aluwalia, its gardeners now cultivating its land for their own living (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 352, folio 359); Bagh Lala Ratan Chand to Lala Bhagwan Das, with European gentlemen paying rent as tenants in its four towers (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 355, folio 362); and the gatehouse of Bagh Nau Lakha to Sardar Lehna Singh Majithia, who took it as his own residence, and which now houses high officials (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 356, folio 363). The Golian Wali sarai at Ichhra became family property when Jamadar Khushhal Singh petitioned Ranjit Singh for it, and its proprietary possession is now Raja Harbans Singh's (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 366, folio 373).

## A City of Saints

Saints founded the quarters of the outer city in Kanhaiya Lal's account: Mauj Darya Bukhari and Sayyid Chiragh Shah Gilani (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 79, folio 82), Sayyid Abd al-Razzaq (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 80, folio 83), Shah Sharaf (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 80–81, folios 83–84), Shah Dargahi (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 82, folio 85), Shah Badr al-Din Gilani (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 82–84, folios 85–87), Shaikh Muhammad Tahir (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 85, folio 88), Shah Abu al-Maali (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 87, folio 90), Pir Aziz Mazang (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 77, folio 80) and the faqir Taja (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 78, folio 81). Five of the mohallas listed in the book's contents for its second part are named after saints or their descendants (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 2).

The pattern recurs across the book. Mughalpura was settled around Khawand Mahmud's mazar, and it was the wealthy quarter whose plunder so enriched Ahmad Shah Durrani's army that it had no need to loot the rest of the city (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 241, folio 244). Villages settled inside shrine enclosures: the village of Begampura stands within one (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 275, folio 278), and the village of Mian Mir within Mian Mir's own (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 242, folio 245). A dervish compound became Garhi Shahu, where possession passed from a plunderer to the displaced residents of a mohalla and then to four Shaikhs whose descendants still hold it, and who also took into their own possession whatever ownerless land lay waste around (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 364, folio 371); the dervishes' cells and towers round Abul Khair's grave are now all houses of zamindars (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 365, folio 372).

A living saint could protect a quarter. Ahmad Shah Durrani came to visit Haji Muhammad Saeed and set a royal guard in the mohalla so that no plunderer should touch it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 80, folio 83). Some holy places had no establishment at all: the two graves of Pir Zaki have neither urs, custodian, offering nor endowment, yet devotees come to recite the *fatiha* at both, and both are places of visitation for high and low alike (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 20–21, folios 15–16).

Relics came and went with the regimes. At the Qadam-e Rasul in Begampura a stone once bore the print of the Prophet's foot, and no one knows who carried it off in the time of the Sikhs (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 277, folio 280). At Sharaf un-Nisa Begum's tomb a Quran and a sword were laid out every day after the noon prayer and willed to remain in the tomb (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 264, folio 267); in the Sikh period the Sikhs took them out, and the book values them only as worth some thousand rupees, it is said (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 265, folio 268). At the Imambara the taziya is carried on replica graves of Imam Hasan and Imam Husain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 302, folio 305).

Kanhaiya Lal warns his reader about the age of what stands. In the Sikh period, he writes, even temples of old foundation were built anew, so that their fabric looks fresh (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 93, folio 96). He shows it on the next site, where an old mosque enclosure became a mud shiwala and then a masonry temple of Ranjit Singh's time (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 94, folio 97). The enclosure had belonged to the Wazir Khan mosque in the time of the Muslim kings and its rents were spent on the mosque; when that rule passed, Bawa Thala Gir, his name uncertain, took up residence, obtained a lease from one of the rulers and built a small mud shiwala, and Raja Dina Nath then built the present structure at great expense (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 94, folio 97). Four of six mosques in one part of the book replace an earlier mosque on the same ground (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 178, 181, 182, 183; folios 181, 184, 185, 186), and he ranks four of the shrines in the Mehr Das enclosure by age, naming the oldest the first and most ancient building of all (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 129, folio 132).

He copies dates off the buildings, and almost every Hijri date he gives is uncertain in the printed text. The niche of the Masti Darwaza mosque bears 1023, with no era stated and its chronogram not fully legible (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 142, folio 145). Shah Zia Qadri died in a year read as 1126, era not stated (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 144, folio 147). The Sunehri Masjid is dated 1163 AH on the mihrab beneath the Ayat al-Kursi, the year printed twice in one sentence (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 147, folio 150), and Nawab Bukhari Khan died in 1165 AH (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 148, folio 151). The mosque of Muhammad Salih Kambo is dated 1058 in the three niches over its gate (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 152, folio 155). The Wazir Khan mosque records that it was completed in the reign of Shah Jahan, the second Lord of the Conjunction, and two of its chronograms give 1043 and 1044 AH, the second by the abjad reckoning, while a third names Wazir Khan as founder of a second House of God (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 157, folio 160). Sayyid Ishaq Gazruni died in 706, the *bismillah* itself serving as the chronogram (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 163, folio 166). Imam Ghulam Muhammad died in 1234 AH and his son in 1289 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 163, folio 166). Sayyid Jan Muhammad Huzuri died in 764 AH (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 167, folio 170) and Hassu Teli in 1000 AH (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 169, folio 172). The mosque of Takiya Sadhuwan is dated 1266 AH (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 170, folio 173). Zabita Khan died in 1251 AH and his mosque was rebuilt in 1278 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 171, folio 174); Sayyid Amir Shah's mosque was rebuilt in 1285 AH (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 172, folio 175); Nura's first mosque dates from 1239 AH (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 174, folio 177) and the second Nur Muhammad mosque from 1297 AH, with other figures in its margin (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 176, folio 179). Moran's mosque is dated 1223 AH (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 179, folio 182), Bokan Khan's 1255 AH (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 181, folio 184) and Imam Shah Wali's 1294 AH (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 182, folio 185). Shah Bilawal Qadri died in the year one thousand and forty six of the Shahjahani period, no era printed and the second figure uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 235, folio 238), Lal Husain in 1008 AH and Madho in 1057 AH (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 233, folio 236). Hamid Qari's mosque bears 1141 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 252, folio 255) and the Zakariya Khan mosque at Madho Lal Husain 1144 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 263, folio 266). The dome of Data Ganj Bakhsh is dated 1247 AH and its mosque was rebuilt in 1868 AD (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 290, folio 293). The tomb of Shah Kanhh, his name uncertain, was rebuilt in 1294 AH (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 304, folio 307). A mosque's marble founding slab names its founder as Allah Jawai, her name uncertain, sister of Muhammad Sultan the contractor, and dates it 1247 AH (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 294, folio 297). Sarai Golian Wali was built in 1035 AH by order of Jahangir (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 365, folio 372). Sayyid Badal-din Gilani, his name uncertain, died in 1072 AH and his tomb of the Shahjahani period was given its dome by his descendants (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 394, folio 401; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 395, folio 402). Shalamar's quatrain is followed by 1057, with no era printed (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 348, folio 355). For Shaikh Muhtaram he works the abjad in the open: the letters of the blessing on the saint total one thousand one hundred and seven, and after a subtraction one thousand one hundred and two remain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 257, folio 260).

A few of these dates are printed without doubt. Masjid Pholiyan Wali was rebuilt in 1282 AH, the only year legible on its marble slab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 176, folio 179). Nawab Imam ud Din Khan's mosque is dated 1266 AH (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 177, folio 180). Gulabi Bagh carries 1066 as the date of the garden, and its tilework is said to look fresh after two hundred and thirty five years (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 307, folio 310).

Hindu and Sikh foundations are dated in the Samvat, the Bikrami era. The memorial shivala of Sukh Ram Das Khatri Kapur and the two Mehr Das shivalas are dated Samvat 1930 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 130, folio 133). The Devi's mandir followed her appearance in Samvat 1931, which the book equates with 1875, and the donor's brick is inscribed February 1875 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 131, folio 134). Radha Kishan's shivala is dated Samvat 1847 Bikrami (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 132, folio 135) and the Radha Kishan thakurdwara Samvat 919, both readings uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 134, folio 137). Chhajju Bhagat died in a Samvat year of the sixteen hundreds, uncertainly read as ninety six, which the book equates with 1053 AH (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 186, folio 189). Ram Singh's samadh dates from Samvat 1895 Bikrami and was built in Jamadar Khushhal Singh's own lifetime, and Raja Teja Singh died in Samvat 1919 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 190, folio 193). Rani Partab Kunwar died in Samvat 1914 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 212, folio 215), and the epigraph of Rani Rind Awi, her name uncertain, reads the fourteenth of Maghar, Samvat 1927, on a Sunday, the figures uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 211, folio 214). Chamna Das's mandir at Tali Sahib is dated Samvat 1898 Bikrami, printed without doubt (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 228, folio 231). Dhani Nath was appointed in Samvat 1880 Bikrami (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 231, folio 234). The Anarkali shiwala and the thakurdwara of a Jangam, his name uncertain, are dated Samvat 1910 and Samvat 1937 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 445, folio 452). Pandit Bishnath's mandir is dated Samvat 1904 Bikrami (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 451, folio 458), Chhujhi Mal's Samvat 1933 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 452, folio 459), Atar Singh Subedar's Samvat 1934 Bikrami (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 453, folio 460) and Dharm Chand's rebuilt shiwala Samvat 1935 Bikrami (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 454, folio 461). Rai Singh Risaldar's thakurdwara carries on a marble slab over its east door its own name, the name of his father Karam Singh and Samvat 1938 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 455–456, folios 462–463). One shivala was built in Samvat 1900 Bikrami, the year uncertain, and the date of its completion is written on the temple (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 100, folio 103). Two Sikh deaths are dated in the Christian era: Khushal Singh in 1844 and Jawahar Singh, killed late in 1847, both years uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 192, folio 195; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 197, folio 200).

He measures. Mian Mir's platform is nineteen by nineteen *zira* square (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 243, folio 246). Bahadur Khan's octagonal platform has sides of seventeen or eighteen *gaz*, the figure uncertain, mihrabs four and three quarter *gaz* high and two and a quarter wide, a two storeyed gallery in each face, two staircases, and eight corner domelets of which one has fallen and seven stand (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 258, folio 261). Nusrat Khan's tomb has eight *gaz* of platform clear on every side (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 261–262, folios 264–265). Ali Mardan Khan's tomb is three storeys high with a basement holding three masonry graves, and no tomb in Lahore is as tall (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 255, folio 258). Jahangir's tomb is measured in feet: a marble platform fourteen feet long, the figure uncertain, nine wide and a foot and a half high, forty cells, four minarets of sixty one steps each, the number uncertain, a platform of two hundred and some feet, the figure uncertain, and eleven surviving tanks of twelve and three surviving wells of four (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 316–319, folios 323–326). The marble platform of the Hazuri Bagh baradari is eight feet wide and three feet above the ground, with fifteen red stone steps down to its basement and sixteen marble steps up to its third storey (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 340–342, folios 347–349). The tank in the Wazir Khan courtyard is a hundred *gaz* square (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 156, folio 159), and at Shah Muhammad Ghaus a tank ten by ten is always full of water (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 249, folio 252). East of Government House a stepped tank, sixty by thirty feet, is kept full from the canal (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 398, folio 405). The number of wells at the Bhauri Sarkar thakurdwara cannot be read (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 201, folio 204). One well is big enough for twelve Persian wheels to run on it with ease, and an arched opening inside it is marked by Kanhaiya Lal as his own conjecture (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 260, folio 263). Shalamar's garden land is measured in bighas with figures that do not add up (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 351–352, folios 358–359).

The gilded finial, the *kalas*, marks whether a Sikh era building was finished. Khushal Singh's samadh has none (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 192, folio 195), and in another dome an iron fitting, the metal uncertain, waits empty for a *kalas* to be raised (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 193, folio 196). Golden finials gleam at Udham Singh's and Suchet Singh's samadhs (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 194, 195; folios 197, 198); none has yet been raised at Shiv Gar's shivala (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 199, folio 202); Bhauri Sarkar's has a brass one (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 201, folio 204); none was ever set on Sher Singh's dome nor on Rani Rind Awi's (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 210–211, folios 213–214); and gilded finials stand throughout at Rani Jindan's (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 205, folio 208) and over each samadh at Kharak Singh's and Nau Nihal Singh's (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 222, folio 225). His verdicts on the unfinished buildings are short: one grand building remained incomplete (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 192, folio 195); another was left incomplete because of the revolution of the kingdom, and now lies heirless, ruined and decaying (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 196, folio 199); a third is not yet ready (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 190, folio 193). After Raja Teja Singh's death Mul Singh, the agent of the state, took charge of building his samadh, and now Raja Harbans Singh, heir to the state, has gained full authority and will perhaps bring the building to completion (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 190, folio 193). At Khushal Singh's, on Teja Singh's death the property and jagir were divided between Raja Harbans Singh and Sardar Bhagwan Singh, and the work passed to Bhagwan Singh, who neglected it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 192, folio 195).

Wills governed the fabric of graves, and were sometimes built against. Mian Wada's tomb has no dome and his grave is unbaked earth, because he willed that his grave not be made of masonry (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 239, folio 242). Faqir Sain Qutb Shah Qadri's grave was likewise left unpaved by his will, though the wheeled well there is masonry (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 271, folio 274), and Shaikh Sadi Balkhari willed that no dome be built over his grave, so none was (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 298, folio 301). By Sayyid Ishaq Gazruni's will the enclosure was made of masonry and the grave of earth, but when the Nawab built a mosque there the grave was raised in masonry too (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 162–163, folios 165–166). Daryagar Sanyasi asked that his samadh be made of earth and not masonry, and so it was, yet a masonry shivala has also been built at the same place (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 210, folio 213). Sharaf un-Nisa's tomb was built without stairs and kept so by her will; the Begum climbed up every day by setting a wooden ladder against it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 264, folio 267). Many tombs were built in their occupants' lifetimes or by their disciples: Mauj Darya's by Akbar's order in his lifetime (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 279, folio 282); Abul Muali's laid out by himself and finished by his son (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 284, folio 287); Shah Kamal's by the disciples of his family (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 313, folio 316); and Shaikh Mahmud Shah Naqshbandi's by his disciples in his lifetime, with a basement made first, into which his coffin was placed at his death and the door shut (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 260–261, folios 263–264). Haji Nur's enclosure was built in his lifetime on the plan of the Kaaba (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 295, folio 298); Fazl Shah's grave was made before his death by his patron (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 325, folio 332); and Makhduma Begum had her own tomb built smaller than her husband's, out of respect (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 260, folio 263).

The working fabric of the holy places is described closely. The Kotwali shiwala has separate buildings for staff quarters, kitchens and linen stores (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 95, folio 98); there are small rooms for pujaris at the Bhagat Ram shiwala (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 100, folio 103) and at Ragnath Mishr's (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 115–116, folios 118–119), and masonry rooms for faqirs at the dharamsala (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 103, folio 106). Mahants' samadhs are built in masonry inside the Shivala Tarpoliya's own halls (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 107, folio 110); two anonymous mahants' domed samadhs and a *majzub*'s samadh stand at the Wazir Khan shiwala (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 94, folio 97); and many domed masonry samadhs of former successors and managers stand at Bhauri Sarkar's (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 201, folio 204). The Bhagat Ram shiwala has thirteen fountains and an underground cold room (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 100, folio 103), and the Begampura mosque a great tank with a fountain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 278, folio 281). There are wells at most sites (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 94, 95, 99, 101, 103, 105, 106, 109, 115, 117; folios 97, 98, 102, 104, 106, 108, 109, 112, 118, 120) and bel trees at four (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 101, 106, 107, 109; folios 104, 109, 110, 112). Bakhshi Bhagat Ram has a gilded samadh and a painted portrait inside his own temple courtyard, which those who come to worship look upon, and a marble platform two *gaz* square is called the Dharamshala because he sat on it to give alms to Brahmans (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 99, folio 102). The Shivala Tarpoliya's silver *jalhari* was remade by subscription after a theft, four hundred rupees' weight of silver (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 107, folio 110). A well at Mian Mir took fifty years to make (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 243, folio 246); at Sayyid Abdul Wahab Qadri's tomb a great well deserves to be called a tank (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 265, folio 268); there are a wheeled well and two bathhouses at Hamid Qari's khanqah (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 252, folio 255) and again at the Zakariya Khan mosque (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 262, folio 265); a well and a water house at the Muhammad Sultan mosque (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 294, folio 297); a Persian wheel mounted by a living pir on his own shrine's well (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 296, folio 299); and at Shaikh Musa's enclosure a well worked by the wooden wheel of the zamindars who farm there (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 245, folio 248). The shrines built seats for their offices into the fabric: at Mian Mir a grand hall is the seat of the *mujawirs* and a separate building the special seat of the *sajjada nashin* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 243, folio 246); at Shah Muhammad Ghaus a windowed masonry seat for the *sajjada nashin* is built into the western side of the great enclosure wall (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 249, folio 252); and outside the enclosure of Data Ganj Bakhsh the *mujawirs* have many seats (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 289, 291; folios 292, 294).

Water was part of the endowed fabric. The water of one low lying mosque goes nowhere outside and soak pits are built on the spot (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 151, folio 154); an ancient well survives where the tank of Masjid Chinian Wali has vanished (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 154, folio 157); and at the Thibi Wala shivala an ancient well serves as a soakaway for the water that drips on Shiv ji night and day, the one thing Raja Dina Nath left untouched when he rebuilt everything else (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 123–124, folios 126–127). There is a *sabil* for giving water at Mozang (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 225, folio 228), and at the Multani Dharmsala a masonry room for giving water, where a Brahman lives to serve it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 226–227, folios 229–230). At the Sant Shah mandir a newly dug well and a red stone *sabil* serve high and low alike, the well head roofed and pierced so that people draw water and bathe there (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 432, folio 439; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 433, folio 440). The Chhote Lal shiwala's well can be drawn from outside as well (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 447, folio 454), and the Akbari Mandi thakurdwara's well on the bazaar front is open to all for drawing water and bathing (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 451, folio 458). Over one Shivling water falls through a brass snake fed from the well terrace, and a soakage well takes it away (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 435, folio 442); a brass pipe does the same at Mahin Lal's shiwala, his name uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 447, folio 454). Against all these, Rai Singh's thakurdwara has no well, to the great hardship of those who live in the temple (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 456, folio 463).

Canal water ran through the garden belt and the institutions. It enters Shalamar from the south and runs inside every one of its gardens (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 351, folio 358), and Farah Bakhsh drains the upper gardens through the Sawan Bhadon (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 350, folio 357). Bagh Raja Teja Singh owes its green to the canal reaching it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 353, folio 360), and Bagh Lala Ratan Chand has been perfectly green since water was brought to it from the canal (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 355, folio 362). The contractor's famous cold well is fed with canal water through a channel (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 359, folio 366). Mela Ram's public tank by the railway station is filled from the canal and has a women's ghat built in full purdah (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 367, folio 374; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 368, folio 375), and Diwan Ratan Chand's tank is filled with canal water (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 224–225, folios 227–228). At the railway works the canal water is stored in two tanks, passed into two wells, raised by engine to an iron cistern on a very high masonry building, and distributed to the engines and to every workshop (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 424, folio 431).

The tilework of the city's tombs and mosques is named in two ways. At the Dai Anga mosque and on Jani Khan's dome the book calls it *kansi kar* work in yellow (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 268, folio 271; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 270, folio 273); at the Begampura mosque, whose minarets are green and yellow, and at the Nila Gumbad, whose grand dome is green and turquoise, it calls it *kashi kari* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 277, folio 280; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 286, folio 289). The same word recurs on Makhduma Begum's dome (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 260, folio 263), on the inscriptions of the Zakariya Khan mihrab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 262, folio 265), on Miskin Shah's walls (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 263, folio 266), and on Sharaf un-Nisa's tomb, where it forms great painted cypresses and gives the building its by-name, the Cypress Tomb (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 264, folio 267).

## Communities

Kanhaiya Lal surveys Muslim, Hindu, Sikh, Brahmo and Christian foundations in one frame, and he records traffic between the communities in both directions without comment. Hindus attended the Guru Ram Das gurdwara, and *qawwals* sang *kafis* of divine unity there (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 101–102, folios 104–105). Hindus and Muslims came together to Shah Dargahi's well (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 82, folio 85; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 299, folio 302). Kashmiri Pandits staffed two Shaiva temples founded by officers of the Sikh state (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 94, 96; folios 97, 99). The Baoli was chosen for any gathering of any kind in the Hindu religion (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 108, folio 111). The shrine of Sitla Mata was the resort of both Hindus and Muslims, and Muslims too, after their children recovered from smallpox, brought them there and made offerings (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 187, folio 190). Hindus and Muslims attended Miran Badshah's mazar on Thursdays (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 161, folio 164), both melas at Madho Lal Husain drew Hindus and Muslims (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 234, folio 237), and Muslims too mostly went to the mela of Bhadar Kali (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 229, folio 232). On the twenty eighth of Shaban Hindus and Muslims held a mela together at Shah Bilawal's khanqah (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 236, folio 239). At a Hindu bhagat's samadh *qawwali* was sung in the mystic raga on Mondays and Tuesdays (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 185, folio 188), and at an Ekadashi assembly inside a thakurdwara *qawwals* sang ragas of divine unity (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 462, folio 469).

Some of the city's crowds gathered at no shrine at all. The mouth of the contractor's cold water well was closed all winter and opened at the height of the hot season; thousands of water skins were carried from it into the city, and Hindus, Muslims and Christians crowded there, so that for two months a mela of onlookers was held at it every day (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 359, folio 366). Hindus, Muslims and Christians all gladly joined the subscription to repair a mosque (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 142, folio 145), and the Hindus and Muslims of Lahore alike acknowledged the holiness of Bhai Rupa (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 198, folio 201).

One exchange across communities founded a temple outright. Kanhaiya Lal relates that a Muslim Turkish soldier, of the Lodhi period by an uncertain reading, had taken an image from a mandir the Turks demolished in Hindustan and bathed and dressed it every day, because whatever his heart desired came to him; he sold it for a great deal of money to the Hindus of the mohalla, who placed it in the haveli that is now the Mata temple, and the soldier himself gave the image its name (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 457, folio 464; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 458, folio 465).

He divides the city's Muslims four ways. The Ahl-e Sunnat wa Jamaat come first, and in Lahore proper the Hanafis, the reading uncertain, are the most numerous of the Sunnis; the Shia Imamiya mourn greatly in the ten days of Muharram and make taziyas; the Wahhabiya Najdiya follow no imam and call themselves *muwahhid*; and the Sufis bear enmity to no one, mostly listen to *sama*, and are called the sect of peace with all and without prejudice (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 44–45, folios 47–48). He prints the orders on a line of their own, Qadiri, Chishti, Suhrawardi, Naqshbandi, two further names uncertain in the printed text, Naushahi and others, a list attached to no shrine, saint, khanqah or place (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 44, folio 47). Against his irenic picture of the Sufis stand the Wahhabis' possession of a mosque, who oppose both Sunni and Shia (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 154–155, folios 157–158), and a dated riot. On the tenth of Muharram the Zuljanah, the model horse of Imam Husain, was led round the whole city and brought to the Imambara, a building of the Shia Imamiya whose patrons were Nawab Nawazish Ali Khan Qizilbash and Nawab Nasir Ali Khan; in 1849 AD, a year printed without doubt, there was a fierce quarrel between Shia and Sunni near Shah Alami Gate that came to blows, and that day the Sunnis pulled down the enclosure's inner buildings, threw down the battlements of the tomb, filled the well with bricks and beat Gami Shah unconscious, until the Deputy Commissioner summoned a troop of cavalry from the Anarkali cantonment, the crowd dispersed, and those arrested received some punishment (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 302, folio 305).

Membership of a Sufi order appears in the professional classes rather than at the shrines. A physician's adopted son of a Sayyid family had taken the oath of allegiance in the Naushahi Qadiri line, and so joined the word *faqir* to his name that his descendants, for all their wealth, are still called Faqir (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 57, folio 60). A practising Lahore physician was also a pir, through whom the grace of the Chishti and Qadiri lines flowed, and who had many disciples (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 58, folio 61). A graveyard of Chishti faqirs, its name read uncertainly as Khair Shahi, was built on Zain Khan's open ground with a masonry mosque (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 87, folio 90). At Teel Pura a khanqah and madrasa survived the collapse of the quarter, its dervishes and students staying on, and the madrasa still flourishes (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 89, folio 92). Imam Ghulam Muhammad, a faqir of the Qadiri order, left as his memorial *Ganj-e Makhfi*, a book of Sufism in verse (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 163, folio 166).

Hindu, Sikh and Brahmo foundations stand in the same register as the Muslim ones. Every Sunday followers of the Brahmo faith gather at the Brahmo Samaj to worship the one God, and a paid chowkidar guards the building (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 449, folio 456). One reform controversy is noticed from the temple's side: the discourse of Sardha Ram Phaluri, his name uncertain, was directed against the new faiths, the Arya and the Brahmo, which are wholly opposed to the worship of images (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 441, folio 448). The Sikh establishments include the Baoli with its shops, its naqara and a building fund drawn from Khalsa salaries (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 109–114, folios 112–117); Baba Khuda Singh's dharamsala with its *gaddi nashin* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 102–103, folios 105–106); the Kuka dharamsal and its prosecution by the state (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 105, folio 108); the bhang dharamsal, kept with the government's permission (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 213, folio 216); the Nihangs of Shahid Ganj (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220); the regular gathering at Mozang (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 226, folio 229); Tali Sahib, its stipend stopped and its tree felled (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 228–229, folios 231–232); the royal samadhs kept by a single salaried boy (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 212, folio 215); and the eleven samadhs of the eleven ranis who became sati with Ranjit Singh (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 219, folio 222).

The Christian foundations are counted with the rest. The Mian Mir church seats about eight hundred and was built for ninety thousand rupees, the first figure uncertain, of which forty five thousand came from the government and the rest from subscription (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 390, folio 397; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 394, folio 401). At another, unnamed church a bell is rung not only for prayer but every day at twelve o'clock (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 426, folio 433). The *padri* bought Bagh Bhai Mahan Singh from Bhai Sawaya Singh, his name uncertain, changed the form of the garden and built houses there in his own fashion; the Christian women and men living there receive stipends from the padris and are taught every day (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 355, folio 362). There is a Mission School among the buildings near Fazl Shah's grave and the Rang Mahal (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 329, folio 336).

The book records separation as well as mixing. The settlement of the cow killing butchers was set apart by royal order because the Hindu subjects loathed them, and a magnificent masonry mosque was built there (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 89, folio 92); the quarter was later abandoned and its people mutilated, and only a masonry mosque still stands (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 90, folio 93). Under the British the honorary magistracy was a communal quota of eight of Lahore's notables, four Hindu and four Muslim (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 61, folio 64), and a hereditary Chief Pandit is said to be distinguished by the honorary magistracy (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 134, folio 137). The hospital kept four separate kitchens, one each for Hindu, Muslim, and Christian and European patients and a shopkeeper in the fourth, with Hindu and Muslim cooks and servants (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 380, folio 387). Women's and men's ghats were built apart at Diwan Ratan Chand's tank, with a gate to the women's ghat where women in purdah bathe, and at the bathing tank of the cremation ground (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 223, folio 226).

Women founded and patronised holy places in all three traditions. Musammat Jwala Dai, wife of Ram Kishan, built her mandir and made it *waqf*; it was built nine years ago and she died once the building was finished, and her son in law Har Narain is now its guardian and gives five to seven rupees a month (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 126–127, folios 129–130). The mother of a Kanhaiya Lal Kambo, the second name uncertain, built Chor Mor Wala with her own money in Ranjit Singh's time and gave it to Mahant Balram Das (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 124, folio 127). The third shivala at Mehr Das was founded by a woman called Bhagtan who kept a shop selling *paan* under Sikh rule (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 130, folio 133). The founder of the Masti Darwaza mosque was Maryam Zamani, wife of the emperor Jahangir (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 142, folio 145). Dai Anga, the royal wet nurse, lived in Jahangir's household; her name, read uncertainly, was Zeb-un-Nisa and Anga her title (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 167, folio 170). Kanhaiya Lal explains the title: *ang* means the body, and an *anga* is one with whom a person is one soul in two bodies (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 267, folio 270). As Sultan Begum's wet nurse she was given a garden outright once it was finished, and her grave is in it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 307, folio 310). Daya Lado, wet nurse of Prince Salim, built a mosque in her own lifetime and made her grave in its courtyard (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 300, folio 303), and after her death, while Muhammad Shakur looked after the mosque, a learned Maulvi Ismatullah taught there (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 300, folio 303). Moran the courtesan built the mosque that bears her name, which Kanhaiya Lal calls a renowned mosque of Lahore, in a year read as 1223 AH; people held her order stronger than the Maharaja's, and her family still lives in the same bazaar, headed by a chaudhri of the courtesans' community (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 179–181, folios 182–184). Zeb-un-Nisa Begum is credited as owner and founder, as a poet, as builder of her own tomb in her lifetime, and as builder of Shah Rustam Ghazi's red stone tomb and marble grave (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 309–312, folios 312–315). Begampura is named after a woman: Begum Jan, her name uncertain, mother of Nawab Khan Bahadur, built all of it in her own lifetime, and so it is known as Begampura to this day (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 278, folio 281). Rani Lachhmi built a thakurdwara out of deep devotion and left it as her memorial in this transient world (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 199, folio 202). Kanhaiya Lal explains Bhauri Sarkar's name from her reddish hair, *bhura* being the Punjabi word for a red shading into black, and her work was carried out in the time of Maharaja Sher Singh (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 200, folio 203). Rani Jindan built the shivala beside her thakurdwara while she held power (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 205, folio 208), and laid the foundation of Sardar Jawahar Singh's samadh, the work continuing until she was exiled to Hindustan by order of the English government (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 196, folio 199). Rani Gul Begum held the largest jagir of all and twelve hundred rupees a month under the English (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 293, folio 296). Nawab Zakariya Khan built a haveli for Kallo Bai of the *mutriba* community, the singing women, because she could not enter the Nawab's harem (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 334, folio 341). And one mosque was the women's mosque of a palace, where the women of the household of the governor of Lahore came to pray by a path from inside the palace (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 143, folio 146).

Sati is recorded as a fact of the city's fabric. There are eleven samadhs with Ranjit Singh's (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 219, folio 222). At Jamadar Khushal Singh's are the samadh of his wife, who became sati with him, and two more whose names are unknown, perhaps, Kanhaiya Lal thinks, those of their slave women (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 193, folio 196). There are others at Sher Singh's (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 211, folio 214) and for Sardar Bur Singh's wife (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 212, folio 215), four at Kharak Singh's for the women who burned with the Maharaja's body and two of the same kind for Nau Nihal Singh (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 222, folio 225). At Bhai Wasti Ram's, women's samadhs are of marble, his mother's marble from head to foot (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 202, folio 205).

## Shrines, Endowments and the State

Kanhaiya Lal is himself a party to part of what he reports. He was the Executive Engineer through whom the state's repairs to Lahore's buildings ran, and his book records the charge: an annual repair grant for Shalamar Bagh administered by him (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 348, folio 355); the Tehsil building, whose supervision and annual repair, the book says, belong to its author (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 371, folio 378); Montgomery Hall, under his supervision, whose necessary annual repairs are carried out through him by the Municipal Committee of Lahore, and for which his replacement roof was ready at the end of 1865 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 402, folio 409); and the women's jail, whose repair and supervision are still his (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 409, folio 416). Where he reports a repair, a grant or a state building in his own division, the evidence is his own administrative record, and he petitions in print for more of it, writing of the Badshahi Masjid that if the administration would turn its attention to it once more, the mosque would be fully repaired (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 142, folio 145). His description of compensation as full and uncontested (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 38, folio 41), his framing of Sikh loss against English restitution (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 148, 150, 153, 158; folios 151, 153, 156, 161), and his relay of the administrations' own reasons for demolition (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 15, 21; folios 10, 16) come from the same position.

The state administered, licensed and suppressed. The one explicit act of state mela administration in the book concerns the *chiraghan* mela held inside a garden: it used to last one day, and now the government has made it two, and merchandise comes there for sale; the book ties it to no shrine or saint (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 351, folio 358). Outside the Delhi Gate stood the house of the Rasul Shahi order, among whom, Kanhaiya Lal writes, the drinking of wine comes before every act of worship, and where a still ran day and night. On the recommendation of Raja Dina Nath the British licensed them to distil for their own drinking only; for fifteen or sixteen years the still was left to them and the house flourished as before, and then, on information from someone whose description is uncertain, the government grew suspicious and closed the still, and from that day no one came to the house (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 247–248, folios 250–251). At the bhang dharamsal, built expressly for Sikhs to lodge the night and drink bhang, bhang was ground day and night and its Sikh drinkers gathered with the government's permission, and no one hindered them (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 213, folio 216).

The Settlement department appears as the forum that dismissed a claim to custody of a shrine (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 231, folio 234). The *sair* of the whole city, the duty on articles of trade, was held by Harbari Singh, his name uncertain, whose Lahore garden the government hospital replaced (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 377, folio 384), and on the one well kept when that garden was cleared the same wooden Persian wheel was retained (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 377, folio 384).

The courts of the Muslim kings are described as they were and as they ended. In their time Lahore had four muftis and one qazi; each mufti was reckoned the ruler of a quarter of the city, every case was completed in the mufti's court and then sent to the qazi, and the final order was issued by the qazi's department; of the old muftis, Kanhaiya Lal writes, only the name is left (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 51, folio 54). Under colonial sanction hereditary claim gave way to qualification: Shams-ud-Din drew up a petition and had the seals of the people, elders, chiefs and gentry of Lahore set on it, but the officials preferred a qualified jurist and another man was made qazi, and the court sought its fatwas from the blind Hafiz Wali Allah (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 51, folio 54). Earlier, a king wished to make Pir Siraj-ud-Din the city's qazi; he refused the office, and for this disobedience was ordered beheaded in the crossroads bazaar, until the ulama who had brought him to court won his pardon by much pleading; from that day he closed the door of teaching and instruction, the last word uncertain, renounced the world and withdrew into seclusion (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 149, folio 152). In the Sikh period an oath on the Quran was court procedure: when investigation showed one such oath to be false, the Maharaja had both the man's hands cut off, and he was known thereafter as Hafiz Tunda, the name uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 178–179, folios 181–182). In the case of Haqiqat Rai, the Qazi of the city wrote a fatwa for his death and sent it to the governor of Lahore for approval, and Zakariya Khan told him that if he became a Muslim his life could be saved and otherwise he would be beheaded; he refused and was beheaded. The book gives no date of any kind for it, and states his offence twice and differently (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 208, folio 211).

Teaching passed from the mosques to the government. Under the Sikhs there was no government madrasa, but four madrasas in mosques and faqirs' lodges, one of them teaching thousands of Hindus and Muslims, and boys were taught in every mosque besides; the course was Persian, Arabic, grammar, syntax, logic, rhetoric, hadith and Quranic commentary, and the first lesson was the Quran (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 38–39, folios 41–42). Kanhaiya Lal then writes that the English government removed all those defects and opened government schools everywhere (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 39, folio 42). A single state college taught Hindu law, the *shastri*, under Pandit Gur Parshad, his name uncertain, and Muslim *sharia* questions under Maulvi Abd al-Hakim, beside English law (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 39, folio 42), and in one school boys were made to learn by heart *ghazals* in praise of Jesus Christ (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 40, folio 43). At Moran's mosque the mechanism is stated plainly. By Maharaja Ranjit Singh's order the imamat of the mosque was given to Maulana Ghulam Rasul and Ghulam Allah, because there was then no maulvi or teacher in Lahore greater than they and they were the master teachers of the whole Punjab; with them a madrasa began, the mosque's fame spread through other lands and students came from far away; and when both died and government schools were opened under English rule, the chain of the madrasa broke (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 180, folio 183). Of Ghulam Allah's five sons, Imam al-Din is dead; Nizam al-Din teaches without payment, for the sake of God, in Bombay; Ahmad Bakhsh is a teacher in the government school at Jhelum; Khalifa Ghulam Muhyi al-Din teaches at Rahon in the Jalandhar district, the place name uncertain; and Khalifa Hamid al-Din is a teacher in the Lahore Government School and still holds the imamat of the mosque (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 180–181, folios 183–184). Three of the five sons were in state teaching posts within two generations.

The shrine and mosque schools that survived are listed beside this. The Wazir Khan mosque has cells on three sides for scholars and students to live in, sixteen great cells set round the mosque wall (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 156–157, folios 159–160). The Badshahi Masjid has cells for dervishes and students (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 140, folio 143), and a Madrasa Islamiya was started in it with a named imam and preacher (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 141, folio 144). A madrasa is attached to a Suhrawardi tomb (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 169, folio 172), and in the cell of one mosque Maulvi Muhammad Bakhsh teaches (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 183, folio 186). Mian Wada's madrasa teaches Quran, commentary, hadith and jurisprudence (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 238, folio 241); it was finished in the Akbari period in a year read as 1000 AH (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 237, folio 240), it maintains the blind and lame by the hundred, and it is endowed on the grain of a *rakh* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 239, folio 242). The expense of another madrasa was fixed on the treasury of Lahore by the emperor's order (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 363, folio 370). Craft and copying went on in the holy places too: Miran Bakhsh the illuminator, famed for illumination in printing ink and for gold work, worked in a room of a mosque, a scribe and an illuminator beside the *mujawirs*, and the bookbinders' endowment gave way to employment in the printing presses (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 162, folio 165).

The railway acquired religious property. It took the Dai Anga mosque and paid its private occupant (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 168, folio 171; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 268, folio 271); it bought for five hundred rupees the land that closed a temple's western gate (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 214, folio 217); it took Tali Sahib's land and cut down its worshipped tree (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 228–229, folios 231–232); it kept its material in the Shahjahani sarai (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 320, folio 327); it lodged its men in a converted tomb (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 257, folio 260) and its workshop officer in a converted mosque (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 268, folio 271); and it brought mela crowds from Amritsar and distant cities (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 351, folio 358). In one sarai the shops between its northern gate and the bazaar were occupied by women of the *kasbi* trade, and a government police post stood inside it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 359, folio 366; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 360, folio 367).

The furthest passage of a holy place into state hands is Gilani's tomb. Desolated by Sikh plunder, its dome was made a wrestlers' arena, and a jamadar demolished the grave and built an octagonal house round it; the house was occupied by British Deputy Commissioners and a Commissioner and then taken into government as the Lieutenant Governor's residence, and the old tomb remains as it was in the lower storey, with the doorway of one dome blocked (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 394–396, folios 401–403).

## Festivals and Practice

Kanhaiya Lal dates the Muslim observances of the city one by one. On the twenty eighth of Shaban Hindus and Muslims hold a mela with fireworks, if the word is read rightly, at Shah Bilawal's khanqah (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 236, folio 239). Mian Mir has five melas a year, one of them the urs of two days and two nights and four on the four Wednesdays of Sawan (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 245, folio 248). There is an annual mela at Ghore Shah (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 237, folio 240), and the annual urs of Shaikh Musa is run by the blacksmiths (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 246, folio 249). At Hamid Qari's khanqah the urs falls on the thirteenth and fourteenth of Jumada al-Thani, the second figure uncertain, the date on which, the book says, he died (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 253, folio 256). There is an annual urs at Shaikh Mahmud Shah Naqshbandi's tomb (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 261, folio 264), and Hassu Teli's annual urs is held on the third of Shawwal (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 169, folio 172; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 269, folio 272). Faqir Sain Qutb Shah Qadri's is held twice a year, with a *bhandara* and *qawwali* all day (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 271, folio 274). Fateh Shah Mast's falls on the seventh of Shawwal (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 274, folio 277); Mauj Darya's annual urs draws a great crowd (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 280, folio 283); and Shah Chiragh's falls on the seventeenth of Rabi us-Sani (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 282, folio 285). At Shah Abu Ishaq Qadri's, on the fifth of Muharram, the Quran is read all night and then, by an uncertain reading, songs are listened to (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 283–284, folios 286–287). Abul Muali has three melas a year, with illumination and dancing courtesans on the first night and *qawwali* on the second, besides both Eids (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 285, folio 288). Taj Shah Majzub's urs falls on the date of his death (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 286, folio 289). At Data Ganj Bakhsh there are a Friday mela, the urs on the eighteenth of Safar, the figure uncertain, the mela of the Last Wednesday and a Thursday night vigil (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 291, folio 294). Muhammad Fazil's falls on the thirteenth of Rajab and Shah Sharf's on the thirteenth of Safar, both figures uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 297, folio 300), and Shah Sarwani's on the ninth of Safar and the ninth of Rabi al-Awwal (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 303, folio 306). Shah Yaqub Zanjani had a Thursday mela once and has a great mela in Rajab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 301, folio 304). The Huzuri khanqah holds a two day annual mela, lamps on the first day and *qawwali* on the second (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 167, folio 170; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 267, folio 270). At a Qadiri and Shattari mazar a mela is held every year at which Sufis gather to hear *sama* and fall into ecstasy (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 144, folio 147). And at Shah Abu al-Maali's *rauza* there are the melas of both Eids, offerings, and an annual urs attended, by an uncertain reading, in thousands (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 87, folio 90).

The Hindu and Sikh calendars are recorded as closely. On every Ekadashi one temple holds a great mela, devotees gather in numbers, bhagats sing bhajans all night and offerings are made in abundance (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 120, folio 123), and at an Ekadashi assembly inside a thakurdwara *qawwals* come and delight the listeners with songs of divine unity (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 462, folio 469). At the Kali Mata mandir a mela gathers every day of the nine days of Navratri and offerings are many; hundreds of Hindus come to bow their foreheads, and Hindus from other lands too come there and are blessed (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 133, folio 136). On the day of Basant a mela is held at the samadh of Haqiqat Rai, and the income from its offerings is good (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 208, folio 211). On the night of the fourteenth of Phagan a great mela is held at the shivala by a well whose name, Badhu-wala, is uncertain, and countless people come from the city to worship (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 214, folio 217). The Devi Dwara of Rajo and Dharman holds two annual melas, on the Ashtami of Chait and the Ashtami of Asoj, the month names uncertain, when great crowds gather and offerings are abundant, and through the year it holds vigils at which the bhagats sing the Devi's hymns all night; the book does not say who takes the offerings (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 439, folio 446). The Mata temple holds eight vigils a month, one every Sunday, the day uncertain, and one every Tuesday, and another on the *panchami*, at which *karah prashad*, the word uncertain, is distributed (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 457, folio 464). At Mozang the Sikhs gather on the fifth day of every bright half of the moon, when *prashad* is distributed and lamps are lit (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 226, folio 229).

The greatest mela of the city is Bhadar Kali's at Niaz Beg, held every year in the month of Jeth; no mela in Lahore is greater, and Muslims too mostly attend (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 229, folio 232). Because it falls in the hot season people suffer, and relief comes largely from the city's nobles, the word uncertain: at the mela stalls of sharbat, water and pounded bhang are set up and sharbat is given to all alike; there is little shade and tents are pitched; the crowd stays a night and a day; and shopkeepers move their shops there in advance and prepare sweets (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 230, folio 233). Inside the walls, on the day of Bhadar Kali's mela at Niaz Beg, the city temple of Bhadr Kali also draws an immense gathering of women and men, those who do not go out to the village coming there to worship (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 126, folio 129). Than Bhairo's great mela falls on a Sunday at the new moon of Bhadon, with offerings and an all night vigil of bhajans, and besides it hundreds of people come from the city every Sunday to bow their heads there (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 232, folio 235).

Many melas, urs and *sama* gatherings were in decline when he wrote, and he keeps the tenses apart. At the mazar of Miran Badshah inside the Wazir Khan mosque, in the time of the Sikhs, a mela was held every Thursday and thousands gathered, and the annual urs and *sama* were held with great pomp; now Hindus and Muslims still attend on Thursdays and the annual urs is still held, but the *sama* is spoken of only in the past (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 161, folio 164). At Imam Ghulam Muhammad's tomb an annual mela and a *sama* gathering used to be held, and both have now stopped entirely (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 163, folio 166). At Sayyid Sarbuland's mazar crowds gathered on Thursdays in the Sikh period and the annual urs was held with pomp, but that brisk trade is no more (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 164, folio 167); he uses the same formula of the tomb at the Masjid Suf (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 163, folio 166) and of the bookbinders' shops (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 162, folio 165). At Fateh Shah Mast's there was a great mela in the time of the Sikhs, but now not such a crowd (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 274, folio 277). At Shah Husain Zanjani's mazar a great annual mela was held in the Sikh period, but little by little its splendour has gone (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 275, folio 278). Shah Yaqub Zanjani's melas were killed by his neighbours' building: on two sides Lala Ratan Chand's sarai and the buildings of his tank have hidden the khanqah, it is hemmed in on all four sides, and its graveyard too has been closed (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 301, folio 304). Fazl Shah Majzub's urs shrank to an annual *fatiha* with the death of his patron (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 325, folio 332). Against these, two melas grew: Mian Mir's, with the population of the garrison (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 245, folio 248), and the *chiraghan* mela, whose crowds come from Lahore, Amritsar and distant cities because of the railway (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 351, folio 358).

Some festivals were paid for privately. At Taj Shah Majzub's the annual mela is held on his date, and its managers, a man called Nur Muhammad and his son Bota, hold it at their own expense, in fulfilment of a prayer that was granted (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 286, folio 289).

Daily and weekly rites are timed. At the Kotwali shiwala *arti* is performed at four in the morning and seven in the evening, and also whenever a notable comes, so that no hour is empty of worship (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 95, folio 98), with special worship on Mondays (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 96, folio 99). The great kettledrum, the pipe and the gong are its instruments of *arti* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 95, folio 98), and the pipe, the gong and the conch sound so loudly that no one can hear anyone else speak (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 96, folio 99). *Arti* is performed twice daily at the Bhagat Ram shiwala (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 100, folio 103); the Granth is read twice daily at the Guru Ram Das gurdwara (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 102, folio 105); at the Ram Dwara the *katha* of the Bhagavata, the Ramayana and other holy books is recited twice daily (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 128, folio 131); and worship is performed twice daily at the Radha Kishan thakurdwara (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 134, folio 137) and in Chhajju Bhagat's house, whose great hall, Kanhaiya Lal says, should be thought of as a shivala (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 135, folio 138). At Chor Mor Wala the mahant himself gives the *katha* every evening, being a learned man (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 125, folio 128). A lamp burns night and day at the Bhairo ji *asthan* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 128, folio 131) and at Than Bhairo's fire place, never going out (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 231, folio 234). A naqara is played at two times of day at the Baoli (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 109, folio 112) and at *arti* time at Banke Bihari (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 118, folio 121), and mystic songs are sung in the evening at the Kuka dharamsal (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 105, folio 108). At a Hindu bhagat's samadh, on every Monday and Tuesday, Hindu devotees, women and men, attend and *qawwali* is sung in the mystic raga (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 185, folio 188). A saint's shop became a second site of his cult: a flag still flies over it, a lamp is lit there every day, and on Thursdays many lamps are lit (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 168–169, folios 171–172; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 269, folio 272). At Pir Bhola's grave boys play all day and climb up on the grave as though it were a horse, the word uncertain, and every Friday the boys of the city make a mazar of Pir Bhola in lane after lane, offering flowers on it and lighting lamps (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 150, folio 153). At the Ram Dwara every attender is handed a rosary of *tulsi* by the mahant and told to sing the praise of Ram; a heap of rosaries lies in the middle of the circle, and one man is appointed solely to look after them (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 128, folio 131).

Vows were tied to sickness. At Sitla Mata worship goes on all the time, but the special worship comes when smallpox is raging, and thousands whose children have recovered from it come in joy (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 187, folio 190); the same is done at the Sitla Devi mandir with cash and a goat (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 452–453, folios 459–460).

Congregations are counted only once. Six or seven thousand worshippers came to gather for the Eid prayers at the Badshahi Masjid (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 142, folio 145). Elsewhere visitors are not numbered except in round terms, thousands at Miran Badshah's Thursday mela (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 161, folio 164), hundreds of Hindus at Kali Mata (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 133, folio 136) and hundreds every Sunday at Than Bhairo (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 232, folio 235), and about eight hundred seats, by estimate, at the Mian Mir church (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 394, folio 401).

The *langar* fed the poor across the traditions. At the Ram Dwara *langar* is given twice a day, the verb uncertain: by former custom any poor person who attends is fed, and any traveller who comes is given food too, and the offerings go to two heads only, the necessary expenses of Pandit Kanshi Ram and the cost of the daily *langar* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 128, folio 131). Shah Bilawal Qadri spent thousands of rupees a day on a public *langar*, and thousands of the poor ate at meal times from his guest house, the last words uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 235, folio 238). A public *langar* ran continuously at Mauj Darya's house (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 279, folio 282). At Than Bhairo a masonry building is called the *langar* house, the word uncertain, because *langar* is served there on mela day (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 231, folio 234); the temple's mahant feeds the faqirs and jogis who gather for the mela, and lay visitors cook their own food (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 232, folio 235). At both of Shah Sharf's melas faqirs gather and food is distributed (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 297, folio 300); at Shah Sarwani's tomb faqirs gather on both urs dates to eat and to hear *qawwali* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 303, folio 306); at Faqir Sain Qutb Shah Qadri's a *bhandara*, the word uncertain, is distributed among the faqirs (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 271, folio 274); and at Mubarak Haveli the food given to the poor and faqirs for the ten days of Muharram is cooked in one part of the building (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 332, folio 339).

Land and mills stood behind the kitchens. A dharamsal has forty bighas and a running well, whose income Nihang Singh takes and with which he feeds travellers, and a mill built to grind flour for them (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 213–214, folios 216–217). A mill runs continuously inside Mian Wada's enclosure (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 238, folio 241), and another was built for grinding flour on the Bhauri Sarkar estate (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 201, folio 204). Mian Wada's madrasa was a charity for the disabled with a headcount and a curriculum: under the Sikhs the blind, the lame, the crippled, a hundred dervishes and faqirs, and students in hundreds received bread and clothing from it, and in the author's own day about two hundred blind and lame and five dervishes are maintained by the khanqah, all are taught the Quran, and the blind are made to learn it by heart (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 239, folio 242). Hospitality was the mahant's rule at one establishment (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 204, folio 207). One dharamsala lived wholly on offerings, its faqirs supported only by the service and gifts of devotees, with bhajans daily in the third watch, offerings made on the Granth and food for any travelling faqir (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 103, folio 106). Before moving the image of Thakur ji from Rangilpur to Lahore with great honour, Hari Narayan first spent a great deal of money there feeding sadhus and Hindus (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 120, folio 123).

Bhang, *sama* and music were part of shrine practice. At the samadh of Guru Arjan a building called the Sikhs' cauldron, the word uncertain, has a stone mortar sunk in the ground and a pestle for grinding bhang (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 206, folio 209). At Shahid Ganj Nihang Sikhs grind bhang all day in a roadside hall where its cauldrons stand (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220), and the stalls of the Bhadar Kali mela serve pounded bhang (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 230, folio 233). *Sama* and *qawwali* are recorded where they are held and where they have stopped (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 144, folio 147; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 161, folio 164; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 163, folio 166; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 167, folio 170; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 185, folio 188; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 271, folio 274; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 303, folio 306), with Punjabi *kafis*, *barah mah* and *abyat* sung in assemblies by singers whose name is uncertain in the printed text (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 144, folio 147).

## In Verse

Kanhaiya Lal copies the verse inscriptions of the city's mosques. Over the gate of the mosque of Muhammad Salih Kambo three niches carry two lines naming its founder, and the year 1058 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 152, folio 155):

بانی این مسجد زیبا نگار
بنائے آل محمد صالح است

The lines name the founder of the mosque, and the book dates it 1058, uncertainly (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 152, folio 155).

The outer gateway of Masjid Chinian Wali carries a chronogram couplet:

طرفہ معمار خرد تاریخ سال
گفت زیبا مسجد از افراز خان[OCR?]

Kanhaiya Lal reads it as 1082 AH, against 1080 on the inner mihrab, both readings uncertain, and from the two dates he concludes that two years passed between the building of the interior and of the outer gateway (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 153, folio 156).

At Roshnai Gate he reads a fatal accident as recompense. A parapet fell suddenly on two men and dashed out their brains, and he quotes a Persian couplet on the requital of deeds; the two dead are not named (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 222, folio 225):

از مکافات عمل غافل مشو
ہو[OCR?] گندم از گندم بروبید[OCR?] جو ز جو

A named local poet supplied one chronogram: Kanhaiya Lal says the quatrain is the composition of Farid, a poet of Lahore, and that Farid's line is written beneath it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 182, folio 185). Three of the four chronograms in that part of the book call the mosque a second Kaaba (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 177, folio 180; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 178, folio 181; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 182, folio 185), and all four put the date in the mouth of the unseen voice, the *hatif* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 177, 178, 180, 182; folios 180, 181, 183, 185).

## The Place in the Books

Kanhaiya Lal writes as an engineer and a resident, and his hand shows in the description. He reconstructs vanished features in the presumptive mood: when the mosque was built its founder must have kept the plinth very high, and there must have been shops beneath the northern wall (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 153, folio 156). Where a building has no vocabulary of its own he supplies one, again and again with the phrase that a thing should be called so: the door of a veranda should be called the outer door of the temple (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 199, folio 202), one part should be called the fifth degree (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 197, folio 200), another a three arched seat (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 200, folio 203), and another the northern wall (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 199–200, folios 202–203); a house may rightly be called a collection of temples (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 129, folio 132), and a great hall should be thought of as a shivala (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 135, folio 138).

His judgements of beauty are either warranted by European opinion or delivered flat. The Wazir Khan mosque is famous among Europeans, it may rightly be called the pride and glory of Lahore, great painters learn their art from it, and travellers from far away come to Lahore and carry off copies of its niches (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 155, folio 158). The Badshahi Masjid is larger than the Jami Masjid of Delhi (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 139, folio 142). Of the Sufi Wali mosque he writes that it is a pity its dome was never built, for its beauty would have been doubled (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 173, folio 176). A parapet is shaped so that from a distance it looks like domes (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 172, folio 175), and the unrepaired Shiv Gar shivala in truth needs no repair, so solidly is it built (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 199, folio 202). At Sher Singh's modest samadh he gives a moral reading: the visitor's tears fall unbidden, and he becomes certain that the world is transient (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 210, folio 213). He reports a private donor's structural change with an engineer's interest: Nura Iman Wala repaired the Wazir Khan mosque at a cost of thousands of rupees, and of the eight openings above the dome of each of the four minarets he had four closed and four left open for greater strength (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 174, folio 177). At Nawab Mian Khan's tomb, he notes, when a mosque was built on the west a matching one was built on the east, purely for beauty (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 273, folio 276).

He dates many buildings by the years elapsed since their foundation, reckoned from a present he does not fix. The Teja Singh thakurdwara was built under English rule twenty five years ago on the site of a demolished old haveli (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 103, folio 106); Baikunth Das's thakurdwara was raised by subscription ten years ago (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 114, folio 117); the Shivala Ragnath Mishr is forty years old (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 116, folio 119), the Kuka dharamsal fifteen (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 105, folio 108), and Khuda Singh died eighteen years ago (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 102, folio 105). Bishno Devi's *devi dwara* is forty years old (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 121, folio 124), Chor Mor Wala was founded fifty years ago in Ranjit Singh's time (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 124, folio 127), Jwala Dai's mandir nine years ago (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 127, folio 130), the Ram Dwara six years ago, the figure uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 127–128, folios 130–131), Mehr Das's own mandir not less than two hundred years ago (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 129, folio 132), and the Chhajju Bhagat thakurdwara ten or twelve years ago (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 135–136, folios 138–139). Masjid Muhammad Salih Kambo is two hundred and twenty six years old, the figure uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 152, folio 155), and the Wazir Khan mosque two hundred and fifty one (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 155, folio 158). Shiv Gar died forty years ago (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 199, folio 202) and Daryagar ten (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 209, folio 212); the Ganga Parbat mandirs were founded twenty five years ago, the figure uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 215, folio 218); two hundred years have passed since Shah Bilawal's exhumation (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 235, folio 238); and the Pir Sabz tree is nearly two centuries old (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 162, folio 165). The only statement of his own moment in these pages dates the present time to Samvat 1937, the year uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 130, folio 133), so none of these spans can be turned into a year without fixing when the book was written.

Where he has no year at all he dates by reign: in the reign of Shah Jahan (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 184, folio 187); in Akbar's time, when the foundation of a mandir was laid that still stands, and to which devotees come in numbers because of its antiquity (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 214, folio 217); of former times (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 181, folio 184); built entirely in the Sikh period (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 230, folio 233); built in the reign of Ahmad Shah (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 169, folio 172); in the time of the Tughluq sultanate (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 162, folio 165) and of Sultan Muhammad Tughluq (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 149, folio 152); in the Alamgiri period (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 150, folio 153; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 145, folio 148); and in the Lodi sultanate (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 151, folio 154). In the last part of the book the frames of the Chughtai kings, the kings of old, the Sikh government and the English government stand side by side without comment, with the revolution of the kingdom among them, and both 1857 and the fall of the Sikhs are called only a disturbance, *mufsida* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 396, folio 403; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 399, folio 406; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 444–455, folios 451–462).

He frames the city's history as a sequence of destructions. His contents list thirteen numbered shocks, *sadme*, running from his printed page 17 to 31, followed by the present form of the city of Lahore at 32 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 2), announced as an account of the ages in which the shocks of plunder and demolition fell upon the city (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 11, folio 6; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 12, folio 7).

One passage must be read as his judgement rather than as description: a hostile portrait of the patron community of a mosque. Of the Sadhus of Takiya Sadhuwan, Muslim Kashmiris who came under Sikh rule, he writes that they were all tenants once and have now become owners of property and wealthy; that they ply no visible trade or calling, their business being to bring in money by every stratagem; that wherever they go they change their names, one calling himself Sufi Shah, another Shatranji Shah, another Miskin Shah; and that one becomes a faqir on the path, another a *majzub*, another a prince (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 170–171, folios 173–174). A named author here accuses a named Lahore community of impersonating faqirs for a living.

He names the texts he relies on, and where they fail him. *Tuhfat al-Wasilin* is his textual authority twice, its title spelt two ways (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 164, folio 167; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 165, folio 168), and it is silent where he needed it: of Pir Bai, the name uncertain, no one knows the real name of the person buried there, and the author of *Tuhfat al-Wasilin* did not record it in his book either (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 165, folio 168). *Haqiqat al-Fuqara* by Pir Muhammad is a Persian verse life of Lal Husain written by an eyewitness (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 233, folio 236). Munshi Inayatullah, author of *Bahar-e Danish*, was one man's maternal uncle and father in law (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 152, folio 155). One sect has its own granth in a script whose name is uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 186, folio 189). And for the marble of Bahadur Khan's tomb he has only the testimony of old people (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 258, folio 261).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **Mauj Darya Bukhari's endowment from Akbar.** In one place it is a jagir of two lakh rupees with the township of Batala (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 79, folio 82); in another a jagir of one lakh twenty five thousand at Batala, the place name uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 279, folio 282).
- **Shah Sharaf's reburial.** In one place it was carried out through Faqir Aziz-ud-Din, in Haji Muhammad Saeed's enclosure at Dola Wari, with a small masonry platform by the Maharaja's order (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 80–81, folios 83–84); in another through Faqir Nur-ud-Din, under a very small platform and enclosure wall, when Khwaja Muhammad Saeed's tomb and mosque were demolished (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 287–288, folios 290–291).
- **Shah Dargahi's well.** In one place the bread and the one paisa lamp offering are taken by the zamindar of the well (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 82, folio 85); in another the bread is distributed to the people and the one paisa given to the zamindars of the well (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 299, folio 302).
- **Shah Abu al-Maali's festivals.** In one place the melas of both Eids and an annual urs attended in thousands (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 87, folio 90); in another three melas a year, with illumination and dancing courtesans, *qawwali*, and both Eids (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 285, folio 288).
- **Major MacGregor's office.** He is the Deputy Commissioner of Lahore restoring a mosque in a year read as 1850 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 143, folio 146), and elsewhere an Assistant Agent to the Resident, the title uncertain, dismissing a faqir's petitions (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 300–301, folios 303–304).
- **Mr Cope.** The private occupant of the Dai Anga mosque is spelled both Cope and Coop, and once given the office of superintendent of the Lahore press (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 168, folio 171; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 268, folio 271).
- **Jahangir's tomb.** The book gives 1034 AH, while the inscription on the grave reads 1037, both uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 314, 316; folios 321, 323).
- **Masjid Chinian Wali.** The outer gateway's chronogram reads 1082 AH and the inner mihrab 1080, both uncertain; Kanhaiya Lal reconciles them rather than choosing, concluding that two years passed between the interior and the outer gateway (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 153, folio 156).
- **Mulla Majid's mosque.** One inscription carries both 1235, uncertain, for the earlier building and 1294 for the present one (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 183, folio 186).
- **The Mian Mir church.** The book says it was built between 1850 and 1857, both years uncertain, and in the same sentence that this was a period of about six years (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 390, folio 397).
- **Wasdev's renovation.** The book dates it to the present time, Samvat 1937, while his own brick reads Samvat 1936, both uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 130, folio 133).
- **Two ages that cannot share one moment of writing.** Masjid Muhammad Salih Kambo is said to be two hundred and twenty six years old, the figure uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 152, folio 155), and the Wazir Khan mosque two hundred and fifty one (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 155, folio 158); reckoned from their founding years, the two figures do not point to the same year of writing.
- **Schooling under the Sikhs.** Kanhaiya Lal records thousands taught and the respect of the whole Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 38, folio 41), and then that the English government removed all its defects (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 39, folio 42).
- **Haqiqat Rai's offence.** The book states it twice, and differently (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 208, folio 211).
- **The name of a rakh.** It is spelt Rakh Chalu (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 239, folio 242) and Rakh Jalu (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 360, folio 367; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 360–361, folios 367–368), both uncertain.
- **The name of the tilework.** It is *kansi kar* at the Dai Anga mosque and Jani Khan's dome (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 268, folio 271; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 270, folio 273), and *kashi kari* at the Begampura mosque, the Nila Gumbad, Makhduma Begum's dome, the Zakariya Khan mihrab, Miskin Shah's walls and Sharaf un-Nisa's tomb (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 277, folio 280; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 286, folio 289; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 260, folio 263; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 262, folio 265; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 263, folio 266; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 264, folio 267).
- **The name of Nau Lakha.** The book explains it as the garden whose annual income was nine lakh rupees, the reading uncertain, and, on the word of some, as the garden on which nine lakh were spent (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 355, folio 362; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 356, folio 363).
