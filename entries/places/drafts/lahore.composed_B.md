## A City of Saints: The Sacred Landscape

Chishti presents his book as a guide to a landscape of saints. It reports, he writes, on the tombs of the Muslim elders, on other buildings and on the old customs of Lahore, but at root it is a history of the saints (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 29). In the afterword he describes its subject as the history of all the mazars on every side of Lahore that are memorials of the elders of the past (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 872). The survey proper opens under a heading announcing an account of the graves, mazars and tombs of the elders of Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 45). A modern translator speaks in the same way of the city's shrines as a whole: the translator of Madho Lal Hussain writes, "I might not have taken up this project if it hadn't been for Ida Sofie Matzen who took me along to the shrines in Lahore while doing her doctoral research." (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 188, folio 188).

Bhandari's city is thick with places of prayer: in every street and bazaar, he writes, there are mosques in great abundance (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 524). Beside Hujwiri, he writes, other intimates of the Eternal Lord lie at rest in that city, though he names none of them, and on the outskirts of the city lie many gardens that delight the heart and thousands of flower gardens that add to its joy (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 523). In his description of the rivers, the Ravi adds to the splendour of Lahore, the seat of the sultanate, and the royal crossing, the *shahguzar*, lies at the foot of the royal buildings; the royal canal drawn from the river runs to the Shalamar garden at Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 512).

For Schimmel the oldest point in this landscape is Hujwiri's grave: "His shrine, called that of Data Ganj Bakhsh, is still a popular place of pilgrimage in Lahore." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 119, folio 88). She adds that "his tomb still provides a place of pilgrimage for the Panjabis" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 376, folio 345). Omar Kasmani calls Data Darbar "Lahore's most prominent shrine complex" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 65, folio 48), and Kugle calls ʿAli Hujwiri "Lahore's patron saint" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 206, folio 185). Kugle gives the same title, in a plural form, to Shah Hussayn, "one of Lahore's patron saints" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 204, folio 183) and "one of the patron saints of Lahore" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 241, folio 220). Schimmel writes that Madho Lal Husain "is buried together with his Hindu beloved in a modest tomb near the Shalimar gardens in Lahore" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, pp. 170–171, folios 152–153).

In the Mughal period, Schimmel says, the Qadiriyya was "represented in Lahore by Mian Mir, who had come there from Sind, together with his mystically inclined sister Bibi Jamal" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 392, folio 361). She describes the *Sakinat al-auliya*, a work she associates with the Mughal heir apparent, as "a fine biography of Mian Mir and the Qadiriyya in Lahore", and gives it no date (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 392, folio 361). Kugle finds the city's antinomian Sufis still attached to its great Qadiri saints: "In Lahore today, the Qalandars (who completely abandon legal norms and social conventions and even gender distinctions) are still associated with the great" Qadiri "saints of the city, like Shah Hussayn, Shah Jamal, and Miyan Mir" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 326, folio 305, ch. 4 n. 7). Robert Rozehnal records the opposite caution from inside a living Sufi order: "There are many mazars of unknown shaykhs in Lahore. Some of them are qalandars", who "can only give their visitors what they have", so that "they make them qalandars as well"; "In our silsila we avoid this." (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 207, folio 199). The statement names no shrine and speaks of the city's lesser tombs as a class.

Devotion did not always need an establishment. The two graves of Pir Zaki have neither urs, custodian, offering nor endowment, yet devotees come to recite the *fatiha* at both, and both are places of visitation for high and low alike (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 20–21, folios 15–16). The people of Lahore revere the mazar of Miran Badshah (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 749). Fakhr-ud-Din, on his first journey toward Ajmer, met in Lahore a Mir whose printed name is uncertain, took him along, and visited all the mazars (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 756). Kasmani's ethnography adds a women's shrine of the Shiʿa: he describes "Bibiyan-pak-daman, a shrine in Lahore dedicated to women saints and particularly revered by the Shiʿa" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 125, folio 108).

The saints, in Chishti's telling, guard the city and claim it. Of a *majzub* faqir buried at a khanqah, he writes that it is said Lahore was kept safe from Ahmad Shah Abdali by his kindness (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 91). When Ahmad Shah came to Lahore, Shah Kaku Chishti told him that this was his own city, before going as envoy to his death (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 218). In Kanhaiya Lal's account a living saint could protect a quarter: Ahmad Shah Durrani came to visit Haji Muhammad Saeed and set a royal guard in the mohalla so that no plunderer should touch it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 80, folio 83). Khalid opens a chapter on Abdali's siege with "a small shrine in the outskirts of Lahore raised by the villagers there" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).

Saints founded the quarters of the outer city in Kanhaiya Lal's account: Mauj Darya Bukhari and Sayyid Chiragh Shah Gilani (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 79, folio 82), Sayyid Abd al-Razzaq (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 80, folio 83), Shah Sharaf (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 80–81, folios 83–84), Shah Dargahi (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 82, folio 85), Shah Badr al-Din Gilani (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 82–84, folios 85–87), Shaikh Muhammad Tahir (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 85, folio 88), Shah Abu al-Maali (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 87, folio 90), Pir Aziz Mazang (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 77, folio 80) and the faqir Taja (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 78, folio 81). Five of the mohallas listed in the book's contents for its second part are named after saints or their descendants (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 2). The pattern recurs across the book. Mughalpura was settled around Khawand Mahmud's mazar, and it was the wealthy quarter whose plunder so enriched Ahmad Shah Durrani's army that it had no need to loot the rest of the city (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 241, folio 244).

Villages settled inside shrine enclosures: the village of Begampura stands within one (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 275, folio 278), and the village of Mian Mir within Mian Mir's own (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 242, folio 245). A dervish compound became Garhi Shahu, where possession passed from a plunderer to the displaced residents of a mohalla and then to four Shaikhs whose descendants still hold it, and who also took into their own possession whatever ownerless land lay waste around (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 364, folio 371). The dervishes' cells and towers round Abul Khair's grave are now all houses of zamindars (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 365, folio 372).

The city has also grown over places that once lay outside it. This Mauza Mozang, Chishti writes, a former settlement outside the city of Lahore, was one of the mohallas of the city of Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 115). In his section on the *takia* of Shah Mir, a garbled passage speaks of a founder of Mohalla Mozang, perhaps someone called Fateh Mozang, the name uncertainly printed, who came with the first Chaghtai kings when they came here (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 207). Elsewhere Mozang is said to have been settled in connection with Pir Shirazi Baloch, and the Baloch people of Mozang came and went to him (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 211). Rizvi records an outlying burial that the city has since absorbed: "Zinjani was buried in Chah Miran, which is now a suburb of Lahore" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112). The translator of Madho Lal Hussain places his saint's shrine in "Baghbanpura, Lahore" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 6, folio 6), and Kugle describes "Baghabanpur on the outskirts of Lahore (the neighborhood is now within the expanding city just next to the Shalimar gardens)" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 215, folio 194). In Amar Sadhu, an old village now inside the Defence Housing Authority, stand Gurdwara Bibi Kaulan and the shrine of Baba Mastan Shah (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).

Kugle gives the rest of the city's Sufi geography in a few strokes: "the empty lands outside the city walls" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 206, folio 185); the Ravi River (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 221, folio 200); and, beyond the city, "the village of Babupur, far from Lahore" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 212, folio 191). Even the landscape around the city is holy in Chishti's pages. He relates that Mian Natha told Mian Mir that he used to sit in seclusion in Mauza Achhra, the name uncertainly printed, and that he left because all the date palms there, the words uncertainly printed, recited the *tasbih*, glorifying and praising God, which disturbed his concentration (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 287). Inside the fort, too, there were graves. Describing the Nigah Khana, Chishti notes a similar building in the fort of Lahore, facing the throne on its south side, in which he himself saw a grave built; common people called it the Nigar Khana too (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 787).

The burial ground of Miani lies west of Mauza Mozang (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 211). Chishti gives it a section of its own, headed as the graveyard of the Miani tract, Panj Dhera, the last words uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 186), and closes it with a table of the named sites in the graveyard (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 192). Because there are many graveyards and graves in the land of the Miani tract, he promises a plan of the graves for the book (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 211). He says that he has described the graveyard of the Parsis in his account of Miani Sahib (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 733).

He gives several accounts of Miani's name and history. In Akbar's reign, he writes, the place was a village in which only learned men lived, and because Punjabis call the ulama *mian*, it was called Miani; later, when its ulama gained nearness to the king, it was given another name, of which only a first word, perhaps Shah, can be read (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 191). Its chief then was Hafiz Jan Muhammad, father of Hazrat Abu Muhammad Qadiri; the ulama shared a grant, the word uncertainly printed; after his death Abu Muhammad Qadiri was made its head; then a split arose over a point of the *shariat*, many left, and the village was deserted (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 191). Hazrat Mir Ali, the name uncertainly printed, was brought from a place printed as Gumhat, also uncertain, appointed by the king and settled there; the headship was shared between Abu Muhammad and Mir Ali, and the place was settled again (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 191). A Pir whose printed name may be Alima had the coffins of five elders brought from Gumhat and buried here; a grave mound is a *dher* in Punjabi, hence the name Miani Panj Dher, the word uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 191). East and south of one enclosure, he writes, lies a graveyard of the Kanjar, the word uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 191).

The gravediggers of Miani told him their own version. On their word, there was first a mauza here, and the reason it was called by a name printed as Siani is given, but the key word cannot be read (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 211). Their account, fragmentary at this point, speaks of fruit brought from *wilayat*, of digging earth, and of a gravedigger appointed at four rupees, the figure uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 212). The village of Miani was inhabited, they said, in the time of Muqim Sahib of the Hujra (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 212). In his account of the gravediggers, Chishti follows the place through the reigns of Shah Jahan and Alamgir; under Muhammad Shah the graveyard itself became deserted, and under Maharaja Ranjit Singh burials resumed and it grew, though the passage is heavily damaged and the order of events approximate (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 204). The Sikhs, in a damaged passage, looted Mauza Miani and all its people scattered; later a settlement was made, at a rent of one rupee a year for each house, and it grew to about two hundred houses, the figures uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 212). When Miani was deserted, he writes, it became this graveyard (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 213).

Other burial grounds belong to the modern city. Khalid describes "this historical graveyard situated in Sultan Pura, the heart of Lahore", seat of the shrine of Ghore Shah, where families gathered at graves on a Sunday morning while children played cricket, and where junkies "often end up finding a permanent abode in this land of the dead" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated). There is no Tower of Silence in Lahore, and "the Parsi community buries its dead in a graveyard next to the Minar-e-Pakistan facing the ancient walled city of Lahore" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 144, folio not stated).

The great Mughal buildings appear in the books as settings for devotion. Aurangzeb, entering the city after the war of succession, prayed in congregation at the Wazir Khan mosque and camped in the Shalamar garden (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 64). Khalid adds that the Wazir Khan Mosque was "constructed in the seventeenth century by Wazir Khan, Mughal Emperor Shahjahan's governor of Lahore" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). Abbas describes the Shalimar as "Gardens built by Shah Jehan, the Mughal emperor", and gives no date for their building (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 188, folio not stated). The one named building in the city that Rizvi mentions which is not a shrine is "the Padishahi mosque at Lahore", in a footnote on Goldziher's discussion of the veneration of relics (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 339, folio 317, footnote 3).

The fort and its gates stand at the centre of the Sikh memory of the city. Guru Arjan's torture took place "Outside the Alamgiri gate of the Lahore fort" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 195, folio not stated). Khalid writes that Guru Ram Das "was also born in present day Pakistan, in the city of Lahore", that "Guru Arjan spent a lot of time in the same city, where he was assassinated and his remains interred", and that Guru Hargobind "also spent a lot of time here" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated). The domes of the Badshahi Masjid face "the Alamgiri gate of the Lahore Fort, named after the imperial title of the Mughal Emperor Aurangzeb" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 205, folio not stated). "The Roshani gate, one of the thirteen gateways that lead into the ancient walled city of Lahore, is between the Alamgiri Gate and the Royal Mosque"; it "was once reserved for the prince and the princess, who would go from the Fort to the city through this entrance", and lamps "were lit all along the passageway", hence its name (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 205, folio not stated).

Relics came and went with the regimes. At the Qadam-e Rasul in Begampura a stone once bore the print of the Prophet's foot, and no one knows who carried it off in the time of the Sikhs (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 277, folio 280). At Sharaf un-Nisa Begum's tomb a Quran and a sword were laid out every day after the noon prayer and willed to remain in the tomb (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 264, folio 267); in the Sikh period the Sikhs took them out, and the book values them only as worth some thousand rupees, it is said (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 265, folio 268). At the Imambara the taziya is carried on replica graves of Imam Hasan and Imam Husain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 302, folio 305).

Khalid's city has temples as well as shrines. In 2008 he began to visit "an obscure monument around Lahore and write about it", and found "a Hindu temple, lying in the middle of a congested locality in the heart of Lahore", which he does not name (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 9, folio not stated). The Neela Gumbad Valmiki Mandir stands in "the historical market of Anarkali" and serves the city's Balmiki Hindus (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 66, folio not stated). He lists the Sitla Mandir at Shah Alami, the Jain Mandir at Anarkali and the Bhadra Kali Mandir at Thokar Niaz Baig among the city's prominent temples, with the Krishna Mandir on the Ravi road and the Valmiki Mandir at Neela Gumbad among its functional ones (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 21–22, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* he writes: "In Lahore, I found that Muslims and Christians, whose ancestors were once Hindus, continue to visit a Hindu temple their ancestors visited" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 32, folio 14).

The smaller sacred places of the modern city appear in Khalid's fieldwork. A shrine of cats, the "Billiyon Wala Mazaar", stands "next to Bhatti gate", one of the entry points of the walled city, and he writes that "The fact that the shrine is located in the heart of Lahore, a major metropolitan city, is probably why this practice has diminished much faster" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated). Masjid Shuhada at the Regal Chowk junction is "a small white structure with an open courtyard", which on Fridays is "teeming with pilgrims", with mats spread on the road for "hundreds of men" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 100–101, folio not stated). The churches and the Baha'i centre of the city's other communities complete the landscape. On Mall Road, "the Lahore Cathedral built in the late nineteenth century is a model of the Gothic architectural tradition" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 100, folio not stated). The Baha'i assembly meets at the Baha'i Centre "near the Ganga Ram Hospital" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 149, folio not stated).

The city is also the resting place of modern figures. Schimmel notes that Muhammad Iqbal "is buried in Lahore", and that a few years before she wrote a *maqam* in his honour was erected in the garden of Jalaluddin Rumi's tomb in Konya (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 270, folio 239). Michel Boivin records that "Muhammad Iqbal was a poet born in Lahore, who was granted a PhD by the University of Munich." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 212, folio 199, Ch. 3 n. 14). Schimmel says that Bullhe Shah, whom she dates 1680 to 1752, "lived near Lahore", without naming his burial place (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 419, folio 388). Rozehnal relates that Faruq Ahmad "died in Lahore on February 13, 1945 after a prolonged bout of pneumonia", and that "Throughout the time of the funeral, a light rain fell from that cloud of mercy only above that spot in Lahore" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 75, folio 65).

## A City of Saints: The Fabric of the Holy Places

Kanhaiya Lal warns his reader about the age of what stands. In the Sikh period, he writes, even temples of old foundation were built anew, so that their fabric looks fresh (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 93, folio 96). He shows it on the next site, where an old mosque enclosure became a mud shiwala and then a masonry temple of Ranjit Singh's time (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 94, folio 97). The enclosure had belonged to the Wazir Khan mosque in the time of the Muslim kings and its rents were spent on the mosque; when that rule passed, Bawa Thala Gir, his name uncertain, took up residence, obtained a lease from one of the rulers and built a small mud shiwala, and Raja Dina Nath then built the present structure at great expense (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 94, folio 97). Four of six mosques in one part of the book replace an earlier mosque on the same ground (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 178, 181, 182, 183; folios 181, 184, 185, 186), and he ranks four of the shrines in the Mehr Das enclosure by age, naming the oldest the first and most ancient building of all (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 129, folio 132).

He copies dates off the buildings, and almost every Hijri date he gives is uncertain in the printed text. The niche of the Masti Darwaza mosque bears 1023, with no era stated and its chronogram not fully legible (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 142, folio 145). Shah Zia Qadri died in a year read as 1126, era not stated (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 144, folio 147). The Sunehri Masjid is dated 1163 AH on the mihrab beneath the Ayat al-Kursi, the year printed twice in one sentence (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 147, folio 150), and Nawab Bukhari Khan died in 1165 AH (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 148, folio 151). The mosque of Muhammad Salih Kambo is dated 1058 in the three niches over its gate (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 152, folio 155). The Wazir Khan mosque records that it was completed in the reign of Shah Jahan, the second Lord of the Conjunction, and two of its chronograms give 1043 and 1044 AH, the second by the abjad reckoning, while a third names Wazir Khan as founder of a second House of God (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 157, folio 160).

The list continues through the deaths of saints and the building of later mosques. Sayyid Ishaq Gazruni died in 706, the *bismillah* itself serving as the chronogram (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 163, folio 166). Imam Ghulam Muhammad died in 1234 AH and his son in 1289 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 163, folio 166). Sayyid Jan Muhammad Huzuri died in 764 AH (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 167, folio 170) and Hassu Teli in 1000 AH (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 169, folio 172). The mosque of Takiya Sadhuwan is dated 1266 AH (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 170, folio 173). Zabita Khan died in 1251 AH and his mosque was rebuilt in 1278 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 171, folio 174); Sayyid Amir Shah's mosque was rebuilt in 1285 AH (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 172, folio 175); Nura's first mosque dates from 1239 AH (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 174, folio 177) and the second Nur Muhammad mosque from 1297 AH, with other figures in its margin (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 176, folio 179). Moran's mosque is dated 1223 AH (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 179, folio 182), Bokan Khan's 1255 AH (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 181, folio 184) and Imam Shah Wali's 1294 AH (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 182, folio 185).

Outside the walls the pattern is the same. Shah Bilawal Qadri died in the year one thousand and forty six of the Shahjahani period, no era printed and the second figure uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 235, folio 238), Lal Husain in 1008 AH and Madho in 1057 AH (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 233, folio 236). Hamid Qari's mosque bears 1141 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 252, folio 255) and the Zakariya Khan mosque at Madho Lal Husain 1144 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 263, folio 266). The dome of Data Ganj Bakhsh is dated 1247 AH and its mosque was rebuilt in 1868 AD (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 290, folio 293). The tomb of Shah Kanhh, his name uncertain, was rebuilt in 1294 AH (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 304, folio 307). A mosque's marble founding slab names its founder as Allah Jawai, her name uncertain, sister of Muhammad Sultan the contractor, and dates it 1247 AH (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 294, folio 297).

Sarai Golian Wali was built in 1035 AH by order of Jahangir (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 365, folio 372). Sayyid Badal-din Gilani, his name uncertain, died in 1072 AH and his tomb of the Shahjahani period was given its dome by his descendants (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 394, folio 401; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 395, folio 402). Shalamar's quatrain is followed by 1057, with no era printed (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 348, folio 355). For Shaikh Muhtaram he works the abjad in the open: the letters of the blessing on the saint total one thousand one hundred and seven, and after a subtraction one thousand one hundred and two remain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 257, folio 260).

A few of these dates are printed without doubt. Masjid Pholiyan Wali was rebuilt in 1282 AH, the only year legible on its marble slab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 176, folio 179). Nawab Imam ud Din Khan's mosque is dated 1266 AH (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 177, folio 180). Gulabi Bagh carries 1066 as the date of the garden, and its tilework is said to look fresh after two hundred and thirty five years (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 307, folio 310).

Hindu and Sikh foundations are dated in the Samvat, the Bikrami era. The memorial shivala of Sukh Ram Das Khatri Kapur and the two Mehr Das shivalas are dated Samvat 1930 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 130, folio 133). The Devi's mandir followed her appearance in Samvat 1931, which the book equates with 1875, and the donor's brick is inscribed February 1875 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 131, folio 134). Radha Kishan's shivala is dated Samvat 1847 Bikrami (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 132, folio 135) and the Radha Kishan thakurdwara Samvat 919, both readings uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 134, folio 137). Chhajju Bhagat died in a Samvat year of the sixteen hundreds, uncertainly read as ninety six, which the book equates with 1053 AH (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 186, folio 189). One shivala was built in Samvat 1900 Bikrami, the year uncertain, and the date of its completion is written on the temple (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 100, folio 103).

The samadhs of the Sikh nobility carry the same era. Ram Singh's samadh dates from Samvat 1895 Bikrami and was built in Jamadar Khushhal Singh's own lifetime, and Raja Teja Singh died in Samvat 1919 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 190, folio 193). Rani Partab Kunwar died in Samvat 1914 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 212, folio 215), and the epigraph of Rani Rind Awi, her name uncertain, reads the fourteenth of Maghar, Samvat 1927, on a Sunday, the figures uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 211, folio 214). Two Sikh deaths are dated in the Christian era: Khushal Singh in 1844 and Jawahar Singh, killed late in 1847, both years uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 192, folio 195; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 197, folio 200).

The later temples are dated with the same care. Chamna Das's mandir at Tali Sahib is dated Samvat 1898 Bikrami, printed without doubt (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 228, folio 231). Dhani Nath was appointed in Samvat 1880 Bikrami (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 231, folio 234). The Anarkali shiwala and the thakurdwara of a Jangam, his name uncertain, are dated Samvat 1910 and Samvat 1937 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 445, folio 452). Pandit Bishnath's mandir is dated Samvat 1904 Bikrami (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 451, folio 458), Chhujhi Mal's Samvat 1933 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 452, folio 459), Atar Singh Subedar's Samvat 1934 Bikrami (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 453, folio 460) and Dharm Chand's rebuilt shiwala Samvat 1935 Bikrami (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 454, folio 461). Rai Singh Risaldar's thakurdwara carries on a marble slab over its east door its own name, the name of his father Karam Singh and Samvat 1938 (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 455–456, folios 462–463).

He measures. Mian Mir's platform is nineteen by nineteen *zira* square (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 243, folio 246). Bahadur Khan's octagonal platform has sides of seventeen or eighteen *gaz*, the figure uncertain, mihrabs four and three quarter *gaz* high and two and a quarter wide, a two storeyed gallery in each face, two staircases, and eight corner domelets of which one has fallen and seven stand (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 258, folio 261). Nusrat Khan's tomb has eight *gaz* of platform clear on every side (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 261–262, folios 264–265). Ali Mardan Khan's tomb is three storeys high with a basement holding three masonry graves, and no tomb in Lahore is as tall (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 255, folio 258). Jahangir's tomb is measured in feet: a marble platform fourteen feet long, the figure uncertain, nine wide and a foot and a half high, forty cells, four minarets of sixty one steps each, the number uncertain, a platform of two hundred and some feet, the figure uncertain, and eleven surviving tanks of twelve and three surviving wells of four (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 316–319, folios 323–326). The marble platform of the Hazuri Bagh baradari is eight feet wide and three feet above the ground, with fifteen red stone steps down to its basement and sixteen marble steps up to its third storey (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 340–342, folios 347–349).

His measures reach the water too. The tank in the Wazir Khan courtyard is a hundred *gaz* square (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 156, folio 159), and at Shah Muhammad Ghaus a tank ten by ten is always full of water (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 249, folio 252). East of Government House a stepped tank, sixty by thirty feet, is kept full from the canal (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 398, folio 405). The number of wells at the Bhauri Sarkar thakurdwara cannot be read (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 201, folio 204). One well is big enough for twelve Persian wheels to run on it with ease, and an arched opening inside it is marked by Kanhaiya Lal as his own conjecture (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 260, folio 263). Shalamar's garden land is measured in bighas with figures that do not add up (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 351–352, folios 358–359).

The gilded finial, the *kalas*, marks whether a Sikh era building was finished. Khushal Singh's samadh has none (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 192, folio 195), and in another dome an iron fitting, the metal uncertain, waits empty for a *kalas* to be raised (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 193, folio 196). Golden finials gleam at Udham Singh's and Suchet Singh's samadhs (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 194, 195; folios 197, 198); none has yet been raised at Shiv Gar's shivala (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 199, folio 202); Bhauri Sarkar's has a brass one (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 201, folio 204); none was ever set on Sher Singh's dome nor on Rani Rind Awi's (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 210–211, folios 213–214); and gilded finials stand throughout at Rani Jindan's (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 205, folio 208) and over each samadh at Kharak Singh's and Nau Nihal Singh's (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 222, folio 225).

His verdicts on the unfinished buildings are short: one grand building remained incomplete (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 192, folio 195); another was left incomplete because of the revolution of the kingdom, and now lies heirless, ruined and decaying (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 196, folio 199); a third is not yet ready (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 190, folio 193). After Raja Teja Singh's death Mul Singh, the agent of the state, took charge of building his samadh, and now Raja Harbans Singh, heir to the state, has gained full authority and will perhaps bring the building to completion (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 190, folio 193). At Khushal Singh's, on Teja Singh's death the property and jagir were divided between Raja Harbans Singh and Sardar Bhagwan Singh, and the work passed to Bhagwan Singh, who neglected it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 192, folio 195).

Wills governed the fabric of graves, and were sometimes built against. Mian Wada's tomb has no dome and his grave is unbaked earth, because he willed that his grave not be made of masonry (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 239, folio 242). Faqir Sain Qutb Shah Qadri's grave was likewise left unpaved by his will, though the wheeled well there is masonry (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 271, folio 274), and Shaikh Sadi Balkhari willed that no dome be built over his grave, so none was (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 298, folio 301). By Sayyid Ishaq Gazruni's will the enclosure was made of masonry and the grave of earth, but when the Nawab built a mosque there the grave was raised in masonry too (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 162–163, folios 165–166). Daryagar Sanyasi asked that his samadh be made of earth and not masonry, and so it was, yet a masonry shivala has also been built at the same place (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 210, folio 213). Sharaf un-Nisa's tomb was built without stairs and kept so by her will; the Begum climbed up every day by setting a wooden ladder against it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 264, folio 267).

Many tombs were built in their occupants' lifetimes or by their disciples: Mauj Darya's by Akbar's order in his lifetime (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 279, folio 282); Abul Muali's laid out by himself and finished by his son (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 284, folio 287); Shah Kamal's by the disciples of his family (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 313, folio 316); and Shaikh Mahmud Shah Naqshbandi's by his disciples in his lifetime, with a basement made first, into which his coffin was placed at his death and the door shut (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 260–261, folios 263–264). Haji Nur's enclosure was built in his lifetime on the plan of the Kaaba (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 295, folio 298). Fazl Shah's grave was made before his death by his patron (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 325, folio 332), and Makhduma Begum had her own tomb built smaller than her husband's, out of respect (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 260, folio 263).

The working fabric of the holy places is described closely. The Kotwali shiwala has separate buildings for staff quarters, kitchens and linen stores (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 95, folio 98); there are small rooms for pujaris at the Bhagat Ram shiwala (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 100, folio 103) and at Ragnath Mishr's (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 115–116, folios 118–119), and masonry rooms for faqirs at the dharamsala (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 103, folio 106). Mahants' samadhs are built in masonry inside the Shivala Tarpoliya's own halls (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 107, folio 110); two anonymous mahants' domed samadhs and a *majzub*'s samadh stand at the Wazir Khan shiwala (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 94, folio 97); and many domed masonry samadhs of former successors and managers stand at Bhauri Sarkar's (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 201, folio 204). The Bhagat Ram shiwala has thirteen fountains and an underground cold room (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 100, folio 103), and the Begampura mosque a great tank with a fountain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 278, folio 281). Bakhshi Bhagat Ram has a gilded samadh and a painted portrait inside his own temple courtyard, which those who come to worship look upon, and a marble platform two *gaz* square is called the Dharamshala because he sat on it to give alms to Brahmans (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 99, folio 102). The Shivala Tarpoliya's silver *jalhari* was remade by subscription after a theft, four hundred rupees' weight of silver (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 107, folio 110).

The shrines built seats for their offices into the fabric. At Mian Mir a grand hall is the seat of the *mujawirs* and a separate building the special seat of the *sajjada nashin* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 243, folio 246). At Shah Muhammad Ghaus a windowed masonry seat for the *sajjada nashin* is built into the western side of the great enclosure wall (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 249, folio 252), and outside the enclosure of Data Ganj Bakhsh the *mujawirs* have many seats (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 289, 291; folios 292, 294).

There are wells at most sites (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 94, 95, 99, 101, 103, 105, 106, 109, 115, 117; folios 97, 98, 102, 104, 106, 108, 109, 112, 118, 120) and bel trees at four (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 101, 106, 107, 109; folios 104, 109, 110, 112). A well at Mian Mir took fifty years to make (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 243, folio 246); at Sayyid Abdul Wahab Qadri's tomb a great well deserves to be called a tank (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 265, folio 268); there are a wheeled well and two bathhouses at Hamid Qari's khanqah (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 252, folio 255) and again at the Zakariya Khan mosque (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 262, folio 265). There are a well and a water house at the Muhammad Sultan mosque (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 294, folio 297); a Persian wheel mounted by a living pir on his own shrine's well (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 296, folio 299); and at Shaikh Musa's enclosure a well worked by the wooden wheel of the zamindars who farm there (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 245, folio 248).

Water was part of the endowed fabric. The water of one low lying mosque goes nowhere outside and soak pits are built on the spot (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 151, folio 154); an ancient well survives where the tank of Masjid Chinian Wali has vanished (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 154, folio 157); and at the Thibi Wala shivala an ancient well serves as a soakaway for the water that drips on Shiv ji night and day, the one thing Raja Dina Nath left untouched when he rebuilt everything else (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 123–124, folios 126–127). Over one Shivling water falls through a brass snake fed from the well terrace, and a soakage well takes it away (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 435, folio 442); a brass pipe does the same at Mahin Lal's shiwala, his name uncertain (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 447, folio 454). Against all these, Rai Singh's thakurdwara has no well, to the great hardship of those who live in the temple (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 456, folio 463).

Several holy places gave water to the public. There is a *sabil* for giving water at Mozang (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 225, folio 228), and at the Multani Dharmsala a masonry room for giving water, where a Brahman lives to serve it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 226–227, folios 229–230). At the Sant Shah mandir a newly dug well and a red stone *sabil* serve high and low alike, the well head roofed and pierced so that people draw water and bathe there (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 432, folio 439; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 433, folio 440). The Chhote Lal shiwala's well can be drawn from outside as well (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 447, folio 454), and the Akbari Mandi thakurdwara's well on the bazaar front is open to all for drawing water and bathing (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 451, folio 458).

Canal water ran through the garden belt and the institutions. It enters Shalamar from the south and runs inside every one of its gardens (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 351, folio 358), and Farah Bakhsh drains the upper gardens through the Sawan Bhadon (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 350, folio 357). Bagh Raja Teja Singh owes its green to the canal reaching it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 353, folio 360), and Bagh Lala Ratan Chand has been perfectly green since water was brought to it from the canal (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 355, folio 362). The contractor's famous cold well is fed with canal water through a channel (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 359, folio 366). Mela Ram's public tank by the railway station is filled from the canal and has a women's ghat built in full purdah (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 367, folio 374; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 368, folio 375), and Diwan Ratan Chand's tank is filled with canal water (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 224–225, folios 227–228). At the railway works the canal water is stored in two tanks, passed into two wells, raised by engine to an iron cistern on a very high masonry building, and distributed to the engines and to every workshop (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 424, folio 431).

The tilework of the city's tombs and mosques is named in two ways. At the Dai Anga mosque and on Jani Khan's dome the book calls it *kansi kar* work in yellow (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 268, folio 271; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 270, folio 273); at the Begampura mosque, whose minarets are green and yellow, and at the Nila Gumbad, whose grand dome is green and turquoise, it calls it *kashi kari* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 277, folio 280; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 286, folio 289). The same word recurs on Makhduma Begum's dome (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 260, folio 263), on the inscriptions of the Zakariya Khan mihrab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 262, folio 265), on Miskin Shah's walls (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 263, folio 266), and on Sharaf un-Nisa's tomb, where it forms great painted cypresses and gives the building its byname, the Cypress Tomb (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 264, folio 267).

## Communities

Bhandari's Lahore is a gathering of peoples: craftsmen of every land live there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 524). Chishti claims for his book a breadth that matches the city's. In the afterword he writes that it covers the elders of the Hindus and of the people of Islam alike, and that in its spirit of universal peace, *sulh-e kull*, it respects every religion (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 872). Kanhaiya Lal surveys Muslim, Hindu, Sikh, Brahmo and Christian foundations in one frame, and he records traffic between the communities in both directions without comment.

He divides the city's Muslims four ways. The Ahl-e Sunnat wa Jamaat come first, and in Lahore proper the Hanafis, the reading uncertain, are the most numerous of the Sunnis; the Shia Imamiya mourn greatly in the ten days of Muharram and make taziyas; the Wahhabiya Najdiya follow no imam and call themselves *muwahhid*; and the Sufis bear enmity to no one, mostly listen to *sama*, and are called the sect of peace with all and without prejudice (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 44–45, folios 47–48). He prints the orders on a line of their own, Qadiri, Chishti, Suhrawardi, Naqshbandi, two further names uncertain in the printed text, Naushahi and others, a list attached to no shrine, saint, khanqah or place (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 44, folio 47).

Membership of a Sufi order appears in the professional classes rather than at the shrines. A physician's adopted son of a Sayyid family had taken the oath of allegiance in the Naushahi Qadiri line, and so joined the word *faqir* to his name that his descendants, for all their wealth, are still called Faqir (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 57, folio 60). In Chishti's account the Faqir family was known in Lahore as the family of the Faqir sahibs and of the physicians (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 584); every notable of Lahore knows their nobility, and all the people of Lahore, indeed of the Punjab, call their mohalla by a name printed as Qutb Yunan, the reading uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 585). A practising Lahore physician was also a pir, through whom the grace of the Chishti and Qadiri lines flowed, and who had many disciples (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 58, folio 61). A graveyard of Chishti faqirs, its name read uncertainly as Khair Shahi, was built on Zain Khan's open ground with a masonry mosque (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 87, folio 90). At Teel Pura a khanqah and madrasa survived the collapse of the quarter, its dervishes and students staying on, and the madrasa still flourishes (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 89, folio 92). Imam Ghulam Muhammad, a faqir of the Qadiri order, left as his memorial *Ganj-e Makhfi*, a book of Sufism in verse (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 163, folio 166).

The words Lal uses for the holders of religious buildings follow the community that holds them. A *mahant* holds Hindu and Sikh mandirs and dharamsalas, a *sarparast* a thakurdwara, a *pujari* the shivalas and thakurdwaras, and a *mutawalli*, *imam* or *mujawir* the mosques and mazars (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 133–147, folios 136–150). Yet the titles cross from one tradition to another. *Sajjada nashin* is used of the office at a Hindu *samadh*, Chhajju Bhagat's *chaubara*, and *gaddi nashin* of the same office on the same page (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 186, folio 189); *gaddi* and *masnad* of a faqir's seat of the Sikh period (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 203, folio 206); and *gaddi nashin* of a Sikh *mahant* at Mozang (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 225, folio 228). Lal's vocabulary of office is wider still. He also uses *khadim* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 209, folio 212); *muhtamim* of a Kuka dharamsal (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 105, folio 108); a trustee and helper of the congregation, *mutamid o muawin halqa*, at the gurdwara of Guru Ram Das (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 102, folio 105); a disciple successor, *chela janashin*, and a *gaddi nashin* at Baba Khuda Singh's dharamsala (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 102–103, folios 105–106); *bairagi* as a holder's own designation (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 200, folio 203); *mujawir o sajjada nashin* as one compound title (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 239, folio 242); and a caretaker and priest, *khabar gir o pujari* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 457, folio 464).

Some rites and holy places were shared. On Sundays Hindus and Muslims alike bring children suffering from an illness the book names to Shah Dargahi's well and bathe them there (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 82, folio 85; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 299, folio 302). Hindus attended the Guru Ram Das gurdwara, and *qawwals* sang *kafis* of divine unity there (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 101–102, folios 104–105). Kashmiri Pandits staffed two Shaiva temples founded by officers of the Sikh state (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 94, 96; folios 97, 99). The Baoli was chosen for any gathering of any kind in the Hindu religion (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 108, folio 111). The Hindus of a whole quarter worshipped at one site: all the Hindus of Anarkali, Lal writes, come to that place to worship and make offerings (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 445, folio 452). The shrine of Sitla Mata was the resort of both Hindus and Muslims, and Muslims too, after their children recovered from smallpox, brought them there and made offerings (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 187, folio 190). The Hindus and Muslims of Lahore alike acknowledged the holiness of Bhai Rupa (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 198, folio 201).

The calendar of shared gatherings was full. Hindus and Muslims attended Miran Badshah's mazar on Thursdays (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 161, folio 164), both melas at Madho Lal Husain drew Hindus and Muslims (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 234, folio 237), and Muslims too mostly went to the mela of Bhadar Kali (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 229, folio 232). On the twenty eighth of Shaban Hindus and Muslims held a mela together at Shah Bilawal's khanqah (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 236, folio 239). At a Hindu bhagat's samadh *qawwali* was sung in the mystic raga on Mondays and Tuesdays (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 185, folio 188), and at an Ekadashi assembly inside a thakurdwara *qawwals* sang ragas of divine unity (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 462, folio 469). Some of the city's crowds gathered at no shrine at all. The mouth of the contractor's cold water well was closed all winter and opened at the height of the hot season; thousands of water skins were carried from it into the city, and Hindus, Muslims and Christians crowded there, so that for two months a mela of onlookers was held at it every day (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 359, folio 366).

The communities also acted together. Hindus and Muslims together protested the demolition of the mosque at Shah Muhammad Ghaus's mazar (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 249–250, folios 252–253). Hindus, Muslims and Christians all gladly joined the subscription to repair the Badshahi Masjid (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 142, folio 145). The Qizilbash household's Muharram food was given to all, to those of the Sunni and the Shia persuasion alike (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 71, folio 74).

One exchange across communities founded a temple outright. Kanhaiya Lal relates that a Muslim Turkish soldier, of the Lodhi period by an uncertain reading, had taken an image from a mandir the Turks demolished in Hindustan and bathed and dressed it every day, because whatever his heart desired came to him; he sold it for a great deal of money to the Hindus of the mohalla, who placed it in the haveli that is now the Mata temple, and the soldier himself gave the image its name (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 457, folio 464; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 458, folio 465). Buildings also changed hands between communities, and a mosque could become a temple: Ranjit Singh renamed the Moti Masjid the Moti Mandir (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 339, folio 346).

Two mosques passed to Hindu holy men under the Sikhs and were taken back under the English. In Ranjit Singh's time a Hindu sanyasi jogi named Basant Gar took possession of Daya Lado's mosque, built a shivala on one side and a thakurdwara on the other, and lived there for nearly thirty years; under English rule one Mehr Shah, a Qadiri faqir, took a few Muslims with him from Lahore and evicted the Hindu faqir by force, and although the sanyasi submitted petitions in the court of Major McGregor, the Assistant Agent to the Resident, the name and title uncertainly printed, nothing came of it. Mehr Shah built the outer enclosure wall, and the Muslims of the surrounding *kothas* now pray in it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 300–301, folios 303–304). Inside the city the same thing happened: under the Maharaja a Hindu sadhu, the word uncertainly printed, had made his home in a mosque and set images of the thakurs in it, and under the English a Muslim faqir whose name is printed uncertainly as Nadh Shahi gathered a few Muslims and evicted him from the mosque by force; the mosque remains in that faqir's possession (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 86, folio 89).

Possession could divide Muslims as well. Lal records one mosque that is still in use but is held by the Muslims of the Wahhabi persuasion, who are opposed to both the Sunni and the Shia sects, and the Wahhabis have now spent a great deal of money repairing it; the holders gave it a grand new door and a tank kept full from the tap (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 154–155, folios 157–158). This possession stands against his irenic picture of the Sufis. He notes, too, that a usurper of mosque land was himself a Muslim: the mosque stayed small because Dilawar Khan, a Muslim though he was, had built a haveli on the ground of its courtyard (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 170, folio 173).

The city's elites drew lines of their own. Kugle's Lahore has a Brahmin community with standing in the city: "It is not clear how the two managed to overcome this conflict with Madho's family and the Brahmins of Lahore." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 212, folio 191). The city's Muslim elite judged its saints too: "Shah Hussayn earned himself disrepute among the Muslim notables of Lahore, who saw him as a dissolute drunk" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 208, folio 187 [`l&J`, text-layer?]), and "this open passion ruined his reputation throughout Lahore" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 210, folio 189).

Chishti shows the city's communities through the violence that passed between them. Under Mir Mannu, in Muhammad Shah's reign, the Sikhs had grown in number and there was a general order to seize and kill them (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 121). Later, in the time of persecution by the Sikhs, the property of hundreds of people in the city is said to have been lost, in a very damaged passage (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 722). During a disturbance in Lahore, which a garbled passage connects with Sher Singh, the Gilani household raised a flag so that the Sikhs would know theirs as a house of the sayyids (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 525). When the Gilani hazrat moved into a donated haveli, he writes, the whole city was in uproar (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 525), and in a garbled passage all the nobles of Lahore are named at the ancestor's death or funeral (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 526).

Lal also records a dated riot between Shia and Sunni, which stands against the same picture. On the tenth of Muharram the Zuljanah, the model horse of Imam Husain, was led round the whole city and brought to the Imambara, a building of the Shia Imamiya whose patrons were Nawab Nawazish Ali Khan Qizilbash and Nawab Nasir Ali Khan. In 1849 AD, a year printed without doubt, there was a fierce quarrel between Shia and Sunni near Shah Alami Gate that came to blows, and that day the Sunnis pulled down the enclosure's inner buildings, threw down the battlements of the tomb, filled the well with bricks and beat Gami Shah unconscious, until the Deputy Commissioner summoned a troop of cavalry from the Anarkali cantonment, the crowd dispersed, and those arrested received some punishment (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 302, folio 305). A state prosecution emptied one religious building: when the Kukas made a disturbance in Maler Kotla and were punished, the splendour of this dharamsal departed too, and Ram Singh was sent to Rangoon (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 105, folio 108).

The book records separation as well as mixing. The settlement of the cow killing butchers was set apart by royal order because the Hindu subjects loathed them, and a magnificent masonry mosque was built there (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 89, folio 92); the quarter was later abandoned and its people mutilated, and only a masonry mosque still stands (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 90, folio 93). Under the British the honorary magistracy was a communal quota of eight of Lahore's notables, four Hindu and four Muslim (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 61, folio 64), and a hereditary Chief Pandit is said to be distinguished by the honorary magistracy (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 134, folio 137). The hospital kept four separate kitchens, one each for Hindu, Muslim, and Christian and European patients and a shopkeeper in the fourth, with Hindu and Muslim cooks and servants (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 380, folio 387). Women's and men's ghats were built apart at Diwan Ratan Chand's tank, with a gate to the women's ghat where women in purdah bathe, and at the bathing tank of the cremation ground (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 223, folio 226).

Some communities ran their own observances. Of Shaikh Musa the blacksmith, Lal writes that many blacksmiths of his family are his disciples, and the blacksmiths also hold the annual urs (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 246, folio 249). The Khoja community appeared as witnesses for their own mosque's hereditary *mutawalli* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 84–85, folios 87–88). Hindus at Bhadar Kali at Niaz Beg serve the pujaris with heart and soul and count their service an honour (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 230, folio 233). Chishti records a community of Jains. Of the panth of Parasnath he writes that most of its disciples in Lahore now come from a group whose printed name is uncertain; they do not tie the mouth cloth and do not marry, and anyone who marries is expelled (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 544). In a largely illegible passage, people of Lahore and elsewhere are said to gather, and the elders of Lahore and Amritsar are named (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 541).

Other households and peoples appear in Chishti's pages in passing. The Baloch of Mozang came and went to Pir Shirazi Baloch (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 211), and Chishti promises a later account of the houses of the people of Delhi in the city of Lahore, the words uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 292). The gravediggers of Miani were a community of their own. Mian Murad Bakhsh, of the gravedigger people, tired of the work and took one Khwaja as his pupil, and that man's descendants are now among the gravediggers (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 204; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 209). Chishti records their genealogy twice (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 209; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 213), and twice lists the gravediggers working there in his day, once on the word of Sayyid Mansur, the *sajjada nashin* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 209), and once again, with names such as Rahmat, Nabi Bakhsh, Miran Bakhsh, Shahab al-Din, Nur Din and Chiragh Din (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 213).

Hindu, Sikh and Brahmo foundations stand in the same register as the Muslim ones. Every Sunday followers of the Brahmo faith gather at the Brahmo Samaj to worship the one God, and a paid chowkidar guards the building (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 449, folio 456). One reform controversy is noticed from the temple's side: the discourse of Sardha Ram Phaluri, his name uncertain, was directed against the new faiths, the Arya and the Brahmo, which are wholly opposed to the worship of images (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 441, folio 448). The Sikh establishments include the Baoli with its shops, its naqara and a building fund drawn from Khalsa salaries (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 109–114, folios 112–117); Baba Khuda Singh's dharamsala with its *gaddi nashin* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 102–103, folios 105–106); the Kuka dharamsal and its prosecution by the state (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 105, folio 108); the bhang dharamsal, kept with the government's permission (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 213, folio 216); the Nihangs of Shahid Ganj (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220); the regular gathering at Mozang (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 226, folio 229); Tali Sahib, its stipend stopped and its tree felled (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 228–229, folios 231–232); the royal samadhs kept by a single salaried boy (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 212, folio 215); and the eleven samadhs of the eleven ranis who became sati with Ranjit Singh (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 219, folio 222).

The Christian foundations are counted with the rest. The Mian Mir church seats about eight hundred and was built for ninety thousand rupees, the first figure uncertain, of which forty five thousand came from the government and the rest from subscription (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 390, folio 397; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 394, folio 401). At another, unnamed church a bell is rung not only for prayer but every day at twelve o'clock (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 426, folio 433). The *padri* bought Bagh Bhai Mahan Singh from Bhai Sawaya Singh, his name uncertain, changed the form of the garden and built houses there in his own fashion; the Christian women and men living there receive stipends from the padris and are taught every day (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 355, folio 362). There is a Mission School among the buildings near Fazl Shah's grave and the Rang Mahal (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 329, folio 336).

Women founded and patronised holy places in all three traditions. Musammat Jwala Dai, wife of Ram Kishan, built her mandir and made it *waqf*; it was built nine years ago and she died once the building was finished, and her son in law Har Narain is now its guardian and gives five to seven rupees a month (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 126–127, folios 129–130). The mother of a Kanhaiya Lal Kambo, the second name uncertain, built Chor Mor Wala with her own money in Ranjit Singh's time and gave it to Mahant Balram Das (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 124, folio 127). The third shivala at Mehr Das was founded by a woman called Bhagtan who kept a shop selling *paan* under Sikh rule (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 130, folio 133). Rani Lachhmi built a thakurdwara out of deep devotion and left it as her memorial in this transient world (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 199, folio 202). Kanhaiya Lal explains Bhauri Sarkar's name from her reddish hair, *bhura* being the Punjabi word for a red shading into black, and her work was carried out in the time of Maharaja Sher Singh (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 200, folio 203). Rani Jindan built the shivala beside her thakurdwara while she held power (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 205, folio 208), and laid the foundation of Sardar Jawahar Singh's samadh, the work continuing until she was exiled to Hindustan by order of the English government (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 196, folio 199). Rani Gul Begum held the largest jagir of all and twelve hundred rupees a month under the English (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 293, folio 296).

Muslim women of the Mughal household and the city built mosques, tombs and quarters. The founder of the Masti Darwaza mosque was Maryam Zamani, wife of the emperor Jahangir (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 142, folio 145). Dai Anga, the royal wet nurse, lived in Jahangir's household; her name, read uncertainly, was Zeb-un-Nisa and Anga her title (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 167, folio 170). Kanhaiya Lal explains the title: *ang* means the body, and an *anga* is one with whom a person is one soul in two bodies (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 267, folio 270). As Sultan Begum's wet nurse she was given a garden outright once it was finished, and her grave is in it (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 307, folio 310). Daya Lado, wet nurse of Prince Salim, built a mosque in her own lifetime and made her grave in its courtyard, and after her death, while Muhammad Shakur looked after the mosque, a learned Maulvi Ismatullah taught there (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 300, folio 303). Zeb-un-Nisa Begum is credited as owner and founder, as a poet, as builder of her own tomb in her lifetime, and as builder of Shah Rustam Ghazi's red stone tomb and marble grave (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 309–312, folios 312–315). Begampura is named after a woman: Begum Jan, her name uncertain, mother of Nawab Khan Bahadur, built all of it in her own lifetime, and so it is known as Begampura to this day (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 278, folio 281).

Women outside the nobility appear too, and so do the limits placed on women. Moran the courtesan built the mosque that bears her name, which Kanhaiya Lal calls a renowned mosque of Lahore, in a year read as 1223 AH; people held her order stronger than the Maharaja's, and her family still lives in the same bazaar, headed by a chaudhri of the courtesans' community (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 179–181, folios 182–184). Nawab Zakariya Khan built a haveli for Kallo Bai of the *mutriba* community, the singing women, because she could not enter the Nawab's harem (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 334, folio 341). And one mosque was the women's mosque of a palace, where the women of the household of the governor of Lahore came to pray by a path from inside the palace (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 143, folio 146).

Sati is recorded as a fact of the city's fabric. There are eleven samadhs with Ranjit Singh's (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 219, folio 222). At Jamadar Khushal Singh's are the samadh of his wife, who became sati with him, and two more whose names are unknown, perhaps, Kanhaiya Lal thinks, those of their slave women (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 193, folio 196). There are others at Sher Singh's (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 211, folio 214) and for Sardar Bur Singh's wife (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 212, folio 215), four at Kharak Singh's for the women who burned with the Maharaja's body and two of the same kind for Nau Nihal Singh (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 222, folio 225). At Bhai Wasti Ram's, women's samadhs are of marble, his mother's marble from head to foot (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 202, folio 205).

Abbas places her own family among the city's old religious households. She writes of "the old walled city of Lahore from where I trace the ancestry of my maternal family" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 24, folio not stated), and of "the Muftis and Gardezis who belonged to Lahore and Multan" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 26, folio not stated). "Mufti Ghulam Sarwar, one of my ancestors, is mentioned together with my paternal grandfather, Mufti Muzaffar Din, in Kanhyalal's history of Lahore." She adds that Mufti Ghulam Sarwar wrote a history of the Panjab, *Tarikh-e Makhazan Panjab* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 193, folio not stated).

Khalid's Lahore is a city of minorities. He counts "the five religious communities living in and around Lahore (Hindus, Christians, Sikhs, Baha'is and Zoroastrians)" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 9, folio not stated), and elsewhere "the four religious minorities living in Lahore (Hindu, Sikh, Christian and Baha'i)" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 10, folio not stated). His project on "religious minorities in Lahore" "officially began in September 2010" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 10, folio not stated).

Partition runs through the memories he gathers. An informant he calls Lal "was a teenager when partition riots spread in Lahore", and his grandfather "refused to leave his ancestral home" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 22, folio not stated). Bishna Ram recalls that friends and relatives told his family "that Lahore was no longer safe for us" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 67, folio not stated), while Shazia Waleed's father was "a prominent Hindu businessman from Lahore who continued living here despite partition" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 77, folio not stated). Mary, an eighty three year old widow, recalls that her family lived "near Shahjamal on the canal that now divides Lahore into two parts", that near partition "we would frequently sight mutilated bodies floating in the water", and that her mother made the children wear a cross to show "to anyone who would come asking for our religion" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 132, folio not stated). A Protestant poet tells him: "We Christians were living here before Lahore became a part of Pakistan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 128, folio not stated). Khalid notes that Alfred Woolner's statue outside the old campus of the University of the Punjab is "one of the few statues of pre-partition Lahore that has survived", "defying the iconoclastic streak of the country which emerged post 1947" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 100, folio not stated).

The Hindus of Lahore are few and careful. Prem Gupta, "a 40 year old Hindu activist from Lahore" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 46, folio not stated), lives in a Muslim neighbourhood where "theirs are the only Hindu houses. Unlike the majority of the Hindus, living in Lahore, they have refused to take up dual names" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 48, folio not stated). He tells Khalid: "If you want to see the lights of Diwali, go to Peshawar. You will find nothing in Lahore" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated). "A lot of prominent Hindus from Lahore went into hiding during the wars" of 1965 and 1971 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 74, folio not stated), and after the sixth of December 1992 the city's temples were attacked, and its functional temples on the Ravi road and at Neela Gumbad "were also burned down" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 22, folio not stated); Shami Lal, "Another Hindu from Lahore", shut his business for several months (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 22, folio not stated). The Neela Gumbad temple "serves a small community of Balmiki Hindus in Lahore, numbering only a few hundred, most of whom are Christians on record" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 66, folio not stated). Pilgrims went from the city to Katas in 2010 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 47, folio not stated), and in 2011 the Neela Gumbad priest took "a delegation of three hundred people from Lahore and neighbouring regions" to Ram Thamman (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 63–64, folio not stated).

Christians in Khalid's Lahore meet an old prejudice. "Even in prominent cities like Lahore, several Muslims refuse to eat with Christians and consider utensils used by them to be impure", and "A lot of low end hotels and restaurants not only in rural areas but even in metropolitan cities like Lahore have separate utensils for Christians" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 90, folio not stated). Colonel Chaudary, "a high-caste Christian who resides in Lahore", found that Muslim labourers in his house refused his cook's food "because of the impurity associated with eating at a Christian's house" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 91, folio not stated). Khalid reports that "a school principal in Lahore was accused of blasphemy" over a multiple choice question that listed his own name beside the Prophet's (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 104, folio not stated). In *In Search of Shiva* Khalid reports that "an angry mob of Muslims in Lahore had burned down 170 Christian houses", the rage "ignited by an argument between two friends, a Christian and a Muslim, while they were enjoying their evening drinks", while "the 'blasphemous' Christian was already in police custody" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated). The missionary colleges belong to this story: "the Forman Christian College Lahore, one of the oldest colleges set up by Christian missionaries" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 48, folio not stated), "one of the first English-medium institutes to be set up here, during the latter half of the nineteenth century" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 102, folio not stated).

The Sikhs, once the city's rulers, are now rare in it: "it is now rare that one runs into a Sikh in this city, a city which was once ruled by a Sikh and the seat of their political strength" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 196, folio not stated). Ranjit Singh, Khalid writes, built "a mosque at a mohalla called Gul Begum in Lahore", and Fakir Azizudin's "family still lives in Lahore in their ancestral house, located inside the walled city" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated). An informant he calls Kartar was refused service at a restaurant in Model Town because "they did not have 'our' utensils" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 186, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* Khalid lists Lahore among the cities whose Sikh communities have "Only recently" been able "to exert themselves" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 88, folio 70), and meets Ghulab Singh, "the first Sikh police officer in the revamped traffic police of Lahore" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 35, folio 17).

The Parsis are "microscopic" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 136, folio not stated). Farah Desai, eighty two, recalls that "At the time of partition, there were quite a few Parsis ... living in Lahore and Amristar", "all well to do", most of whom later emigrated; "There are only a handful of us left here" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 136, folio not stated), and she counts "only thirty five Parsis left in Lahore" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 141, folio not stated). Sadaf, born after partition, grew up in "a Lahore that was different from the multi-religious city that Farah grew up in", where the sari "had become the symbol of an Indian woman and wasn't encouraged in Lahore any more" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 138–139, folio not stated). Daria Boatwala, who moved to the city in the eighties, finds that "Lahore is much more monolithic and I would say religiously conservative" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 142, folio not stated). "Because of no priest there is no way to perform a traditional Parsi marriage in Lahore. The same applies for the rites after death" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 144, folio not stated).

The Baha'is and the Ahmadiyya appear in the same record. "The LSA at Lahore was established before partition" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 149, folio not stated), and about "forty odd people" attend its meetings "out of the one hundred and fifty Baha'is registered with the LSA" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 147, folio not stated). Usman Nawaz, a Baha'i of Shahdara, was "eventually forced to resign because of the hostile attitude of his colleagues" at the Sohrab Cycle Factory after a Muslim worker injured him with a stone, and the management "were afraid to take any action" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 152–153, folio not stated). "On 28th May 2010, two houses of worship of the Ahmadiyya community in Lahore were attacked, resulting in the death of 94 people and all political leaders refrained from visiting the site or sympathizing with the community" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 152, folio not stated).

Among the Muslim majority Khalid observes the rise of a public piety. In 1935, he writes, "The Deobands, who by now had established themselves as a formidable religious force in the city", opposed the first Milad procession (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). In his own day, the walkers in "a quintessential middle-class park, located in a middle-class locality, in the heart of Lahore" carry "rosary beads", and some play "an Arabic recitation of the Holy Book, along with its Urdu translation, a public display of their religiosity" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 7, ch. 1, folio not stated). A friend from Mumbai, by contrast, said: "I was surprised to see that hardly any women in Lahore were wearing a burqa" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated). At the Lahore University of Management Sciences "there have been rumours about the religious right slowly dominating the administration of the university" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated). On the national day of protest against a film, at the old village of Hanjarwal, "Temporary gallows had been erected on the road", and "An effigy, dressed in a shirt with the US flag on it, was being dragged to the gallows" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 7, ch. 1, folio not stated); days earlier, "a group of lawyers along with members of religious parties had attacked the US consulate in Lahore, taking down its flag and burning it" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 7, ch. 1, folio not stated). Raiwind is "a small village in the outskirts of Lahore", and during its annual congregation the cars heading there cause a traffic jam at a junction Khalid regularly uses (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 10, ch. 4, folio not stated).

Abbas makes an argument about women and shrines for Pakistan as a whole that frames the city's shrines too. "In the major mosques in Pakistan, for instance, there is a small space where women can go and pray on Friday or on religious festivals such as the Eid." In the local neighbourhood mosques, she writes, a woman cannot offer her ritual prayers: "Thus, the important spheres of religious and spiritual participation for women are the Sufi shrines" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 18, folio not stated).

In Rozehnal's account of the Sufi order he studied, Lahore is where its devotion is most dense. A murid "now runs an informal religious school (madrasa) for women in Lahore" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 44, folio 34). Two weekly circles of remembrance meet in private homes: "a senior male murid hosts a weekly halqa-i zikr in his home", and "A female disciple of Shaykh Wahid Bakhsh Rabbani does the same, leading a separate session comprised mostly of women" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 201, folio 193).

Kasmani follows fakirs who move between Sindh and Lahore. One of them, Murad, "relocated to Lahore" after leaving a lodge at Sehwan and was reported at Bibiyan Pak Daman (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 125, folio 108). The young woman he married was "a young pilgrim visiting from Lahore" whom he had consulted at the shrine of Bodlo (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 125, folio 108).
