## Overview

The Punjab of these books is not one unit but several, each drawn by its own author in his own time: for Sujan Rai Bhandari, writing about 1107 AH, it is the Mughal *suba* of Lahore, a country of five *doabs* whose rivers join the Indus beyond Multan, with the *suba* of Multan beside it, while in his narrative of earlier reigns it is the *wilayat* of Punjab (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 510; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 513; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 405). For Kanhaiya Lal, whose *Tarikh-e Lahore* is a history of one city, it is by turns a territory given in *jagir*, the Mughal *suba* with its *diwan*, the region that Ranjit Singh ruled, the country that the English annexed and the *suba* whose capital in his own day is Lahore (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 14, folio 9; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 152, folio 155; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 9, folio 4; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 68, folio 71; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 60, folio 63). For Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori, writing in 1875, it is all the country from Delhi to Peshawar then attached to Punjab, where the tombs of famous saints lie (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 37, folio 25), and for Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti it is the land of Punjab that the British conquered and governed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 137). The modern translators and annotators write of "the Punjab" or "the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 3, folio 2; Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 16, folio xvi), and Michel Boivin reminds the reader that the Siraiki speaking south, with Multan as its capital, was a part of Sindh during the Middle Ages (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 216, folio 203). Haroon Khalid writes of the modern province, Pakistani Punjab, which he calls the most prosperous province of Pakistan (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 15, folio not stated; Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated). Lahori's modern editor holds that no regular and researched history of Punjab has yet been written (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 23, folio 11).

The province's history in these books begins before Islam: following the *Tuhfat al-Wasilin*, Kanhaiya Lal relates that Raja Deb Chand of Delhi gave the territory of the Punjab in *jagir* to his brother Lohar Chand, who made the city of Lahore his capital (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 14, folio 9). Mahmud of Ghazni, in Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi's account, established Ghaznavid rule over the Punjab (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 83, folio 67), and the province became the last refuge of the house of Ghazni, whose government, in Kanhaiya Lal's account, ended with Khusrau Malik's surrender to Sultan Shihab al-Din Ghori (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 24, folio 19). Mongol armies came again and again, and Kanhaiya Lal counts the blows of plunder, famine and deserted villages one after another (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 26, folio 29), while Richard M. Eaton describes how Mongol pressures drove waves of Turkish settlers into the province's towns, Lahore, Dipalpur and Multan among them (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 217, folio 205). In the fifteenth century, Rizvi writes, the Punjab enjoyed a peaceful period under Afghan rule, until Babur invaded it (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 408, folio 384; Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 409, folio 385). Under the Mughals it was a prince's *jagir* and a *suba*, governed for a time by Dara Shikoh, who put up a great many buildings there (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 76, folio 79; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 139, folio 142). The eighteenth century is a time of ruin, the Punjab a battlefield of Afghans, Sikhs and Marathas, and the introduction to Rafat's selection dates the beginning of its misfortunes to the period of Muhammad Shah (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 46, folio 37; Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, pp. 26–27, folios 6–7). Ranjit Singh then held the whole region of the Punjab, with Kashmir and the Derajat besides, and ruled it, in Kanhaiya Lal's account, for forty years (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 112, folio 115; Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220). On 29 March 1849, the date uncertainly printed, the whole country entered the British dominion (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 592), and in Kanhaiya Lal's own day Lahore is the capital of the *suba* of the Punjab, the seat of its Lieutenant-Governor and its courts (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 60, folio 63). The colonial state connected the five rivers with canals and settled farming families on the new land, Khalid writes, and the province's history in these books ends with its division between India and Pakistan (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated).

For Lahori the province is first of all a land of saints' tombs, and he includes a saint when his tomb lies in the soil of Punjab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 127, folio 115). Rizvi makes the Ghaznavid conquest the beginning of its Sufi settlement, with Hujwiri reaching Lahore in about 1035 (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112). Baba Farid's settling at Ajudhan was, for Eaton, the religious side of a very slow change of the Punjab's cities from a Hindu to a Turkish-Islamic orientation (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 217, folio 205), and the great shrines of Punjab and Sindh drew pastoral clans into their ritual, social, economic and political orbits (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 48–49, folios 36–37). The Chishti order is dominant in Punjab, in Boivin's account (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 217, folio 204), the Qadiris had made the Punjab the main centre of their activities by Guru Nanak's day (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 420, folio 396), the Naushahi faqirs in Punjab number in the thousands (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 70, folio 58), and Shaikh Sulayman Taunsawi, who died in 1850, consolidated the Chishti order through religious schools throughout the Punjab (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, pp. 121–122, folios 108–109). The province is also the home ground of Guru Nanak's journeys, and by his return to Talwandi in 1506, a year imperfectly printed, there were very few in the Punjab who did not know of him, as Harish Dhillon retells the Janamsakhis (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 123, folio 137). Not every holy place belongs to a named saint: there are countless shrines to unknown saints on the outskirts of Punjabi villages (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 23, folio 12). Today southern Punjab is believed, Khalid writes, to be the hub of the country's shrine culture (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).

The books weigh the meeting of the province's faiths differently: Dhillon describes Islam and Hinduism existing side by side in the Punjab for over three hundred years in a state of constant conflict (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 16, folio 18), while Eaton traces a very slow, probably unconscious conversion of the Punjabi clans, many of which claim to have been converted by Baba Farid (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 234–235, folios 222–223; Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 227, folio 215). In Kanhaiya Lal's Punjab Hindus and Muslims served the same state, a Hindu amir serving as *diwan* of the Mughal *suba* (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 336, folio 343). The annotator of Waris Shah describes the village of the old Punjab as one social unit irrespective of caste or creed (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 20, folio 19), and Annemarie Schimmel notes that Hindus shared in the Muslim orders, though more visibly in Sindh than in the Punjab (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 418, folio 387). Khalid's travels find a Christian community with a formidable presence in the Punjab (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 87, folio not stated), a significant Hindu population still living in Southern Punjab (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 31, folio not stated), and Sikhs, many of them Pathans who came from the tribal areas (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 203, folio not stated). After Partition, he writes, he has seen gurdwaras and temples taken over and destroyed by the migrants (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated), and among the country's elite the farming festivals of Vaisakhi, Lohri and Basant were given up (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).

The Punjab of these books is above all a land of mystical poetry in its own tongue, which Schimmel traces in one book to the fifteenth century and in another suggests may have emerged as early as the days of Fariduddin Shakarganj (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384; Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 16, folio 12). The *kafi*, easy to understand and memorise, carried the mystics' teaching to people who heard rather than read it (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 15, folio 15). Its golden age, for Schimmel, was the late seventeenth and the eighteenth century, when the province was invaded one time after the other, and it reached its apex with Bulleh Shah (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 17, folio 13; Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 154, folio 154), whose lament that Punjab has gone to the dogs is the best known of the verses in which the province itself is a figure (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 16, folio xvi). The love of Hir and Ranjha is the province's own story, on the lips of everyone in the country of the Punjab in Kanhaiya Lal's day (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 10, folio 5), and Waris Shah's version is sung, Schimmel writes, wherever Punjabis meet (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 171, folio 153). The Punjab is one of the two principal regions of the qawwali tradition in Regula Burckhardt Qureshi's study (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 73, folio 53), and in the south its speech shades into Siraiki, spoken in Northern Sindh and Southern Punjab (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 220, folio 207). With the British conquest of the 1840s, Christopher Shackle writes, Persian was quite rapidly replaced by Urdu and English (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, pp. 11–12, folios x–xi); Punjabi became, in Schimmel's account, a literary medium used almost exclusively by the Sikhs (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396), and Khalid finds it looked down upon by the educated elite of Punjab (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 128, folio 110).

Rulers bound themselves to the province's holy places: the Delhi sultans and Mughal emperors granted huge tracts of rich land for the ceremonies of the great shrines of Punjab and Sindh (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 48–49, folios 36–37), and Akbar, in Carl W. Ernst and Bruce B. Lawrence's reading, anchored his imperial legitimacy in the Punjab through Chishti loyalty (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, pp. 112–113, folios 99–100). Lahori's editor credits the Mufti with recording, as an eyewitness, the destruction of the tombs of the shaikhs at the hands of the Sikhs (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 33, folio 21), while Ranjit Singh attached vast tracts of land to the gurdwaras (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16), and under him the Moti Masjid in the fort was renamed the Moti Mandir and held the treasury of the state (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 339, folio 346). The British, Robert Rozehnal writes after Katherine Ewing, treated the hereditary saints of the Punjab and Sindh as they treated landlords and tribal leaders (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 33, folio 23), and many important gurdwaras came to be administered by Hindu mahants (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 189, folio not stated). After Partition hundreds of temples and gurdwaras all over Pakistani Punjab were taken over by refugees and converted into households (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 58, folio not stated), and Omar Kasmani, after Umber bin Ibad, sets the colonial state, which did not try to control shrines directly, against a postcolonial state that has gone further (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 69, folio 52). Reformist movements, the Tableeghi Jamaat and the Deobandi school among them, have turned to the shrine culture of the south (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 10, ch. 4, folio not stated; Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated), and the killing of the Governor of Punjab, Salman Taseer, on 4 January 2011 runs through Khalid's books (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 19, Notes, note 44, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The seat of Raja Jaipal.** Bhandari's text names the fort that was Jaipal's seat in one form, uncertainly printed, and the editor's footnote gives a different reading (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 427).
- **Who first brought Islam to Lahore.** Lahori, citing *Tuhfat al-Wasilin*, calls Shaikh Muhammad Ismail Muhaddith Lahori, who settled in Lahore in the time of Sultan Masud of Ghazni, the first of the callers to Islam to come to Lahore (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 191, folio 179). Rizvi writes that Khattali ordered his disciple Shaikh Husain Zinjani to move to the Punjab, and later sent Hujwiri after him (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112).
- **When Shaikh Ismail Muhaddith Lahori died.** Lahori, following *Tuhfat al-Wasilin*, places him in the time of Sultan Masud of Ghazni and gives his death as 448 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 191, folio 179). His modern editor calls that source a fabricated tazkira and gives the correct year of his death as 980 AH, not 448 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 191–192, folios 179–180).
- **The Ghaznavid account of *Tuhfat al-Wasilin*.** Lahori retells from it the Ghaznavid rule of Punjab, its weakening under Bahram Shah and Raja Anangpal's taking of Lahore (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 264, folio 252); his editor calls *Tuhfat al-Wasilin* a fabricated tazkira (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 192, folio 180).
- **The end of Ghaznavid rule in the Punjab.** Kanhaiya Lal ends Khusrau Malik's rule over the Punjab with his surrender to Sultan Shihab al-Din Ghori, when the Ghurid sultanate in Hind began (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 24, folio 19). Bhandari ends Khusrau Malik's government of the *wilayat* when he went to Ghazni in 582 AH (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 405). Dhillon writes that "Shahabuddin Muhammad Ghori captured Punjab in 1192" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 136, folio 151).
- **Chingiz Khan in Punjab.** Lahori, from *Tazkira-e Hamidiya*, has Chingiz Khan's army plunder the whole land of Punjab and the people of Lahore fight it for two months, while himself doubting whether anyone will believe it (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 248, folio 236). His editor's addenda note that Shihab al-Din Nasawi does not mention an attack by Chingiz Khan on Lahore at all (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 352, folio 340).
- **The year the Mongols came to Punjab under Jalal al-Din Khalji.** Bhandari's text gives 693, uncertainly printed; the editor's footnote gives 691 from Firishta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 370).
- **The sultan of the fourth blow.** Kanhaiya Lal sets it in the time of Sultan Jalal al-Din Firuz Shah Khalji, but names the sultan who was away on the Gujarat campaign, in an uncertain reading, as Shams al-Din Iltutmish (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 26, folio 29).
- **Jasrath's tribe.** Kanhaiya Lal makes Jasrath the brother of Sikha Gakkhar (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 29, folio 32); Bhandari calls him Jasrath Khokhar, the name uncertainly printed (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 191).
- **Which Lodi sultan made Tatar Khan governor.** Bhandari has Tatar Khan sent to govern Punjab by Sultan Bahlul Lodi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 190; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 296), and places his governorship of Lahore in Bahlul Lodi's time, where the editor's footnote gives the variant Sikandar Lodi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 522–523).
- **The bounds of the Baluch *jagir*.** In one passage Bhandari has Sultan Husain Langah give Malik Sohrab a *jagir* from the fort of Kot Kardaz to Dhankot, the Baluch later holding the land from Sitpur to Dhankot (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 295); in his description of the *suba* of Multan the grant to Malik Sohrab and the other Baluch runs from Karor Kot to Mankot (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525).
- **The title of Zakariya Khan's office.** The tile inscription on his mosque calls him Zakariya Khan of the *suba* of the Punjab, while the book's own heading to the same entry calls him Nawab Zakariya Khan, known as Khan Bahadur, of the *suba* of Lahore (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 262–263, folios 265–266); elsewhere the book calls him Nawab Zakariya Khan Bahadur, *suba* of Lahore (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 337, folio 344) and counts him among the rulers of the Punjab (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 90, folio 93).
- **Ahmad Shah Abdali's dates.** Lahori gives a year read as 1120 AH, uncertainly printed, for Ahmad Shah Durrani's *farman* to Mufti Muhammad Mukarram; his editor calls the year wrong, writing that Abdali began his attacks on Punjab in 1161 AH, 1748, and died in 1187 AH, 1773 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 277, folio 265).
- **Abdali's invasion of 1748.** Rafat's introduction calls the invasion of 1748 Ahmad Shah Abdali's second invasion of the Punjab, followed by a third in 1752 (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 27, folio 7). The annotator of Waris Shah writes that Abdali "in 1748, began a series of invasions of India through the Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 8, folio 7), and Lahori's editor dates Abdali's first attack on Lahore to 1161 AH, 1748 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 214, folio 202).
- **The year of Shaikh Karam Shah's death.** Lahori gives it in words as one thousand two hundred, and in the line of his chronogram as 1201 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 190, folio 178).
- **When Ranjit Singh became ruler of the Punjab.** *Walking with Nanak* has him become "the sovereign of Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16), and elsewhere calls him "the Sikh ruler of Punjab, Maharaja Ranjit Singh in the nineteenth century" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 87, folio 69); *A White Trail* has him "crowned as the Ruler of Punjab" in 1801 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated).
- **How long Ranjit Singh ruled the Punjab.** Kanhaiya Lal writes that he ruled the Punjab for forty years (Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220), and Khalid that he brought stability "in Punjab which lasted a good forty years" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 208, folio not stated). Khalid's own dates have him "crowned as the Ruler of Punjab" in 1801 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated) and give his life as "Maharaja Ranjit Singh (1780-1839)" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 42, folio not stated).
- **Baba Farid and the troubles of Punjab.** Waris Shah writes that when Shakarganj settled at Pakpattan "the Punjab was delivered of all its troubles"; his annotator replies that this "is not historically correct" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 2, folio 1).
- **Whether Baba Farid converted the Punjab's clans.** Most Jat and Rajput groups that became Muslim in the medieval period claim conversion by Baba Farid or by Baha' al-Haqq Zakariya (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 227, folio 215), and the clans of the southwestern Punjab said so in court in the 1930s (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 241, folio 229); but the earliest sources on Baba Farid "make no mention of his having converted anyone", and many of these clans, "in the course of their northward migrations up the rivers of the Punjab, had not yet reached the Ajudhan area during Baba Farid's lifetime" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 227, folio 215).
- **The Sufism of the Punjab in Guru Nanak's time.** W. H. McLeod, as Rizvi quotes him, holds that "the Panjabi sufism of Guru Nanak's period had evidently departed radically from the classical pattern of Arab and Persian Sufism", and that most Muslims Nanak met represented "the blend of modified orthodoxy and debased Sufism" then dominant in the Muslim community of the Panjab (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 419–420, folios 395–396); Rizvi answers that "all literate Panjabis, both Hindus and Muslims, at the time of Guru Nanak were conversant with" 'Attar, Rumi and Sa'di, and that the Qadiris had made the Panjab the main centre of their activities (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 420, folio 396).
- **The Rasul Shahi houses.** Chishti writes that the order had no house in all Punjab but this one, and no other house, *takia* or *khanqah*, and then names one more house near Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 145).
- **What the Ara'in of the Punjab were.** In her text Abbas calls them "gardeners or small cultivators who worked on their own land" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 108, folio not stated); her glossary calls them "A caste of landowners in the Panjab" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 178, folio not stated).
- **How many Hindus live in the Punjab.** In one passage of *A White Trail* most Hindus are scattered in other provinces, "with only a few in Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 87, folio not stated); in others a significant Hindu population continues to live in Southern Punjab (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 31, folio not stated), and "The majority of the Hindu community is based in Sindh and Southern Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 233, folio not stated).
- **When mystical poetry in the Punjab's own language began.** Schimmel writes in one book that "Reliable tradition traces the use of the native language for mystical poetry both in Sind and in the Punjab back to the fifteenth century" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384); in another, that popular mystical poetry in the Punjab "may have emerged as early as in the days of Fariduddin Shakarganj", who died in 1265 (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 16, folio 12).
- **When the poets began to use folk tales as mystical teaching.** In *As Through a Veil* Schimmel places the development of folk tales as media for mystical instruction by the poets of Sindh and the Punjab only in the centuries after the death of the historian Badaʾuni (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 154, folio 136); in *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* Madho Lal Hussein's allusions to the stories of Hir and of Sohni and Mahiwal seem to inaugurate, or to continue from unknown beginnings, the insertion of folk tales into the context of mystical thought (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384).
- **Whether the Siraiki of Sindh and of the Punjab is one language.** Boivin's glossary calls Siraiki "a language spoken in Northern Sindh and Southern Punjab" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 220, folio 207); in his text he reports that for some specialists the Siraiki of Northern Sindh is "a distinct language from the Siraiki of South Punjab" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 171, folio 158).
- **The armies of the Punjab.** Waris Shah's verse speaks of the armies of the Punjab falling upon Hindustan; his annotator answers that the armies of the Punjab are known to have defended Hindustan, never to have fallen upon it (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 7, folio 6; Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 8, folio 7).
