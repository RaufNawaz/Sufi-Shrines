## The Province in History

H. T. Sorley's Sind is above all the Sind of Shah Abdul Latif's lifetime, and he frames his history of it as a history of the Mughal power in decline: "Nowhere is the need for an adequate description of the Moghul power in its decline felt more than in the case of Sind", where the growing ineffectiveness of Delhi's suzerainty "produced local events which shaped the destiny of the province" in a manner of its own (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 14, folio 5). He warns that the task is made hard by the scarcity, indeed the almost total absence, of reliable contemporary record for the years 1690 to 1760 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 14, folio 5). The East India Company's records for the poet's lifetime, which he gives as 1689 to 1752, are in his words "totally devoid of any reference to Sind" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 28, folio 19), and he finds no European record for Sind between 1662, as the damaged printed figure is read, and 1758 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 42, folio 33).

For Sorley the land itself shaped this history. "Sind is difficult country for the historian", he writes, a terrain "woefully devoid of informative historical monuments" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 19–20, folios 10–11). It is an alluvial tract over which the Indus has wandered through the centuries in a multitude of shifting channels, and its buildings were made mostly of mud, sticks or wattles, "just as they are outside all the large towns today" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 19–20, folios 10–11). For six months of the year, he says, the climate is as hot as anything in India and cursed with dust storms, and the whole is "A shifting alluvial plain liable to inundation, surrounded on every side but that of the sea by forbidding desert or barren hilly country" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 19–20, folios 10–11). "For the most part Sind has remained apart from the really crucial events happening in the rest of neighbouring Asia", he writes, and he calls its history episodic and isolated, showing to a late date the theocratic foundation of Islamic society (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 21, folio 12). In his judgement no event of prime importance graced the annals of Sind between 1659 and 1758, its history being monotonous and self-contained, cut off by "the nature of the country with its fortresses of desert and barren land on all sides" and by a climate that confined campaigning to a few months a year (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 22, folio 13). From 1000 A.D. onwards, he writes, Sind receives hardly any mention in Indian history, and Moreland found it impossible "to include Sind in his economic reconstruction of the Moghul Empire" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 23, folio 14).

Sorley's account of the earlier centuries begins with the Arabs. Sind, he writes, was under Arab governors for nearly three hundred years, and remained under Arab influence for longer still while the early dynasties of Multan and Mansura held Upper and Lower Sind until nearly the end of the tenth century; yet the Arabs "left no permanent impress upon the land" and "were merely a military garrison living amongst strangers" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 247, folio 238). Quoting Ray, he writes that "The internal administration of the country was necessarily left largely in the hands of Hindu landlords paying the land tax (kharaj) and the capitation tax (jizya)" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 248, folio 239). Quoting Masudi, he records that Arab merchants "formed the commercial communication between Sind and the neighbouring countries of India", bringing the produce of China and Ceylon to the seaports of Sind and carrying it on by way of Multan to Turkistan and Khurasan (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 248, folio 239). Towards the end of the tenth century, he writes, "Sind was infected by Qarmatian heresies from Egypt" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 248, folio 239), and elsewhere, again after Ray, that Islam in Sind then appears to have been touched by a wave of Qarmatian heresy (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 174, folio 165).

When Sind ceased to receive Arab governors from the Umayyad empire, Sorley writes, independent Sind dynasties arose, among which the Sumra soon became prominent, "a local dynasty of Rajput origin"; quoting Ray, he adds that southern Sind was never thoroughly conquered by the house of Ghazni (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 247, folio 238). He dates the Sumra regime from about 1025, as the damaged first figure is read, to about 1360, and notes the confusion "as to the authority wielded over Upper and Lower Sind, which have had very different histories" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 174, folio 165). He sets two older authorities beside each other: the *Ain-i-Akbari* says that a Sumro Rajput line of thirty-six princes reigned for five hundred years, and the *Tarikh-i-Tahiri* that the Hindu tribe of Sumra ruled Sind from the Hijri year 700, the figure imperfectly printed, until 843; a Sumro king, Chanesar, reigned in Lower Sind for about eighteen years around A.D. 1228 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 270, folio 261). His glossary calls the Sumro a Sindhi tribe that was at one time ruler of Sind (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 431, folio 422). Of the Samma, he writes that the Kalhora were "the first Sindhi dynasty to wield permanent power since the fall of the Samma" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 36, folio 27).

On how Sind became Muslim, Sorley gives more than one account. In one place he writes that the indigenous population of the lower Indus valley in the eighth century consisted of Hindu races speaking a Sanskritic language, and that "Most of the population was converted to Islam in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 174, folio 165); under Jam Nizamuddin, about A.D. 1461, Islam spread widely over Sind and the mass of the common people became Muslim (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 175, folio 166). In another he writes that "Sind was converted to Islam not by the Arabs but by the central Asian peoples who took up the tale of Muslim penetration of India from the twelfth century onwards", that this penetration went on through the reigns of the Sumro and Sammo dynasties, and that by the time of the Lodi Afghans in the sixteenth century the agricultural population and the landholding class were almost entirely Muslim, the Hindus being confined to the business of writing and to trade and commerce (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 248, folio 239). That state of affairs, he says, persisted down to the British annexation, whose year is lost in the printed text at this point, and Sind became a predominantly Muslim land at a time when Central Asian and Persian influences were at full strength (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 248, folio 239). He counts the comparatively late conversion of the bulk of the population among the peculiar circumstances of the province (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 245, folio 236). Christopher Shackle, in the introduction to his translation of Shah Abdul Latif's *Risalo*, writes that the Muslim conquests of northern India "extended this Persianate cultural world to Sindh", where Persian remained the dominant literary language of the Muslim elite down to the Mughal period and beyond (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 12, folio not stated), and he speaks of the Mughal capital of Thatta and the other urban centres of Sindh as homes of Persian poetry written by the educated elite (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 25, folio not stated).

The Mughal unit was not Sind alone. "Sind was added to the Moghul Empire by Akbar in 1592", Sorley writes (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 18, folio 9), and his chronological table records for that year the defeat of Mirza Jani Beg Turkhan, the name imperfectly printed, and the annexation of Sind by Akbar (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 200, folio 191). Under Akbar, he writes, the country as a whole was made part of the Subah of Multan, with sarkars established at Bakhar in the north and at Tatta in the Indus delta (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 31–32, folios 22–23). He restates it later: Sind consisted of two sarkars, or divisions, of the subah, or province, of Multan, and "The headquarters of the sarkars were Bakhar for Upper Sind and Tatta for Lower Sind" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 150, folio 141). He quotes Major P. Goldney's report that Upper Sind, at the time of Nadir Shah's invasion of Hindustan and sack of Delhi, formed part of the Subah of Multan, and that its "Moghulee" portion, Sukkur, Bukkar and Shikarpur, was then annexed to the Durani kingdom (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 38–39, folios 29–30; Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 164, folio 155). "What is certain is that Upper and Lower Sind were never definitely united till a much later day", he writes, and even as late as 1843 their unification was not complete (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 31–32, folios 22–23). From the time of Shah Jahan until the first decade of the eighteenth century the emperor's authority was wielded by officials sent from other parts of the empire; with the Kalhora, the emperor's representative was a native of Sind (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 31–32, folios 22–23). Callendar's account of 1775 says that "While the Mogul's government flourished Scindy was a province of the Empire", an honorary appointment bestowed each year on some person of rank whose expenses generally exceeded the revenues (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 61, folio 52).

Sorley's table of events gives the Mughal century in outline: in 1605 the rise of the Daudpota in Shikarpur and Upper Sind; in 1648 to 1651 Aurangzeb as governor of Multan and Sind; in 1658 the civil war between Aurangzeb and Dara Shikoh, ending in Dara Shikoh's defeat and his death after pursuit through Sind; and the Kalhora beginning to oppose the Mughal troops (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 200, folio 191). He judges that "The internal history of Sind for one hundred and fifty years after its annexation by Akbar shows as clearly as any other part of the Moghul dominions these characteristic deficiencies of Moghul government", and that the history of the Mughal occupation is full of conflicts between local and imperial revenue demands (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 33–34, folios 24–25). Withington's narrative, he writes, shows the disorder of that part of Sind in the early seventeenth century and how weak Mughal control was outside easy distance of the larger towns (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 78, folio 69; Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 81, folio 72). The Sameja were in rebellion against the Mughal governor in 1640, disturbing the peace of central Sind (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 51, folio 42), and in 1656 they were pillaging in Middle Sind (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 56, folio 47); a letter of 1656 shows how far the Baluchi population lay outside the effective control of the Mughal officers (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 55, folio 46). In a Company letter of 1644 Spiller wrote that "the people are so exceedingly opprest and kept so miserably poor that notwithstanding the soil is fertile and propper and would produce large quantities of good indicoes they have nether the will nor means to manure and sow the ground" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 52, folio 43). Trade in Upper Sind, he notes, was harder to finance than in Lower Sind (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 52–53, folios 43–44). In 1647, Sorley writes, there was much unsettlement in Upper Sind from a conflict of authority (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 53, folio 44), and the bad government of Upper Sind and the corruption of its petty officials draw caustic comment in the records of 1652 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 55, folio 46). "In that year and the next, Sind was ravaged by famine and plague", he writes of 1659, and a Company letter says that "neaver famine raged worse in any place, the living being hardly able to burye the dead" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 56, folio 47). By 1699, in his words, "travelling in Sind was still an undertaking not to be lightly faced", the countryside infested by bands of robbers (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 90, folio 81), and "the later Moghul empire in Sind was no better than the early one in preserving law and order" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 91, folio 82). Hamilton's picture of Mughal Sind at the end of the seventeenth century shows lawlessness in the countryside, the absolute autocracy of the governor and a thriving river trade, yet near the centre of government the administration was, within its limits, "not lacking in practical efficiency" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 94, folio 85).

The eighteenth century loosened the empire's hold. Sind, Sorley writes, remained an integral part of the empire until the disastrous advent of Nadir Shah in 1737, when it fell first under Persian and then, in 1747, under Afghan dominance; the Mughal governors appointed from Hindustan gave way to Sindhi governors bowing the knee "as little as they dared" to Mughal, Persian and Afghan in turn, and by 1760 the ruling Kalhora dynasty was "almost in fact but not at all in theory independent" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 18, folio 9). The Talpurs after them, he adds, were never in fact completely independent of their Afghan overlords until the first decade of the nineteenth century (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 18, folio 9). He sets out the stages: Kalhora governors from 1701; semi-independence by 1736; Persian suzerainty and tribute after the invasion of Nadir Shah in 1739; about 1747 the passing of suzerainty to the Pathan kingdom at Kabul; and from 1778 the civil war between the Kalhora and the Talpurs (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 35, folio 26). A Company letter of 8 April 1758 has the Pathans subduing "the whole Principality of Scindy" and making it tributary to them, as it had been to the Persians since Nadir Shah's conquest (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 63, folio 54). In the poet's own life, Sorley writes, Nadir Shah made Sind tributary to Persia and Ahmad Shah Durani made it subject to Kabul (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 180, folio 171). The tribute, he records, fell from 21 lakhs under Nadir Shah to 14 lakhs under Ahmad Shah in 1747, to 11 lakhs about 1760 and to 7 lakhs under Timur Shah about 1775, several of the printed figures imperfect, and it "was always in arrears" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 154, folio 145). The government of Sind regarded the Afghan government at Kabul as a form of unwarranted alien oppression (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 57–58, folios 48–49).

The same table carries the Kalhora and Talpur years, its figures imperfectly printed: Yar Muhammad Kalhoro obtains a grant from Aurangzeb and defeats the Daudpota in Upper Sind in 1701, and extends his power in Upper Sind in a year read as 1711; Nur Muhammad Kalhoro becomes Subedar of Sind on a line with no year that stands opposite 1739; Nadir Shah invades Sind, which becomes subject to Persia, in 1740; Sind becomes subject to the Afghan kingdom of Ahmad Shah Durani in 1750; Ahmad Shah Durani invades Sind in 1754, and Nur Muhammad Kalhoro flees and dies; and in 1757 to 1758 commotion and civil war end in the ascendancy of Ghulam Shah Kalhoro (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 201, folio 192). It continues with misgovernment in Sind in 1775, the deposition of Sarfaraz Khan Kalhoro for misgovernment in 1776, Ghulam Nabi Khan Kalhoro becoming ruler of Sind and the beginning of the civil war between the Kalhora and the Talpurs in 1778, and the final establishment of Talpur ascendancy in 1803 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 202, folio 193). From 1756 to 1758, Sorley writes, Sind was in "a pitiable condition of unrest", though its troubles in 1754 to 1758 were "merely typical of what was happening elsewhere" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 39, folio 30), and in 1758 "Sind no less than the rest of India was in confusion" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 46, folio 37). Robert Erskine wrote in 1760 that it would profit the Company if Sind were under as good and quiet a government, and as plentiful in grain, as it had been until about 1757, the year imperfectly printed (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 66, folio 57). Callendar wrote in 1775 that "Scindy tho a small track of country is naturally fertile and was rich and populous when it unfortunately fell under the government of this family about 35 years agoe, but has gradually declined ever since", and that "Its chief political connections are with the Patan and Siccs" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 61–62, folios 52–53).

Sorley describes the Kalhora as a Sindhi tribe of obscure origin, of the type now classed as Jamot, that is, non-Baluch original inhabitants of the Indus valley (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 36, folio 27). "It was certainly unfortunate for the masses of the Sind population", he writes, that a dynasty of their own people proved so poor a substitute for the government of the Mughals, while the Talpurs "did succeed in giving a distracted land peace for forty years" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 40–41, folios 31–32). The Baluchis, settled in Sind in considerable numbers, helped the Talpurs destroy "the failing powers of the Sindhi ruling family" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 35, folio 26); by then, he writes, they had become the strongest military force in the land, the earlier Kalhora having encouraged them to settle in Sind (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 40, folio 31). Under the Kalhora the government became a sort of cooperation between the ruling house and the chief Baluchi tribes, and the Talpurs welded Sind into a more complete unity than it had ever known, but only by asserting a Baluch hegemony that bore hard on the non-Baluchi elements of the population, especially the Hindus; under the Mughals and the Kalhora, he writes, Sind was never at peace, under the Talpurs it was, and the price of that peace was the tyranny of a Baluch minority (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 151–152, folios 142–143).

Between them, in Sorley's account, the Kalhora and the Talpurs established in Sind a petty Muslim state which kept, until the British conquest in 1843, the characteristic features of Mughal administration, adding to them "a peculiar brand of Islamic quasi-theocracy" and "a policy of splendid isolation" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 19, folio 10). "The aim of all Sind policy from 1701 onwards was to make Sind independent of Moghul, Persian and Pathan", to diminish the tribute and "to preserve the land as a closed terrain into which no foreigners of any sort were allowed entrance except with the utmost difficulty"; in 1843 Sind "was then to all intents and purposes a sovereign and petty Muslim state" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 35–36, folios 26–27). The two houses, he writes, kept Sind shut to the influences of the outside world for a hundred and fifty years (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 41, folio 32). Beneath these changes he finds a deep continuity: there is little reason to doubt, he says, that the peoples of Sind in the first half of the eighteenth century lived much as Sir Charles Napier found them in 1843 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 19, folio 10), and "the main administrative structure of the government remained largely unaltered from the days of Shah Jahan to the days of Sir Charles Napier" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 30, folio 21). The economic foundation changed little, he writes, despite the rise in the second half of the eighteenth century of the four large towns of modern Sind, Karachi, Hyderabad, Sukkur and Shikarpur, which displaced Tatta, Nasarpur, Rohri, Sehwan and Kandiaro (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 30, folio 21). The Sind of Napier was not very different in essentials from the Sind of Ghulam Shah Kalhoro, which in turn did not differ from the Sind of Akbar, Jahangir, Shah Jahan and Aurangzeb (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 31, folio 22), and "the framework of the Moghul administration in Sind remained little altered in essentials from 1600 to 1840, the change in spirit was considerable" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 153, folio 144).

The British, Sorley writes, entered on Sind "as if it were almost an unknown land" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 23, folio 14). For Shackle, only with the British conquest of Sindh in 1843 did the literary culture of the Sufi tradition come to be overlaid by the new patterns of modernity (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, pp. 12–13, folio not stated), and he notes that Sindh was administered from Bombay rather than Calcutta (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 25, folio not stated). He carries the story to "after independence in 1947, when the mass emigration of the Hindu population from Pakistan to India took place" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, pp. 12–13, folio not stated).

## The Land and Its Livelihood

Sorley's Sind of the seventeenth century was a place of trade. With its internal waterway system of the Indus, its easy connexion with Lahore and Multan, its thriving cotton industry, "and its vast emporium at Tatta with its busy port at Laribunder, was soon envisaged as a profitable centre of trade with Europe" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 97, folio 88). Fremlen wrote in 1636: "For of all Indian goods none are in such request as those of Synda" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 51, folio 42). Tatta and Nasarpur, Sorley writes, were the main centres of the cotton industry in Sind (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 102, folio 93). His table records an English factory established at Tatta in 1636 and the English factory in Sind given up in 1662 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 200, folio 191), and a factory established again at Tatta in 1758 and given up in 1775 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 202, folio 193). In the Company's press lists he finds "a complete blank till 25 November 1744" after a letter of 13 October 1657, references to Sind growing more numerous but not common until the factory's re-establishment in 1758, a regular record of the Sind factory from August 1762 to July 1764, and a list of covenanted servants for 1712 to 1738 that names no servant residing in Sind (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 204, folio 195). "The Company was always very much the timid guest of the Sind Government", he writes, and its customs and transit dues were "as unreasonable and oppressive as they had been in the Moghul days" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 58, folio 49; Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 57–58, folios 48–49).

Within Sind, Sorley writes, transport was all by pack animals, mostly camels, and wheeled traffic was practically unknown (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 98, folio 89). Yet "trade in Sind was on a very well-organized basis", by land and by water, the business soundly established and the government's supervision very thorough (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 100, folio 91). Pirates in those days infested the Gujarat, Kathiawar and Sind coasts (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 58, folio 49), and their presence on the Sind coast caused shippers continual anxiety (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 71, folio 62); a Company letter of 1758 says that the whole coast are rogues on both sides (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 63, folio 54). The Company's letters of the 1760s complain of "the paucity of skilled workmen available in Sind" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 67, folio 58), of "The inability of Sind to provide suitable artisans" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 72, folio 63), and Sorley writes that "It was impossible to get Sindhi craftsmen capable of making the kind of article needed" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 75, folio 66); artificers for the saltpetre coppers could not be procured in Sind (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 105, folio 96). Of the Company's own servants he observes, in his own voice, that "As Sindhi Muhammadans are notoriously unhappy away from Sind", their fondness for desertion perhaps made them unsuitable in Bombay (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 64, folio 55).

"During Moghul and Kalhoro times the chief industries were the manufacture of cotton goods, saltpetre and indigo, leather and fish", Sorley writes, and hand flour-milling, oil-pressing and the potter's craft can still be seen in Sind exactly as they were then (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 101–102, folios 92–93). The popular modern idea of Sind as a desert is, he says, as mistaken as the view that it was a purely agricultural country, for "Sind supported half a dozen thriving industries", with camels and buffaloes supporting much of the population; he quotes Crowe that "Camels are bred in every part of Sind" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 110, folio 101). Saltpetre production was estimated at up to 10,000 maunds a year (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 104, folio 95), but "The supply from Sind was never more than a useful auxiliary of the main supply obtained from Bengal and Bihar" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 105, folio 96). Indigo was grown mostly in Middle Sind, and its quality was inferior (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 106, folio 97). "Sind has always been a land with large numbers of buffaloes and oxen", with a plentiful supply of hides, and its saddlery and ornamental leatherwork were famous (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 107, folio 98). "In Sind, a Muslim land, this prejudice against leather is not felt by three-quarters of the population" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 108, folio 99). Sind has always been noted for its fresh and salt water fish, the *Ain-i-Akbari* giving rice and fish as the staple food of Lower Sind, and the Lower Sind littoral was, with Gujarat, Kanara and Malabar, one of the great sources of India's seafaring population (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 108–109, folios 99–100). The country, he adds, is deficient in building stone (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 101, folio 92), and metal does not exist in it (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 108, folio 99).

The trade declined. By 1770, Sorley writes, "Sind suffered with the rest of cotton manufacturing India", and the later Kalhora turned it into a self-contained Muslim state closed to Englishmen for nearly three-quarters of a century, so that it relapsed into lethargy until after 1843; yet he holds that the decline of its trade from 1775 was not due to the incapacity of the Kalhora or the parochialism of the Talpurs (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 116, folio 107). About the time of the British annexation, after half a century of Talpur rule, Sind had for nearly a hundred years ceased to supply cottons to the European market, and "In fact Sind was now importing English chintzes and calicoes instead of exporting her own"; its exports were rice, ghi, opium, indigo, dried fish and leather, Upper Sind sending dry grains, ghi, cotton and indigo to Lower Sind and receiving rice, fish, leather goods and arms (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 100–101, folios 91–92).

The river made the land. Sorley writes that "Sind is one of those lucky lands that are not seriously troubled if the monsoon" rains fail, "because Sind is the creation, the slow geographical accretion, of a river", its calamities being unchecked inundation, drifting sand and changing river channels (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 118, folio 109). Within its limits, he writes, it has always supported a tolerably well-fed population in modest comfort, and "The land was considered a sort of Arabia Felix with a quiet, industrious and intelligent people living in isolation from the rest of the Indian continent" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 119, folio 110). Famines such as devastated less fortunate parts of India "had ordinarily no terrors for the Sindhi peasant" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 129, folio 120), and "Sind was relatively immune from famines" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 143, folio 134). On the same page he records Aurangzeb's jagir yielding little because of drought, locusts, plague and floods in three successive seasons, from 1648 to 1652, and Hamilton's plague of 1696, and judges that "in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries public health in Sind was not good" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 143, folio 134); Burnes wrote of the unhealthiness of Lower Sind, its epidemics, agues, fevers and ophthalmia from the dust (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 144, folio 135). Greenery, Sorley writes, is not common in the arid land except close to the river and its large branches (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 182, folio 173), and his glossary gives Sawan, the word uncertainly printed, as the rainy season in Sind, July and August (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 430, folio 421).

Irrigation, for Sorley, is the key. Its place in Sind, where rainfall is scanty and never regular, cannot be exaggerated (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 121, folio 112). The canals, Persian wheels and wells of Sind "were constructed by the natives of the land without any assistance from the Empire of Delhi" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 122, folio 113), skilful irrigation has existed there since the earliest days (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 135, folio 126), and "Sind has always been a pioneer of irrigation in India" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 138, folio 129). He thinks Kalhora irrigation probably superior to that of the Talpurs, and quotes Lieutenant-Colonel Scott in 1853 that "the whole system of canals in Scinde is one of makeshifts and expedients" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 138, folio 129). The old lift methods, he writes, are still in use and can be seen all over Sind (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 139, folio 130), and the canal system of Mughal and Kalhora days must, within its limits, have been a very important source of the country's wealth (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 142–143, folios 133–134). Forced labour, *begari*, had always been practised in Sind, and several of the biggest canals were dug by labour conscripted in this way (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 130, folio 121). The Commissioner in Sind reported in 1847 that bajri and jowari were more common in the upper part of the country and wheat and barley in the lower, rice growing most extensively in the low southern parts (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 127, folio 118); "The Chandookah and the Lar were the two great rice producing areas just as they are today", and cultivators were then thought better off than in most parts of India (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 128, folio 119).

The people were fewer than in Sorley's day. "In the Moghul and Kalhoro days Sind did not support one quarter of the population it now supports", he writes (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 118, folio 109). Del Hoste calculated in 1832 that the population was 850,000, about a quarter of them Hindus, and Burnes in 1828 put it at about one million (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 126, folio 117). Between 1740 and 1800, the second figure imperfectly printed, the chief concentration of population shifted from Tatta to Shikarpur, Hyderabad and Karachi; the Commissioner in Sind in 1847 put the population at 1,274,732 on 48,000 square miles, both figures imperfectly printed, and in Mughal and Kalhora days, Sorley judges, it cannot have exceeded one million (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 127, folio 118). "The society was an agricultural and pastoral one with cottage industry well-diffused and with town-life, as we understand it today, concentrated in Tatta only" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 121, folio 112).

The village, in Sorley's account, changed little. The agricultural and pastoral population lived in huts that could be put up and moved with the floods, and nineteenth century accounts "accord very much with present-day conditions in agricultural and pastoral areas in Sind" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 123, folio 114). The Talpurs, like true Baluchis, set little store by magnificent buildings, and Baluch influence was, he thinks, partly responsible for a decline in the standard of building (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 123–124, folios 114–115). Of von Orlich's picture of the clay houses of Lower Sind in 1842 he writes that "All who are acquainted with Lower Sind today will recognize the truthfulness of this picture" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 124, folio 115). Wind scoops are "still in universal use in Lower Sind to catch the south-west wind" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 49, folio 40). "The Sindhi village must in fact have reminded one strongly of the villages of early medieval Europe", he writes, a description still true of the small villages of rural Sind in his day (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 125, folio 116), with Hindu traders in the bazaar and "the Musulman cultivators" in *landhis* round it (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 126, folio 117). "The labourers, artisans and peasants were therefore relatively little worse off in Moghul and Kalhoro times than they are today" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 131, folio 122), the moneylender advancing money against a share of the crop, a system that still prevails, and children herding cattle are still one of the chief obstacles to the education of the peasant class (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 132, folio 123); yet throughout the Mughal and Kalhora periods, he writes, circumstances continually conspired to grind the poor in their poverty (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 135, folio 126). Three kinds of rupee were in circulation in Sind (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 70, folio 61), and the lari was in common use in southern Persia, Sind and western Asia (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 133, folio 124).

Government rested on the land. The system at the time of the British occupation, Sorley writes, "did not differ greatly from the system of the Moghuls", the state's share taken in kind and taxation as a whole "extraordinarily comprehensive" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 153–154, folios 144–145). Sind was then approaching a natural and self-contained economy that argued a great increase in administrative efficiency from 1740 onwards (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 155, folio 146). Local cesses were not abolished under the Kalhora, and under the Talpurs their collection was tightened (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 156, folio 147). Tax farming, he writes, was one of the great curses of Mughal, Kalhora and Talpur rule alike, the farmers being mostly Hindus (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 157, folio 148), and a poll tax, the *sar shumari*, fell on non-Baluchi and Jat Muslims (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 155, folio 146). This taxation, backed by the Baluch soldiery, made the government far from popular with the common man and enriched the ruling house (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 159, folio 150). Land tenure in Sind, so far as is known, "has always been feudal" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 163, folio 154). In 1847 jagir and inam land was put at one-tenth of the government land; jagirs were held mostly by Baluch chiefs, and *puttadaris*, in Upper Sind especially, mostly by Afghan settlers dating from the time of Nadir Shah and earlier (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 164, folio 155). The zamindar's representative was called Mukhadam or Arbab, "a title that has not yet died out in Sind" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 165, folio 156).

## Saints and Pilgrimage

For Sorley, Sind drew holy men to it. "The extent to which Sind attracted contemplative and religious-minded persons during the middle age of Islam is well known and is the foundation of its school of Sufi thought" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 173, folio 164). Sufi mysticism, he writes, was "introduced into Sind centuries ago and found there a congenial home" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 236, folio 227), Sufistic thought has been very prominent in Sind at various times (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 249, folio 240), and "The fact that Sind was the home of several skilled exponents of Sufi thought during the middle ages accounts fully for the mastery of Sufi expression exhibited by Shah Abdul Latif" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 252, folio 243). "From the thirteenth century onwards there was a great immigration into Sind of learned and religious-minded men from the centre of the Muslim world", and "The great ascendancy of the Sayids in Sind doubtless dates from about this time" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 302, folio 293). Most of these Sayyids, he writes, were Shia, and "it is possible that Sind was for a time a congenial home for these immigrants" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 302, folio 293). He adds that "down the centuries Sayids in Sind have produced a number of men famous for their learning and their saintliness" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 212, folio 203).

Sorley is cautious about the history of Sufism itself. "It is not possible to trace in any detail the progress of tasawwuf in Sind", he writes, and there is no reliable account of the movement's progress there (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 298, folio 289). "That there has been any consistent progress of Sufism in Sind I do not believe" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 302, folio 293); in his view it has been handed down from age to age by a handful of learned men (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 303, folio 294). Between the thirteenth century, when Lal Shahbaz flourished, and the eighteenth, he writes, "there is very little that can be said to be securely established in the way of verified historical fact" about the Sufi poets of Sind (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 299, folio 290). Shackle gives a different emphasis: as in other regions of South Asia, he writes, a strong Sufi presence was rapidly established across Sindh with the arrival of charismatic figures, often associated with one of the main Sufi orders, such as the Suhravardis and the Qadiris (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 12, folio not stated).

The pir stood at the centre of religious life in the countryside. Shah Abdul Latif, Shackle writes, belonged to one of the many lineages of hereditary Sufi saints established in the countryside of Sindh, "where they have always enjoyed great prestige and power as pīrs or holy men with a special spiritual authority" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 8, folio not stated). Unlike many of the pirs of Sindh, he adds, the poet was not formally affiliated, through a chain of spiritual descent, to any of the great Sufi orders (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 9, folio not stated). Sorley writes that the practice of Sufism by the common people "was a very different thing from the refined system of the poets and theologians" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 298, folio 289). He quotes Elliot on the saints of the *Tuhfat al-Kiram*, "so numerous that there is scarcely a village in that priest-ridden country which has not its tombs of holy men" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 298, folio 289). "The Pirs held positions of great personal authority", he writes, and "The Sufi fakirs of Sind belonged to the two great orders of Jelali and Jemali darwishes" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 299, folio 290). The Jemali fakirs of Sind belonged to four orders, the Kadiri, the Nakshbandi, the Suhrawardi and the Chishti, and beside them were the Kalandars and the "so-called Sufi beggars who wandered far and wide over Sind taking their toll of the superstitious country people" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 300, folio 291). The countryside, he writes, "became in this way the home of an immense number of these petty imperia" of the pirs (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 176, folio 167). Of the Kalhora's descent from a sainted mendicant, Sorley writes that such a figure has always appealed vividly to the Muslims of the lower Indus valley (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 36, folio 27).

Sorley gathers the travellers' testimony to the veneration of holy men. The extent to which they "were reverenced and pampered in Sind" is, in his words, almost beyond belief, and a Persian proverb about Multan, that it consisted of heat, dust, beggars and tombs, applied equally to Sind; "The social religion of Sind was built up round the reverence of the murid for the murshid" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 166, folio 157). Dr Burnes wrote that "There is no country in Asia or rather on earth that is so perfectly priestridden", the country being said "to possess no fewer than one hundred thousand tombs of saints" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 167, folio 158), and Sir Alexander Burnes that "The mendicants in Sind are more numerous than in any other country in Asia" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 168, folio 159). The tendency, Sorley writes, grew more pronounced under the Kalhora; yet "The real form and meaning of Islam in Sind is not to be found in these ebullitions", but "is to be read in the beautiful spiritual poems of Shah Abdul Latif" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 169, folio 160). The belief that the saints sleep in their tombs is, he writes, "the solid foundation of the extraordinary devotion paid in Sind to the tombs of the deceased saints, pirs and holy men", bound up with the bond of pir and disciple (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 302, folio 293).

The tombs were places of gathering and of trade. "The oriental system of markets and fairs on saints' days at saints' tombs was well established", Sorley writes, so that in this respect the commerce of Sind followed the old medieval system (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 98, folio 89). The river had its own saint. In Sind, under the name of Khwajo Khizr, the saint "has always exercised a great power" and has in popular belief become identified with the spirit of the waters and the deity of the river Indus, and "The Daryapanthis of Sind are believers in the sanctity of the River God or the God of Waters, and the belief affects Hindu and Muslim alike" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 301, folio 292). Of the superstitious practices he lists, "Examples of all could be cited from Sind" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 177, folio 168). The cult of Khwajo Khizr, he writes elsewhere, was linked with the worship of the Indus and supported by Muslim and Hindu alike (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 177, folio 168), and in Sind Khizr is called Zinda Pir (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 178, folio 169).

Sindh was also crossed by Hindu pilgrims. Three of the *Risalo*'s surs, Shackle writes, are devoted to "the wandering yogis who traversed Sindh during their pilgrimage to the shrine of the goddess at Hinglaj in Balochistan", and as a young man Shah Latif is believed to have spent time with them (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 20, folio not stated). Sur 18, *Ramakali*, praises the groups of Hindu yogis "who wandered from one place to another in Sindh or beyond as ideal practitioners of the spiritual life" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 334, folio not stated). The *Risalo*'s many references to places in and around Sindh, Shackle adds, support the claim that the poet travelled widely as a young man (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 10, folio not stated).

## Communities

In seventeenth and eighteenth century Sind, Sorley writes, "a great gulf yawned between Islam's ideal and the practical working" of Islamic belief, in a Muslim state that struggled to semi-independence in the mid-eighteenth century (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 172, folio 163). It was a state with a population "composed for the most part of converted Jats"; "The majority of the population were followers of the Sunnat. But very influential sections held Shia tenets" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 172, folio 163). The Sayyids, he writes, were nearly all Shias, and "The Sindhi is by nature an easy-going being, not given to religious subtlety" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 173, folio 164). The mass conversions, he argues, broke down the clear distinction between Sunni and Shia and left the Islam of the common people much influenced by Hindu predilections, with "Hindus visiting Mussulman shrines and making votive offerings" and "the general easy-going character of Sind orthodoxy" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 175, folio 166). He traces what he calls the debasement of Islam to the popular religion of the indigenous Sindhis, sometimes called Jats, who were the majority of the population (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 178, folio 169). From a simple conviction of the tenets of a tolerant Sufism, he writes, flowed a Sufi school of thought, a more pantheistic conception of Islamic doctrine than elsewhere, "an absence of bitterness between Sunni and Shia", and a kind of rapprochement between the deeper mystical ideas of Muslim and Hindu thought (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 225, folio 216).

The Hindus kept the province's money and its trade. Even in the 1640s, Sorley writes, "big business was in the hands of Hindu traders", some of whom acquired considerable wealth (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 54, folio 45); the brokers, agents and dalals were mostly Hindus, who could not display opulence (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 100, folio 91), and "In Sind there were many considerable Hindu traders on whom" the Company's agents relied (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 113, folio 104). He quotes Pottinger's division of the population into three classes: the privileged, Amirs, chiefs, Baluchis, Sayyids and religious mendicants; the pastoral, agricultural and other Muslim tribes who made up the majority; and the Hindus, tyrannised over in the most infamous and open manner (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 170, folio 161). Under the Kalhora, he writes, "the true Sindhis, the Jats and camelmen, the fishermen and hunters" were fully exploited, and the position of the Hindus deteriorated further; yet they continued to live in Sind, advancing money to the zamindars, financing trade, farming the revenue and owning all the petty shops in the villages (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 171, folio 162). Shackle writes of the long-distance sea voyages "annually undertaken by the Hindu traders of Sindh to locations like Aden, Gujarat, or Lanka" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 16, folio not stated), glosses Aden as "The port in Yemen that was a regular destination for traders from Sindh", and notes that Portuguese pirates had an evil reputation in the seas around Sindh (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 323, folio not stated). He writes that it was the custom for the wives of the Hindu traders of Sindh to make offerings to the water deity for their husbands' safe return, that one distant place was famous for its wealth, "which attracted sailors from distant Sindh", and that the Hindu festival of lamps in the autumn marked the beginning of the trading season (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 324, folio not stated).

The Baluchis, Sorley writes, have been in Sind for many generations, tribes of them settled in various parts of the country long before the Kalhora called them in during the eighteenth century to stiffen the forces of the prince of Sind, the moment from which their political importance dates (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 255, folio 246). In 1613 the Baluchis of the lower country had no good reputation for law-abiding habits, and Upper Sind rather than Lower Sind drew them most; in his own day most of the Baluchi population was found in the Upper Sind Frontier, Larkana and Sukkur districts, the first still a kind of Baluchi preserve (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 255, folio 246). The modern Baluchi in Sind, he writes, "prefers the open fields to a house in a street" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 256, folio 247). Baluchis and Sindhis, he writes, had by then lived together long enough for their poetry to share common ground, and "The Doms visited the villages of Sind wherever there were" Baluchis to listen to them (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 257–258, folios 248–249). The Baluchis "as a class are not at all intolerant", he writes, tracing intolerance in Sind instead to the Kalhora "as fanatical Muslims" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 152, folio 143). The Jatois, the name imperfectly printed, and the Mazaris are, in his words, "tribes strongly represented in Sind" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 256–257, folios 247–248). Hindu races who came from the Punjab, he adds, form an important element in the population (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 270, folio 261).

Some communities stood low. The castes of fishermen named in sur 16, *Kamod*, as Gandiri, the first name uncertain in the printed text, Mangar, Me and Muhano, "were traditionally ranked as the lowest of the low in Sindhi society" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 333, folio not stated). Professional musicians in Sind, Sorley writes, are drawn mostly from the minstrel class, which is held in low esteem (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 232, folio 223).

The common life of the country was shared. "The peasantry of Sind are, and always have been, a happy-go-lucky people fond of laughter and amusement", Sorley writes, fond of conversation and of listening to songs and stories, and keeping the Id and Holi and Diwali with whole-heartedness (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 145, folio 136). Wrestling was as popular then as in his day, when it had a claim to be the most popular sport of the country; Sind possessed and still possesses a great variety of folk dances; polo does not seem to have been played there (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 148–149, folios 139–140). Intoxication with liquor and drugs, he writes, was undoubtedly a curse of the country (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 146, folio 137), very prevalent everywhere on Burton's evidence (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 147, folio 138).

The colonial order and partition changed the balance. It was the Sindhi Hindus, Shackle writes, who were first drawn to take part most actively in the colonial system, much of the new secondary literature being "the work of Hindu scholars", a situation that lasted until the mass emigration of the Hindu population from Pakistan to India after independence in 1947 (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, pp. 12–13, folio not stated).

## Language, Poetry and Music

For Sorley, Shah Abdul Latif's *Risalo* "is the only classic which the language of Sind has yet produced in the realm of deeply imaginative literature", its poems held in universal and popular esteem and known in some form to all classes (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 7, folio not stated). The poetry "exercises over Hindus in Sind a spell almost as complete as that exercised by it over Muslims" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 175, folio 166), and its appeal lies "deep in the hearts of all classes of Sind's population, Muslim and Hindu, lettered and unlettered" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 178–179, folios 169–170). The poet's achievement came in days when Sind was still medieval in outlook, and "the probabilities are all against Sind's producing a second Shah Abdul Latif", though his poems "still bind Sind with a powerful spell of love and admiration" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 215, folio 206). His poetry is "the only gem cast up from the sea of Sind's vernacular literature" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 216, folio 207). Shackle writes that the *Risalo* "is universally acknowledged to be the greatest classic of Sindhi literature in both Sindh itself and other parts of Pakistan, as well as among the Sindhi émigré population in India and the wider diaspora" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 8, folio not stated). He adds, however, that there was already an established culture of vernacular Sufi poetry in Sindh before Shah Latif, with poets such as Shah Abdul Karim, who died in 1623, and Shah Inat Rizvi, who died in 1711, though it tends to be overshadowed in the literary histories by Shah Latif's unique reputation (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 9, folio not stated).

Sorley explains the poem's place by the province's circumstances. The cultural heritage of Sind, he writes, came to fruition in Shah Abdul Latif's poetry at a time when the Mughal Empire was showing clear signs of its dissolution; the province's peculiar circumstances were the late conversion of most of its people to Islam and the absence of any considerable body of native literature in Sindhi, because its intelligentsia was drawn mostly from the learned and privileged classes, many of them immigrants rather than original inhabitants (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 245, folio 236). During the Muslim domination of Sind from the fourteenth century onwards, he writes, "the thoughts and religion of the Hindu population received but scant acknowledgement in the literary forms of the day" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 246, folio 237), while the Sufi strain of thought has in Sind always appealed to Hindus (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 247, folio 238). The *Risalo*, he says, is understood and loved in Sind by Hindus as much as by Muslims (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 225, folio 216). Its stories, in his reading, "are all Hindu in origin. It is plain that most of them relate to the Sind of pre-Islam days" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 269, folio 260); apart from the Punjab and Cutch tales they are stories of Lower Sind current among the Hindu peoples on the borders of the Thar before their general conversion to Islam, so that the Hindu peoples of Sind in his day feel that their culture, no less than that of Islam, went into the making of the poem (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 271, folio 262). Shackle, for his part, writes that the heroines' stories "are loosely set in the pre-Mughal period when Sindh was ruled by the Muslim Rajput dynasties of Sumiros and Samos, but they are not related in detail" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 17, folio not stated), and that other surs are based on male characters "from the heroic Rajput period of Sindhi history" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 21, folio not stated).

These legends give the province a heroic past. Shackle glosses Lakho Phulani as "a legendary bandit from the heroic age of Sindh, who is remembered for his fearless attacks on the ruling Jarejo Samos", and Jakhiro as "a Rajput ruler of medieval Sindh famous for his bravery and generosity", whose verses are to be read as an extended panegyric to the Prophet; in a famous episode of these legends, the invasion of the Muslim emperor of Delhi, Sultan Alauddin, who died in 1316, was resisted by Jakhiro, who took under his protection the women of his fellow Rajputs, the Sumiros (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 342, folio not stated).

The *Risalo* makes a world out of Sindh. It "draws upon a wide-ranging set of interlocking references to the scenery, society, and legends of Sindh to create a whole new imaginative world", Shackle writes (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 15, folio not stated). In sur 22, *Sarang*, "the transformation of the landscape in Sindh and far beyond is interpreted as a manifestation of the universal extent of divine grace" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 21, folio not stated), the monsoon rains being conceived as a manifestation of divine power both in Sindh, where the normally dry countryside is transformed by them, and across the rest of the world (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, pp. 337–338, folio not stated). After a catalogue of the places the rains reach, ending "Some have passed over Umarkot and have made the ground green", the verse, in Shackle's translation, asks God to make Sindh flourish forever, and closes: "Dear friend and sweet beloved, make the whole world prosperous" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 265, folio not stated). That final invocation of God's blessings on Sindh, Shackle writes, is one of the most often quoted verses of the *Risalo* (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 338, folio not stated). In sur 18, *Ramakali*, the yogi's horn is praised: "There is nothing like it in the north, in Sindh, or in Hind" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 244, folio not stated), and Shackle explains that "Hind denotes the Gangetic region, as opposed to Sindh and the Indus valley" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 336, folio not stated). "The crane (S. kūṉja) comes to Sindh as a winter migrant", he writes in a note to sur 28, *Dahar*, its plaintive cry heard as the pain of separation from the flock (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 341, folio not stated). Sorley too finds the countryside in the poem: "few countries are as rich as Sind is in bird life", and in Sind the crow is "a harbinger of good tidings" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 266, folio 257).

The province's learned languages were not its spoken one. The five native histories of Sind, Sorley writes, are all in Persian, "which was the Court language of the ruling houses of the country up to the days of the British conquest" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 24, folio 15). The learned men of Mughal and Kalhora Sind, though not few, do not make a company imposing in achievement, and they wrote "in Persian and Arabic and not in the language of the countryside" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 217, folio 208). Most of them were Sayyids, Kazis and Mullas, or men concerned with teaching the Koran, and the writers belonged largely to families that had immigrated into Sind; "They were not Sindhis by birth" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 218–219, folios 209–210). In the days of the Afghans before the Mughals, and earlier, "Sind proved a favourite home for learned men", drawn by the reputation Sind had gained for its Sufi philosophy, and the age of the Arghuns and Tarkhans was specially notable for the entry of "this kind of intellectual aristocracy", which had "but little effect upon the unlettered cultivator class"; "In this respect Sind was merely typical of Muslim India generally" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 219, folio 210). Arabic, he writes, has always been known and spoken in Sind by a few learned men, and the Koran "is known by heart by large numbers of devout Muslims in the province", but the effect of Arabic on the poetical literature of Sind is negligible (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 248, folio 239). "Urdu has never been a language which has taken kindly to Sind soil", and "the literary currents which played in Sind came from Persian and Arabic sources" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 258, folio 249). The Sindhi language itself, in his view, suits "a peace-loving agricultural and pastoral culture elaborated by an industrious and non-martial people" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 120, folio 111).

Learning reached few. Sind has always had a number of persons called *hafiz*, which does not mean, Sorley writes, that they know Arabic as scholars (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 219, folio 210). There is no contemporary account of the working of its educational system in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, the best description being Burton's, gathered orally (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 220, folio 211). Sind in Mughal days, he concludes, showed merely the working of the Islamic educational system, and "The great mass of the people remained illiterate" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 222, folio 213). Sind, he writes, has never been distinguished for learning and could have had few chances in medieval times of keeping anything like a Muslim university (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 224, folio 215). The teaching profession, he writes, does not seem ever to have been held in great respect in Sind, teachers seldom being paid more than half a rupee a month by each pupil; in the time of the Talpurs Sind had six madrasas, at Sehwan, at Trippat, at a place north of Sehwan whose printed name cannot be read with certainty, at Matiari, at Mohar or Walhari near Umarkot, and at Chothiyari on the Nara (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 225, folio 216). The Sindhi language "was much neglected as a medium of instruction except by Hindus, who used a non-Arabic script", and conditions were unlikely to have been better under the Mughals and the Kalhora (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 225–226, folios 216–217).

Music was everywhere, and largely in Hindu hands. The *Ain-i-Akbari*, Sorley writes, describes the music of Sind as *kami*, amatory (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 229, folio 220). "Most of the musicians in Sind have been Hindus and not Mussulmans", one reason, he thinks, why the poetry "exercises so strong a spell over the non-Muslim inhabitants of the land" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 231, folio 222). "There is nothing distinctive in the music of Sind", whose instruments are chiefly the ektar, the sitar, the sarangi, the tambur and the pipes and drums used elsewhere in India, yet "Sind has long had a reputation for proficiency in musical execution prevailing amongst all classes" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 232, folio 223). He quotes Crowe: "The Sindians are excessively fond of singing" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 146, folio 137).

Under British rule, the poem acquired a scholarship and an examination syllabus. The poems, Sorley writes, lived as sung verse in the days of the Kalhora and the Talpurs, but "it was only after the settled days of the British occupation of the country that there was born that type of literary curiosity which calls forth books of comment and criticism"; the literature on the poet owes its origin largely to English education in the eighty years before he wrote, Trumpp having worked in the days when Bartle Frere was Commissioner in Sind, and the vast growth of general education in Sind under British rule has spurred the study of the poems (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 233, folio 224). Shackle writes that after 1843 Persian was quite rapidly replaced as the language of education, administration and elite literature by English and by Sindhi, "whose development was actively encouraged by the colonial authorities in Bombay", and that the *Risalo*, a classic that had always appealed to all sections of the Sindhi population, "was pressed into service for the examinations prescribed by the new education syllabus" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, pp. 12–13, folio not stated). Because Sindh was administered from Bombay rather than Calcutta, he adds, Urdu was not installed as the immediate successor of Persian (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 25, folio not stated).

## Shrines and the State

In Sorley's account religion and government were bound together in Sind for centuries. Since the time of the Sumra in the thirteenth century, he writes, "the land has been predominantly Muslim in population and the government had a strong theocratic basis with much virtual power in the hands of the priestly class of Sayids" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 23, folio 14). The Kalhora's deference to the priestly class "led to the extraordinary reverence for pirs, Sayids and holy men, which may be said to have been the predominant characteristic of eighteenth century Sind" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 163, folio 154). MacMurdo put ecclesiastical expense at one-third of the gross revenue (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 167, folio 158). Of the order under the Kalhora, in which the true Sindhis were exploited and the position of the Hindus grew worse, Sorley writes that a quasi-theocratic state could have taken no other form (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 171, folio 162).

Burton, as Sorley quotes him, saw Sufism in Sind as a power in politics: *tasawwuf* "under the native governments was as formidable a political engine as most of the secret confraternities recorded in history"; "Even among the mild Sindhis a noted Pir" might formerly safely order one of his disciples to murder an enemy; "Yet the native princes encouraged it partly from superstition and partly because the price of every Pir was well known to them" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 299, folio 290).

The law was the law of a Muslim state. "Sind was a Muslim state and in theory justice was administered according to the tenets of the Koran", Sorley writes, though in Mughal and Kalhora days every man was to some extent the defender of his own rights and the police were lamentably insufficient (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 159, folio 150). Collective village responsibility, as nineteenth century officers described it, might apply word for word to the Sind of Mughal and Kalhora days, and crime, he writes, was comparatively little rife in Sind, apart from cattle stealing and the wildness of the Baluchi tribes, though the country held many expert thieves and equally expert trackers (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 160–161, folios 151–152). Trial by ordeal was practised, and under the Talpurs there were no regular civil courts (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 161, folio 152). Burnes wrote that as the state religion of Sind was taken from the Koran, so was its system of jurisprudence; Hindus settled disputes "by panchayats", and the Baluchis seemed generally to take the law into their own hands (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 162, folio 153).

Learning, too, was supported through religious property. Sorley finds no evidence that a single educational institution in Sind owed its origin to any act of an emperor of Delhi (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 221, folio 212). The chief support of the literary groups, he writes, came from revenues granted as *inams* to mosques and to the *maktabs* attached to them (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 222, folio 213).

## The Province in the Books

Sorley dedicated his book "TO THE PEOPLE OF SIND" with a verse of his own on a peace that followed an age of war: "The drums of peacefulness are struck", runs one line, and the next, "Your land a full abundance yields" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 9, folio not stated). For him, eighteenth century Sind was a survival of an older world. In the days when the verses were composed, he writes, "Islam in Sind preserved the true native form of its characteristic power"; the ideas that then permeated popular thinking, he adds, penetrated the Muslim consciousness much more deeply than in the Sind of his own day, when that territorial dominion had been lost; and the Muslim India of the Middle Ages, "of which eighteenth century Sind was a relic", recalled in some features medieval Christian Europe (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 213, folio 204). The Sind from which the poetry could spring, he writes, was more like a "Merrie England" of village self-sufficiency: "It was a society of landholders, petty cultivators, herdsmen, craftsmen and traders, knowing little of the outside world except what could be seen of it at markets and fairs, in the gatherings of strangers at tombs on saints' days or in the company of fellow pilgrims" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 213–214, folios 204–205). Summing up his history, he writes that Sind in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries "was a land of cultivators and herdsmen, keepers of camels, buffaloes, sheep, goats and oxen, of tombs and Sayids, of dust and heat, of the annual rise and fall of the waters of the Indus but inefficiently controlled by a crude system of irrigation" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 265, folio 256). Of the poem's rustic scenes, he writes, "No one familiar with Sind will dispute how true a portrayal this is of a simple rustic civilization of husbandmen and cattle herds" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 267–268, folios 258–259), and the poems are loved by all classes in Sind (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 268, folio 259).

Sorley argues with the nineteenth century writers. He holds that "many of the criticisms of Victorian writers are unjust" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 30, folio 21). Burton, he writes, "despised Sind for what he considered its lax Muhammadanism" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 120, folio 111), and Burton's view that Sind came into British hands in an almost exhausted state cannot be accepted "without considerable qualification" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 143, folio 134). Against such critics he quotes Assistant-Surgeon Heddle in May 1836: "The large masses of the indigenous population are particularly industrious", the merchants of Sind are active and intelligent, and "The people are orderly and obedient and the laws are respected" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 116–117, folios 107–108). He sets his own judgement that "The peoples of Sind, whether indigenous or immigrant, have never shown any military genius", and that "The people as a whole have always been peaceful and industrious" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 23, folio 14). Lieutenant James, reporting in 1847 on the Chandookah pargana, wrote of Sind as one continued battlefield, the scene of usurpation, tyranny and bloodshed, "till finally the British bayonet gave the worn-out country peace" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 36–37, folios 27–28).

The word itself is not always the province. Baluch poetry, Sorley writes, has many references to Sindh, but the word so used means not the modern political division of that name but the valley of the Indus, though some places named are actually Sindhi places in the country south of Multan that reaches far down the river towards the sea (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 256, folio 247). One Baluchi bard, as Longworth Dames records him, sings that drunkards "are the young men of the Sindh country", since "there is much water and bhang is cheap" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 256–257, folios 247–248).

Shackle's Sindh is a land read through its poet, and he warns against reading the poet too narrowly as its voice. Sur 15, *Marui*, he writes, "also allows for a nativist interpretation that exalts the authenticity of the values and landscape of Sindh" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 332, folio not stated), but his introduction cautions against seeing Shah Latif "too narrowly" as "an authentic spokesman of the Sindhi folk tradition or as an advocate of Sindhi nationalism" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 13, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- On the year of Nadir Shah's coming, Sorley gives the disastrous advent of Nadir Shah as 1737 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 18, folio 9); elsewhere Persian suzerainty follows the invasion of Nadir Shah in 1739 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 35, folio 26); and his chronological table has Nadir Shah invade Sind in 1740 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 201, folio 192).
- On when Sind passed under the Afghans, Sorley gives 1747 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 18, folio 9) and about 1747 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 35, folio 26); his chronological table has Sind become subject to the Afghan kingdom of Ahmad Shah Durani in 1750 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 201, folio 192).
- On when the people of Sind became Muslim, Sorley in one place says most of the population was converted in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 174, folio 165), with the mass of the common people becoming Muslim under Jam Nizamuddin about A.D. 1461 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 175, folio 166); in another, that conversion came from the twelfth century onwards through the central Asian peoples, the agricultural population and landholding class being almost entirely Muslim by the time of the Lodi Afghans in the sixteenth century (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 248, folio 239).
- On the dates of Sumra rule, Sorley gives about 1025 to about 1360 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 174, folio 165); the *Tarikh-i-Tahiri*, as he quotes it, gives the Hijri years 700 to 843, and the *Ain-i-Akbari* a line of thirty-six princes reigning for five hundred years (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 270, folio 261).
- On who the Sumra were, Sorley calls them a local dynasty of Rajput origin (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 247, folio 238); the *Tarikh-i-Tahiri*, as he quotes it, calls them a Hindu tribe (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 270, folio 261); Shackle speaks of the Muslim Rajput dynasties of the Sumiros and Samos (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 17, folio not stated).
- On the unit Sind formed under the Mughals, Sorley makes it part of the Subah of Multan, divided into the sarkars of Bakhar and Tatta (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 31–32, folios 22–23; Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 150, folio 141); Callendar's account of 1775, which he quotes, calls Scindy a province of the Empire (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 61, folio 52); and his chronological table names Aurangzeb governor of Multan and Sind (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 200, folio 191) and Nur Muhammad Kalhoro Subedar of Sind (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 201, folio 192).
- On how the Sufi presence grew in the province, Shackle writes that it was rapidly established across Sindh with the arrival of charismatic figures of the main orders (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 12, folio not stated); Sorley writes that no reliable account of its progress exists and that he does not believe there was any consistent progress of Sufism in Sind (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 298, folio 289; Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 302, folio 293).
- On Sindhi literature before Shah Abdul Latif, Sorley writes of the absence of any considerable body of native literature in Sindhi (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 245, folio 236) and calls the *Risalo* the only classic of the language (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 7, folio not stated); Shackle writes that there was already an established culture of vernacular Sufi poetry in Sindh, overshadowed by Shah Latif's reputation (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 9, folio not stated).
- On the setting of the *Risalo*'s stories, Sorley holds that they are all Hindu in origin and relate mostly to the Sind of pre-Islamic days (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 269, folio 260); Shackle writes that the heroines' stories are loosely set in the pre-Mughal period of the Muslim Rajput dynasties of the Sumiros and Samos (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 17, folio not stated).
- On peace under the Talpurs, Sorley writes that they gave a distracted land peace for forty years (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 40–41, folios 31–32) and that under them Sind was at peace, at the price of a Baluch minority's tyranny (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 151–152, folios 142–143); Lieutenant James, whom he quotes, saw Sind as one continued battlefield until the British bayonet gave it peace (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 36–37, folios 27–28).
- On famine, Sorley calls Sind relatively immune from famines (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 143, folio 134; Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 129, folio 120); he also records the famine and plague of 1659 and 1660, of which a Company letter says no famine ever raged worse (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 56, folio 47), and drought, locusts, plague and floods in three successive seasons from 1648 to 1652 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 143, folio 134).
- On the population, Del Hoste put it at 850,000 in 1832 and Burnes at about one million in 1828 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 126, folio 117).
- On the state of Sind at the British conquest, Burton held that it came into British hands almost exhausted; Sorley says this cannot be accepted without considerable qualification (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 143, folio 134).
