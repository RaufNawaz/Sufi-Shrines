## Overview

The books mean different units by Sindh: for Annemarie Schimmel it is the lower Indus valley (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 61, folio 30), and in *Pain and Grace* the southern part of present day Pakistan (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 6, folio 2). Sujan Rai Bhandari, in a work he dates to the year 1107 of the Hijra (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 581), calls it the *wilayat* of Sindh known as Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 581), and in his own day it is a Mughal *suba*, the *suba* of Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530), made up of four *sarkars*, Thatta, Siwistan, Nasirpur and Umarkot (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 528–529). H. T. Sorley has Akbar make the country part of the *suba* of Multan, with *sarkars* at Bhakkar in the north and at Thatta in the Indus delta, and holds that Upper and Lower Sindh were never definitely united until a much later day, their unification not complete even in 1843 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 31–32, folios 22–23). Michel Boivin notes that the Siraiki speaking area of Southern Punjab was a part of Sindh during the Middle Ages (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 216, folio 203), and in Baluch poetry, Sorley writes, the word means not the modern political division but the valley of the Indus (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 256, folio 247). Under the British, Sindh was administered from Bombay rather than Calcutta (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 25, folio not stated), and Jürgen Schaflechner, following Oscar Verkaaik, traces Sindhi nationalism to the struggle for independence from the Bombay Presidency in the 1920s and 1930s (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 88, folio not stated). Today, Boivin writes, Sindh is the province of Pakistan with the most significant Hindu population (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 176–177, folios not stated and 164).

Bhandari begins its history with its ancient capitals, Brahmanabad, then Adpur, then Dewal, with Thatta and Debal the seat of government in his own day (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530). In Schimmel's account the Arabs reached Sindh in 711 and laid the foundation of a Muslim rule (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 61, folio 30), and Sorley writes that it was under Arab governors for nearly three hundred years, though the Arabs left no permanent impress upon the land (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 247, folio 238). Local dynasties followed, the Sumra and then the Samma (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 173), and from 1520 the Turkish Arghun and then the Tarkhan, under whom Sindh fell to the Mughal Empire (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 207, folio 207). Sorley dates its annexation by Akbar to 1592 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 18, folio 9). In the eighteenth century the Kalhora rose from governors to semi-independence, paid tribute to Persia after Nadir Shah's invasion and passed under the suzerainty of the Pathan kingdom at Kabul (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 35, folio 26), and the Talpurs who overthrew them ruled the country until the British conquered it in 1843 (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, pp. 30–31, folio not stated). Beneath these changes Sorley finds a deep continuity, the Sindh of Napier not very different in essentials from the Sindh of Akbar, Jahangir, Shah Jahan and Aurangzeb (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 31, folio 22). When Persian was given up as the official language in the 1850s, Sindhi and English became the two languages of administration (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 44, folio 31). At Partition the departure of the Hindu elite and the arrival of Muslim refugees from India altered the province's demography (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 87, folio not stated).

For Schimmel, Sindh never ceased after the Arab conquest to be a centre of Islamic studies and a seat of Islamic mysticism (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 6, folio 2). Hallaj travelled throughout it in 905 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 376, folio 345), and from the thirteenth century, when Lal Shahbaz Qalandar settled in Sehwan and Baha'uddin Zakariya made Multan a Suhrawardi centre, Sindh produced an immense number of mystics whose shrines are scattered all through the Indus Valley (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 25, folio not stated). Dr Burnes, as Sorley quotes him, found the country priest ridden and said to possess a hundred thousand tombs of saints (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 167, folio 158), and in Sorley's own words its social religion was built round the reverence of the disciple for the master (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 166, folio 157). Boivin names the Qadiriyya, the Qalandariyya and the Sohrawardiyya as the orders dominant among the Hindu Sufis of Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 227–228, folios 214–215), while Sorley divides its fakirs into the two great orders of Jelali and Jemali darwishes (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 299, folio 290). The river had its own saint, Khwajo Khizr, whose cult was linked with the worship of the Indus and supported by Muslim and Hindu alike (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 177, folio 168). Wandering yogis traversed Sindh on pilgrimage to the goddess at Hinglaj in Balochistan (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 20, folio not stated), and Schaflechner finds in the south of Pakistan, especially Sindh, a network of pilgrimage routes visited by different religious communities (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 107, folio not stated). Boivin sorts the ties of the province's Hindus to Sufism into three kinds: a tomb visited by both Muslims and Hindus, such as Bhit Shah; a saint with a double identity, such as Udero Lal; and a Sufi *darbar* managed by Hindus, such as that of Pithoro Pir (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 177–178, folios 164–165).

Sorley's eighteenth century Sindh is a Muslim state whose population was composed for the most part of converted Jats, the majority Sunni but very influential sections Shia (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 172, folio 163). The Baluchis had lived in the country for many generations before the Kalhora called them in during the eighteenth century, the moment from which their political importance dates (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 255, folio 246), and the Hindus advanced money to the zamindars, financed trade, farmed the revenue and owned all the petty shops in the villages (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 171, folio 162). In Boivin's account the Lohanas made up half of the whole Hindu population and were divided into Amils and Bhaibands (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 40–41, folios 27–28), and the Nanakpanth was probably the dominant religious path among the Hindu Sindhis of the nineteenth century (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 90, folio 77). Before the census, he argues, people had identified themselves according to their locality, caste and ethnicity rather than by affiliation to the great religions (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 23, folio 10). The Sidis, first brought as slaves from Africa, now speak Sindhi as their native language (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 67–68, folios not stated). Sindh is home to the majority of Pakistan's Hindus, over 90 per cent of whom, in Schaflechner's figures, belong to underprivileged tribes or subcastes such as the Menghwar, the Bhil and the Kohli (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 13, folio not stated), and Haroon Khalid finds the Nanakpanthi Sikhs in very large numbers in the province (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 197, folio not stated).

In Sindh, Schimmel writes, the Sufis made the spoken language into a literary one (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 161, folio 161), though Persian was the court language of the ruling houses until the British conquest (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 24, folio 15). Siraiki, the dialect of Northern Sindh spoken especially among its Baluchi population, gave many of its Sufi poets a second language (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 171, folio 158). The range of folk tales used for mystical purposes is wider in Sindh than elsewhere, from Qadi Qadan's verse in the first half of the sixteenth century to the *Risalo* of Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit in the eighteenth (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 171, folio 153). For Sorley the *Risalo* is the only classic the language of Sindh has yet produced (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 7, folio not stated), and Christopher Shackle calls it universally acknowledged as the greatest classic of Sindhi literature (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 8, folio not stated); it is understood and loved in Sindh by Hindus as much as by Muslims (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 225, folio 216). Its printing in 1866 was the main event in the relation of print to Sufism in the province (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 47–48, folios 34–35), and Hindu Sindhis played a leading part in publishing Sufi books in the late nineteenth century (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 173, folio 160). Most of the musicians of Sindh, Sorley writes, have been Hindus (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 231, folio 222); Shemeem Burney Abbas finds that there can be no ritual there without the Manganhar, the community musicians (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 77, folio not stated), and that *sufiana kalam*, sung in the vernacular, is a feature of the shrines of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar and of Shah Abdul Latif at Bhit (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 85, folio not stated). The majority of the Naqshbandis in Sindh, Boivin writes, are strongly opposed to music (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 228, folio 215).

Holy places and power were long bound together. Since the time of the Sumiros, Sorley writes, the government had a strong theocratic basis with much virtual power in the hands of the Sayyids (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 23, folio 14), and under the Kalhora the reverence for pirs, Sayyids and holy men became the predominant characteristic of eighteenth century Sindh (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 163, folio 154). Burton, as Sorley quotes him, saw Sufism under the native governments as a formidable political engine (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 299, folio 290). British policy in the Punjab and Sindh treated the hereditary saints much as it treated landlords and tribal leaders (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 33, folio 23), and the *hurr*, the obedient disciples of the Pir Pagaro, joined the struggle against the British and played a minor role even in the war of 1971 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 428, folio 397). Omar Kasmani finds that the state's Sindhi publications hardly mentioned Sehwan until the late 1960s while regularly featuring other saints of Sindh (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 70, folio 53), and Schaflechner saw many temples renovated within the past decade, in step with changes in the political landscape (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 16, folio not stated). Sindhi nationalists, as he reports them, joined the province to Sufism, G. M. Syed finding Sufism in its purest form there (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 88–89, folio not stated); Schaflechner calls the notion that the history of Sindh holds no religious tension between Hindus and Muslims a nationalist utopian ideal (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 90–91, folio not stated), and Boivin argues that, despite the spread of Islamist terrorism in Sindh, the Sufi culture still pervades Sindhi society (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 177, folio 164).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The date of the Arab conquest.** Schimmel and Eaton give 711 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 61, folio 30; Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 30, folio 18); Rizvi has Muhammad bin Qasim begin his advance in 710 and conquer Sindh "between 711 and 712" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 127, folio 109); Chishti has the country of Sindh conquered under Walid between 705 and 715 of the Christian era, both numbers uncertain in the printed text, after earlier attacks under Uthman and a partial conquest under Ali (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 802; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 802–803).
- **Who held Mansura in the tenth century.** Rizvi writes that Mansura, the capital of Sindh, was ruled by the Sumira dynasty, which had accepted the overlordship of the Fatimid Caliphs of Egypt (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 128, folio 110); Schimmel has the Carmathians rule northern Sindh and Multan (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 98, folio 67); Sorley has the early dynasties of Multan and Mansura, under Arab influence, hold Upper and Lower Sindh until nearly the end of the tenth century (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 247, folio 238).
- **Whether Sufism came early to Sindh.** Abbas writes that the Sufi traditions came to the subcontinent through the province of Sindh and that a long line of Sufi mystics followed Muhammad bin Qasim's conquest (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 50, folio not stated); Rizvi writes that "no such developments in Sind are recorded" in the early period and explains the lack of information about Sufis there by slow land routes and Fatimid overlordship (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 127, folio 109; Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 128, folio 110); Schimmel finds the pious of the region interested in the early centuries mainly in hadith and in transmitting Indian learning westward (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 375, folio 344).
- **Who the Sumra were.** Sorley calls them a local dynasty of Rajput origin (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 247, folio 238); the *Tarikh-i-Tahiri*, as he quotes it, calls them a Hindu tribe (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 270, folio 261); Shackle speaks of the Muslim Rajput dynasties of the Sumiros and Samos (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 17, folio not stated).
- **The dates and length of Sumra rule.** Sorley dates the Sumra regime from about 1025 to about 1360 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 174, folio 165); the *Tarikh-i-Tahiri*, as he quotes it, gives the Hijri years 700 to 843, and the *Ain-i-Akbari* a line of thirty-six princes reigning for five hundred years (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 270, folio 261); Rizvi has the Sumira rule Lower Sindh until the middle of the fourteenth century (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 128, folio 110); Bhandari has the Sumra take over from the descendants of Bani Tamim Ansari, who ruled for one hundred and thirty nine years, and keep the government in their family for five hundred years before it passed to the Samma (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 173).
- **When Samma rule began.** Boivin writes that the Sammas officially took power in 1351, while the Sumras kept control of some outlying areas of Sindh such as Umarkot (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 189, folio 176); Bhandari's list has the first Samma ruler, Jam Anzar, reign from the year 735 of the Hijra, itself uncertain in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 172–173).
- **Who ruled Sindh when Firuz Shah of Delhi came against it.** Boivin writes that in 1367 Firuz Shah Tughluq laid siege to Thatta when Jam Juna II controlled Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 189, folio 176); Bhandari sets Firuz Shah's three campaigns against the Jam in the time of Jam Bani Thatta son of Jam Anzar, and makes Jam Junan Anzar's brother and predecessor of Bani Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 173; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 172–173).
- **When the people of Sindh became Muslim.** Sorley in one place says most of the population was converted in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 174, folio 165), with the mass of the common people becoming Muslim under Jam Nizamuddin about A.D. 1461 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 175, folio 166); in another, that conversion came from the twelfth century onwards through the central Asian peoples, the agricultural population and landholding class being almost entirely Muslim by the time of the Lodi Afghans in the sixteenth century (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 248, folio 239). Boivin writes that historical sources show that before the eighteenth century the people who identified themselves as Muslims were few, probably because the name was restricted to the *sayyids* and their dependents (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 22–23, folios 9–10).
- **How the Sufi presence grew in the province.** Shackle writes that it was rapidly established across Sindh with the arrival of charismatic figures of the main orders (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 12, folio not stated); Sorley writes that no reliable account of its progress exists and that he does not believe there was any consistent progress of Sufism in Sindh (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 298, folio 289; Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 302, folio 293).
- **The Chishtiyya in Sindh.** Boivin writes that, maybe because of the Sohrawardiyya's hold on Sindh, the Chishtiyya could not spread there as it did in the rest of South Asia (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 227–228, folios 214–215), and that it was not widespread in Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 217, folio 204); Sorley counts the Chishti among the four orders to which the Jemali *faqirs* of Sindh belonged (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 300, folio 291).
- **The year of the Mughal annexation.** Sorley writes that Akbar added Sindh to the empire in 1592 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 18, folio 9), and his chronological table records the annexation in that year (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 200, folio 191); Schimmel writes that Sindh was part of the Mughal Empire since 1595 (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 25, folio not stated); Bhandari has Mirza Jani Beg come to court with the Khankhanan in the year 1001 of the Hijra, a year uncertain in the printed text, and the country enter the protected dominions of the empire (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 171).
- **The unit Sindh formed under the Mughals.** Sorley makes it part of the Subah of Multan, divided into the sarkars of Bakhar and Tatta (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 31–32, folios 22–23; Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 150, folio 141); Callendar's account of 1775, which he quotes, calls Scindy a province of the Empire (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 61, folio 52); and his chronological table names Aurangzeb governor of Multan and Sindh (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 200, folio 191) and Nur Muhammad Kalhoro Subedar of Sindh (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 201, folio 192). Bhandari knows it as a *suba* of its own, the *suba* of Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530), made up of four *sarkars*, Thatta, Siwistan, Nasirpur and Umarkot (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 528–529), with governors of its own appointed by the emperor (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 105; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 64).
- **The year of Nadir Shah's coming.** Sorley gives the disastrous advent of Nadir Shah as 1737 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 18, folio 9); elsewhere Persian suzerainty follows the invasion of Nadir Shah in 1739 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 35, folio 26); and his chronological table has Nadir Shah invade Sindh in 1740 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 201, folio 192).
- **When Sindh passed under the Afghans.** Sorley gives 1747 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 18, folio 9) and about 1747 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 35, folio 26); his chronological table has Sindh become subject to the Afghan kingdom of Ahmad Shah Durani in 1750 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 201, folio 192).
- **To which invader Divan Gidumal gave the dust of Sindh's saints.** In *Pain and Grace* the gift of two bags holding the holy dust of the tombs of the saints and pirs of Sindh is set at Ahmad Shah Abdali's invasion, which took place in Shah Abdul Latif's lifetime (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 25, folio not stated); in *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* the dust is given to "the invading Nadir Shah (1739)" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396).
- **When the Talpurs came to power.** Schimmel writes that the Talpurs came to power in 1781 and ruled the country until the British conquest of 1843 (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, pp. 30–31, folio not stated); Sorley's chronological table has the civil war between the Kalhora and the Talpurs begin in 1778 and the final establishment of Talpur ascendancy in 1803 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 202, folio 193).
- **Peace under the Talpurs.** Sorley writes that they gave a distracted land peace for forty years (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 40–41, folios 31–32) and that under them Sindh was at peace, at the price of a Baluch minority's tyranny (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 151–152, folios 142–143); Lieutenant James, whom he quotes, saw Sindh as one continued battlefield until the British bayonet gave it peace (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 36–37, folios 27–28).
- **Famine.** Sorley calls Sindh relatively immune from famines (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 143, folio 134; Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 129, folio 120); he also records the famine and plague of 1659 and 1660, of which a Company letter says no famine ever raged worse (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 56, folio 47), and drought, locusts, plague and floods in three successive seasons from 1648 to 1652 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 143, folio 134).
- **The population before the conquest.** Del Hoste put it at 850,000 in 1832 and Burnes at about one million in 1828 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 126, folio 117).
- **The state of Sindh at the British conquest.** Burton held that it came into British hands almost exhausted; Sorley says this cannot be accepted without considerable qualification (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 143, folio 134).
- **The census of 1872.** Within one sentence of Boivin's, the account opens by saying that Sindh's population statistics are not available in the first census of 1872, then gives a population printed as 2,333,527 million and a breakdown by religion (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 38, folio 25).
- **How many Hindus left Sindh at Partition.** Boivin puts them at closer to 0.8 million, if not fewer, and reports a kind of consensus of around a million (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 61, folio 48).
- **Whether the province's history knew religious harmony.** G. M. Syed, as Schaflechner reports him, found Sufism in its purest form in Sindh, proven by shrines visited by Hindus and Muslims alike, and Verkaaik calls mysticism and tolerance the essence Sindhi Hindus and Muslims shared (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 88–89, folio not stated; Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 89, folio not stated); Schaflechner calls the notion that the history of Sindh holds no religious tension between the two communities a nationalist utopian ideal (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 90–91, folio not stated); Boivin, against the trope that Sindh did not suffer Punjab's violence, cites the Sukkur riots and the rising riots from the 1920s (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 61, folio 48; Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 64, folio 51).
- **When Hindus began visiting Sufi shrines.** Boivin writes that Hindus have been coming to the Sufi mausoleums of Sindh since the nineteenth century (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 183, folio 170); Sorley, writing of the Islam of the common people in the Sindh of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, speaks of "Hindus visiting Mussulman shrines and making votive offerings" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 175, folio 166).
- **Caste.** Thakur wrote that the caste system is almost absent in Sindh (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 277–278, folio not stated); Boivin writes that, contrary to popular belief, there were many untouchables in Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 40, folio 27), while allowing that Sindh may have had a social fabric of its own (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 36–37, folio not stated / folio 24).
- **The caste make up of the Hindus of Sindh today.** Schaflechner writes that over 90 per cent of them belong to underprivileged tribes or subcastes (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 13, folio not stated; Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 276, folio not stated), and Boivin that most of them are from the scheduled castes (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 175, folio 162); Khalid writes that the Hindu community of Sindh and Southern Punjab has a proportionate representation from the lower and the upper castes (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 233, folio not stated).
- **The share of the Lohanas among the Hindus.** Boivin gives them half of the whole Hindu population (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 40–41, folios 27–28); McMurdo, as Schaflechner quotes him, calls them the great body of Hindus in Sindh, and Thakur the general body of the Sindh Hindus (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 79, folio not stated; Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 80, folio not stated).
- **The Shikaris and the Dheds.** Burton held that they were neither Hindus nor Muslims, while other authors count the Shikaris as Muslims and the Dheds as Hindus (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 187, folio 174).
- **Shah Abdul Latif's dates.** Schaflechner dates him 1690 to 1751 (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 95); Schimmel gives 1689 to 1752 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 414, folio 383), and so does Sorley (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 28, folio 19).
- **Sindhi literature before Shah Abdul Latif.** Sorley writes of the absence of any considerable body of native literature in Sindhi (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 245, folio 236) and calls the *Risalo* the only classic of the language (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 7, folio not stated); Shackle writes that there was already an established culture of vernacular Sufi poetry in Sindh, overshadowed by Shah Abdul Latif's reputation (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 9, folio not stated); Schimmel dates native language mystical poetry in Sindh from the fifteenth century (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384), and finds the first allusions to the folk tales in Qadi Qadan's verse in the first half of the sixteenth century (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 171, folio 153).
- **The setting of the *Risalo*'s stories.** Sorley holds that they are all Hindu in origin and relate mostly to the Sindh of pre-Islamic days (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 269, folio 260); Shackle writes that the heroines' stories are loosely set in the pre-Mughal period of the Muslim Rajput dynasties of the Sumiros and Samos (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 17, folio not stated).
- **Where the Sassi story belongs.** The annotator of Usborne's Waris Shah calls it "a story of old Sind" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 12, folio 11, note 5); Abbas writes that it "originated in Baluchistan and Sind" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 156, folio not stated).
- **What *sung* means.** Abbas calls *sung* the form of choir singing in Sindh (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 40, folio not stated); her informants Sikander Baloch and Naseer Mirza use it for the practice in which the lover woos the estranged beloved through a serenade on the castanets (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 55, folio not stated); her glossary has a choir that sings at the Sufi shrines in Sindh (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 188, folio not stated); and a note records that a native Sindhi informant corrected the term to mean a choir of mendicants who sing mystical poetry (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 198, folio not stated).
