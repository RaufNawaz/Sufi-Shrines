## Overview

Sujan Rai Bhandari's Mughal chronicle speaks of the chiefs of Baluchistan and records that Rustam Mirza's jagir was the wilayat of Multan and Baluchistan, which yielded more than the revenue of Qandahar (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 175); it does not call that Baluchistan a suba, and gives it no bounds (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 175). For Jürgen Schaflechner, writing of the modern state, it is "a province that has been in a state of a latent insurgency since the establishment of Pakistan" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 169, folio not stated). For Richard M. Eaton, who crossed it by train in the mid 1960s, it was the road "from Iran across the Baluchistan desert and Sulaiman Mountains, down through the Bolan Pass until the fertile, green strip of the Indus Valley hove into view" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 13, folio not stated).

Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi writes of a conquest: "In 710 Muhammad bin Qasim al-Saqafi began his advance through Makran and Baluchistan with the conquest of Sind between 711 and 712" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 127, folio 109). Seen from Sindh, H. T. Sorley writes that Sindh had its own trouble with dynasties that did not last, with civil wars and with the depredations of hill hordes from Balochistan (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 23, folio 14), and that in time the offspring of 'mendicants and shepherds' from the hills of Balochistan sat one after another upon the throne of Sindh (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 118, folio 109).

Shemeem Burney Abbas writes that after Muhammad bin Qasim's conquest "a long line of Sufi mystics came to the region through Baluchistan, Mekran, Multan, and Sind itself" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 50, folio not stated), and Annemarie Schimmel that proper names like Ghauth Bakhsh, 'gift of the help', in Sindh and Balochistan point to the popularity of Gilani (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 279, folio 248). Michel Boivin calls Hinglaj "a pilgrimage centre located in the Sindhi-speaking area of Baluchistan" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 50, folio 37), and Abbas gives guāṭi as the name in Balochistan for healing ceremonies to which families bring emotionally disturbed women (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 66, folio not stated), and in her glossary for a ritual of making vows at Sufi shrines there (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 182, folio not stated).

Haroon Khalid counts Balochistan among the provinces where "most of the Hindus are scattered" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 87, folio not stated), Boivin writes of a part of Balochistan where Sindhi is spoken (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 59, folio 46), and Abbas writes of the Sidi communities of Balochistan, among others, who hold the mugarmān drum sacred (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 185, folio not stated). Sorley finds that the poetry of Sindh and that of Balochistan have 'certain elements in common' (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 256, folio 247), and Abbas that "The mystical lore of the region that includes Sind, Baluchistan, and the coastal belt of Iran shares myths, melodies, and linguistic codes" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 47–48, folios not stated).

Schaflechner writes that Indian pilgrims "must obtain an additional visa allowing travel to Balochistan" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 169, folio not stated), and that a bomb blast at Lahoot la Makan in November 2016 "has created a volatile situation for Balochistan's shrines" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 108, folio not stated). Robert Rozehnal records that in the national elections of October 2002 the MMA gained "an upper hand as the largest party in Baluchistan" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 32, folio 22).

## The Province in History

Rizvi dates the advance of Muhammad bin Qasim: "In 710 Muhammad bin Qasim al-Saqafi began his advance through Makran and Baluchistan with the conquest of Sind between 711 and 712" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 127, folio 109).

Sorley, whose subject is Sindh, writes of its history from 1000 A.D. onwards (the printed year is damaged in the text and is read as 1000), and says that Sindh had its own trouble with dynasties that did not last, with civil wars and with the depredations of hill hordes from Balochistan (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 23, folio 14).

Sorley quotes Burton's *History of Sindh* on Mian Mir Mahomed, the first prince of the Kalhoro dynasty, who made 'the fatal mistake' of sending to the Baluchi country and inducing two of his 'mountaineer Murids' to leave their barren hills and settle in the low country (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 41, folio 32). Writing in his own voice of the Talpur rise, Sorley says that the offspring of 'mendicants and shepherds' from the hills of Balochistan sat one after another upon the throne of Sindh (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 118, folio 109).

Bhandari's chronicle of the Mughal empire knows a Baluchistan with chiefs of its own: among the Khankhanan's orders during the emperor's Qandahar campaign, for which the chronicle here gives no year, it was better if the chiefs of Baluchistan carried out what submission and obedience required and joined in the campaign, and if they did not, they were to be brought to fitting punishment (the reading of that last word is uncertain in the printed text) (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 175). On the same page Bhandari records that the jagir of Rustam Mirza was the wilayat of Multan and Baluchistan, which yielded more than the revenue of Qandahar (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 175). The chronicle does not call this Baluchistan a suba and gives no account of its extent (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 175).

Schaflechner calls Balochistan "a province that has been in a state of a latent insurgency since the establishment of Pakistan", citing Axmann (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 169, folio not stated). Rozehnal records that in the national elections of October 2002 the MMA gained "an upper hand as the largest party in Baluchistan" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 32, folio 22).

## Land and Routes

Sorley writes that in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries the camel kafilas from Bakhar to Afghanistan and Persia kept "regular seasons for travel which avoided the worst of the hot weather and inundation in Upper Sind and the intense winter cold" of the highlands of Balochistan and Persia (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 98, folio 89).

Eaton remembers his first journey to the Subcontinent, in the mid 1960s, "travelling by train from Iran across the Baluchistan desert and Sulaiman Mountains, down through the Bolan Pass until the fertile, green strip of the Indus Valley hove into view" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 13, folio not stated). In an essay on the western Punjab he contrasts the pastoralists there, who stayed on the Punjab plains, with the nomadism of Balochistan or Afghanistan, where pastoral tribes move between the plains in the wet winter season and the mountains in the dry season (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 223, folio 211).

Schaflechner writes that mud cones last because, with Balochistan's low average rainfall, the mud is not subject to rapid erosion, citing Snead (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 211, folio not stated). "Unlike many other rivers in Balochistan, the Hingol flows throughout the year" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 215, folio not stated). Travellers of the past came "through the dry and barren landscape of Balochistan" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 251, folio not stated), and during the spring gatherings "the extreme heat in Balochistan makes fieldwork a grueling task" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 293–294, folio not stated).

Khalid, looking out from Manora, sees "the Makran mountain range, the boundary line between Sindh and Baluchistan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 228, folio 202). Boivin, recalling a visit of 2016, calls the Kohistan "the western part of Sindh that stands close to Baluchistan" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 184, folio 171), and he calls Fathepur "a village in the Kachhi plain about 100 kilometres from Jacobabad, but located in the Sindhi-speaking part of Baluchistan" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 59, folio 46).

In a note on Michael Wood's film, Abbas mentions "the Indus along the Baluchistan coast back through Persia as a route that seems to have been used for centuries" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 210, folio not stated). Schaflechner writes that after the first part of the Makran Coastal Highway, the N 10, was completed in 2001, the area around the River Hingol became connected to National Highway 25, "a heavily traveled road that links Karachi with Quetta" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 83, folio not stated). The highway was "originally intended to boost economic development of the Balochistan coast", he writes, and it reshaped the pilgrimage; he sets "the recent infrastructural development in Balochistan's coastal area" against the hardship of the older journey (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 322, folio not stated).

## Saints and Pilgrimage

For Abbas, Balochistan was one of the roads by which the Sufis came: after Muhammad bin Qasim's conquest, she writes, "a long line of Sufi mystics came to the region through Baluchistan, Mekran, Multan, and Sind itself" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 50, folio not stated).

Schimmel writes that proper names like Ghauth Bakhsh, 'gift of the help', in Sindh and Balochistan point to the popularity of Gilani (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 279, folio 248).

Boivin calls Hinglaj "a pilgrimage centre located in the Sindhi-speaking area of Baluchistan" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 50, folio 37), and places the village of Fathepur in the part of Balochistan where Sindhi is spoken (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 59, folio 46). Schaflechner writes of Lahoot la Makan that "a terrible bomb blast in November 2016, killing more than fifty people, has curbed the growing influx of visitors and has created a volatile situation for Balochistan's shrines" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 108, folio not stated).

Abbas writes: "Many times, families will bring women who are emotionally disturbed to participate in the dhammāl, which devotees claim has healing powers. In Baluchistan, such healing ceremonies are called guāṭi" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 66, folio not stated). Her glossary defines the word differently, as "A ritual for making vows at Sufi shrines in Baluchistan" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 182, folio not stated).

## Communities

Khalid writes that the majority of the non-Muslims who remain in Pakistan "are Hindus living in the 'faraway' 'peripheral' regions of Southern Punjab, inner Sindh and Baluchistan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 12, folio not stated). Citing the census of 1998, he names Balochistan among the provinces where "most of the Hindus are scattered" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 87, folio not stated).

Boivin twice speaks of a part of Balochistan where Sindhi is spoken: Hinglaj lies in "the Sindhi-speaking area of Baluchistan" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 50, folio 37), and Fathepur in "the Sindhi-speaking part of Baluchistan" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 59, folio 46).

Abbas writes that the mugarmān drum is "held sacred by the Sidi communities in Sind, Baluchistan, Rajasthan, and Gujrat who use them in their shrines and for rituals" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 185, folio not stated). She speaks also of "the Brahui who live in Baluchistan", though only in a conditional argument about the Sassi myth: "Assuming that there were matrilineal structures among the Brahui who live in Baluchistan, the myth expresses the conceptual worldview of a community" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 156, folio not stated).

Sorley reckons that near the poet's residence, "in the Kohistan and Kotri localities", there must have been many Mekranis and other inhabitants of the southern Baluch country (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 255, folio 246).

## Language, Poetry and Music

Sorley holds that the poetry of Sindh and that of Balochistan have 'certain elements in common' (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 256, folio 247). Quoting Longworth Dames's *Popular Poetry of the Baloches* on the poetry of the Baluchis, he writes: "There are no ghazals, no artificial arrangements of poems in diwans, none of the pedantry of Persian prosody", and "As might be expected in a parched-up land where water is scarce and rain seldom falls, the poets delight in describing the vivid thunderstorms" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 255, folio 246). That poetry is described in the same pages as 'wholly oral', "sung by professional minstrels called Doms or Dombs who sing the songs in the gatherings of the clans" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 255, folio 246).

Abbas, reporting the work of ethnomusicologists, writes that "The mystical lore of the region that includes Sind, Baluchistan, and the coastal belt of Iran shares myths, melodies, and linguistic codes" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 47–48, folios not stated). The "Mast Qalandar" dhun, she writes, is "reported to be sung all along the Rajasthan, Sind, Baluchistan, and Iran continuum" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 57, folio not stated).

The tale of Sassi belongs to the province in Abbas's account. The myth is "told in the lands of Sind and Baluchistan" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 118, folio not stated) and sung "in the regional languages and dialects of Sind, Baluchistan, and the Siraiki-speaking regions" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 120, folio not stated). "There is evidence of the female power principle in the Sassi myth that originated in Baluchistan and Sind", she writes, and "Sassi is said to have been a ruler" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 156, folio not stated).

Schimmel, in *Pain and Grace*, lists "the hill slopes of Balochistan" beside Thar Parkar, the Indus plains and the Indus mouth among the parts of the country where the folk tales of Sindh are located (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 161, folio 161).

## Pilgrimage and the State

Schaflechner writes that Indian pilgrims "must obtain an additional visa allowing travel to Balochistan, a province that has been in a state of a latent insurgency since the establishment of Pakistan" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 169, folio not stated). Access to Balochistan, he writes, is further limited by its legal ambiguity, since 'Non-Objection Certificates' must be obtained from the government while the legal status of an application for one is unclear; he quotes Ratnu, who writes that Pakistan "actually does not issue visas for the journey to Balochistan", so that "the journey has no proper legal status and thus is likely to involve bribery and nepotism" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 169, folio not stated).

Elsewhere he speaks of 'a restricted area of Balochistan' (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 179, folio not stated), writes that "the status of visas for Balochistan is legally ambiguous" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 184, folio not stated), and that "there is currently no solid legal base for the journey into Balochistan" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 192, folio not stated).

Rozehnal records that in the national elections of October 2002 the MMA gained "an upper hand as the largest party in Baluchistan" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 32, folio 22). He gives, in his own translation, Wahid Bakhsh's list of the slogans of "Nationalist parties", among them "Azad Balochistan", which the book glosses as "a free home for the ethnic Baluchis"; their aim, Wahid Bakhsh says, would leave "four smaller countries" to "be swallowed by India overnight" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 127, folio 118). Khalid, rendering the kind of sermon a local maulvi preached at the time of a protest in September 2012, has him pray for the demise of the United States, Israel and India, which he holds responsible for everything wrong in the country, among it "a separatist movement in Baluchistan" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 7, ch. 1, folio not stated).

## The Province in the Books

Bhandari, writing for a Mughal reader, names Baluchistan beside Multan as a wilayat that could be given as a jagir, and its chiefs as men to be brought to submission and obedience (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 175).

For Sorley, writing of Sindh, Balochistan is hill country: the home of hill hordes (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 23, folio 14), of the disciples a Kalhoro prince called down to the low country (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 41, folio 32), of highlands of intense winter cold (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 98, folio 89), and of a poetry that shares certain elements with that of Sindh (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 256, folio 247).

Schimmel, in *Pain and Grace*, counts the hill slopes of Balochistan within the folk tale country of Sindh (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 161, folio 161). Abbas sees one region of shared lore that includes Sindh, Balochistan and the coastal belt of Iran (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 47–48, folios not stated).

Eaton knew it first as a desert to be crossed on the way to the Indus (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 13, folio not stated). Schaflechner writes of it as a land of low rainfall and extreme heat (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 211, folio not stated; Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 293–294, folio not stated), and as a restricted area (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 179, folio not stated). For Khalid it is one of the 'faraway' and 'peripheral' regions where Pakistan's Hindus live (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 12, folio not stated).

Badar Abro, whom Schaflechner quotes, makes the "real Sindh" cover "the valleys between Rohri and east Balochistan" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 94, folio not stated). Schaflechner gives this as Abro's claim about an ancient past (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 94, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **What guāṭi is.** In her text Abbas calls guāṭi the name in Balochistan for the healing ceremonies to which families bring emotionally disturbed women (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 66, folio not stated). Her glossary defines it as "A ritual for making vows at Sufi shrines in Baluchistan" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 182, folio not stated).
- **Where Sindh ends.** Khalid calls the Makran mountain range "the boundary line between Sindh and Baluchistan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 228, folio 202). Abro, as Schaflechner quotes him, makes the "real Sindh" run to "the valleys between Rohri and east Balochistan" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 94, folio not stated).
