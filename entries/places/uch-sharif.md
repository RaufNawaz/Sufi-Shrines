## Overview

Shemeem Burney Abbas calls Uch Gilanian "the city of saints in Bahawalpur" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 107, folio not stated). Robert Rozehnal, who spent a day there, describes "this ancient center of learning, trade, and pilgrimage built on a hill overlooking the confluence of the Sutlej and Chenab rivers" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 21, folio 10), and places it as "an ancient city located seventy miles southwest of Multan in Pakistan" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 210, folio 202). Annemarie Schimmel places Ucch northeast of Multan (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354).

Schimmel writes that "Ucch was, for a while, the center of the Suhrawardiyya", thanks chiefly to Jalaluddin Makhdum-i Jahaniyan (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354), and that it was in Ucch that the first missionaries of the Qadiriyya settled in the late fifteenth century, the order spreading from there into the Subcontinent (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354). Rozehnal names Shaykh Muhammad al-Husseini al-Jilani as "the founder of the first Qadiriyya khanaqah at Uch" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 259, folio 252). Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi tells a legend in which a Shaikh founded the town where a camel halted in the desert, a story he himself calls mythical in appearance (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 129, folio 111).

The books know the town through its holy families. Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti writes that the Sadat of Uch are famous, and that once they accept a man as of sound descent no one dares dispute it (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 526–527). Haroon Khalid saw on the mound the massive havelis of the shrine's caretakers, "the Gilanis and the Makhdooms" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 276, folio 250), and a family of *qawwals* at Kasur told Abbas that they came with Bulle Shah's family from Uch Sharif (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 112, folio not stated).

Khalid sets Uch Sharif where "the cultures of Punjab and Sindh merge", its language Siraiki (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 276, folio 250). He follows Guru Nanak there on his way to Sindh (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 278, folio 252), and was shown the abandoned well of Nanak and a grassy mound where the Gurdwara of Nanak had stood (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 286, folio 260). The river Sutlej, he writes, used to flow past the town before it was lost after the Indus Water Treaty of 1960 (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 281–282, folios 255–256).

## History

Rizvi records a story of the town's beginning, told in connection with Shaikh Safiuʾd-Din Kaziruni: an order to mount a camel, to travel in whatever direction the animal led, and to remain where the camel finally halted; the halt came "in the middle of a desert", and there "the Shaikh founded a town later called Uch" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 129, folio 111). Rizvi adds his own judgement, that "Although this story would appear mythical, it is typical of medieval tales relating to sufis" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 129, folio 111).

The Persian chronicle of Sujan Rai Bhandari meets Uch as a prize of campaigns. Shihab al-Din's first campaign took Uch from a people whose name is uncertain in the printed text, read as Hatahata, and left a deputy there; his second campaign came again to Multan and Uch and went on by way of the desert to Gujarat (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 405). Mirza Pir Muhammad, the chronicle says, crossed the Sind and took the fortress of Uch and Multan into his possession (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 333). Khawas Khan's pursuit went as far as Multan and Uch (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 285). In another passage of the same page, whose reading is uncertain in the printed text, a march from Thatta toward Maldeo's country went apparently by way of Uch, called a dependency of Multan, and then by Bikaner toward Jodhpur (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 285).

Schimmel ties the town to the Suhrawardiyya: "Ucch was, for a while, the center of the Suhrawardiyya, thanks chiefly to the active and pious Jalaluddin Makhdum-i Jahaniyan", "whom all the inhabitants of the world serve", who died in 1383 and was "a prolific writer in almost all religious fields" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354). Michel Boivin speaks of "one of the masters of the second Sohrawardiyya period, then based in Utch near Bahawalpur in Punjab" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 56, folio 43). Rozehnal calls Baha ad-Din Zakaria "the well-known Suhrawardi saint of Uch Sharif" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 57, folio 47). Omar Kasmani names Jalaluddin Surkhposh Bukhari, who died in 1291, as "of Uchh" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 17). Of one man Rizvi writes that "he went to Multan and Uch. At Uch it is possible that he was formally initiated into the Suhrawardiyya order as is suggested by local legends" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 337, folio 315).

The town was also a gate for the Qadiriyya. "It was in Ucch that the first missionaries of the Qadiriyya settled in the late fifteenth century; from there this order spread into the Subcontinent, where it soon gained a firm footing and was carried to Indonesia and Malaysia", Schimmel writes, without naming the missionaries (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354). Rozehnal gives the founder of the first Qadiriyya *khanaqah* at Uch as Shaykh Muhammad al-Husseini al-Jilani: "A native of Turkey, Shaykh Muhammad traveled to Khurasan and then Multan before settling with his family in Uch" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 259, folio 252). His eldest son was Shaykh 'Abd al-Qadir Jilani Thani (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 259, folio 252), who died in 1533 and is "the famous Qadiri saint of Uch Sharif", yet "there seems to be no extant archive of the saint, no textual record to document his life and legacy" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 210, folio 202).

Families went out from Uch and came into it. Chishti cites a genealogy of Hazrat Pir Muhammad Shah Sahib for the statement that the Gilani ancestor came from Uch Sharif in the reign of Akbar Shah (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 525). In the account that follows, the ancestor came to Multan, where a man gave him his daughter Fatima in marriage and presented him with the territory of Uch, the name uncertain in the printed text; he settled there, and his descendants, the book says, are settled there still, a phrase whose reading is also uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 526). Abbas writes of a man whose original name was Abdullah Shah that "his family were Syeds who had settled in Uch Gilanian, the city of saints in Bahawalpur, where Bulle Shah was born" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 107, folio not stated).

Khalid follows Guru Nanak to the town. Nanak's visit, he writes, took place on his way to Sindh, "after he had settled down" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 278, folio 252). In Khalid's reconstruction of the visit, "Uch Sharif was one of the most famous Muslim shrines in Southern Punjab and Nanak had arrived here only a day ago, travelling from Kartarpur" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 288, folio 262). He writes that "River Sutlej, the lost river of Pakistan, used to flow from here before it was lost forever after the Indus Water Treaty was signed between India and Pakistan in 1960", and that "Nanak must have seen these buildings intact with a mighty river flowing next to it" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 281–282, folios 255–256).

## A City of Saints

Abbas gives the town its epithet: Uch Gilanian is "the city of saints in Bahawalpur" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 107, folio not stated). Kasmani, in the note that names Jalaluddin Surkhposh Bukhari of Uchh, adds that "Their shrines are spread across southern Punjab in Pakistan" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 17).

Rozehnal came to "the nearby town of Uch Sharif" and "spent a day wandering through the magnificent blue and white tiled mosques and desolate tombs of this ancient center of learning, trade, and pilgrimage built on a hill overlooking the confluence of the Sutlej and Chenab rivers" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 21, folio 10).

Khalid describes the older city as standing on a mound, with massive havelis (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 276, folio 250). He reached the group of "thirteenth and fourteenth century shrines" through the market, and Iqbal Qaiser remarked: "This place has changed since the last time I came here" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 281, folio 255). On the mound he found several graves "which were covered arbitrarily with the blue and white Multani tiles that had been recovered from these historical shrines" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 285, folio not stated).

The books name particular tombs and mosques. Chishti writes that the four sons of Hazrat Muhammad Ghaus rest at Uch Sharif, beside the tomb of Shah Muhammad Ghaus (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 527). Of the town's mosques, Carl W. Ernst and Bruce B. Lawrence name the Hajj mosque, in whose well they place Baba Farid's inverted fast (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 168, folio 156), and Rizvi writes that "The Baba found a lonely mosque in Uch" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 158, folio 140).

There is a Sikh trace too, now almost gone. "Do you want to see the well of Nanak?", Iqbal Qaiser asked Khalid, adding "There is nothing left there anyway"; the well "was hidden within the field, shielded by a grove of trees. It was abandoned and juice tetrapaks and wrappers were floating in the black water", and beside it was a grassy mound: "This was the Gurdwara of Nanak", and "Now nothing remained" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 286, folio 260).

## Communities

At the centre of the books' Uch stand the Sayyids. Chishti writes that the Sadat of Uch are famous, and that where a dispute over someone's descent arises, once they accept a person as of sound descent no one dares say a word against it (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 526–527). He writes of one family, the Faqirs, that their first elders lived at Uch Sharif Sayyidan and served the Sadat of Uch (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 584).

Families and their musicians carried Uch elsewhere. Abbas records the words of Muhammad Bakhsh at Kasur: "We came with Bulle Shah's family from Uch Sharif, and we have learned his poetry from our ancestors, who have always sung here" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 112, folio not stated). In prose, too, her book has it that Bulle Shah's mentor Inayat Shah belonged to Uch city (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 104, folio not stated).

Khalid came to the town by road "through Bahawalpur, heading towards our destination at Uch Sharif", a place where "the cultures of Punjab and Sindh merge"; its language is Siraiki, which he could not understand (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 276, folio 250). Abbas likewise counts Bahawalpur within the Siraiki belt (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 148, folio not stated). Khalid writes that "The southern region of Punjab, where Uch Sharif lies, has more traces of religious and cultural syncretism compared to the northern and central region" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 277, folio 251).

The older stories tell of rivalry with the yogis. Rizvi observes that "all the stories dealing with three generations" of the Sufis he treats in Uch "recount conflicts with Hindu Yogis" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 304, folio 282), and he tells of a flying contest with a yogi at Uch (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 129–130, folios 111–112).

Bhandari's chronicle sets Uch on the edge of other peoples' lands. Describing the province of Thatta, it has the province run from the bounds of Multan and Uch to Thatta and Kach Makran; to the north lie high mountains of hard rock and the dwellings of Baloch tribes and some Afghans, and to the south, from Uch to Gujarat, hills of sand and the homelands of the Bhatti (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529).

## Custodians, Patrons and the State

Khalid writes of the older city on its mound with "massive havelis" belonging to the current caretakers of the shrine, "the Gilanis and the Makhdooms" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 276, folio 250). Chishti writes that some of the descendants of Abd al-Qadir Sani hold the *sajjada* in Uch (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 527).

In Chishti's account of the Gilani ancestor, the passage that follows, on the tomb of the hazrat there, is badly damaged in the printed text. As far as it can be read, it speaks of mosques and other buildings made there and kept flourishing, and of something confiscated in the time of Alamgir (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 526).

Rulers appear beside the saints. Rizvi speaks of "a Malik of Uch and Multan" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 172, folio 154), and Bhandari, in a passage whose reading is uncertain, calls Uch a dependency of Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 285). A Nawab, too, sought out the town's Sayyids, as told under Devotion and Practice below (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 584).

Of the wider district, Abbas writes that Bahawalpur, with Multan, is the seat of landowning Syeds who "support the shrines of their ancestors" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 168–169, folios not stated); that passage does not name Uch.

## Devotion and Practice

Uch was a place of austerities. Ernst and Lawrence write that Farid ad-Din performed the inverted fast of forty days, the *chilla-i ma'kus*, in the well of the Hajj mosque at Ucch: "For 40 days they would suspend him from a branch of the tree that overhung the well every night. At daybreak they would pull him out" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 168, folio 156).

It was a place of initiation. Local legends, Rizvi writes, suggest that one man who "went to Multan and Uch" may have been formally initiated into the Suhrawardiyya at Uch (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 337, folio 315).

Its Sayyids were sought for their prayers. Chishti tells of a Nawab, whose title the printed text gives uncertainly as Khan Bahadur, who had at Uch Sharif a boil on his cheek that would not heal, the word for the ailment also uncertain in print; he went to the Sadat of Uch Sharif for prayer, and they named Ghulam Muhyi-ud-Din, the name uncertain in print, as a skilled physician; the Nawab brought him to Lahore and was cured (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 584). Chishti attributes the story to Hazrat Shah Sardar Sahib, son of Hazrat Pir Muhammad Shah Sahib Gilani, and says his own account is given separately (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 584).

And its poetry travelled with its families. The *qawwals* of Kasur who came with Bulle Shah's family from Uch Sharif say that they learned his poetry from ancestors "who have always sung here" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 112, folio not stated). Abbas gives the words "Thou art an inhabitant of Uch city", explaining that Uch literally means high or great (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 104, folio not stated).

## The Place in the Books

Each book spells and places the town in its own way. Schimmel writes Ucch and puts it northeast of Multan (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354). Boivin writes "Utch near Bahawalpur in Punjab" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 56, folio 43), and his index gives it as Utch, in Bahawalpur, Punjab (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 251, folio 238). Kasmani writes Uchh (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 17). Abbas writes Uch city and Uch Gilanian (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 104, folio not stated; Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 107, folio not stated), Chishti Uch Sharif Sayyidan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 584), and Bhandari calls it the *qasba* of Uch, a small town (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527). Rizvi places Uch near Multan (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 258, folio 238), and Rozehnal seventy miles southwest of it (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 210, folio 202).

For Schimmel, Ucch is a station in the story of two orders: the centre of the Suhrawardiyya for a while, and the door by which the Qadiriyya entered the Subcontinent (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354). Boivin and Kasmani touch it once each, as the seat of a Suhrawardi master and the home of Jalaluddin Surkhposh Bukhari (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 56, folio 43; Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 17).

Rizvi gives Uch a section of its own (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 299–304, folios 277–282), in which, he observes, all the stories of three generations of Sufis there recount conflicts with Hindu yogis (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 304, folio 282). He treats its founding story with caution, as mythical in appearance but typical of medieval tales of Sufis (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 129, folio 111), and returns to Uch in his conclusion, where it figures in a statement about the economy (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 421, folio not stated).

Rozehnal saw the town as a visitor, wandering for a day among its tiled mosques and desolate tombs (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 21, folio 10). He notes the absence of any archive for its famous Qadiri saint (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 210, folio 202), and for the town points his readers to Mas'ud Hasan Shahab's *Khitta-i pak Uch* (Bahawalpur: Urdu Academy, 1967/1993) and to the second volume of Rizvi's history (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 259, folio 252).

Khalid's Uch is a stage on Nanak's road, seen in the company of Iqbal Qaiser: a town of old shrines reached through a market, with a lost river and a vanished gurdwara (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 281, folio 255; Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 281–282, folios 255–256; Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 286, folio 260). Earlier in the book Uch Sharif is named, and no more, on the caravanserai route (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 119, folio 101).

Chishti's Uch is the home of Sayyid families, the place a Gilani line came from, the home of the elders of a family who served the Sayyids, and of Sayyids to whom a Nawab turned for prayer (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 525; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 584). Bhandari's is a town of campaigns and boundaries, taken and passed through by armies and marking the edge of the province of Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 405; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529). For Ernst and Lawrence it is the town of the Hajj mosque where Baba Farid hung in the well (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 168, folio 156), and for Abbas it is the city of saints from which a poet's family and its singers came (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 107, folio not stated; Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 112, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **Where Uch lies from Multan.** Schimmel places Ucch northeast of Multan (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354). Rozehnal places it "seventy miles southwest of Multan in Pakistan" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 210, folio 202). Rizvi says only that it is near Multan (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 258, folio 238).
- **When the Qadiriyya came.** In one passage Schimmel writes that the first missionaries of the Qadiriyya settled in Ucch in the late fifteenth century and that the order spread from there into the Subcontinent (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354); in another she writes that "the order was introduced in the late fourteenth century" in the Subcontinent, naming no place (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 278, folio 247). Rozehnal gives no date, but names Shaykh Muhammad al-Husseini al-Jilani as the founder of the first Qadiriyya *khanaqah* at Uch (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 259, folio 252).
- **The rivers at Uch.** Rozehnal describes the town, as he saw it, on a hill overlooking the confluence of the Sutlej and Chenab rivers (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 21, folio 10). Khalid writes that the Sutlej used to flow from there "before it was lost forever after the Indus Water Treaty was signed between India and Pakistan in 1960" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 281–282, folios 255–256).
