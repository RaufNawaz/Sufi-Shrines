## Overview

Haroon Khalid writes that "The boundary between Sindh and Punjab becomes a blur as we drive through Bahawalpur" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 276, folio 250), and Shemeem Burney Abbas counts Bahawalpur within the Siraiki belt (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 148, folio not stated). Abbas calls Uch Gilanian "the city of saints in Bahawalpur" and names Bahawalpur, with Multan, as the seat of landowning Syeds who "support the shrines of their ancestors" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 107, folio not stated; Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 168–169, folios not stated). In Khalid's account of Hindu festivals Bahawalpur is "an area with a considerable population of Hindus", from which musicians, boys and pilgrims travel out to festivals and holy places (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 31, folio not stated; Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 37, folio not stated; Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated). For Robert Rozehnal the city is a base for fieldwork, and its Nawab a patron of Wahid Bakhsh (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 26, folio 15; Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, pp. 72, 74, 87, 88, folios 62, 64, 77, 78).

## Between Punjab and Sindh

Khalid finds Bahawalpur on the edge of two provinces. On the road, he writes: "The boundary between Sindh and Punjab becomes a blur as we drive through Bahawalpur" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 276, folio 250). Abbas counts Bahawalpur as a point in the Siraiki belt (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 148, folio not stated).

Rozehnal measures the countryside of southern Punjab from the city: he places the rural village of Allahabad sixty miles southwest of the city of Bahawalpur, in southern Punjab (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 21, folio 10).

## Saints, Syeds and Patrons

The one holy place the books set expressly in Bahawalpur is Uch Gilanian. Abbas writes of a family of Syeds "who had settled in Uch Gilanian, the city of saints in Bahawalpur, where Bulle Shah was born" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 107, folio not stated).

In Abbas's account the Syeds also sustain shrines. Bahawalpur, she writes, is with Multan the seat of the landowning Syeds who "support the shrines of their ancestors" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 168–169, folios not stated).

A Nawab of Bahawalpur appears as a patron too. Rozehnal names "the local Nawab of Bahawalpur" as a patron of Wahid Bakhsh, behind his commission, his schooling and his post in the Civil Secretariat (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, pp. 72, 74, 87, 88, folios 62, 64, 77, 78).

## Communities and Festivals

Khalid's *A White Trail* knows Bahawalpur through its Hindus. Organisers at Bahawalnagar, he writes, "called a band of musicians from Bahawalpur, an area with a considerable population of Hindus, about one hundred and sixty five kilometres from here" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 31, folio not stated).

Boys from Bahawalpur came to the festivals as well. Twelve boys on bicycles came to Navratri from Bahawalpur, chanting to the Mata as they came (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 37, folio not stated).

Its Hindus went on pilgrimage too. On the way to Katas, Khalid writes, buses of Hindu pilgrims "from Rahim Yaar Khan and Bahawalpur are also on their way" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated).

## The Place in the Books

Each book sees Bahawalpur from a different road. In *A White Trail* Khalid sees it as a home of Hindus who travel out: a band of musicians called to Bahawalnagar, boys cycling to Navratri, buses bound for Katas (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 31, folio not stated; Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 37, folio not stated; Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* it is a stretch of road where the provinces blur (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 276, folio 250), and the home of Ayesha Siddiqa, who "belongs to Bahawalpur, which is only about seventy kilometres from here" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 284, folio 258).

Rozehnal knows the city as a scholar at work. It is a base for his fieldwork (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 26, folio 15), and he dates interviews held there to 28, 29 and 30 April and 2 May 2001 (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, pp. 79, 80, 93, 174, 191, 220, folios 69, 70, 83, 166, 183, 212). He measures the village of Allahabad from it (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 21, folio 10), names its Nawab as a patron (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, pp. 72, 74, 87, 88, folios 62, 64, 77, 78), and, among the further reading he gives on Uch, cites Mas'ud Hasan Shahab's *Khitta-i pak Uch*, with the imprint Bahawalpur: Urdu Academy, 1967/1993 (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 259, folio 252).

For Abbas, Bahawalpur belongs to the Siraiki belt (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 148, folio not stated), to the landowning Syeds who support their ancestors' shrines (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 168–169, folios not stated), and to the city of saints at Uch Gilanian (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 107, folio not stated).
