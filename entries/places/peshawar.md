## Overview

Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian chronicle calls Peshawar an ancient city, which the books of earlier writers also write as Parshawar and Farshawar (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 503). Haroon Khalid places it as "a city on the western border of Pakistan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 161, folio not stated).

Bhandari dates Sultan Mahmud's first campaign to the year 391, when Raja Jaipal set out to oppose him and, reaching Peshawar, drew up his army for battle (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 424). From the city of Peshawar, Bhandari writes, three roads go to Kabul (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 502; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 501). The whole *tuman* around it, he writes, is the home of the Mohmand Afghans and of others, who pay revenue and live as subjects (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 504–503).

Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi writes that "From the eleventh century the Nath yogis began to spread throughout northern India, and from their centre at Peshawar moved to all parts of Central Asia and Iran, at the same time influencing both qalandars and sufis" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 355, folio 333). A woman disciple from "the northwest frontier town of Peshawar", contrasting her home region with Pakpattan, said in Robert Rozehnal's account: "In the frontier, we have so many shrines where you can walk into the saint's shrines" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 219, folio 211).

Carl W. Ernst and Bruce B. Lawrence name Peshawar as one of four cities, with Lahore, Hyderabad in Sind and Karachi, where Zauqi Shah, a Chishti of the twentieth century, worked for newspapers (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 139, folio 126). Rozehnal writes that Zauqi Shah's *Sirr-i dilbaran*, a "comprehensive encyclopedia of Sufi terminology", was "Begun in Peshawar in 1923", compiled over a number of years, first published by the *silsila* in 1951, and is now in its fifth edition, of 1985 (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 106, folio 97).

Sikh refugees from the tribal areas, Khalid writes, "moved to Peshawar, Hassan Abdal and Nankana Sahib, and started living at Gurdwaras, just like the migrants before them" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 175, folio not stated). In 2011, he reports, "Hindu pilgrims from Peshawar have arrived in two large buses" at Katas, about a hundred people (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated). An informant he calls Prem Gupta told him: "If you want to see the lights of Diwali, go to Peshawar" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated).

## History

Bhandari's chronicle gives the city its older names twice. In one passage it speaks of Peshawar, which the books of the ancients write as Farshawar, Parshawar and Bagram; the editor's note on the name Farshawar cannot be read in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 424). Elsewhere it says that Peshawar is an ancient city, which the books of earlier writers also write as Parshawar and Farshawar (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 503).

The books set a battle at Peshawar. Bhandari dates Sultan Mahmud's first campaign to the year 391, when Raja Jaipal set out to oppose him and, reaching Peshawar, drew up his army for battle (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 424). Five thousand of the unbelievers, as the chronicle calls them, were put to the sword in that battle, and the chronicle as printed has Raja Jaipal taken captive with fifteen of his sons, brothers and kin, and put to death (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 424). Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti tells the same meeting in brief: in the month of August Sultan Mahmud came to Peshawar with a force of ten thousand horsemen, faced Raja Jaipal and made him prisoner (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 805).

Bhandari also names the city as a seat of command. Dara, he writes, sent robes of honour to the armies of Peshawar and Kabul, where Mahabat Khan held the governorship (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 77). He records a march as well: after the fort of Attock had been laid out, the court turned toward Peshawar, and from that stage a *farman* of counsel was sent to Hakim Mirza (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 201).

Nur Ahmad Chishti mentions Ranjit Singh's conquest of Peshawar only as the occasion on which a Qur'an was sent from there to a shrine (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 169).

In the twentieth century the city has a place in the story of the Chishti Sabiri order. Carl W. Ernst and Bruce B. Lawrence name Peshawar as one of four cities, with Lahore, Hyderabad in Sind and Karachi, where Zauqi Shah, a Chishti of the twentieth century, worked for newspapers (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 139, folio 126). Rozehnal has Zauqi Shah move "between Agra, Jaipur, Bombay, Peshawar, Hyderabad, Ajmer, and, finally, Karachi" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 62, folio 52). He writes that Zauqi Shah's *Sirr-i dilbaran*, a "comprehensive encyclopedia of Sufi terminology", was "Begun in Peshawar in 1923", compiled over a number of years, first published by the *silsila* in 1951, and is now in its fifth edition, of 1985 (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 106, folio 97). Rozehnal also places Shahidullah Faridi in Peshawar (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 77, folio 67).

## The City and Its Country

Bhandari describes the country around the city. The *tuman* known as Peshawar, under a name whose reading is uncertain in the printed text, lies toward Hindustan; its grapes, peaches and melons are like those of Turan, and its heat and cold, its seasons and its rain like those of Hindustan; a rice grown there, whose name is uncertain in the printed text, is better than the rice of Bengal; and crops and grain are plentiful (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 504).

From the city of Peshawar, Bhandari writes, three roads go to Kabul (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 502; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 501). The first is the Nikshab road, with many hard defiles and a long way to go, which armies pass only with difficulty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 502). The second, whose name is uncertain in the printed text, joins the highway at Jalalabad; its defiles are narrow, its passes hard and its water scarce, and Afghans raid it (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 502). The third is the road of Ali Masjid and the Khyber. It runs eighteen *kos* from the spring of Jamshid, known as Jamrud, to a place on the bank of the Nilab whose name is uncertain in the printed text; the Khyber is hard going for two *kos*, yet this road is easier than the others, and armies and caravans take it; beyond, it runs thirty *kos* without hills between two places whose printed names are uncertain, and forty *kos* from the second of them to Kabul, with passes that are not very hard (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 501).

## Holy Places

Rizvi makes Peshawar the centre of the Nath yogis: "From the eleventh century the Nath yogis began to spread throughout northern India, and from their centre at Peshawar moved to all parts of Central Asia and Iran, at the same time influencing both qalandars and sufis" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 355, folio 333). He writes of "a place called Gorakhtari", a name he gives as "either 'Gorkhattari' or perhaps 'Gorakhtari'", and adds that "McLeod rightly locates the site in Peshawar" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 413, folio 389). Among his plates is one captioned "Babur visiting Gorkhattri" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 392, folio not stated).

The shrines of the frontier, in one woman's account, are open to women. A woman disciple from "the northwest frontier town of Peshawar", contrasting her home region with Pakpattan, said in Robert Rozehnal's account: "In the frontier, we have so many shrines where you can walk into the saint's shrines" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 219, folio 211). Her words set the frontier, where women may walk into the saint's sanctum, against Pakpattan, where they may not; she names no shrine in Peshawar itself (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 219, folio 211).

## Communities

Bhandari's Peshawar is a country of Afghans: the whole *tuman* around it, he writes, is the home of the Mohmand Afghans and of others, who pay revenue and live as subjects (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 504–503).

Khalid's Peshawar is a city to which Sikhs move. The elders of Surjeet Singh, as Khalid names him, moved to the city from a village in Khyber Agency "for better economic opportunities" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 161, folio not stated). After the attacks of 1971, an informant Khalid calls Tarvinder Kaur told him, her family "fled to Peshawar, where there was a considerable Sikh population", and stayed "for a few months" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 184, folio not stated). Sikh refugees from the tribal areas, Khalid writes, "moved to Peshawar, Hassan Abdal and Nankana Sahib, and started living at Gurdwaras, just like the migrants before them" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 175, folio not stated).

The city's Hindus appear in Khalid as pilgrims who travel from it: Hindus from Peshawar came to Katas in 2010 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 47, folio not stated), and again, about a hundred of them, in 2011 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated). At Peshawar University, Khalid writes, Diyal, as he gives the name, "teaches comparative religion" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 50, folio not stated).

## Festivals and Practice

Khalid records one festival as a reason to go to Peshawar. An informant he calls Prem Gupta told him: "If you want to see the lights of Diwali, go to Peshawar" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated).

The city's Hindus also travel as pilgrims. Hindus from Peshawar came to Katas in 2010 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 47, folio not stated). In 2011, Khalid reports, "Hindu pilgrims from Peshawar have arrived in two large buses" at Katas, about a hundred people (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated). Of a musical band Khalid writes that its members "have also come from Peshawar" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 51, folio not stated).

## The Place in the Books

Most of the books name Peshawar in passing. Annemarie Schimmel never discusses the city in *As Through a Veil*; it appears there only through the word Peshawari, in her paraphrase of the monism of folk poetry: "there is no longer Turk, Hindu, or Peshawari" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 176, folio 158). In *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* the same word stands in a like line, her paraphrase of the boldest Panjabi and Sindhi mystical verse: "the Sufi is no longer Arab, Hindu, Turk, or Peshawari; eventually Hallaj and the judge who condemned him, the lover and the theologian, are seen as nothing but different manifestations of the one divine reality" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 417, folio 386). That book says nothing else about Peshawar; the city's own name occurs in it only as the place where a Pashto selection of Khushhal Khan Khattak was published in 1958 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 483, folio 452). In both books Peshawari stands beside Hindu and Turk as a distinction that the mystical verse she paraphrases does away with (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 176, folio 158; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 417, folio 386).

For Ernst and Lawrence the city is one of the places where Zauqi Shah worked for newspapers, and their book says nothing else about it (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 139, folio 126). For Rozehnal it is a stage in Zauqi Shah's travels and the place where his lexicon of Sufi terms was begun (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 62, folio 52; Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 106, folio 97), and, in the words of a woman disciple, a frontier town in a region with "so many shrines where you can walk into the saint's shrines" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 219, folio 211).

Harish Dhillon's retelling of the *janamsakhis* names the city as a stage on the return from Baghdad: "He visited Kabul before reaching Peshawar" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 224, folio 246). In Jürgen Schaflechner's study of Hinglaj Devi it is named once, and only as the home of robbers in Abadhut's novel (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 128–129, folios not stated).

Among the older histories, Bhandari's chronicle gives the city the most room: its ancient names, its battle, its armies, its roads to Kabul, and its fruits and rice (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 424; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 503; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 77; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 502; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 501; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 504). Nur Ahmad Chishti names it for Sultan Mahmud's battle with Raja Jaipal and for Ranjit Singh's conquest (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 805; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 169). Rizvi sees it as the centre from which the Nath yogis spread (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 355, folio 333).

Khalid, whose book is a journey among Pakistan's religious minorities, places it as "a city on the western border of Pakistan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 161, folio not stated), and as the place where the lights of Diwali are to be seen (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated). It is where Sikh families went for work and for safety (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 161, folio not stated; Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 184, folio not stated), and where Hindu pilgrims set out from (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **What became of Raja Jaipal at Peshawar.** Bhandari's chronicle, as printed, has Raja Jaipal taken captive with fifteen of his sons, brothers and kin, and put to death (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 424). Nur Ahmad Chishti has Sultan Mahmud make him prisoner (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 805).
- **The city's older names.** In one passage Bhandari writes that the books of the ancients give Peshawar as Farshawar, Parshawar and Bagram (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 424); in another, that the books of earlier writers also write it as Parshawar and Farshawar (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 503).
- **The name of Gorakhtari.** Rizvi writes of "a place called Gorakhtari" and gives the name as "either 'Gorkhattari' or perhaps 'Gorakhtari'" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 413, folio 389); his plate is captioned "Babur visiting Gorkhattri" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 392, folio not stated), and his list of illustrations gives "Gorakhattri" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 10, folio not stated).
