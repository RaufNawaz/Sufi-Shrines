## Overview

Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian chronicle sets Sialkot in the Rachna Doab and calls it a city of the ancients (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). Harish Dhillon calls it "a bustling city that was situated in the foothills of the Himalayas", and writes that its earliest recorded mention is in ancient Greek texts, where it is called Sagala (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 136, folio 151). The books give it legendary founders: Bhandari a Raja Shal and a Raja Salbahan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517), Dhillon a Raja Sela, "the uncle of the Pandavas" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 136, folio 151), and Haroon Khalid Raja Salivahan, father of Puran Bhagat (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 184, folio 158).

Bhandari writes that in former times Sialkot was the seat of government of the province of the Panjab (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). His chronicle follows the fort through Shihab al-Din's repairs and Khusraw Malik's siege (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 404), records an order of Babur's that the town be laid waste (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 304), and names the Mughal governors who rebuilt the fort and improved the city (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). Dhillon, by contrast, writes that under Babur "Sialkot had offered no resistance and had been spared the usual bloody massacre and plunder" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 224, folio 246).

Annemarie Schimmel calls it "one of the centers of Islamic scholarship during the Mogul period" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 398, folio 367), and Bhandari calls it an abode of learning and a gathering place of scholars, naming the teachers who taught there under Akbar and Shah Jahan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 516).

Khalid writes of the well of Puran Bhagat, around which devotees raised "the most important temple in the ancient city of Sialkot" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 184, folio 158), and Nur Ahmad Chishti relates that the well is still there (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 554). In Dhillon's retelling of the *janamsakhis*, Guru Nanak's third *udasi* starts and ends at Sialkot (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 191, folio 210). Dhillon writes that "many Sufi missionaries settled here", making the region a predominantly Muslim one (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 137, folio 152), and Khalid counts it among places of "contested history", where there used to be a major Sikh shrine (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 267, folio 241).

Bhandari praises its paper, its silk embroidery, which he says brings in close to a lakh of rupees a year, and its blades (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). He tells how all the people of the city, great and small, play in its stream when it floods in the rains (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 517–516). Khalid finds a city "right on the border" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 193, folio 167) that "has become one of the most important cities in Pakistan", whose business community financed its own airport (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 191, folio 165).

## History

Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian chronicle gives Sialkot two older names and two founders. The city, he writes, was called Shalkot, and its founding is attributed to a Raja Shal, whose name and whose tie to the Pandavas are uncertain in the printed text; the city and the raja, he adds, are mentioned in the Mahabharat, of whose composition nearly five thousand years have passed (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). It is also called Salkot and attributed to Raja Salbahan, and a fort of baked brick, Bhandari writes, is his memorial (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). The editor's note on the name Shalkot cannot be read in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517).

Harish Dhillon relates that legend has the city "founded by Raja Sela, the uncle of the Pandavas", and rebuilt by "Raja Satya Vachan, when it became part of the kingdom of Kashmir" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 136, folio 151). "The earliest recorded mention of the city", he writes, "is found in ancient Greek texts going as far back as 322 B.C., where it is referred to as Sagala" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 136, folio 151). Haroon Khalid writes that "Puran Bhagat was the son of the King of Sialkot, Raja Salivahan", and that "It is believed that Sialkot was founded by this king" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 184, folio 158). In Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's telling of the Puran legend, Raja Salwan, as the uncertain printed name reads, held the throne of Sialkot (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 553).

Bhandari writes that in former times Sialkot was the seat of government of the province of the Panjab, with habitation stretching over two or three *kos* (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). Known now as Sialkot, he writes, it exceeds all the towns of the province in population (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). In another passage he explains that after Lahore fell into ruin in early times the city of Sialkot became the seat of government of the province, until Sultan Mahmud's conquest and Malik Ayaz's rebuilding of Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525).

The chronicle tells of Shihab al-Din's fifth campaign: between the Ravi and the Chenab he renewed and repaired the fort of Sialkot, whose foundation is old and whose pillars had fallen into disrepair, left his deputy there and turned back (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 404). Khusraw Malik, with the Khokhar tribe, then besieged the fort of Sialkot for a time and went back to Lahore without gaining his end (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 404). In his description of the province Bhandari dates Shihab al-Din's coming to the year 580, a year whose editor's notes are partly illegible: having failed at Lahore, he came to Sialkot, repaired and rebuilt the old fort and left his army there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). Dhillon writes that "Sialkot became a part of the Muslim Sultanate when Shahabuddin Muhammad Ghori captured Punjab in 1192", and that a garrison was left there (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 136, folio 151).

Bhandari records that Shahi Khan, later Sultan Zain al-Abidin, came out of Kashmir to Sialkot, joined Jasrath Khokhar, whose printed name is uncertain and who had escaped from Timur's custody in the Punjab, and marched against Ali Shah (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 191).

Babur's wars reach the town in both books. In Bhandari's account Daulat Khan and Ghazi Khan gathered forty thousand horse and foot, took Kalanaur, and seized Sialkot as well from the emperor's men (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 304). Babur camped on the Chenab near the town of Bahlolpur, and an order was given that Sialkot be laid waste and its inhabitants settled in Bahlolpur (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 304). Alam Khan and others, having reached a place whose printed name is uncertain, heard that the royal standards had arrived near Sialkot and came over; from Sialkot Babur went on by way of Pasrur, as the uncertain printed name reads, to Kalanaur (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 304). Dhillon writes that during "the Mughal Emperor Babur's third incursion into India", "Sialkot had offered no resistance and had been spared the usual bloody massacre and plunder" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 224, folio 246).

Under Akbar, Bhandari writes, the land later made the *pargana* of Gujrat had been part of Sialkot (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 177). On the Kashmir journey of the year 42, the subjects of a *tappa* dependent on Sialkot, whose printed name is uncertain, complained of Muhammad Beg, a *karori*, and a dagger was drawn as a lesson to others from the deeds of an oppressor; this seems to mean that he was put to death, but the reading is not certain (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 177).

The Mughal governors kept up the fort. Raja Man Singh of Akbar's time, *faujdar* of Jammu and *jagirdar* of Sialkot, repaired the fort and improved the city; after him Safdar Khan of Jahangir's time, also *faujdar* of Jammu and holding this *pargana* in *jagir*, rebuilt the fort and its tower; and most of the governors after them made repairs too (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). Elsewhere the chronicle names Kunwar Man Singh as *faujdar* of Sialkot at the time he relieved the fort of Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 203).

## The City and Its Country

Bhandari sets the city in the Rachna Doab and calls it a city of the ancients (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). Dhillon calls it "a bustling city that was situated in the foothills of the Himalayas" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 136, folio 151). For Khalid it is the city in the foothills of Jammu, the name being uncertain in the printed text (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 185, folio 159), and he writes, in a passage whose printed text is uncertain, that the mountains of Jammu can be seen from the rooftops of the city (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 194, folio 168).

Khalid stresses the border. "Sialkot is located right on the border", he writes, and "Historically, Sialkot provided Punjab with a connection to Kashmir" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 193, folio 167). Days after his visit, he reports, firing on the LOC was reported, with shells falling, in a phrase whose printed text is uncertain, on many villages in the Sialkot district (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 193, folio 167). He found the fields flooded at the start of the monsoon, which Iqbal Qaiser put down to India releasing water from its dams (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 193, folio 167).

Bhandari also measures the country around from the city. Dhonkal, as the uncertain printed name reads, lies twelve *kos* from Sialkot, and Purmandal, also uncertainly read, fifteen (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 516); the Chenab passes the villages of the *pargana* of Sialkot (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 512); and the villages of Gujrat were taken out of the *pargana* of Sialkot (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 515). Khalid notes that "Sialkot is not very far from Eminabad" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 189, folio not stated), and that Pasrur lies "About thirty kilometres from Sialkot", on "perhaps one of the worst roads" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 199–200, folios 173–174).

A stream, whose name is uncertain in the printed text, rises in the hills of Jammu and flows below the city, Bhandari writes; ten *kos* beyond, where the lie of the land is uncertain in the printed text, it takes another name, also uncertainly read, and spreads out until it loses its name (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). When it floods in the rains, he writes, all the people of Sialkot, great and small, play in the water, naked but for a loincloth and carrying leather water skins, and anyone from the city who is far away remembers it with pleasure (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 517–516).

Gardens surround the city, Bhandari writes, above all the garden of Nazar Muhammad, the rest of whose name is uncertain in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). He finds the buildings of the *qanungos* of a people whose printed name is uncertain, and of some others, exceedingly pleasing and delightful (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517).

The city's crafts fill much of Bhandari's account. Its paper, the Mansinghi and the kinds called *hariri* and *khassa jahangiri*, is white and durable and is sent to other regions (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). Its *karchikan* embroidery in silk and *kalabatun* is worked on the *futa*, the *suzani*, the *dastarkhwan* and the *khwanposh*, with one more item whose printed name is uncertain; every year close to a lakh of rupees, he writes, comes in from the buying and selling of this embroidery, and it goes all over the world (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). The *jamdhar*, the *katari* and the *barchhi* are well made there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). Gujrat, he notes, produces more embroidery than Sialkot (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 514). Dhillon calls the city "famous for its silk" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 136, folio 151), and "an important post on the trade route" between West Asia and Delhi (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 137, folio 152).

Khalid describes the city of his own day. He and his companions took "the newly constructed motorway that connects the Grand Trunk Road with Sialkot", and he adds that "Nanak too must have followed a similar route" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 191, folio 165). "Sialkot has become one of the most important cities in Pakistan", he writes; its business community "financed its own airport, which now handles international flights", and the highway as well (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 191, folio 165). On the way he saw several factories and a lot of mounds, "probably archaeological ruins, converted into graveyards" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 191, folio 165).

## A Seat of Learning

Annemarie Schimmel writes of Ahmad Sirhindi that "Ahmad had studied in Sialkot, one of the centers of Islamic scholarship during the Mogul period" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 398, folio 367).

Bhandari praises the city in the same terms: it is an abode of learning, a gathering place of scholars, a mine of excellence and a dwelling of the learned (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 516). Under Akbar, he writes, Maulana Kamal al-Din Husain, aggrieved with the Khan who ruled Kashmir, came to Sialkot in the year 971 of the Hijra and established teaching there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 516). In an earlier passage he tells the same scholar's coming: in the reign of Husain Khan of Kashmir, Maulana Kamal, most excellent of the excellent and most perfect of scholars, famous in his day for the excellence of his dervish life, left Kashmir, reached Sialkot and gave himself to teaching (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 187). He became famous in Hindustan, Bhandari writes, for his excellence in the knowledge and worship of God, though some words of the passage are uncertain in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 187).

Under Shah Jahan, Bhandari writes, Maulvi Abd al-Hakim spread the sciences in Sialkot and wrote glosses, and students came to his blessed madrasa (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 516). His son Maulvi Abdullah, whom the printed text makes his second son with some uncertainty, ran the madrasa after him, was called the imam of the age, and died in the year 26 of Alamgir, a numeral uncertain both in the text and in the editor's note (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 516).

Khalid names a Sikh writer of the city: he writes that the author of *Tarikh Guru Khalsa*, a Bhai whose name is uncertain in the printed text, "belonged to Sialkot" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 203, folio 177).

## Holy Places and Legends

The holy place the books tell most of is the well of Puran Bhagat. Khalid writes that "The well of Puran Bhagat was at a little distance from the city of Sialkot", and that devotees raised a temple around it, "the most important temple in the ancient city of Sialkot" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 184, folio 158). Chishti relates that the well into which Puran was thrown, his hands and feet cut off, is still there at Sialkot (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 554). In his telling Puran later came to Sialkot at a command, and a garden that had dried up revived on his arrival (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 557).

Guru Nanak's journeys run through the city in the *janamsakhi* tradition that Dhillon retells. In the Puratan account the first *udasi* ends at Saidpur, Pasrur and Sialkot (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 54, folio 60), and the third *udasi* both starts and ends at Sialkot (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 191, folio 210). Dhillon sets the curse of the faqir Hamza Ghaus in "the centre of the market square" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 150, folio 165), and closes the episode with almost the entire citizenry of Sialkot there to see him on his way (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 159, folio 174).

Khalid tells the same legend from his travels. Sialkot, he writes, "was gripped by fear" of Hamza Ghous, who told "the people of Sialkot" he would pray for forty days; the quarrel began when a Hindu man broke a promise to give his firstborn son (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 197–198, folios 171–172). He tells too of Maula Karar, "one vendor in the city of Sialkot", who told Nanak of "the various shrines here" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 188–189, folio 162 / folio not stated). In the story, Karar hid from Nanak, was bitten by a snake that was "escaping the harsh winter outside" and "died on the spot", and Nanak and Mardana took part in his final rites (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 190, folio 164).

The city appears in the life of a Sufi as well. In *Embodying Charisma*, Shah Daulah is said to have left the city: "Due to some disputes with the local people of Sialkot, he migrated from there and, after some years, moved to Gujrat" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 205, folio 194). Bhandari writes that Shah Daula's first master lived in Sialkot (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 515).

## Communities

Dhillon writes that "many Sufi missionaries settled here", which made the region a predominantly Muslim one, "giving it a rich Sufi tradition" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 137, folio 152). The Sialkot of his retelling of Guru Nanak's time is a city inhabited predominantly by Muslims, with a powerful Hindu trading presence (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 137, folio 152).

Khalid reads the city's past as contested. The words "Allah O Akbar" set on barren rocks, he writes, are "more prevalent in places where there is a contested history, like in Sialkot", where there used to be a major Sikh shrine (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 267, folio 241).

## The Place in the Books

Bhandari's chronicle gives the city the most room. In its description of the province it sets out Sialkot's names and founders, its time as a seat of government, its fort and governors, its gardens and buildings, its paper, embroidery and blades, its stream and its scholars (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 516). Elsewhere the chronicle meets the town in campaigns and in the offices of the men who held it (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 404; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 304; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 203).

Dhillon's retelling of the *janamsakhis* gives the city a short history of its own, from its legendary founding to the Sultanate and the Sufis, as the setting for Guru Nanak's visits (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 136, folio 151; Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 137, folio 152). Khalid, following Nanak's route in his own day, sees a border city of factories and an airport, whose past is contested (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 191, folio 165; Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 193, folio 167; Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 267, folio 241).

For Nur Ahmad Chishti the city is the scene of the Puran legend, and he names it only there (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 553; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 554; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 557). For Schimmel it is a centre of scholarship under the Mughals, in a single sentence about Ahmad Sirhindi (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 398, folio 367). *Embodying Charisma* names it once, as the place Shah Daulah left (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 205, folio 194). Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi names the town once, in Guru Nanak's itinerary, and says nothing else about it (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 409, folio 385).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **Who founded Sialkot.** Bhandari attributes the city, as Shalkot, to a Raja Shal tied to the Pandavas, and, as Salkot, to Raja Salbahan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). Dhillon relates the legend that it was founded by Raja Sela, "the uncle of the Pandavas", and rebuilt by Raja Satya Vachan (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 136, folio 151). Khalid writes that it is believed to have been founded by Raja Salivahan, father of Puran Bhagat (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 184, folio 158). Chishti names the raja who held its throne in the Puran legend as Raja Salwan, an uncertain reading (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 553).
- **When and how Shihab al-Din took Sialkot.** Bhandari has him repair the old fort and leave his deputy there on his fifth campaign (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 404), and, in his description of the province, come to Sialkot in the year 580 after failing at Lahore and leave his army there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). Dhillon writes that Sialkot became part of the Sultanate when Ghori captured Punjab in 1192, a garrison being left there (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 136, folio 151).
- **Sialkot under Babur.** Bhandari records an order that Sialkot be laid waste and its inhabitants settled in Bahlolpur (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 304). Dhillon writes that on Babur's third incursion Sialkot "had offered no resistance and had been spared the usual bloody massacre and plunder" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 224, folio 246). Neither passage says whether it speaks of the same campaign as the other.
- **Man Singh's office.** In one passage Bhandari makes Raja Man Singh *faujdar* of Jammu and *jagirdar* of Sialkot (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517); in another he makes Kunwar Man Singh *faujdar* of Sialkot (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 203).
