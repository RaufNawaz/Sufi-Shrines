## Overview

The shrine of Baba Shah Jamal stands in the Lahore locality that now carries his name, beside Ichhra and opposite Forman Christian College. It honours Syed Shah Jamal, a Sufi of Hussaini Syed descent who lived in Lahore in the reign of the Mughal emperor Akbar, and the site is known as his *Damdama*, the building he is said to have raised and named in his own lifetime. Today the shrine is famous above all for its Thursday nights, when dhol drummers drive crowds of devotees, students, musicians and curious visitors into hours of *dhamal*, ecstatic devotional dancing to the beat of the drum. That weekly spectacle has made Shah Jamal one of the most written about expressions of popular Sufi devotion in the Punjab.

The place has long been famous. In the nineteenth century the tomb was already known as the *Damdama*, the story of its sinking tower was already told, and the shrine had its keepers, its offerings and an annual festival.

## The Saint

Syed Shah Jamal was a Hussaini Syed, a descendant of the Prophet, and the shrine traces his ancestry to Jalaluddin Surkh-Posh Bukhari of Uch Sharif; his full name is given as Syed Shah Jamal Uddin Naqvi Bukhari. His father is remembered as Syed Ahmad Shah, and the family is said to have moved from Kashmir to Sialkot and from Sialkot to Lahore, where Shah Jamal and his brother were both born. The link with Sialkot lasted: in the 1870s his descendants were still said to be living there.

He was one of two brothers who are both counted among the saints of Lahore. His brother, Baba Shah Kamal, is buried close by, and Shah Jamal is said to have come to Ichhra to see him and to have pitched his camp a short distance from his brother's house. Shah Kamal, like his brother, was remembered as devout and ascetic, a man of spiritual rapture and devotional practice, and the marvels of both were widely known. In the nineteenth century Shah Kamal's tomb stood right beside the lodge, or *khanqah*, of Shah Jamal.

Tradition places Shah Jamal's birth in 1587, and he lived in Lahore under Akbar. He is remembered for resisting the emperor's syncretic creed, the Din-i-Ilahi, and for working instead to draw people back towards orthodox Islamic practice. In his lifetime many came to him in search of blessing. Devotees know him for his open hand as *Sakhi Sarwar*, the generous lord, a title that in the Punjab more usually belongs to Sultan Sakhi Sarwar of Dera Ghazi Khan.

There is no settled view of which Sufi order he belonged to; he is most often counted a Suhrawardi, though he is also remembered as a Qadiri. Accounts differ over who his own master was and how his spiritual chain, or *silsila*, ran; the shrine remembers him as a special disciple of Sheikh Khokar Baig Suhrawardi, and his Suhrawardi line is most often traced back to Bahauddin Zakariya of Multan. He is also counted with Shah Hussain and Mian Mir among the great saints of Lahore with whom the city's Qalandars, wandering dervishes, are still associated.

Among his disciples was Hassu Teli of Lahore, Shah Jamal's *murid*, his pledged disciple. Shah Jamal is also remembered as the guide of a Lahore grain merchant whose weights he set right. The shrine's tradition also tells of a disciple born into a Hindu family, Sheikh Fakhr-ud-Din; he stayed in the saint's service all his life, and his grave lies inside the shrine. A house bought for him was still known in the nineteenth century as the house of Shah Jamal, and his descendants made over the rent of a house and four shops as an offering in the saint's name, dedicated to Shaikh Ahmad Bakhsh.

Accounts of the year of his death differ; the year the shrine keeps is 1671. His death is commemorated on the 4th of Rabi-ul-Sani, and a Thursday is remembered as the day he died. The manner of his death is told as one with his devotions. Tradition holds that, coming out of a *chilla*, a retreat of forty days in seclusion, he died when the roof of his cell collapsed. As people struggled to free him, a voice from within is said to have commanded that he should not be pulled out, but that his grave should be raised over that very spot, and so it was.

## The Damdama and Its Legends

Legends gather thickly around the *Damdama*. The best known tells of a princess, the sister of the emperor Akbar, whose palace stood nearby. Annoyed that the saint's tall tower overlooked her palace, she demanded that he lower it. Shah Jamal answered by performing *dhamal* on the topmost floor, and as he danced the building sank into the earth, storey after storey, until only two remained above ground. He then foretold that her palace would not outlast his own, a prophecy that, it is said, came true when Sikh forces later destroyed it. The sinking of the building was already counted among the saint's marvels in the nineteenth century.

Another version, told at the shrine itself, makes the saint's anger larger still: in his wrath he is said to have buried an entire city under his foot. As one writer has observed, the story was told "long before it was discovered that the location of his shrine could be the site of a mound with archaeological importance".

The shrine's tradition preserves another story. A dishonest Hindu merchant was given a cantaloupe by the saint to feed to his wife. Twin sons followed, and one of them, named Sheikh Fakhr-ud-Din by Shah Jamal himself, became his devoted lifelong disciple. The saint is also said to have saved the same family by calling them out of their house moments before its roof fell in.

## The Shrine

Shah Jamal is said to have overseen the building of the *Damdama* himself. It is said to have been one of several places of his austerities. By the 1870s the place was already known as Shah Jamal's *damdama*, his tomb lying in Lahore beside the village of Ichhra, and by the 1880s it was counted among the Muslim sites outside the city, with a *madrasa*, a religious school, beside it. Today the site lies in Shah Jamal Colony, Ichhra, on Achhra Road, facing west.

In the nineteenth century the tomb stood south of the city, towards the Sarai Goliyan Wali, near Ichhra. In that century the tower's story also told of labourers brought from the Sarai, of a building of several storeys, of a *haveli*, a great mansion, and of the objection raised against the building. At the shrine itself there was a square platform with a stair on its southern side, a domed brick cell, a flight of nine brick steps, small towers, and a brick terrace ten *gaz* across, the old yard measure, with a niche for a lamp. There were also a mosque, a *madrasa* and a well. The platform served as the graveyard of the family of the keeper, Shaikh Amir Bakhsh: his mother lay there, and Fateh Bibi was also buried there. Shaikh Amir Bakhsh had built an enclosure at the shrine, and an inscription recording the enclosure wall honoured the saint as Hazrat Shaikh Jamal Sahib Qadiri and named Fazl Bakhsh and Shaikh Ghulam Mustafa among those who raised it. That walled enclosure measured six and a quarter by five and a quarter *gaz*, with four small towers. The marker over the saint's grave was a handsome white.

The shrine has since been rebuilt. The tomb is now enclosed within a mosque of relatively modern construction, crowned by a single dome and four minarets, with a graveyard beside it; what the visitor sees reflects rebuilding rather than preserved Mughal fabric. The shrine follows the Suhrawardi line and the Ahl-e-Sunnat, the Sunni tradition, and since 1960 it has been in the care of the government's Auqaf Department. Before that, its upkeep and adornment fell first to Shah Jamal himself and afterwards to the kings, maharajas and devotees who came after him.

## Keepers of the Shrine

In the nineteenth century the shrine was kept by a family of Shaikhs. Its *sajjada nashin*, the hereditary custodian who sits on the saint's prayer carpet, was Shaikh Amir Bakhsh, son of Shaikh Roshan Ali; Roshan Ali's father in law was a Shah Sharaf. The men of the family included Shaikh Ramzani, Qudratullah, Mehr Bakhsh, Hafiz Amanullah, Saifullah and Haidar Bakhsh.

The shrine also had its own family of sweepers. In the nineteenth century the sweeper was a woman newly converted to Islam. Her daughter was married to Hidayatullah, and her granddaughter was betrothed to Mir Taqi. Mir Taqi's son was Qasim Ali, whose son Sayyid Ahmad was the father of Sultan Shah and Fateh Ali Shah. A woman who had swept the shrine was buried within the enclosure, and so was Mir Taqi. Their place was not uncontested. The keeper, Shaikh Amir Bakhsh, and another man complained that the sweepers came only on Thursdays, for the *chadhat*, the offerings, and declared that they would appoint someone else or sit there themselves. The income was put at about three hundred rupees a year.

Today the *sajjada nashin*, a descendant of the saint, continues to hold ceremonies of *Khatam Khawajgan*.

## Thursday Nights and the Urs

Today the shrine is known not for quiet contemplation but for its exuberant Thursday nights. From dusk until the early hours devotees, students, musicians and curious visitors gather as dhol drummers beat out the rhythm and the crowd gives itself to *dhamal*. The most famous of those drummers was the late Pappu Sain, whose successors carry on his tradition. Alongside the drumming there is *qawwali*, the devotional music of the shrines, there is *langar*, the free communal kitchen, and there is the lighting of lamps.

Thursday was already the shrine's day in the nineteenth century, when offerings came in every week in cash and in kind. The crowds today are large, on the order of 10,000 to 15,000 visitors a day, rising to 200,000 to 250,000 for the annual *ʿurs*, the festival that marks the anniversary of the saint's death. The *ʿurs* is held from the 3rd to the 5th of Rabi-ul-Sani, with the ritual washing, or *gusal*, of the tomb, the offering of the *chadar*, the sheet of cloth laid over the grave, and drumming and *dhamal*. In the nineteenth century, too, the *ʿurs* drew Hindus and Muslims alike.

Besides his cell at the *Damdama*, the shrine's tradition holds that Shah Jamal kept *chillas* at Sheikhupura, Shah Rehman, Shahdara in Lahore and Chanpatpur in India. In the nineteenth century vows were still being offered at Sheikhupura, one of these places of retreat, where there was also a grave.

The shrine shows both the vitality of devotion centred on Lahore's shrines and its shadows. The city's homeless rely on its *langar* and offerings, while noise, crowding and drug use in the surrounding streets, a real concern for the safety of pilgrims, have from time to time drawn official scrutiny.

## Ichhra and the Canal

Ichhra, the village where Shah Jamal is said to have pitched his camp, also appears in Mughal history. In the seventeenth century Ichhra was a halting place outside Lahore. After Dara Shikoh had left the city for Multan, the emperor, on his return, camped on the open ground of Ichhra, received there the nobles who had been in the city, and the next day rode on towards Lahore.

The locality that took the saint's name also lived through the violence of Partition. Mary, a widow of eighty three, remembered how her family had "used to live near Shahjamal on the canal that now divides Lahore into two parts". Before Partition she played on its banks; as Partition came, she saw "mutilated bodies floating in the water".

## Legacy

Shah Jamal's *Damdama* has been a living shrine for as long as anyone has written about it. By the 1870s it already carried the name it still bears; in the nineteenth century it also had its keeper, its family of sweepers and its Thursday offerings, and its *ʿurs* drew Hindus and Muslims alike. In the early twenty first century one scholar of Sufism could call the tomb "still a popular pilgrimage place". Its legends of a tower sunk by a dancing saint and an imperial princess rebuked, and its Thursday nights of drumming and *dhamal*, have made it one of the most written about expressions of popular Sufi devotion in the Punjab, and the neighbourhood that grew up around it still bears his name.

In 1875 a book on the saints of the Punjab gave Shah Jamal a notice of his own. The book closes with a chronogram, a verse written to mark the year of its completion, by the author's son, Mufti Ghulam Safdar, and its couplet names the book's subject: the friends of God in the Punjab, whose story the book makes plain.
