## Overview

Gurdwara Patti Sahib, also known as Gurdwara Maulvi Patti, stands in Nankana Sahib, the town in Pakistan where Guru Nanak Dev Ji, the founder of the Sikh faith, was born. It lies midway between Gurdwara Janam Asthan, the central shrine that marks his birthplace, and Gurdwara Bal Lila, on the road that runs from the town centre towards the railway station, though accounts of its exact distance from the Janam Asthan differ. The gurdwara commemorates the place remembered in Sikh tradition as the village school to which the child Nanak was sent to learn, and where, the *sakhis*, the stories of the Guru's life, tell, he astonished his teacher with the depth of his understanding.

Its name comes from the *patti*, the wooden writing tablet on which the children of the Punjab once learned their letters. Pilgrims come to it on the great anniversaries of the Guru, and in its rooms Sikh families have made their homes.

## The Guru's First Lessons

In the Punjab of the fifteenth century, a *patti* was the smooth wooden board on which a pupil, with a reed pen and ink made at home, practised the letters of the alphabet under the eye of a teacher. Tradition relates that Nanak was taken as a young boy to the village schoolmaster to be taught in this way. The story tells that, rather than merely copying his letters, the child composed and recited an acrostic on the alphabet, a poem in which each verse begins with the next letter in turn, and set out in it deep truths about God and the soul. So amazed was his teacher, the story goes on, that the master confessed the boy had nothing more to learn from him.

Tradition holds that this composition is the *Patti Likhi*, set in *Rag Asa*, one of the musical modes of the hymns, and recorded in the *Guru Granth Sahib*, the Sikh scripture, under Guru Nanak's name. It is to this acrostic that the gurdwara's name ultimately refers, and it is told that it was the first poem Nanak wrote, and that he wrote it here.

The *sakhis* add further episodes of the Guru's early education: his study of Punjabi and of accounts, of Sanskrit with a Hindu *pandit*, or scholar, and of Persian with a Muslim *maulvi*, or religious teacher, named in some tellings Qutbuddin. Accounts of his teachers and of what he was taught differ, but through all these episodes the theme is the same: the learning of the schools was surpassed by the inward wisdom already present in the child. The gurdwara's other name, Maulvi Patti, keeps the memory of this Muslim teacher, and of a schooling that crossed the community lines of the age. At school, the janamsakhis, the cycles of tales told of the Guru's life, relate, the boy showed a brilliance never before seen in any child in Talwandi.

Nanak was born into a Hindu household and educated in a Muslim milieu, and he was neither a practising Muslim nor a Hindu. In his time all literate Punjabis, Hindus and Muslims alike, were conversant with the Persian poets Attar, Rumi and Sa'di. The alphabet poem was a form that the Muslim poets of the Punjab knew too: in the plains of Sind and the Punjab, the Muslim poets of the countryside wrote instructive Golden Alphabets to instil love for the Prophet into the souls of the villagers.

## The Guru

Guru Nanak was born at Talwandi, the village later called Nankana Sahib, in 1469; accounts of the exact day differ. In the janamsakhi telling his father was Kalyan Chand Das Bedi, or Mehta Kalu as he was affectionately called, accountant to the Bhatti family under Rai Bular, and the village astrologer, Pandit Hardyal, foretold the child's greatness. Nanak belonged to the Bedi caste. In his childhood, it is said, signs of spiritual unveiling, of wonders and of marvels beyond the ordinary appeared in him.

At thirteen, the janamsakhis tell, the boy refused the sacred thread, the *janeu*, in his father's courtyard, saying that "it is our actions and our actions alone" that give a man his position among men. At sixteen, in the same stories, he herded cattle, and he became well acquainted with the dervish Sayyad Hussain and gained an intimate knowledge of the Sufi line of thought; accounts of the dervish's name differ. Talwandi lay in the path of numerous mendicants, holy men and *sadhus*, as Hindu ascetics are called, who camped in a grove of trees on the outskirts of the village. It is believed that in the six or seven years between the episode of the *Sacha Sauda*, the "true bargain", and his move to Sultanpur, the young Nanak began to frequent the abandoned mound on the outskirts of the town.

From Talwandi Nanak went to Sultanpur, around 60 kilometres away, as keeper of the stores of Nawab Daulat Khan. He married Sulakhini, daughter of Mul Chand and Chando Rani of the town of Batala, on 24 September 1487, though accounts of the marriage differ, and they had two sons, Sri Chand, born on 8 September 1494, and Lakhmi Das, born on 12 February 1497. At Sultanpur, the janamsakhis tell, he vanished in the stream for a week and came out saying, "There is only one God. There is no Hindu, no Mussalman."

Accounts of how many journeys he made differ, but his *udasis*, the long journeys of his teaching, lasted more than twenty years and took him, mostly on foot, east to Bengal, south to Sri Lanka, north to Kashmir, Nepal and Tibet, and west to Mecca. Whenever he came to an area he would visit its religious shrines, Muslim, Hindu and of any other denomination, and people came from every land to become his disciples, whole multitudes from the far parts of the realm.

Lakhmi Das, it was said, was a worthy son, but spiritual fortune was not his lot, and the Guru passed him over. On his deathbed he appointed not his son but his most loyal student as his spiritual successor, a Khatri named Lehna, to whom he gave the title of Guru Angad. Guru Nanak died on 22 September 1539, though accounts of his death differ.

He lived as a householder, and he taught that all men and women were equal, irrespective of caste, creed, wealth, social standing and gender. The tradition of the *langar*, the communal free kitchen, is believed to have begun with him, and in Aurangzeb's reign his followers counted the service of those who come and go, the traveller, as the highest worship.

## The Gurdwara

The shrine that grew up on the site of the remembered schoolhouse is a substantial building. Set on the road between Janam Asthan and Bal Lila, it forms a natural station on the circuit that pilgrims follow among the places of the Guru's childhood, and its sanctum houses the *Guru Granth Sahib*, before which the visiting faithful make their devotions. It is counted among the well kept shrines of the town, maintained by the authorities charged with the care of Sikh heritage in Pakistan, though accounts of its care differ.

On the night of Lohri in January 2011, an arch led in to a yellow building of two storeys with a white dome, raised on a platform, and a *Nishan Sahib*, the Sikh flagstaff, stood with candles, lamps and incense. The rooms of the gurdwara had been taken over by local Sikhs, among them the family of Kartar, a Sikh of the town.

## A Refuge and a School

As at the other shrines of Nankana Sahib, the community of worshippers departed for India at Partition in 1947. Kartar remembered that no Sikh was left there after Partition, and that the gurdwaras of the town became barren and fell into a bad state. In the 1960s Giani Pratap, an Indian citizen, came to Nankana Sahib with a party of pilgrims, and on seeing the dilapidated condition of the shrines he decided to stay and look after these holy buildings. He housed Kartar's family for a time inside one of the town's gurdwaras and made Kartar's father a *sewaadar*, a worker, there. Giani Pratap died in Lahore.

The Sikhs of Nankana Sahib are not natives of the city but recent migrants from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, where there is a considerable Sikh population. Families came following the alienation after the wars of 1965 and 1971 as well as the demolition of the Babri Mosque in 1992, and most of the newcomers were housed within the gurdwaras, in rooms generally reserved for pilgrims. Their arrival gave a confidence boost to the Sikhs, who until then had been dominated by the Muslim majority. Some of the young men spoke Urdu with a Pashto accent, and not all the Sikhs of the town could speak Urdu or Punjabi. Kartar called Punjabi "the language of Baba Guru Nanak".

In the early days of this migration, after the wars of 1965 and 1971, many Sikh families from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and the Tribal Areas found refuge in Gurdwara Patti Sahib. There Giani Pratap began to teach the young Sikh children the Gurmukhi alphabet, the script of the Sikh scriptures, and the Guru Granth Sahib. So the house remembered for the boy's first lessons became a schoolroom again, where the children of refugee families learnt the script of the Guru's hymns. Jasdeep, a young Pathan Sikh, lived at the gurdwara and had been educated there in Sikh precepts.

Teaching in the Guru's script and scripture went on elsewhere in the town too. Madan Singh, a businessman of Nankana Sahib and a former head of the Pakistan Sikh Gurdwara Prabandhak Committee, ran Guru Nanak High School, the only school in the country where the Granth Sahib was taught to Sikh children.

## Devotion and Festivals

Pilgrim *jathas*, bands of pilgrims, come to Patti Sahib on the great anniversaries, above all the *Gurpurab* of Guru Nanak's birth, when the town fills with visitors from India and the Sikh diaspora.

The gurdwara also keeps a festival of the Punjab. On the night of Lohri in January 2011 the second Lohri fire of the town was lit in its courtyard, where Kartar lived. Kartar broke a wooden window into the pile, oil was poured over it and lit, children added branches, and *kheer*, a rice pudding, was served in steel cups and bowls. Afterwards the older boys went round the homes of the community for more *kheer*. Muslims in the shops and on motorbikes watched the procession through the town with burning curiosity.

Lohri, like Vaisakhi or Basant, is an ancient festival of the Punjab that commemorates the change of season. It is kept on the 13th of January, generally the last night of the month of Poh, and traditionally it means a village bonfire, dry fruit and folk songs; the children collect the wood, and *kheer* is left to cool overnight. After the creation of Pakistan this indigenous festival of the Punjab died down, and the Sikh and Hindu minorities of central Punjab also stopped the celebration. Kartar said that Lohri and Guru Gobind Singh's birthday had only begun to be kept in the last couple of years, made possible by the growth of the Sikh population through migration from the tribal areas; earlier there had been only a prayer. The Lohri of 2011 at Nankana Sahib was an attempt to revive this cultural tradition of the Punjab, carried by a group of Pathan men living in the Punjab.

The great day of the town is the *Gurpurab*. Because Nanak spent his early years in the city there are several gurdwaras in it, and the pilgrims visit all of them; the posters of travel companies promised pilgrims "all" the gurdwaras of Pakistan. Official estimates put the pilgrims who come to Nankana Sahib for the festival at ten to fifteen thousand every year; in 2011, when the festival ran from 8 to 10 November, media reports counted only three thousand. Pilgrims from India crossed at Wagah, where representatives of the Evacuee Trust Property Board and the Pakistan Sikh Gurdwara Prabandhak Committee received them and a *langar* was laid on, and they stayed three days at Nankana. Three days of *Akhand Path*, the unbroken reading of the scripture, ended in the *bhog*, the closing rite, followed by the *Nagar Kirtan*, the procession with hymns. Richer pilgrims from Britain, America and Canada came by chartered plane and hired buses, devotees walked barefoot, and for those few days the whole city looked like a little India.

## Legends

Many wonders are told of the Guru. Legend tells that Nanak once travelled to Mecca and performed the Hajj, and that he slept with his feet towards the Kaaba and was rebuked; he turned his feet, the story goes, and as he did so the Kaaba miraculously turned towards his feet. It is believed, too, that a trader who told Nanak that his mules carried "Just some mud" found his sugar and *gur*, raw cane sugar, turned to mud, and that when he repented they became sugar and *gur* once again. Other legends tell of Nanak humbling a Muslim Sufi at Sialkot and at Pasrur.

A Muslim tradition, which Sikhs do not accept, holds that Guru Nanak was born by the blessing of Baba Naulakh Hazari.

Nanak himself is held to have abhorred the association of miracles with religious personalities, yet the stories of his life are full of them. In one story he tells Karori, who calls his cure a miracle, that "there is no such thing as a miracle". The story told at Patti Sahib is of another kind: its marvel is a poem.

## The Guru's Verse

Guru Nanak's sermon was his *bani*, devotional verses of supplication, entreaty, humility and reverence. His *bani* was compiled as part of the Adi Granth by Guru Arjan Dev, the fifth Guru, in 1604. The Guru Granth Sahib carries also the poetry of other spiritual teachers such as Bhagat Kabir, Baba Farid and Bhai Mardana, and the poems of the later Gurus are recorded not in their own names but in that of Guru Nanak.

He composed verses in Hindi on the truth of God, setting out the unity of the divine, and a singer named Mardana sang his verses. He brought into his poetry several words that were not part of the Punjabi of his time, and poets such as Kabir and Nanak used the *doha*, an old popular Prakrit and Hindi metre in which the two verses of a couplet rhyme, to write their mystical poetry. In Aurangzeb's reign the worship of his followers was the reading of their master's verses, which they recited in song and melody.

Lines from his hymns, in English translation, give a sense of the poetry. His poem on the sacred thread, the *janeu* that the stories say he refused as a boy at Talwandi, begins:

*From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment*

On the oneness of God he sang:

*My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One*

And again:

*God is One, / The Path is One*

His *aarti*, the hymn sung as lamps are waved in worship, begins:

*In the salver of the firmament, / The sun and the moon shine as lamps*

Of washing he sang:

*As hands or feet besmirched with slime, / Water washes white*

The verses are sung beyond the gurdwaras too. The singer Abida Parvin has said: "I sing Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah, Mian Muhammad Bakhsh, Hazrat Khawaja Ghulam Farid, Sacchal Sarmast, Baba Farid, and Guru Nanak." In Sindh, Sain Vasan Shah became famous for reciting the *banis* of Shri Guru Nanak and the *kalams*, the poems, of the Sufis.

## Nankana Sahib

Talwandi, the village later known as Nankana Sahib, lies about forty miles south west of Lahore, in what is now Pakistan. It lay between the rivers Ravi and Chenab, directly in the path of the armies that had set their eyes on Delhi. It is told that the village was destroyed thirteen times and thirteen times rose again from its ashes, like the fabled phoenix, and that its last rebuilding was overseen by Rai Bhoe, a Bhatti Rajput who had become a Muslim. Originally called Rai Bhoi di Talwandi, the town was renamed Nankana Sahib in honour of Guru Nanak. It is held to be one of the holiest cities in the world for the Sikhs, because the founder of their religion was born there, and it has several gurdwaras, most of them associated with incidents from his life, though accounts of their number differ.

One main road ran through the entire city, and most of the buildings were from the late British or the early Pakistan era. From a rooftop one could see the minarets of mosques, the small white domes of the gurdwaras, the remains of a few Hindu temples and the railway station. In 2005 Nankana Sahib was separated from Sheikhupura and made an independent district, with the city as its headquarters. Guru Nanak was celebrated all over the town: there were a Guru Nanak High School, a Guru Nanak grocery store and a Guru Nanak cloth shop, all run by Sikhs. The Sikh community was most often put at about two hundred households, though accounts of its size differ, and Jatha Singh was its leader for several years. Most of the Muslims of Nankana Sahib had migrated from the other side of the border after Partition, and their attitudes are said to have been shaped by those experiences.

## Legacy

Patti Sahib keeps the memory of the schoolboy who taught his teacher, whose first verses, in Sikh tradition, were a meditation on the letters of the alphabet turned towards the divine. Among the shrines of Nankana Sahib it takes its place in the line that marks, step by step, the unfolding of the Guru's early life, and it carries a particular meaning for a faith that has prized both learning and the transcendence of mere learning. That a Muslim *maulvi* is remembered among the boy's teachers ties the site to the plural religious world of the medieval Punjab from which Sikhism arose.

The building that remembers the Guru's first lessons sheltered Pathan Sikh families who came to Nankana Sahib after the wars of 1965 and 1971, and there Giani Pratap taught their children the Gurmukhi alphabet and the Guru Granth Sahib. In January 2011 its courtyard held one of the town's Lohri fires, part of the attempt to revive that tradition of the Punjab.

Devotion to the Guru reaches beyond the Sikhs. Nanak is held to have been a spiritual follower of Baba Farid, and to have travelled to Pak Pattan, where he met the spiritual descendants of Baba Farid and collected the saint's poetry. Farid's texts are revered among the Sikhs, for he is held to have influenced the founder of their religion. A twentieth century Chishti Sabiri shaykh described Guru Nanak, with Krishna, Rama, Sita and Lachman, among the spiritual luminaries outside Islam, as a believer in the one true God and a religious leader with a "Divine connection", *nisbat*.

Nanak Panthis, Hindus who believe in the teachings of Guru Nanak but not in the Khalsa, the order of initiated Sikhs, keep portraits of Guru Nanak in their temples in Sindh and southern Punjab, along with Krishna, Kali Mata and other Hindu deities, and many Lohanas in Sindh follow the teachings of Nanak. In Sindh there is a growing similarity between the iconography of Guru Nanak and that of Jhulelal. In the Hinglaj valley there are the engraved footsteps of Guru Nanak. For Nadeem, a Muslim painter, Guru Nanak is like a Muslim Sufi saint.
