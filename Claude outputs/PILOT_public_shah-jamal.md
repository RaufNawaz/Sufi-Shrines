## Overview

The shrine of Baba Shah Jamal stands in the Lahore locality that now carries his name, beside Ichhra and opposite Forman Christian College. It honours Syed Shah Jamal, a Sufi of Hussaini Syed descent who lived in Lahore in the reign of the Mughal emperor Akbar, and it rises on the spot he himself built and named his *Damdama*. Today the shrine is famous above all for its Thursday nights, when dhol drummers drive crowds of devotees, students, musicians and curious visitors into hours of *dhamal*, ecstatic devotional dancing to the beat of the drum. That weekly spectacle has made Shah Jamal one of the best known and most written about expressions of popular Sufi devotion in the province.

The place is no newcomer to fame. Histories and lives of the saints written in the second half of the nineteenth century already knew the tomb, the name of the *Damdama* and the stories told about it, and found it busy with offerings, keepers and an annual festival. What follows is the story of the saint, of the tower that is said to have sunk beneath him, and of the shrine that grew up on the spot.

## The Saint

Syed Shah Jamal was a Hussaini Syed, a descendant of the Prophet, and the shrine traces his ancestry to Jalaluddin Surkh-Posh Bukhari of Uch Sharif; his full name is given as Syed Shah Jamal Uddin Naqvi Bukhari. His father is remembered as Syed Ahmad Shah, and the family is said to have moved from Kashmir to Sialkot and from Sialkot to Lahore, where Shah Jamal and his brother were both born. The link with Sialkot lasted: in the 1870s his descendants were still said to be living there.

He was one of two brothers who are both counted among the saints of Lahore. His brother, Baba Shah Kamal, is buried close by, and Shah Jamal is said to have come to Ichhra to see him and to have pitched his camp a short distance from his brother's house. The two were remembered together as devout and ascetic men, given to spiritual rapture and constant devotion, and the marvels of both were well known among the people of Lahore. In the nineteenth century Shah Kamal's tomb stood right beside the lodge, or *khanqah*, of Shah Jamal.

Tradition places Shah Jamal's birth in 1587, and he lived in Lahore under Akbar. He is remembered for resisting the emperor's syncretic creed, the Din-i-Ilahi, and for working instead to draw people back towards orthodox Islamic practice. His fame spread among rich and poor alike, and many came to him in search of blessing. Devotees know him for his open hand as *Sakhi Sarwar*, the generous lord, a title that in the Punjab more usually belongs to Sultan Sakhi Sarwar of Dera Ghazi Khan.

There is no settled view of which Sufi order he belonged to, but he is most often counted a Suhrawardi. His spiritual chain, or *silsila*, is traced in eight steps back to Bahauddin Zakariya of Multan, by way of masters including Shah Sharaf and Shah Ma'ruf, and beyond him to Shihab al-Din Suhrawardi, the founder of the order, and finally to ʿAli. He is also remembered as a Qadiri, one of the great Qadiri saints of Lahore alongside Shah Hussain and Mian Mir, and the shrine's own tradition holds that he belonged to the Qadiri, Suhrawardi and Chishti orders at once. Accounts give his own spiritual guide's name in different forms; the shrine remembers him as Sheikh Khokar Baig Suhrawardi.

His disciples carried his name further. The best remembered is Hassu Teli of Lahore, himself a Sufi of the city. Another story makes him the guide of a Lahore grain merchant whose weights he set right. The shrine's tradition also tells of a disciple born into a Hindu family, Sheikh Fakhr-ud-Din, whose story is told below among the legends; he stayed in the saint's service all his life, and his grave lies inside the shrine. A house bought for him was still known in the nineteenth century as the house of Shah Jamal, and his descendants made over the rent of a house and four shops as an offering in the saint's name.

Accounts of the year of his death differ, but the date most often given, and the one the shrine keeps, is 1671. His death is commemorated on the 4th of Rabi-ul-Sani, and a Thursday is remembered as the day he died. The manner of his death is told as one with his devotions. Coming out of a *chilla*, a retreat of forty days in seclusion, he died when the roof of his cell collapsed. As people struggled to free him, a voice from within is said to have commanded that he should not be pulled out, but that his grave should be raised over that very spot, and so it was.

## The Damdama and Its Legends

Legends gather thickly around the *Damdama*. The best known tells of a princess, the sister of the emperor Akbar, whose palace stood nearby. Annoyed that the saint's tall tower overlooked her palace, she demanded that he lower it. Shah Jamal answered by performing *dhamal* on the topmost floor, and as he danced the building sank into the earth, storey after storey, until only two remained above ground. He then foretold that her palace would not outlast his own, a prophecy that, it is said, came true when Sikh forces later destroyed it. The story was already being told in the nineteenth century, when the sinking of the building was counted among the saint's marvels.

Another version, told at the shrine itself, makes the saint's anger larger still: in his wrath he is said to have buried an entire city under his foot. Curiously, as one writer has observed, the story was told "long before it was discovered that the location of his shrine could be the site of a mound with archaeological importance".

The shrine's tradition preserves a tender story too. A Hindu merchant who cheated his customers was given a cantaloupe by the saint to feed to his wife. Twin sons followed, and one of them, named Sheikh Fakhr-ud-Din by Shah Jamal himself, became his devoted lifelong disciple. The saint is also said to have saved the same family by calling them out of their house moments before its roof fell in.

## The Shrine

Shah Jamal is said to have built the *Damdama* and given it its name in his own lifetime, and to have overseen the building himself. It was one of several places of his austerities. By the 1870s the place was already known as Shah Jamal's *damdama*, his tomb lying in Lahore beside the village of Ichhra, and a history of Lahore written in the 1880s counted it among the Muslim tombs outside the city and placed a *madrasa*, a religious school, beside it. Today the site lies in Shah Jamal Colony, Ichhra, on Achhra Road, facing west.

In the 1860s the tomb stood south of the city, near Ichhra, amid a cluster of buildings. Around it lay a square platform with a stair on its southern side, a domed brick cell, flights of brick steps, small towers, and a broad brick terrace with a niche for a lamp. There were also a mosque, a *madrasa* and a well, and the platform served as a family graveyard for the shrine's keepers. The keeper of the shrine, Shaikh Amir Bakhsh, had built an enclosure there, and an inscription on an enclosure wall honoured the saint as Hazrat Shaikh Jamal Sahib Qadiri and named Fazl Bakhsh and Shaikh Ghulam Mustafa among those who raised it. The cover over the saint's grave was a handsome white, and close by lay the graves of the woman who swept the shrine and of a member of her family.

The shrine has since been rebuilt. The tomb is now enclosed within a mosque of relatively modern construction, crowned by a single dome and four minarets, with a graveyard beside it; what the visitor sees reflects rebuilding rather than preserved Mughal fabric. The shrine follows the Suhrawardi line and the Ahl-e-Sunnat tradition, and since 1960 it has been in the care of the government's Auqaf Department. Before that, its upkeep and adornment fell first to Shah Jamal himself and afterwards to the kings, maharajas and devotees who came after him.

## Keepers of the Shrine

In the nineteenth century the shrine was kept by a family of Shaikhs. In the 1860s its *sajjada nashin*, the hereditary custodian who sits on the saint's prayer carpet, was Shaikh Amir Bakhsh, son of Shaikh Roshan Ali, and a household of some ten men of his family is named with him.

The shrine also had its own family of sweepers, whose graves lay within the enclosure. Their place was not uncontested. The keepers complained that the sweepers came only on Thursdays, when the offerings, or *chadhat*, were brought, and declared that they would appoint someone else or sit there themselves. The offerings were worth about three hundred rupees a year.

The line of custodians continues. The current *sajjada nashin*, a descendant of the saint, continues to hold ceremonies of *Khatam Khawajgan*.

## Thursday Nights and the Urs

Today the shrine is known not for quiet contemplation but for its exuberant Thursday nights. From dusk until the early hours devotees, students, musicians and curious visitors gather as dhol drummers beat out the rhythm and the crowd gives itself to *dhamal*. The most famous of those drummers was the late Pappu Sain, whose successors carry on his tradition. Alongside the drumming there is *qawwali*, the devotional music of the shrines, there is *langar*, the free communal kitchen, and there is the lighting of lamps.

Thursday was already the shrine's day in the nineteenth century, when offerings came in every week in cash and in kind and a share went to the keeper. The crowds today are large, on the order of 10,000 to 15,000 visitors a day, rising to 200,000 to 250,000 for the annual *ʿurs*, the festival that marks the anniversary of the saint's death. The *ʿurs* is held from the 3rd to the 5th of Rabi-ul-Sani, with the ritual washing, or *gusal*, of the tomb, the offering of the *chadar*, the sheet of cloth laid over the grave, and drumming and *dhamal*. In the nineteenth century, too, the *ʿurs* drew Hindus and Muslims alike.

Besides his cell at the *Damdama*, the shrine's tradition holds that Shah Jamal kept *chillas* at Sheikhupura, Shah Rehman, Shahdara near Lahore and Chanpatpur in India. These places were not forgotten. In the nineteenth century vows were still offered at the places of his retreats, and at Sheikhupura there was a grave as well.

The shrine shows both the vitality of devotion centred on Lahore's shrines and its shadows. The city's homeless rely on its *langar* and offerings, while noise, crowding and drug use in the surrounding streets, a real concern for the safety of pilgrims, have from time to time drawn official scrutiny.

## Ichhra and the Canal

The village beside which Shah Jamal settled has a long history of its own. In the seventeenth century Ichhra was a halting place outside Lahore. In the years after Dara Shikoh left the city for Multan, the emperor himself camped on the open ground of Ichhra, received there the nobles who had been in the city, and the next day rode on towards Lahore.

The locality that took the saint's name also lived through the violence of Partition. An elderly widow named Mary remembered how her family had "used to live near Shahjamal on the canal that now divides Lahore into two parts". Before Partition she played on its banks; as Partition came, she saw "mutilated bodies floating in the water".

Shah Jamal has his own notice in a nineteenth century book on the saints of the whole Punjab, completed in 1875. The book closes with a chronogram, a verse written to mark the year of its completion, by the author's son, and its couplet could stand as a motto for all of them: it names the friends of God in the Punjab, whose story the book makes plain.

حال ان کا اس سے ہوتا ہے عیان

## Legacy

Shah Jamal's *Damdama* has been a living shrine for as long as anyone has written about it. By the 1870s it already carried the name it still bears; in the 1860s it had its keeper, its family of sweepers and its Thursday offerings, and its *ʿurs* drew Hindus and Muslims alike. In the early twenty first century one scholar of Sufism could still call the tomb "still a popular pilgrimage place". Its legends of a tower sunk by a dancing saint and an imperial princess rebuked, and its Thursday nights of drumming and *dhamal*, have made it one of the most written about expressions of popular Sufi devotion in the Punjab, and the neighbourhood that grew up around it still bears his name.
