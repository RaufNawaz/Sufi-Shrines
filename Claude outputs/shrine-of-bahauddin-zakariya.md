## Overview

In the old city of Multan, the "City of Saints", stands the shrine of Hazrat Bahauddin Zakariya, the saint who planted the Suhrawardi order of Sufis, or Muslim mystics, in the soil of the subcontinent and made Multan one of the first great centres of Islamic mysticism in South Asia. A contemporary and friend of Baba Farid of Pakpattan, and like him a shaper of the spiritual map of the Punjab, he founded a *khanqah*, a Sufi lodge, at Multan whose influence reached across the region for centuries. His tomb, raised in the thirteenth century, is among the oldest and most revered Muslim shrines in Pakistan, and pilgrims come to it throughout the year.

He lived from around 1170 to 1267. He is considered the patron saint of Multan, and his shrine, which stands beside a Hindu temple, became the symbol of the Muslim city. In Punjabi poetry he is counted among the Five Pirs, five great saints who are believed together to possess special powers, and his tomb is a major centre of pilgrimage. His disciples and descendants, among them the Persian poet Fakhr-ud-Din Iraqi and his grandson Shah Rukn-e-Alam, carried the order he brought to Multan across Sindh and the Punjab.

## The Saint

Bahauddin Zakariya was born around 1170 at Kot Karor, near Multan; accounts of the year of his birth differ. He is said to have been born on a Friday morning, the twenty seventh of Ramazan, the month of fasting. He came of a Qureshi family of Arab descent that had settled in the region. His grandfather, Kamal al-Din Ali Shah Qureshi, a dervish by nature and an honoured member of the Quraish, the tribe of the Prophet, left Mecca and lived in Khwarazm, and from Khwarazm he came to Multan, then a centre of the Islamic sciences and arts, where he settled for good and many became his devotees. Accounts of the family's descent within the Quraish differ, but Bahauddin is held to have been a Hashimi, of the clan of Hashim, the Prophet's own clan.

His father was Shaikh Wajih al-Din Muhammad, son of Kamal al-Din, and his mother was Bibi Fatima. Her father is most often named Maulana Husam al-Din Tirmizi, who had left his homeland of Tirmiz when the Tatar plundering began and lived at Kot Karor. It is told that the child was born a *wali*, a friend of God, and that as an infant he would stop suckling to listen whenever his father recited the Quran. His father died when he was twelve. As a boy he memorised the Quran, and as a schoolboy, it is said, he claimed to remember the primordial covenant: he remembered, he said, all that had happened from the moment God said *Am I not your Lord?* until that day.

He came to be known by many names and titles. His *kunya*, the name of honour formed with Abu, "father of", was Abu Muhammad. He was called Shaikh al-Shuyukh, the Shaikh of Shaikhs, Makhdum al-Alam, the Master of the World, and Bahauddin Zakariya Qureshi Multani; he bears the title Baha al-Haq, and in Bulleh Shah's verse he is invoked as Bahaval Haq. Among the titles heaped on him are peerless *pir*, or spiritual guide, and treasury of generosity, and he is counted among the greatest of the *awliya*, the friends of God.

After his father's death he set out in search of learning. Accounts of his route differ, but he is said to have spent seven years at his studies in Khurasan and then to have gone to Bukhara, where he completed his learning and became famous as Bahauddin Farishta, "the angel". From there he went to the Hejaz. At Medina, where he stayed five years, he studied *hadith*, the sayings of the Prophet, with Shaikh Kamal al-Din Muhammad Yamani, one of the great scholars of *hadith*, and obtained an *ijazat-nama*, a written licence to teach. On his travels he met the dervishes of Khurasan, Bukhara, Mecca, Medina and Bait al-Muqaddas, Jerusalem.

At last he came to Baghdad, and there he took *bai'at*, the oath by which a disciple pledges himself to a master, from Shaikh Shihab-ud-Din Abu Hafs Umar al-Suhrawardi, the great systematiser of the Suhrawardi order and author of the *‘Awarif al-Ma‘arif*. Under him he completed his training with remarkable speed. Within eighteen days, it is said, he received the *khirqa*, the patched cloak of the Sufi, as the mark of his *khilafat*, his authority to guide others, and he was sent to Multan with leave to carry the order to India.

The chain of masters that ran to him began with Junaid of Baghdad, the chief of the Sufi company, and passed, among others, through Mamshad Alu Dinawari, Ahmad Aswad Dinawari, Abu Abdullah Khafif, Abu al-Qasim Gurgani, Abu Bakr Nassaj, Shaikh Ahmad Ghazali, the brother of Imam Muhammad Ghazali, Ziya al-Din Abu al-Najib Suhrawardi and Wajih al-Din Suhrawardi to Shihab-ud-Din Umar Suhrawardi, and from him to Bahauddin Zakariya.

On the road to Multan, it is told, he met a learned *qalandar*, a wandering dervish, Sayyid Abd al-Quddus of Mosul, who had put on the qalandars' dress at the grave of Sayyid Jamal al-Din Mujarrad; Bahauddin had the qalandar's garment taken off him and brought him from *jazb*, the rapture of a soul drawn towards God, to *suluk*, the ordered journey of the Sufi path. Shaikh Jalal al-Din Tabrizi travelled with him as far as Nishapur, where the two parted; accounts of why they parted differ.

Coming from Baghdad to Multan, he took up permanent residence there; many became his disciples, and he became the foremost Suhrawardi shaykh of the subcontinent. The dervishes already in the city, the story goes, did not want him there and sent him a cup filled with milk, to say that there was no room for another in the city. He laid a flower on the milk and sent the cup back, meaning that his place in the city would be as a flower is on milk; and, the story ends, they were astonished and became his devotees.

He was on good terms with Sultan Shams al-Din Iltutmish and supported him against Sultan Qabacha, and under Iltutmish he became *Shaikh al-Islam*, the chief religious dignitary; accounts of how the office came to him differ.

At Delhi he sat in judgement at a trial. When a complaint against Shaikh Jalal al-Din Tabrizi reached Sultan Iltutmish, the Sultan summoned the notables and the *mashaikh*, the Sufi masters, to Delhi; more than two hundred *awliya* came, and after the Friday prayer all assembled in the Masjid Minar. The Sultan let the accuser name the arbitrator, and the accuser chose Bahauddin, counting on the old estrangement, for he believed that the two saints had fallen out at Nishapur. But when the accused entered and all rose in his honour, Bahauddin lifted Shaikh Jalal's shoes in his own hands and declared that he was bound to make the dust of Shaikh Jalal's feet the kohl of his eyes, because for seven years Shaikh Jalal had stayed in the service of his own *pir*. The Sultan said that since the arbitrator had shown such honour the matter was decided, and Bahauddin then questioned the woman in the case, who confessed the whole plot. Such an assembly, a *mahzar*, was a gathering of *ulama*, the scholars of religion, and of Sufis, called to decide an important dispute.

## Life at Multan

His *khanqah* at Multan drew people of every rank. The kings of the time and people high and low alike entered his *bai'at*, thousands of attendants were present at his court, and through him, it is said, hundreds of thousands benefited from the Suhrawardi way. He also had a *madrasa*, a school, in Multan, where the children of the local Nawab and other important officials were taught; it was an elite school of its era.

Unlike the Chishtis, who embraced absolute poverty and kept their distance from the courts, he represented the Suhrawardi temper. He engaged openly with rulers and mixed freely with the ruling classes, as his own master, Abu Hafs Umar Suhrawardi, had served the caliph an-Nasir, and his association with the rulers in Delhi raised him to importance. He accepted endowments and gifts and managed considerable resources. He was a landlord of means, agriculture and trade were carried on at his house on a large scale, and his *khanqah* prospered on gifts from merchants and trade guilds: many merchants traded outside India, were largely independent of the government, and could not be prevented from giving. Outward and inward wealth were his portion: he was himself extremely rich, and his own *pir* had vast wealth too. He looked after the needs of his family and never failed to keep a supply of grain in his house.

He used his wealth in charity and hospitality on a grand scale, though accounts differ over how freely his table and his company were open to all. He was a lively host who followed to the letter the Quranic injunction to eat of what is pure and act righteously, and he greatly honoured the *ulama*, the great *mashaikh* and his guests. He gave thanks in every condition, was very charitable and unconcerned with the world, and lived with simplicity and contentment, eating very little, but strong food. He completed one reading of the Quran every night, and forbearance and patience, ardour and longing, rapture and absorption were his marked qualities. In his early life he fasted much, but in his last years he did not fast daily.

His house had its rules. He did not encourage his disciples to perform *sajda*, prostration, before him; they greeted him with *as-salam alaikum*, "Peace be with you", and he expected them to finish their obligatory religious duties first and to greet him afterwards. In his lifetime he insisted that Sufis join one order only, though from the fourteenth century Sufis were often initiated into both the Chishti and the Suhrawardi orders.

He disliked the *qalandars* intensely and never encouraged their unwelcome visits. Mongol domination of Central Asia and Iran made it easier for bands of Qalandars and Jawaliqis to travel from Turkey and Egypt to India, and those passing through Multan often came into conflict with him, though the generosity of Baba Farid made up for such experiences. Yet he made exceptions. Fakhr-ud-Din Iraqi went on living as a wandering dervish even after his initiation by him, and Lal Shahbaz he adopted as his disciple.

His wealth was enough to make him the target of accusations by other Sufis. Hamid ad-Din Suwali Nagauri, seeking the attitude of the Suhrawardi order towards wealth, carried on an extensive correspondence with him on the question of poverty and wealth. The first letter set out the difficulty: by the teaching of the Shaikhs, the Quran and the *hadith*, the property of this world is an obstacle to union with God, yet it was heard that some of the Shaikhs of the age enjoyed a handsome share of it and at the same time performed miracles. The letter was addressed to Bahauddin, and it left the wealthy Shaikhs unnamed. Hamid ad-Din was left unsatisfied, and in a second letter he wrote that the answer had come to him instead from God.

The two men met at Delhi during the *mahzar* on the charges against Jalal al-Din Tabrizi. In defence of his wealth Bahauddin said: "Although wealth is a serpent, someone who has learnt the incantation to overcome the venom, need not have any fear from the serpent." Pressed further, he answered: "Your achievement as a dervish is not endowed by such beauty and elegance as to be immune from the evil eye, but our achievements as dervishes are endowed with such great beauty as to gain no harm even if the whole of the world's blackness were applied to its face." It is told that in the end he was left without a reply. The quarrel outlived the letters. One of his sons, annoyed by the controversy over the wealth of the Suhrawardis, visited Ajmer, and finding that Hamid ad-Din did not attend the Friday congregational prayers, used this as a pretext to attack him, and gained some support from a group of *ulama* who demanded that Hamid ad-Din fulfil his religious duties.

His own sayings on wealth were remembered: however much worldly wealth there is, it is still little; the company of a snake harms the one who does not know its charm; in his view worldly wealth has no reality; and for *faqirs*, those who have embraced poverty, its absence and its presence are the same, with no grief at its going and no joy at its coming.

Accounts of his attitude to music and poetry differ, but *qawwals*, singers of Sufi devotional music, performed in his gatherings. A *qawwal* named Abdullah, who came from Turkey, enlivened his assemblies. Another, Abu Bakr, who came from Multan to visit Nizam ad-Din's teacher, recalled how he had been reciting in *samaʿ*, the Sufi session of spiritual listening to music, before Shaikh Baha ad-Din, and reached the line "The snake of desire has stung my heart"; he could not recall the second half of the couplet, but the Shaikh did. He went on to praise the Shaikh's hospice, where devotion to God was such that even the slave girls recited the names of God while grinding grain, though his words made no lasting impression on the heart of the young Nizam ad-Din.

Among his contemporaries, Baba Farid of Pakpattan was his great friend. The two were together for a time, and Farid profited from his company. Bahauddin once wrote that between them there was a play of love, and Farid replied that between them there was love, and no play. The two men, separated by only a few hundred miles, stood for two styles of life, and the difference of method between the Suhrawardi and the Chishti, the two poles of early Indian Sufism, is often illustrated by their cordial contrast. In the same period Bahauddin and Muin al-Din Chishti were both preaching divine love and union. Their spheres had a frontier: Baba Farid, it is told, once said to a traveller that the land from his own town to a certain village was in his charge, and that beyond a certain reservoir the land was in the charge of Shaikh Bahauddin Zakariya.

The Chishti Khwaja Qutb al-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki, coming from Baghdad through Khurasan, reached Multan and formed a friendship with him there, before going on to Delhi in the reign of Iltutmish. Bahauddin was also a great admirer of the weaver Shaikh Ahmad Naharwani.

One book of his survives, the *Kitab al-Awrad*, the Book of Litanies; Maulana Ali bin Ahmad Ghori, a *murid*, or disciple, of Shah Rukn-e-Alam, wrote a commentary on it, the *Kanz al-Ibad fi Sharh al-Awrad*. His sayings were gathered in the *Khulasat al-Arifin* and in a *Malfuzat*, a record of his discourses. Among the sayings remembered from him is a definition of renunciation: *zuhd*, asceticism, is three things, and whoever lacks them is no ascetic: to know the truth of the world and let it go; to serve the Lord and keep to his *adab*, the courtesy due to him; and to long for and seek the hereafter. Safety, he said, lies in little food, the soul's safety in leaving people, and the faith's safety in the prayer of the Best of Creation, the Prophet.

He was a poet too, and wrote a famous *manqabat*, a poem in praise of a saint, in honour of Ghaus al-Azam Shaikh Abd al-Qadir Jilani, with the refrain *Muhyi al-Din Jilani*. One couplet opens with a plea to the Shah of Jilan for help:

مدد یا شاہ جیلانی بریں افتادہ حیرانی

The poem closes on a couplet that carries his own name, calling Baha al-Din Multani a stone of the threshold of Jilani.

He died at Multan in 1267, full of years and honour; accounts of the year of his death differ. The day of his death was the seventh of Safar, the second month of the Islamic year. He is said to have reached the age of a hundred and to have died on reading a sealed letter. He was buried at Multan.

## Disciples and Successors

Bahauddin Zakariya's lasting achievement was to root the Suhrawardi way in the subcontinent. From his khanqah at Multan the order spread through Sindh and the Punjab, carried by his disciples and descendants. It was marked by strict adherence to the Sharia, the religious law, by sober devotion and learning, and by an active engagement with society and state, a pattern that made Multan a hub of both spiritual authority and worldly influence and shaped the religious life of the western Punjab and Sindh for centuries. Less austere in its practices than the early Chishti order, the Suhrawardi order sent its members throughout the country and gained disciples even in eastern Bengal. Shihab-ud-Din Suhrawardi had other disciples, but the continuous history of the Suhrawardi order in India can be traced only through the *khalifas* of Bahauddin Zakariya, the deputies he appointed.

He had many famous disciples. Besides his son, he had five eminent *khalifas*: Sayyid Jalal al-Din Surkh Bukhari; Sayyid Usman, known as Lal Shahbaz; Shaikh Hasan Afghan; Shaikh Fakhr al-Din Ibrahim Iraqi; and Sayyid Sadr al-Din Ahmad, known as Sayyid Husain.

Sayyid Jalal al-Din Surkh Bukhari, also called Jalal Surkh, "the Red", came to Multan and became his *murid*. He received the *khirqa* of *khilafat* from him and, by his *pir*'s order, took up residence at Uch. Bahauddin's own *khirqa* was worn by Makhdum Jahaniyan, who received it from his father, Sayyid Ahmad Kabir.

Hasan Afghan could neither read nor write, yet the Shaikh was proud of him and used to say that if God asked him what he had brought from the world, he would present Hasan as a gift. By the Suhrawardi tradition, Lal Shahbaz Qalandar of Sehwan was initiated by him at Multan; accounts of the relations between the two men differ.

Fakhr-ud-Din Ibrahim, known as Iraqi, one of the leading Persian mystical poets, came to Multan with a party of wandering *qalandars*, who stayed with the Shaikh. When the party broke up in a storm, Iraqi returned to the Shaikh's *khanqah* and began living in a cell the Shaikh had assigned him as a spiritual retreat. He attached himself to Bahauddin at Multan, lived in his circle for nearly twenty five years, and dedicated *qasidas*, odes, to him.

His fellow disciples were shocked to learn that a *ghazal*, a love lyric, of his was being sung in the local taverns to the accompaniment of the harp and zither, and they complained to the Shaikh, but the Shaikh ruled for Iraqi. He declared Iraqi's training complete, ordered him to give up retirement and meditation, gave him his own *khirqa* to wear and later gave him his daughter in marriage; before he died, he appointed him his *khalifa*. Soon after his master's death Iraqi left Multan for Konya, where he met Sadruddin Qonawi and perhaps Jalaluddin Rumi, and he lived in Konya for some years. Iraqi wrote verse for his master, naming him the guide of men:

*If thou shouldst ask of the world 'Who is the guide of men?' / Thou wilt hear from heaven no other answer than 'Zakariyya'.*

His name reaches into Sindh and into devotions beyond Sufism. Pithoro Pir is held to have been a Muslim Sufi initiated by Bahauddin Zakariya, and Udero Lal, too, is called his disciple.

Spiritual chains ran back to him. He stands at the head of the chains of Shah Jamal and of Shah Daula, of eight and of thirteen steps, of the *silsila*, the spiritual lineage, of Mian Wada, also of thirteen steps, and of a Suhrawardi chain of nine steps from the Lahore scholar Shaikh Hamid Qari Suhrawardi, whose last links run through Husam al-Din Muttaqi and Shaikh Sadr al-Din to Shaikh Bahauddin. From him, too, came the gift of *wilayat*, sainthood, to Sakhi Sarwar.

## Family and Descendants

He had seven sons: Shaikh Sadr al-Din Arif, Shaikh Burhan al-Din, Shaikh Ziya al-Din, Shaikh Ala al-Din, Shaikh Shihab al-Din, Shaikh Qudwat al-Din and Shaikh Shams al-Din. A daughter of his married Fakhr-ud-Din Iraqi. Unlike the sons of most of the early Chishti saints, his sons followed in his path, and in the Suhrawardi order succession became, generally, hereditary. On his death his estate was divided.

His son Shaikh Sadr al-Din, known as Arif, "the Gnostic", was his *murid*, his *khalifa* and his successor as *sajjada nashin*, the hereditary custodian who sits on the saint's prayer carpet. He took *bai'at* from his father and received from him the *khirqa* of *khilafat*. When his share of the estate came to him, he gave it all to the *fuqara*, the poor. His reasoning began from this: his father had mastered the world, and so the presence of wealth and gold could do his father no harm. Sadr al-Din died in the year 709 of the Hijri calendar.

Sadr al-Din's wife, Bibi Rasti, was a disciple of her father-in-law in her own right, and through her the Suhrawardi *nisbat*, the spiritual bond of the order, reached many women. She died in the year 695 of the Hijri calendar.

It is told that Bahauddin foresaw the greatness of his grandson Rukn al-Din before the child was born: rising to his feet for his pregnant daughter-in-law, he said that the honour was for the child in her womb, who would be the lamp of the family and the candle of the line. He supervised the boy's education and upbringing. At the age of four Rukn al-Din put on his grandfather's turban, and although his father objected strongly, Bahauddin approved and told Sadr al-Din not to forbid the child. When Rukn al-Din succeeded his father, he wore the same turban. He is remembered as Shah Rukn-e-Alam, another saint of Multan, the grandson and successor of Bahauddin Zakariya.

His descendants appear across the history of Multan and Lahore. When the province of Multan was left without a ruler, its people raised Shaikh Yusuf Qureshi, one of his descendants, to rule them. Shaikh Yusuf struck coin and had the *khutba*, the Friday sermon, read in his own name, and he ruled for two years before the Langahs seized him. He escaped to Sultan Bahlul Lodi at Delhi, who married his own daughter to Shaikh Yusuf's son, Shaikh Abdullah, and at Shaikh Yusuf's urging Bahlul's army was then sent against Multan. Sultan Firuz Shah, on reaching his capital, gave Shaikh Sadr al-Din, another of the saint's descendants, the title of *Shaikh al-Islam* and a place near himself.

One descendant, Shah Rahmatullah Qureshi, came from Multan to Lahore, and by the 1870s his family lived in Lahore and in the village of Dholanwal on the *nazr*, the offerings, of their disciples; one of them was Bahadur Shah. Shaikh Hamza Dharsawi was also of his descendants. The family of the Lahore mufti Shaikh Kamal-ud-Din Qureshi was held to descend from him as well: the mufti's father, Shaikh Makhdum, had been summoned from Multan under Muhammad Tughlaq and appointed to the office of *ifta*, the giving of legal rulings. The nineteenth century mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori claimed the same descent for his own family of muftis, whose first elder, Makhdum Mufti Shaikh Muhammad Qureshi, known as Mian Kalan, came from Multan and settled in Lahore.

## The Shrine

Bahauddin Zakariya lies at Multan, in the old fort area of the city. His mausoleum, built in the thirteenth century, is one of the earliest monuments of Islamic architecture in the subcontinent: a square tomb chamber surmounted by a hemispherical dome, much rebuilt after damage over the centuries, among other times in the siege of Multan in 1848. In Aurangzeb's reign the resting place lay inside the fort, and over the tomb, where divine lights were held to be made manifest, a lofty dome of brick and lime had been raised.

It remains a working shrine and the seat of his descendants, the Qureshi *makhdooms* of Multan, who have been among the most prominent religious and political families of the region.

The shrine stands beside the temple of Prahlad Bhagat, a Hindu temple of Multan, and is one of the most popular Muslim shrines. The saint, one of the Muslim mystics drawn to the holy mound, came and settled next to the temple, and his shrine came to be situated beside it; religious traditions once moved freely between the two shrines. The singer and activist Parvati Devi said that the shrine authorities built on the premises of the Prahlad Temple, in 2006 and 2008, first a place for ablutions and then a *langar* hall for devotees visiting the shrine, and that a stay order of the High Court followed on a petition by Usman Chaudary.

The tomb has had its keepers and its petitioners. When the Khan-e Khanan reached Multan, he presented himself at the saint's khanqah and gave two hundred rupees as an offering; it was accepted, and then returned the next morning because of a dream that had come to the *sajjada nashin*. It is told, too, that Maulana Hussam al-Din, while at Multan, was returning one day from the tomb when he conceived the wish to ask for a plot of ground for his own grave, and that Sadr al-Din Arif knew of it by *kashf*, inner unveiling, and told him that it was not difficult for him to give a place for a grave, nor did he object, but the Prophet had chosen a place at Badaun for him, and there he would be buried.

The khanqah's prosperity ended with the execution of Shaikh Hud and the seizure of the khanqah's property. Shaikh Hud's war cry, it was alleged, had been the ill treatment of the saint's descendants by the Sultan. The Suhrawardi order began to flourish in other regions, while Multan became a shadow of its former glory.

## Devotion and Festivals

Pilgrims come throughout the year to recite the *Fatiha*, the opening chapter of the Quran, to seek blessing and to honour the saint, and the shrine keeps the round of daily prayer, of *qawwali*, Sufi devotional music, and of the *langar*, the free communal kitchen, that marks the great shrines, or *darbars*, rising to its height at the annual *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death. Thousands of devotees visited the shrine every day. In the 1970s the love poems of Fakhr-ud-Din Iraqi were still being sung by Pakistani musicians at the door of his master's tomb.

The tomb drew travelling saints, and an emperor came to it to ask for help. Shaikh Jamali, on a journey that ran from the Haramain, the holy cities of Mecca and Medina, to Egypt, Baghdad and other lands as far as Khurasan, presented himself at the tomb in Multan. One saint, it is told, spent the night of Eid at the tomb in the remembrance of God. When the imperial camp lay near Multan in the pursuit of Dara Shikoh, the emperor went on the ninth of Muharram to visit the tomb, and there sought the help of the saint's spiritual power and the outpouring of his lights.

His image travels beyond Multan. At Uch Sharif posters of him are sold in the market at the Gilani shrine, and a banner at Jalaludin's shrine shows him dressed like a rich man, with pearls around his neck and a fancy gown. At Uch he is also named as one of three saints said to have meditated in the rooms of a mosque.

## Legends

By Aurangzeb's reign many wondrous and astonishing stories of the saint were on the tongues of small and great alike. One tells how he received his robe in a dream. He saw a house of light, with the Prophet present and his own *pir* standing respectfully, and many *khirqas* hanging on a rope; he was summoned, his hand was taken, and the Prophet told Umar to clothe Shaikh Bahauddin Ghaus al-Alam, the Helper of the World, in this *khirqa*. In the morning, the story goes on, his *pir* called him, and he found the house exactly as it had been in the dream. The same *khirqa* was taken down and put on him by hand, and the *pir* disclaimed any authority of his own: all these *khirqas*, he said, were the Prophet's, he himself was only an intermediary, and without the Prophet's leave he could clothe no one in them.

It is told, too, that when Jalal al-Din Surkh Bukhari came to Multan and pined for the weather of Bukhara, the saint knew it by *kashf*. He sent a *khadim*, a servant, to the mosque with orders to roll up the prayer rows and sweep the courtyard; a cloud appeared, heavy rain fell, and hailstones, and outside the mosque, the story goes, there was neither rain nor hail. At the midday prayer he came smiling and asked the Sayyid whether the hail of Multan was better or the snow of Bukhara, and the Sayyid answered that in that case the hail of Multan was a hundred times better. The same day, the story ends, he conferred on the Sayyid the *khirqa* of *khilafat*.

Khwaja Kamal al-Din Masud Shirazi, another story runs, was caught in a storm at sea and called on him, and the saint appeared on the ship, where all the passengers saw him plainly; he gave them tidings of safety, and the ship reached Aden safely. The passengers gave a third of their goods to Khwaja Kamal al-Din as an offering and a thanksgiving, and he sent all of it, with half of his own jewels, to Multan by his nephew, who on arriving, it is said, recognised the saint at once, in the very dress in which he had seen him on the ship. The saint, the story ends, distributed the goods and the jewels to the people within thirty days.

In Sindh he is remembered for a miraculous birth. In most tellings of the legend, one Madan asked for his intercession to obtain a son when the saint was touring in Sindh. Bahauddin offered him a small end, a *pithu*, of a dried date, and at the moment Madan's wife tasted the blessed piece she became pregnant. The son they had was named Pithoro, after the end of the date, and in the legend Pithoro became the disciple of Bahauddin.

The stories of Guru Nanak's life tell that he met the saint at Multan, though Bahauddin belonged to the thirteenth century, before Nanak's time; Nanak did enjoy the company of Sufis in that town.

## Poetry

The Punjabi poets remembered him too. Bulleh Shah often refers with reverence to Multan as a holy city and to Bahauddin Zakariya, and in a lyric that may be a later addition to his verse, Hir calls Ranjha to the tomb:

*Come, Ranjha, let us go to Multan, to pay homage to the great saint Bahaval Haq. Let us gain our desire immediately. My heart longs for the lord* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

Bulleh Shah's lyrics also invoke the Five Pirs, five great saints who are believed collectively to possess special powers: the legendary Khwaja Khizr, Farid Shakarganj of Pakpattan, Baha ud Din Zakariya of Multan, Jalal ud Din Makhdum Jahaniyan of Uch and Lal Shahbaz Qalandar of Sehwan. The Pirs guard the beloved:

*The Five Pirs protect my beloved, they search for him among people* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

And they are propitiated:

*I belong to the yogi, after propitiating the Five Pirs.* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

Waris Shah's romance of Hir and Ranjha also brings the Five Pirs to Ranjha, among them Zakaria, saint of Multan, and among their gifts to Ranjha his is a thick blanket.

## The Setting

In 711 the Arabs conquered Sind and the provinces to the north of it up to Multan. Multan had become a dependency of the Fatimids of Egypt, by the middle of the eleventh century Sufism had penetrated the lands around it, and in 1175 Mu'izz al-Din Muhammad bin Sam seized the city. During the twelfth century many Sufi scholars made Multan their base, and conversion to Islam gained momentum in the early thirteenth century.

By the time Guru Nanak came to Multan, the city was famous as a major centre of Sufi learning and philosophy. It was then one of the biggest cities of the Punjab, known for the shrines of Muslim saints and for its Jain and Hindu temples, a sacred space for people of all religious denominations. In Aurangzeb's reign it was counted one of the ancient cities, with a fort of brick; people of every kind lived there, and the goods of every land were bought and sold; its winter was mild, its summer excessively hot, and it had little rain; and many *awliya* rested in that blessed city.

Under Sultan Balban, when Prince Muhammad Sultan governed Multan, Amir Khusrau and Amir Hasan were in the prince's service there, and the prince twice sent to Shiraz for Sa'di, wishing to build a khanqah for him at Multan and endow it with villages; Sa'di could not come because of his age. An older sanctity is remembered too: the tyrant Hiranyakashipu is believed to have ruled the city of Multan thousands of years ago. For the Sindhi and Punjabi poets, Multan, like Mecca and Medina, was inside the heart, and in Bulleh Shah's eyes Takht Hazara, the birthplace of Ranjha, held the same standing as Multan, the seat of the Suhrawardi school of mysticism.

## Legacy

Bahauddin Zakariya stands beside Baba Farid as one of the two founding fathers of Punjabi Sufism, the Suhrawardi to Farid's Chishti, and his khanqah made Multan a wellspring of the tradition. Through his descendants and disciples, and through the later saints of Multan who trace to his line, his influence runs through the whole later history of Sufism in the western subcontinent. His shrine anchors Multan's ancient identity as the "City of Saints", and his memory endures as that of the master who first gave the Suhrawardi order an Indian home.

Of the Jat and Rajput groups that became Muslim in the medieval period, the vast majority claim to have been converted either by Baba Farid or by him. He is named, with Fariduddin Ganjshakr and Jalaluddin Surkhposh Bukhari, among a quartet of mystics, the *chahar-yar* or "four friends"; the shrines of these three are spread across southern Punjab, and Chothanbi, a site between Lal-bagh and Sehwan that pilgrims visit, is believed to be the place where the four friends met.
