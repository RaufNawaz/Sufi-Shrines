## Overview

The Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir is among the most revered and remote Hindu shrines in the world, a temple set within a natural cave in the rugged wilderness of the Makran coast, in the Lasbela district of Balochistan. It lies among the canyons and mud volcanoes of Hingol National Park, along the Hingol River, and it is dedicated to the goddess Hinglaj Mata, also called Hingula Devi and, affectionately, *Nani Mandir*. It is one of the fifty one *Shakti Peethas*, the seats of the Goddess's power venerated across the subcontinent, and every year the *Hinglaj Yatra*, the pilgrimage to the shrine and one of the largest Hindu pilgrimages in Pakistan, draws tens of thousands of devotees along a demanding desert route.

The Goddess goes by many names: Hinglaj Devi, Hinglaj Mata, Mata Hinglaj, the Mother and the Devi, the Goddess. Among Muslims the site and the Goddess are Bibi Nani, Nani Hinglaj or simply Nani, and a signboard at the site calls the temple the Mata Mandir. In the Sanskrit texts the place is Hiṅgulā, Hiṅgulāja and Hiṅgulāṭa. In recent decades Hinglaj has become one of the most important centres of Hindu worship in Pakistan, and it is generally considered the largest active Hindu place of pilgrimage in the country. For many, it is the oldest shrine in the entire Hindu tradition, so ancient and important that Hindus come even from India to visit it. Accounts of its age differ, but its worship is held to reach back well over a millennium.

## The Goddess

The sanctity of Hinglaj rests on the legend of the *Shakti Peethas*, told across India, and it is the story most often heard at the shrine today. Tradition tells that the Goddess was born as Sati, daughter of King Daksha, and married Shiva against her father's will. When Daksha performed the *ashvamedha*, the horse sacrifice, and invited every god except Shiva, Sati, in grief and outrage at the insult to her husband, jumped into the sacrificial fire. Shiva killed Daksha and, cradling her body, began the *tandava*, a frantic dance that threatened to destroy the whole world. To end it, Vishnu released his discus, the *Sudarshana Chakra*, which cut Sati's body into many parts; they fell out of Shiva's arms and scattered over the subcontinent, and wherever a part came to earth a *Shakti Peetha* arose, charged with the presence of the Goddess.

At Hinglaj, tradition holds, it was Sati's head that came to rest: the *brahmarandhra*, the crown of the head, an opening through which the soul is said to escape when it leaves the body. Accounts differ over which part of her fell here, but it is most often said to be the head, an association that gives the site especial preeminence among the seats. At the image itself, one of the round stones on the eastern side is held to be Sati's skull. The Goddess who presides here is known by names such as Kottari, and her guardian *Bhairava*, the form of Shiva associated with the seat, is named Bhimalochana.

There is no settled view of where the name Hinglaj comes from, though it is linked to the Sanskrit word *hiṅgula*, meaning vermilion or cinnabar. It is said that Sati, as a pious married Hindu woman, wore vermilion, *sindoor*, in her hair, so that when the upper part of her head fell in Balochistan the place became known as Hiṅgula, a name that in time became Hinglaj.

The shrine also has a foundation legend of its own, though it is rarely heard there today. The demon Vichitra, the legend tells, had two sons, Hingol and Sindur. Sindur looted China, whose people, worshippers of Shiva, petitioned the god, and Shiva promised that within three days Ganesha would come and relieve them of their afflictions; Ganesha came and killed the tyrant. Hingol retreated to a mountain, raised his hands toward the sky and stood on one toe for thousands of years, until Brahma granted that he could not be killed in war, by any weapon or by any human being, neither under the sky nor on the earth. Parvati appeared to him as a beautiful woman, and he pursued her from the Himalaya to the Indus and into Sindhudesh, the land of Sindh, until, at the place where the shrine stands today, the Goddess absorbed him into her body.

In the legend, the naming of the place follows in the Goddess's own words. Hingol's last wish was: "You should remain here at this very place under my name and fulfill all wishes that people bring to you!" The Goddess answered: "From now on I will be called Hinglaj Devi, and this place should be my place of worship. I will stay here forever with Hingol."

In the eyes of many Pakistani and Indian Hindus, Hinglaj is the oldest and therefore the greatest of all the seats of the Goddess. Devotees ground the claim in two ways. Some call it a *khalis sthan*, a "real" place of pilgrimage, a claim tied to its being *svayambhu*, arisen of itself; others point out that it is named, as Hiṅgulā, Hiṅgulāja or Hiṅgulāṭa, in various Sanskrit texts, and those mentions are often invoked when devotees speak of the shrine's standing. Hindus and others alike place its heritage in the same age as the Mohenjo-Daro civilisation.

Accounts of the number of seats differ from text to text, but the *Pīṭhanirṇaya* first tied the number fifty one to the parts of Sati's body. Its list names Hinglaj first, with the Goddess who resides there and the form of Shiva associated with her, and gives the crown of the head as the part of Sati that fell there; it is the text most often quoted as proof of Hinglaj's first place among all the seats. There is no consensus on when the name Hinglaj first appears in Sanskrit writing; its first mention is placed between the ninth and the fifteenth centuries. The link with Sati's head, and the idea of Hinglaj as the first of the seats, first appear in the *Pīṭhanirṇaya* itself, which is dated between 1690 and 1720, though accounts of how old the link is differ.

Hinglaj anchors a wider world of goddesses too. Some understand her as the first incarnation, the *adishakti*, the primordial power, and so as the centre of many forms of Goddess worship in western India; these local incarnations became anchored to the shrine of Hinglaj and were further interpreted in its light. Accounts differ over how many of the seats lie in Pakistan, but Hinglaj is the most frequented of those there.

## Clans, Peoples and Faiths

Hinglaj is a *kuldevi*, a clan goddess, for several communities. Before the present temple committee, castes and communities such as the Brahmakshatriyas, the Charans and the Kapris came to the shrine to worship the Goddess as their patron or clan deity, and other local communities of Sindh and Balochistan, Hindus as well as Zikris, a Muslim community of the region, also claim an ancient connection with the desert shrine. She is the clan goddess of the Dewanis, a Sindhi Lohana family from Lasbela, and many of the Lohana communities of Lasbela worship her as theirs. Members of the Brahmakshatriya, Charan, Kapri and Lohana communities in particular have produced ritual manuals, narratives of myth and history, travelogues, video recordings, institutions and other material that has influenced the course of events at Hinglaj in recent times.

The Charans, the bards, claim the closest bond, and for many of them the pilgrimage to her shrine at some point in life is mandatory. Charans often say that when a boy is born into the community a bell rings at the Hinglaj temple, and that every Charan man must visit the Goddess at Hinglaj before his sixtieth birthday; otherwise a rock will tumble down into the sacred valley, until in the end the shrine is buried. She is their *adishakti*, the primordial energy that has spread through their community for many generations. Charan narratives also tell that Hinglaj herself was once a Charani, a woman of their community, indeed the first of countless incarnations of the Goddess in this world, though her place among those incarnations is contested among Charans today.

For the Kapri community, anyone who emerges from Hinglaj's cave in Balochistan is initiated into the group and may take the title Kapri. The Brahmakshatriyas explain their very genealogy through the Goddess's intervention in Balochistan, as their legend of five princes tells. The shrine is one of the main religious centres of the Devipujak community of Karachi, who describe Hinglaj as their Hajj, after the Muslim pilgrimage to Mecca: for them it is the most important place in Hindu cosmology, and every Hindu ought to visit it once in a lifetime.

Muslims venerate her too. Despite the upheavals of Partition and the decline of Hindu institutions in Pakistan, the shrine has not merely survived but is venerated by the local Muslims of the region, who know it as *Nani Mandir* or Bibi Nani and regard it as a place of blessing. By 1842 Muslims revered it alike, as the shrine of Bibi Nani, the lady Nani or the motherly lady, and in 1904 Hindu and Muslim pilgrims alike resorted here, each claiming the protection of the presiding goddess or of the departed saint, according to their own faith.

Legends heard in colonial times told of the valley's Muslim side. Muslims of the area claimed that Hazrat Ali, the son in law of the Prophet Muhammad, split a rock in two with his sword as he made his way into the valley. A legend also told that a Muslim woman had at one time been the priest of the Goddess at Hinglaj, and that by applying the *tika*, the mark on the forehead, she turned the pilgrims into Muslims, so that they had to be reconverted on their way home.

Today such legends have virtually vanished from Hinglaj. Zikri Muslim pilgrims still visit the shrine, and it is widely frequented on Baqra Eid, on the Prophet's birthday and on Ashura. The Zikris see Hinglaj as a Balochi *pir*, a saint who is part of their own culture and has the power to grant wishes, and they consider the shrine to be the grave of one of the family of the Prophet. Yet no story of the shrine is told there today from a Zikri point of view; Muslim symbols are increasingly pushed to the margins inside the valley, and having had no control over the renovations, the Zikris have had few chances to set their own reading of the image at the shrine.

Sindhi nationalists claim the valley too. They hold that it served the ancient Sindhis as a place of contemplation, so that Hinglaj can be called a "thought evolution site". Parsram, in a Theosophical claim, held that universal wisdom had been kept "in Hinglaj to be exact", a pilgrimage centre in the Sindhi speaking area of Balochistan. As the *pir* Bibi Nani, venerated in certain Muslim traditions, Hinglaj carries considerable weight in the Sindhi nationalist discourse of today.

## The Temple

A couple of hundred metres beyond the shrine of Kali Mata, the walls of the Hinglaj Mata temple come into view on the southern side of the valley. They are built on a stone platform leading into a natural cave, which a river apparently carved out of the mountain ages ago, and only the northern and eastern walls are of stone bricks, joined to the natural hollow to form the temple's foundations. The cave makes a natural arch about 9 metres high at its peak and about 64 metres long. The stone walls inside it support no roof, since the overhanging arch shelters the temple from sun and rain. As the temple's priest, Gopal Maharaj, told an interviewer in December 2010: "Every temple in the world is constructed with bricks and stones, but if you look at the roof of this place, you will realize that it has naturally generated. No one constructed it."

By 1842 there was a small *mat*, or temple, at Hinglaj, but the chief attractions appeared to be natural objects: a *kand*, or reservoir of water; a well of unfathomable depth above the temple; and a spot where the lie of the rock looked like a fortress. Figures of the sun and the moon were also said to be hewn on the rock, in a place that could be reached.

The age of the enclosure is unknown. The earliest colonial visitors described no stable stone structure, only a "mud edifice"; a photograph published in 1943 shows that the basic stone structure had been built by then; and a party that visited in 1961 put the stone building at fifty or sixty years old. There is neither a foundation stone nor any visible inscription to date it, and no one at the temple could date the walls. It is often said that they were built gradually, over time, by pilgrims and mendicants with stones from the valley.

The building has been altered within living memory. On its eastern side a set of stairs, built around 1998 to replace a simple heap of stones, leads to the inner courtyard, where larger groups gather for *puja*, worship, and for *bhajans*, devotional songs. The inner space was once divided into two rooms, one where music was played and the other where food was stored, until around 1998 the temple committee, the Hinglaj Sheva Mandali, pulled down the divider to make room for the crowds. During the committee's renovations in the late 1990s the walls were cleaned and the inside of the temple was covered entirely with tiles, and at the end of the 1990s a room was built opposite the entrance for the resident priest, which serves during the festival as a store for drinking water. Pilgrims who reached the shrine used to write their names and addresses on its walls, and many names could be seen inside in Gujarati, Nastaliq, English and Bengali; the renovation wiped out many such traces of the temple's history. Many buildings in the valley were paid for by the provincial government of Balochistan, but the rebuilding of the temple, the committee says, was supported mainly by donations from the Hindu community.

The image of the Goddess lies on the far west side of the temple, where the arch of the cave reaches down to meet the ground. Here she is worshipped not as a carved idol but as a small form of mud and stone daubed with *sindoor*, in keeping with the shrine's ancient character. What is now considered her main image rests on an oblong stone of about 1 metre by 1.5 metres, on the far west side of a wide platform about 1 metre off the ground. The platform, around 5.5 to 7.5 metres wide, is irregular rather than rectangular in shape, and it was originally built on the foundations of a small hill of rock or mud.

The stone seems to rest on an extension of the cave wall, with a small channel beneath it, and it is this connection with the mountain, as devotees understand it, that makes the image *svayambhu*, arisen of itself; the sanctum and the *garbha*, the womb of the Goddess, are also held to be *svayambhu*. The whole cluster can be seen as a human figure lying flat, its head to the east and its legs to the west, formed of several joined stones, or *pind*, lumps of stone worshipped as images of the Goddess. Eyes and noses have been attached to two round stones to give the rocks human faces, and over the years pilgrims have brought women's ornaments, crowns and umbrellas to adorn the image. In recent decades the stones have been covered with layer after layer of vermilion paste, and what are held to be the figure's body parts, from the head downwards, are always covered with red and orange cloth, so that its full shape can no longer be seen.

One face is the Goddess's; accounts differ over whose the other is, and no particular story had fully settled around it. In 2012 the temple's priest explained it as the face of a *vir*, a hero or army commander, of the Goddess Durga, who helped her to kill the demon Mahishasura.

When the oblong stone was tiled, the way the image is seen changed. Colonial visitors had described the sanctum as a rectangular stone resembling a Muslim grave, with some "diminutive domes" on top; the tiling shifted attention from the structure as a whole to the heaps of stone on top, now emphasised as the Goddess's image. Muslim pilgrims who also visited Hinglaj took the stone block, surrounded by a carved railing bearing various sacred emblems, as marking the grave of a saintly wife of Ali, miraculously carried to this spot, though Muslim accounts of who she was differ. Before the present committee there were thus two ways of reading the image: as the gravestone of a saintly figure of Islam, and as the lying figure of the Hindu Devi, the Goddess Nani.

The image also bears inscriptions. A Hindi inscription on the back of the oblong stone, which appeared between 1943 and 1961, is now partly buried under concrete and tiles, and only the name of the donor, who apparently helped to build the image's foundation, can still be seen. In 1961 it stood directly in front of two engraved footprints of a holy man, and the footprints are still clearly visible in front of the writing. A different inscription, made after 1961, read: "I have taken refuge in the fold of the Mother Shri Hinglaj. You keep my sins safe, my [second] birth was successful." The text that can be read today runs: "Following the order of Shri Matavala Baba, Jagat Janani ordered to build this lion's throne for the Mother Hinglaj."

The platform that holds the oblong stone and the image divides the temple in two: the raised western part is used mostly for *puja* and *arti*, the waving of lamps before the deity, and the lower eastern part houses larger groups of pilgrims during the festivals. For a long time the *dhuni*, or *yajna kund*, the fire pit into which ritual offerings to the Goddess are given, stood in the centre of the oblong stone, until in the 1990s it was moved to the front of the image, and in recent years a large *yajna kund* has been built in the yard in front of the temple. A conical stone called 'Sadasheo', a Shiva *linga*, the emblem of Shiva, is gone: the committee removed it in the 1990s and threw it into the Sharan Kund, the pond of refuge, which lay directly in front of the temple before it dried up.

## The Womb of the Goddess

Beneath the image runs the *garbha*, the womb of the Goddess: a tunnel shaped like a U, forming a semicircle underneath the image and opening into the temple yard. Two doors, each around 90 centimetres high, form its entrance and exit. At its tallest the tunnel is no more than 90 centimetres high, and at certain points it is barely large enough for a person to fit through. Visitors had to ask the priest or the volunteer workers at the shrine to open its doors.

Its fabric is partly natural and partly built. The work of human hands, however old, can be clearly told apart from the natural cleavage in the stone of the mountain walls; one wall, of stone and brick, was built, the resident priest says, to stabilise the mud heap; and the crevice in the rock, directly beneath or very close to the oblong stone on which the image rests, appears to be the oldest part of the tunnel. A semicircular tunnel was already there by the middle of the nineteenth century; by the middle of the twentieth, this tunnel, held to be natural, had been supported and stabilised by human hands; and later the committee extended it on both sides to make the full U under the platform.

The passage through it is the heart of the pilgrimage. In the central rite of the shrine, pilgrims crawl through the small tunnel that signifies the womb of the Goddess, from which they are born again, and before a pilgrim may enter it, he or she has to follow a rigid pattern of purification and worship. It is one of the oldest practices at the shrine, and the climax of the journey now as well as then. The crawl becomes an act of rebirth out of the womb of the Goddess, sometimes followed by pilgrims mimicking the cries and gestures of newborn babies, and it is held to cleanse Hinglaj's followers of all their past sins. Every visitor to Hinglaj, devotees believe, is reborn from the womb of the Goddess and so gains the *atma*, the soul, of a newborn child.

Pilgrims enter it with a partner. At Kanda Devi they choose their *sharan bhai* or *sharan bahan*, their brother or sister of refuge, the partner with whom they will enter the Mother's womb. The last place for a purification bath before the tunnel was once the Sharan Kund, a pool directly in front of the temple: devotees would go under its fresh water three times together with their chosen partner before they were allowed to enter the Goddess's abode. In 2009 it was still plain that a large pool had once been there, but by 2011 the area was covered over with concrete.

## The Valley and Its Keepers

The temple stands in a valley of springs, ponds and caves. Today a wall built by the committee in 1996 clearly marks the point where the Hinglaj Valley begins. Among the sacred places of Hinglaj are Anil Kund, a pond atop one of the chains of hills in Chaurasi; Til Kund; the River Aghor; Brahma Kund, named after Brahma; Kali Kund and Kir Kund; and the Kali Mata temple, the main place of the *bali*, the sacrifice. A colonial visitor was told of a place called 'sath durwazu', which held the private rooms of the Goddess, where to spit or to defile the ground was a serious transgression.

New shrines rise in the valley year by year. Gujarati Hindus from Karachi began building a small booth for Melari, or Meladi, Mata in the heart of the valley, and by 2013 it had become a small concrete temple, tiled like Hinglaj and housing a statue of the Goddess about one and a half metres tall. Around the beginning of the millennium another group of visitors had a vision of Momai Mata a short distance up the mountains on the way to Chaurasi, where they built a wooden shrine that has been enlarged over the years. Almost every year pilgrims add to or revise the temples, statues and ritual places, and in 2012, while the front courtyard was being cemented, workers found a new cave to the east of the temple, buried under the sand.

The crowds have changed the valley itself. They are held responsible for major ecological changes inside it, even for the drying up of sacred ponds, and one visitor said that the water at Ashapura and the water at Kali Mata had all dried out because of the crowds.

For many years the Zikris were in charge of the shrine, until in 1986 its organisation was taken over by the Hinglaj Sheva Mandali, the Hindu temple committee of today. Before the 1980s the main caretaker of Hinglaj was Dost Mohammad, whose family had been caretakers of the shrine for many generations. Until his death in the late 1990s he guided pilgrims through the steep and challenging landscape of the valley, explaining the sites along the way. His descendants still work for the committee as cooks, guides and drivers, and in 2012 the tour to Chaurasi was still a family tradition, guided by his son, Hussain. The family could not recall a time when a woman had been in charge at Hinglaj.

The Hinglaj Sheva Mandali was founded on 5 January 1986 at Bela, in the Lasbela district, to promote a yearly pilgrimage to the shrine, and it remains the main body serving the temple and its pilgrims. The yearly gathering at Hinglaj was the idea of one man, Vasrup Chand Tulsi, who helped to found the committee for the express purpose of publicising and organising the pilgrimage. From its beginning the committee has been made up mainly of Lohanas of Lasbela, and that community has a special influence on the stories and practices of the shrine today. For many years the committee has overseen the renovation of temples, the building of bridges and the making of community places inside the valley, and in recent years it has improved the infrastructure around the temple with shelters, water tanks and roads in and near the valley, paid for by private Hindu donors at home and abroad together with grants from the government of Balochistan.

In 2009 the temple had one permanent priest, or *pujari*: Gopal Maharaj, whom the committee had appointed in 2008. Before Hinglaj he had been, in his own words, "the pujārī at the Makli temple in Thatta". As the only permanent priest, and so an authority for many visitors, he has a strong influence on the views pilgrims hold of the shrine's mythology.

The state has shaped the shrine as well. The valley gained prominence from the building of the Makran Coastal Highway. A dam planned on the Hingol would have flooded the roads to the shrine; the committee argued that these sites were not like common temples and could not simply be relocated, and its spokesman said: "This is a site of pilgrimage which is place specific." The building of a bridge over the Hingol changed the way pilgrims moved through the valley and influenced the choice of a date for the main festival in the Hindu calendar.

## The Pilgrimage

The *Hinglaj Yatra* is a pilgrimage of physical hardship and deep devotion. In the past pilgrims crossed harsh desert to reach the shrine, and the journey keeps its arduous, penitential character. Along the route and at the shrine, devotees perform their rites at a series of sacred places: they climb the mud volcanoes of Chandrakup and Khandewari, casting coconuts and offerings into the bubbling craters and voicing their prayers and vows; they bathe in the waters of the Hingol River; and they honour such places as Ganesh Deva, the Brahma Kund and the *dhuni*, the sacred fire, of Guru Gorakh Nath. Only after these observances do they approach the cave sanctuary itself.

The order of these stations binds. Many say that no one may enter the Goddess's valley without having made offerings at Chandrakup, and many pilgrims today believe that Hinglaj's temple may only be entered after visiting Chandrakup and making proper offerings there. At the temple itself the *yagya* and the *havan*, offerings into the sacred fire, are performed, and the *darshan*, the sight of the Goddess, is received. Formerly, after the sacrifice to Kali, pilgrims were not allowed to go straight on to the Hinglaj shrine, just a few hundred metres away; they had to return to the Ashapura *parav*, the halting place, and wait there until nightfall, and only then could they go on to Hinglaj.

Hinglaj has drawn pilgrims from all over the subcontinent for several centuries, and some of their journeys are remembered by year. Hamir Gohil went there in 1301; Raja Bij Singh, an aristocrat from Rajasthan, in 1499; Raja Todarmal and Raja Bharimal in the sixteenth century, though accounts of the year differ; Raja Madhu Singh in 1792; and Raja Jagat Singh in 1801. In Aurangzeb's reign Hinglaj was reckoned seventy *kos*, an old measure of distance, from Thatta, between the north and the west, near the salt sea. The difficulty of the desert road, the scarcity of water and the robbery of the Bhil made it hard for anyone to reach, and only some *faqirs*, poor wandering mystics, enduring hunger and thirst, reached it and worshipped there.

The site is sacred to the Aghori ascetics, who revere Hinglaj Mata as their patron goddess, and it holds a place in the traditions of the Nath yogis and of the Charan bards alike. Tradition has it that Hinglaj was very fond of the Nath community, and especially of Guru Gorakhnath, so that the Goddess waited day after day in Thatta for Gorakhnath to come to meet her. In early colonial times, worshippers were said to strip naked before the temple and, rolling on the rough pavement, call out like madmen to the Mother to cleanse them.

Accounts of how long the journey took differ widely, from more than fifteen days to six months. There was a sea route to Hinglaj as well, but little has been handed down about it. For centuries the shrine was famous for being extremely difficult to reach: its place in the arid desert of Balochistan, far from any town, made the journey exceptionally demanding, and many pilgrims died on the way to the shrine or back. Members of Karachi's 4x4 Off-Road Club found it only after a few years of searching. A mark of purity was once burnt onto the forearm, and a few pilgrims still seek it, though for reasons of hygiene they ask instead for the mark of a cold stamp.

Those who came home earned a title. Asked why they travel to the shrine, by car or on foot, pilgrims by far most often answer "this is our hajj", *yah hajj hai hamara*. The epithet Hinglaji, the equal of the title Hajji, is given to those who have accomplished the journey, though today it is found mainly in India. In Pakistan the word *yatra* is used mostly by Hindu pilgrims, and its Urdu equivalent, *ziyarat*, is rarely used of Hinglaj. For the Charans, those who came home were a divine gift, since, as the Charans say, the Goddess herself had blessed them and let them return safely from the brink of death. One devotee set a hard measure: "Of 1,000 Pakistanis who go to Hinglaj only 50 do it properly. They are the only Hinglajis [here]."

## The Highway and the Festival

The road changed everything. Before the Makran Coastal Highway, the journey to the Goddess's remote abode in the desert of Balochistan was often lethally dangerous for her devotees. The first part of the highway, linking the valley to Karachi, was complete and in use by 2001, and the whole road, from Liari to Gabd, in 2003. The temple, though far from any town, could now be reached comfortably by car, and after 2001 the site quickly grew into the stage for one of the biggest yearly Hindu festivals in Pakistan. The road joined the different routes into the valley, so that many pilgrimage stops fell out of use, yet it brought not only modern transport but also a revival of walking to Hinglaj. Within three decades the *yatra* changed from a strenuous bodily ordeal, undertaken once in a lifetime, into a yearly festival held in one place, now an established event in the calendar of Pakistan's Hindus. Before that, from around the middle of the 1960s until the middle of the 1980s, the shrine seems to have been scarcely visited.

The main pilgrimage now takes place over several days in April. Most pilgrimage groups set out in that month, just before the great *mela*, the festival, which falls in the Hindu month of *phalgun* or *chaitra*; accounts of the festival's month differ, but April is the month most often given. In recent years the festival has officially begun with the visit to Chandrakup on the first day and ended on the third with a collective *havan* in front of the Hinglaj temple. Four festivals matter most at the shrine: the two *Eids*, the great Muslim feasts, Muharram, and the spring *mela*. Holi and Diwali play no significant part, since they are not public holidays in Pakistan.

The Kohlis, with other scheduled castes such as the Meghvar and the Bhil, form the biggest part of the festival's visitors, more than 90 percent of them, which also reflects the Hindu population of Sindh as a whole. Early in the nineteenth century the pilgrims numbered thousands a year, and in 1805 and 1806 members of Seth Hotchand's family travelled to the shrine with 2,000 other pilgrims. Within a couple of decades the numbers have soared, from a few thousand a year to over forty thousand on a single weekend in spring: by the committee's ticket sales, over forty thousand people visited the shrine in April 2010.

The festival is run at a cost. In 2012 the entrance fee of 250 rupees provoked resentment and protest among visitors, and in 2013 the committee abolished the ticket system. Paying pilgrims had worn an ochre plastic wristband, the *bhagva patti*, and the committee engaged many young men who worked shifts day and night checking the wristbands so that no one slipped in without one. During the festival of 2012 every pilgrim also had to pay 100 rupees at a checkpoint on the approach to Chandrakup, one of the main stops on the way to Hinglaj, money that went to the police and not to the committee. The *bhandara*, the community kitchen, fed around forty thousand people on the main days of the April festival, by the committee's count; water was trucked in from the Hingol, since the stream in the valley does not carry enough for the crowds, and generators gave the festival its electricity.

The festival is also a stage. During the festivals many Hindu welfare organisations are active inside the valley, advertising their charitable work for the community, and an announcement of 1987 invited the Hindu community of the whole country, *mulk bhar ki hindu baradari*, to the *yatra*. Rites of passage are performed here: the *upanayana samskara*, the rite of the sacred thread, and the *guru dakshina*, the offering to one's teacher, are traditionally performed in and around Hinglaj, and among the Lohana communities of Lasbela the sacred thread is given to the young at the River Hingol.

For devotees in India the border is the obstacle. While Hinglaj's devotees in Pakistan can easily reach her shrine today, her worshippers in India, faced with the difficulty and cost of a visa, must often still rely on texts, videos or the accounts of the few who have been able to cross the border for the journey. There are many proxy shrines for Hinglaj in India, standing in for the shrine in Balochistan: some were founded long before the subcontinent was divided, others as a result of the strict visa policy that followed. Indian groups at the shrine are frequently treated with respect and curiosity and get regular attention from local and national television channels broadcasting all over the country, and in 2010 a party from Gujarat came under the leadership of Dayaram Kapri.

Today the Goddess Hinglaj is generally understood as a vegetarian deity who accepts only offerings that are not animals. Until recently, though, it was customary for many people to eat the animals sacrificed at the Kali shrine, just before the temple of Hinglaj. After 2001, as the shrine rose in importance as a unifying symbol for all of Pakistan's Hindus, the committee laid more and more stress on vegetarianism, and in 2004 it officially banned animal sacrifice inside the valley. A symbolic offering has appeared in its place: a goat is offered to Kali by cutting into its ear, letting a few drops of blood fall, and then setting it free inside the valley. Yet sacrifice did not simply stop. A rooster was sacrificed at the Kali Mata temple in 2010 and in later years, and members of the committee themselves secretly went on sacrificing goats in the mountains for the Goddess Kali. One visitor explained the ban in other terms: "Well, this is a nature reservoir, it is not allowed to kill animals here."

For the Devipujak-Vagris of Karachi the offering is a duty. At every visit each member of the community must give a rooster or a male goat to the Goddess Kali, or risk her wrath: for them Kali is the guard of the Goddess Hinglaj, who must be appeased before they go on to Hinglaj, and without her blessing the whole *darshan* at Hinglaj is in vain. They hold that every time a festival is held at Hinglaj and the Mother is kept from her sacrifice, she causes accidents and disease among the pilgrims and so takes her own sacrifice. As one of them said in 2012: "Since the Hinglaj Sheva Mandali has prohibited animal sacrifice, every year there are some accidents... In one way or another, the Mother takes her gift". They trace her taste for blood to the Goddess's battle with demons. One of them, Bhao Tulsi, went to Hinglaj exactly once in his life, in the middle of the 1970s, holding that the journey should be made only once in a lifetime, while the young men of his community have been to Hinglaj, most of them several times.

## Legends

A second cycle of legend links Hinglaj to the epic hero Rama. After slaying the demon king Ravana, who was a brahmin and a devotee of Shiva, Rama is said to have been burdened by the grievous sin of *brahmahatya*, the killing of a brahmin. Counselled by a sage that only a pilgrimage to Hinglaj could purify him, Rama, tradition holds, set out for the shrine to do penance. At a pass the Goddess's army, which guards the way into her realm, stopped his company; a fight broke out in which many of Rama's men died, and after twelve years of battle the king from Ayodhya and his army had to retreat. The Goddess told him that he must return to his first stop, Ram Bagh, and make the journey again as a simple pilgrim, and so Rama left his entourage, his army and his vehicles behind and set out to walk to the shrine, with only his closest friends. The Goddess promised them that their descendants would all, at some point in time, come back to make the pilgrimage.

On the way, the legend goes on, Hanuman's stamping brought forth only a dry riverbed and Lakshmana's arrow only split a hill, until, in agony, Sita laid her palm on the soil and made five wells appear, from which the group drank. At last the Goddess granted Rama purification of his sin, and to mark his completed pilgrimage he carved the symbols of the sun and the moon on the mountain opposite the temple. The carvings are still pointed out. The place is said to be unreachable by mortals, and from the bottom of the valley it does look quite inaccessible, though it is possible to climb up there. No figure was ever as present in the lore of the traditional pilgrimage to Hinglaj as Rama, the seventh incarnation of the God Vishnu.

In legend, the Goddess punishes pride. A legend heard in colonial times tells of "A wealthy Shet named Kowaljee", a *shet* being a merchant, who chartered a ship to carry his party to Hinglaj, "when the Goddess, justly indignant at his arrogance and presumption, transformed the boat and every soul on board into stone."

The Brahmakshatriyas tell how five princes, fleeing the curse of Parashurama, came here. The sage Dadhichi told them that they must go to the shrine of Hinglaj to be initiated into her tradition, and in the end they climbed the mountain at Hinglaj, where they fasted and meditated for another twelve years. The Goddess answered them: "How could the words of the mighty Parashurama become a lie? But I will help you and do what is possible"; and she added that whoever enters her cave will gain the *dharma*, the way of life, of an ascetic.

To Mekan Dada, it is told, the Goddess came disguised as an ordinary shepherd, wearing Balochi dress and with a herd of goats she had created by the power of her mantra, and she could not fill his cup: "a hundred thousand liters came out, but still she could not fill the glass". His vow names the whole round of the valley: he would go to Chandrakup, climb up to Anil Kund, worship at her temple, put vermilion on the place where Rama sat down, and see the moon and the sun carved into the stone there. Tradition holds, too, that the Tumbel Charans spread the Goddess's fame in Lasbela, and that Hinglaj therefore decided to settle in a cave close to the Hingol River, where the Charans and other communities have worshipped her ever since.

Some say the Goddess has left her temple. A visitor from Karachi held that she no longer lives at the shrine, having grown annoyed with the crowds herself, and that one must climb two and a half hours up to Anil Kund, where she has taken refuge. A colonial visitor heard that she moves between the valley and an island, where she stays for the greater part of the day, for seven *pruhurs*, the watches into which the day is divided. At Anil Kund, local tradition holds, Shiva came to these mountains to meditate, and some of the water of the Ganges found its way from his hair into the hollow; and it is said that eighty four thousand rivers came together to form the pond, which is why the place is also called Chaurasi, eighty four.

A great stone in the valley is said to have been set there by the Goddess. Sages once meditated in the mountains, so that common people dared not disturb them, and the pilgrims asked the Goddess to arrange some other way for them to receive the merit; accounts of how the stone came there differ, but this is the story the temple's priest tells. He tells, too, of the end of the Sharan Kund as a miracle: after a child drowned in it, "After only fifteen days there was so much rain that a huge landslide filled the pond", and the pond disappeared only because a mother who had lost her child cried before the Goddess.

Devotees hold that the Goddess protects the road. As pilgrims on the way put it: "There are many wild animals on the way, but the Mother always helps, and never has anyone been hurt. Nothing has ever happened to us. This is because we have the power of the triśūl, the chaṛī, and the jhoṃk, the flame that burns all the time in the rath. This flame is like our friend; it is sent by the Mother." A pilgrim named Giresh walked for his sister: "I came since I wanted my sister to have a child. I had already come twice, once on a bus and then on a motorbike, but my Mother told me that I must come on foot. Then [the Goddess] heard my wish and my sister had a child. This is why I am now walking to her shrine."

Indian pilgrims see her hand in their visas: it is she, they say, who moves the staff of the Pakistani embassy to issue them, or makes airport security staff look away at the right moment. There are many legends of pilgrims disappearing in the tunnel. On the road, it is said, the Goddess Ashapura used to sit to welcome the pilgrims, and a Gujarati businessman put up a statue of Ashapura there; and camels are not allowed to cross into the valley, since their feet, it is believed, would swell from the presence of the Goddess.

Dreams have carried her worship to India. Anoop Dan dreamt that, since her shrine in Balochistan could no longer be reached, Charans should visit the site of Temra Rai in its place, and the head of a temple in Delhi said that his guru had been told to build her temple there in India, because there was no proper worship in Pakistan. Rumours that the Taliban had blown up the cave temple, and that three pilgrims from Rajkot had been killed on the road in 2006, both turned out to be sheer fabrications.

## Verse, Song and Story

In the mystical poetry of Sindh, the sacred mountain Hinglaj in Balochistan is often named as "the goal". One of the *surs*, the sections, of Shah Abdul Latif's poetry is written in praise of the yogis who walk from the village of Ganji Takar, near today's Hyderabad, to the sacred mountain Hinglaj and suffer every possible discomfort. In his Sur Ramakali the caravan of the *swamis*, the holy men, comes to Qalat and from there takes the way to Hinglaj; burning in the fire of love, they set out for Hinglaj, and in the end the saints reach Kabul, the distant place beyond the hills. The pilgrims of these verses are called Sami, that is *swami*, Kapari, Nang, "naked", or simply Babu, and they are marked by slit ears and matted hair. They resemble the Nath yogis of Kabir's poetry, who, though worshippers of Shiva, appear as the true monotheists, and the seeker enchanted by the yogis, the true saints, tries to follow them to the inaccessible heights of Mount Hinglaj.

Though Hinglaj lies west of Sindh, in the Lasbela region, the yogis' journey there is still "towards the East". For the poet, East is the eternal spiritual homeland, alluded to in the very name of Sur Purab, "East", and so the pilgrims to Hinglaj are said to go East, that is, to find illumination. Shah Abdul Latif himself is said to have wandered through the country with a group of yogis, perhaps reaching Hinglaj with them, though there is no settled view of whether he did.

The poet also turns the pilgrimage inward:

*Those who are happy when sitting, the head put on their knees, / Yogis make the pilgrimage, they have reached divine essence (uluhiyyat)* (translated by Annemarie Schimmel)

And again:

*the Kaparis have not seen Kabul and Kashmir, / Those who make the journey of truth, sitting they meet the guru* (translated by Annemarie Schimmel)

The true journey is *safar dar watan*, the interior journey, as Sassui, the heroine of a Sindhi romance, was told at the end of her road: the foreign kingdoms are in the seeker's heart.

The poetry of Sachal Sarmast names Hinglaj among the sacred places of the Hindus, with Kasi, or Kashi (Banaras), Sri Lanka and the Ganga. A retelling of the Janamsakhis, the traditional stories of the life of Guru Nanak, brings the Guru here too: from Thatta he went west to Hinglaj, where he met a group of pilgrims who had set out on the Haj.

For the Charans, Hinglaj is the source of verse itself. The Goddess, they hold, grants Charan men the gift of writing and composing stories and poems; she is their muse, and they imagine her sitting on a poet's tongue, quickening his creativity. A Charan saying runs: "From the moment a Charan is born, Mother Hinglaj waits for him to come for the darśan"; if he leaves the world without having visited the shrine, a stone falls from the Mother's mountain. The stories and the poetry of the bards keep alive "a continuous history" that ties local goddesses to the shrine in Balochistan.

Books and films have shaped the pilgrimage as much as roads. The Bengali novel *Marutīrtha Hiṃlāj*, The Desert Pilgrimage to Hinglaj, published in 1957 by Abadhut, that is, Dulalchandra Mukhopadhyay, made the journey known to a wider public together with the film made from it in 1959, and the film above all cast Hinglaj into popular culture and helped to foster the pilgrimage's image of extreme hardship. In the novel, in 1946, pilgrims gather at the Nagnath *akhara*, a lodge of ascetics, in Karachi, from which members of the local Nath community guide pilgrims once or twice a year to the shrine of Hinglaj Mata. People from all parts of India, such as Gujarat, Rajasthan or Bengal, meet there to await the start of the journey into the desert of Balochistan; after a month enough have gathered to make a pilgrimage party, and cameleers from the area around the shrine are engaged to guide them.

The novel follows them to the rite. Early in the morning the pilgrims go to the shrine, bathe and put on fresh clothes to enter the womb of the Mother, and they find only a small altar, a *bedi*, on which a lamp is burning. As leader of the group Abadhut enters first; inside the tunnel he has a vision of the Goddess gazing at him and laughing, and a few minutes later he comes out on the other side. The Aghori Baba advises him never in his whole life to speak of what he has seen in the tunnel. The Aghori Baba also shows the group the symbols of the sun and the moon, carved into the mountain, as he explains, by Ramachandra on his journey to Hinglaj. In a Hindi novel by Devadatta Shastri the sanctum is a cave around fifty feet high holding a *vedi*, a place for sacrifices, on which a lamp is burning. The pilgrims, forbidden to cross the stream toward the cave during the day, crawl on all fours through a small door on one side of the *vedi* into a semicircular tunnel that leads to another door on the other side of the platform; when the pilgrim comes out, the Aghori Baba, who had followed them, garlands him with a *mala*, a garland, from the *vedi*, and the rite is complete.

## The Setting

Hinglaj lies in Balochistan, about two hundred and fifty kilometres from the coastal city of Karachi, in the Sindhi speaking area of the province. Unlike many other rivers in Balochistan, the Hingol flows throughout the year, and the mud cones of the region last because of Balochistan's low rainfall. After the first part of the Makran Coastal Highway, the N 10, was completed in 2001, the area around the River Hingol was connected to the National Highway 25, a heavily travelled road that links Karachi with Quetta. From Manora one looks out on the Makran mountain range, the boundary between Sindh and Balochistan, and the hill slopes of Balochistan are among the places where the folk tales of Sindh are set.

Most of the people of other faiths who remain in Pakistan are Hindus of its faraway, peripheral regions, Balochistan among them. Balochistan has been in a state of latent insurgency since the establishment of Pakistan, and Indian pilgrims must obtain an additional visa to travel there. In November 2016 a bomb blast at the shrine of Lahoot la Makan killed more than fifty people, curbing the growing flow of visitors and creating a volatile situation for the shrines of Balochistan.

## Legacy

Hinglaj Mata Mandir stands as the foremost living Hindu pilgrimage site in Pakistan and a shrine of the very first rank among the *Shakti Peethas*. Its endurance, in a remote and challenging landscape and across the fault lines of religion and history, has made it a powerful symbol of resilience for Pakistan's Hindu minority and a place of extraordinary sanctity for devotees of the Goddess throughout the subcontinent. The wider Hingol cultural landscape has been proposed for recognition on account of its natural and spiritual importance.

Its remoteness is part of its new place. Close to a highway, yet far enough from the cities and from possible threats, Hinglaj has become a safe haven for undisturbed Hindu celebrations, a remote stage for public displays of Hindu faith that draws worshippers from all corners of the country. In 2012 an official of the Jinnah Institute called Hinglaj a "high risk for catastrophe", yet it was one of the few places in Pakistan that offered a relatively secure public space for a massive Hindu festival. Rooted in an ancient history, the site has seen some of its most disruptive changes within only a few decades.
