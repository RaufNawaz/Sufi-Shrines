## Overview

Gurdwara Panja Sahib lies in the small town of Hasan Abdal, in the Attock District of Pakistan's Punjab, some fifty kilometres north west of Rawalpindi. It is among the most revered of Sikh shrines and one of the most important in the country, celebrated above all for the great boulder it enshrines, on which, devout tradition holds, the open right hand of Guru Nanak, the founder of Sikhism, is miraculously imprinted. The shrine takes its name from this relic: *panja* means "the palm" or "the open hand". Set beside pools fed by springs, whose clear water flows over the marked rock, Panja Sahib is at once a place of miracle, a memorial of the Guru's travels through the north west, and the scene of a famous act of self sacrifice in 1922.

The *gurdwara*, the Sikh house of worship, has mountains on one side and the whole town of Hasan Abdal on the other, and from the mountain of Wali Kandhari, the Muslim holy man of its legend, it can be seen in the plain below. A few pilgrims come all year round to pay their respects at the shrine associated with the founder of the Sikh religion, and at the festival of Baisakhi in April they come in their thousands.

## The Guru's Visit

Guru Nanak was born in 1469 in what is today Nankana Sahib, and he died at Kartarpur in 1539. For more than twenty years he travelled on the long journeys known as his *udasis*, mostly on foot, east to Bengal, south to Sri Lanka, north to Kashmir, Nepal and Tibet, and west to Mecca; accounts of how many journeys he made differ, but they are most often counted as four. He preferred to stay on the outskirts of villages, and whenever he came to an area he would visit its religious shrines, Muslim, Hindu and of any other denomination.

The legend of the hand is told in more than one form, and accounts of when the Guru came differ. Tradition sets it on one of his *udasis* in the early sixteenth century, often dated to around 1521, when he was travelling with his devoted Muslim minstrel, Bhai Mardana. Weary and thirsty, the story goes, the Guru and his companion halted at the foot of a hill near Hasan Abdal. On its summit lived a Muslim *pir*, or holy man, remembered as Wali Kandhari, his name recalling an origin in Kandahar, who controlled the only spring in the vicinity.

Tradition tells that Mardana, parched, was sent up the hill to ask for water and was rebuffed; twice more he climbed, and twice more the *pir* refused, taunting the pair. Then, it is said, Guru Nanak touched a stone below, and a fresh spring welled up at the foot of the hill, even as the water at the summit ran dry. Enraged, Wali Kandhari is said to have hurled a great boulder down upon the Guru. Nanak, the story continues, raised his open hand and stopped the falling rock, which yielded to his palm as if it were soft wax and kept the imprint of his *panja*. Humbled by the miracle, the *pir* is remembered as having bowed before the Guru.

The tale's message, that spiritual grace outmatches worldly pride and that mercy answers arrogance, has made it one of the best loved episodes of Sikh sacred lore, and its image has travelled far from Hasan Abdal. In the gurdwara at Manora in Karachi there was a poster of Gurdwara Panja Sahib, and another of Nanak stopping with his hand a rock hurled at him from the top of a mountain where the Muslim saint was.

Other legends tell of Nanak and Muslim Sufis: of Hamza Ghous, who threatened to destroy Sialkot and whose people Nanak saved, and of Mian Mitha of Pasrur, a Sufi who became Nanak's devotee. Yet Nanak himself is remembered as one who abhorred the association of miracles with religious personalities, and in one story he tells Karori, who calls his cure a miracle, that "there is no such thing as a miracle".

## The Gurdwara

A deep imprint resembling a right hand is shown on the rock, over which spring water continually flows, and pilgrims come to touch and reverence it. The place is said to have been named Panja Sahib by Hari Singh Nalwa, the renowned general of Maharaja Ranjit Singh's Sikh Empire, who is credited with raising the first gurdwara here in the early nineteenth century; accounts of who built it differ. The shrine that grew up around the relic includes prayer halls and a *sarovar*, or sacred tank, fed by the springs, whose waters gather into pools where devotees bathe. The *Guru Granth Sahib*, the Sikh scripture, is installed within, and the customary *langar*, the free communal kitchen, is served. The setting, with its clear water, its shade trees and the hill rising behind, is known for its serenity.

In 2011 the main shrine was a grey building crowned with three white and yellow domes, with colourful cloths tied to the railing around it, and buildings of two storeys on all four sides held rooms for the pilgrims. Its façade was pink, and an iron gate closed off the inside. There were four entrances into the main shrine, one from each side, taken to symbolise the openness of the religion. Within, glass patterns recently put up by the Government of Pakistan in its renovation covered the fading frescoes; a wooden palanquin stood in the centre, a young Pathan Sikh read the Guru Granth Sahib, and pilgrims rested.

The sacred pool, said to have been created after the miracle of Nanak, lies in the shadow of the main shrine; accounts of its age differ. In 2011 its water was turquoise, and white marble stairs carrying the names of donors from Canada and the United States led down into it. Water still gushed into the pool through a stream that flowed into the complex from under protective walls recently raised by the Government of Pakistan, and newly built stairs led down to the stream, which came out behind the residential quarters and then flowed out of the complex.

The rock faces the main shrine from inside the pool. It is a grey stone, partly under water, in a small niche whose protective arch is made of ivory, with a black mark in the middle that looks like a handprint. There is a window on each side, through which women offer their devotion, and a marble plaque beside it describes its significance in Gurmukhi, the script of the Sikh scriptures.

Behind the main shrine stands the *kirtan* hall, the hall for the singing of hymns. In 2011 statues of two Sikh soldiers, dressed in yellow clothes and blue turbans and holding spears, stood guard at its entrances; the stage carried a picture of the gurdwara itself in the centre and one of Guru Gobind Singh at the top left, and behind the reader hung a picture of Guru Gobind Singh collecting money for *langar*. A staircase led underground to two spacious corridors where pilgrims without rooms lived. Behind the residential quarters stood the Langar Hall, newly built, of two storeys, and an alley ended in an open space surrounded by more buildings for the pilgrims, with a garden covered by tents to give makeshift rooms.

## The Saka of 1922

To the memory of the Guru's miracle, Panja Sahib later added the memory of martyrdom. Under British rule, amid sectarian divisions among the Sikh communities, a large number of important Sikh gurdwaras in the province had come to be administered by Hindu *mahants*, the heads of religious establishments.

On 30 October 1922, at the height of the Gurdwara Reform Movement, a train carrying Sikh prisoners was to pass through Hasan Abdal railway station. They had been arrested during the *morcha*, or non-violent agitation, at Guru ka Bagh near Amritsar. The local *sangat*, the congregation, wished to serve *langar* to the captives, but were told that the train would not stop. Refusing to let their brethren pass unfed, Bhai Pratap Singh and Bhai Karam Singh, leading the congregation, lay down on the rails, and others followed. The driver braked hard, but not before the engine had struck the foremost of them.

Bhai Karam Singh, a man of about thirty, and Bhai Pratap Singh, some twenty four years old, died of their injuries and were hailed as martyrs. The episode, remembered as the *Saka* Panja Sahib, the martyrdom at Panja Sahib, entered Sikh memory as a supreme example of devotion to the duty of feeding and serving others, and until the Partition of 1947 an annual fair was held at the shrine in the martyrs' honour.

The tradition of *langar* is believed to have begun with Guru Nanak. In Aurangzeb's time the followers of Baba Nanak, as the Guru was also called, counted the service of the traveller, of all who come and go, as the highest worship: whoever arrived at midnight speaking Baba Nanak's name was treated as a brother and a friend and served, even a stranger, a thief or a highwayman.

## Baisakhi at Hasan Abdal

Baisakhi, when Sikh pilgrims gather to commemorate the formation of the Khalsa, the order of initiated Sikhs, is the shrine's great festival. As a boy named Amir put it, Sikhs come from India and other parts of the world to Hasan Abdal for Baisakhi, and they also visit the gurdwara at Mandi Mangat.

In 2011 pilgrims spent the 13th, 14th and 15th of April at Hasan Abdal, and most then went on to the other holy Sikh shrines. For those three days and nights the streets were cordoned off, no Muslim was allowed to venture near the gurdwara, and the local schools closed, their classrooms turned into hostels for the pilgrims. The market filled with thousands of turbaned Sikhs. By local media reports about seven thousand pilgrims came for the festival that year, about two thousand from India and the rest from within Pakistan, and more than two hundred people stood in the line at the entrance.

Not all of them came from across the border. Many were Pathan Sikhs, fair skinned and blue eyed, from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and the tribal areas; others were clean shaven Hindus from different regions of the country. Some walked the tar road without shoes as an expression of extreme respect.

Entry was guarded, and a police officer checked everyone's security card. Until the year before, a turban or head cover had been enough, and those who looked more Muslim were turned away only if they were Muslim; in 2011 the security was intensified, and everyone needed a card from the Special Branch. Until recently Muslims had been allowed to pay their respects at the shrine of Baba Guru Nanak, but no longer. A television crew that threatened to broadcast its treatment was let through. By tradition anyone may enter a gurdwara, but the practice no longer existed in Pakistan.

Inside, *kirtan* in Punjabi, to the harmonium and the *tabla*, a pair of hand drums, carried across the complex. It usually includes verses from the Guru Granth Sahib, a tradition that goes back to Nanak's Muslim devotee, Bhai Mardana, and listeners offered notes and coins to the musicians. At the Granth devotees waved a fly whisk, a tradition that comes from Bhai Bala, the Hindu devotee of Guru Nanak, and the final chapters were read, marking the conclusion of the *Akhand Path*, the unbroken reading of the whole scripture. Paintings popular in Sikh hagiography show a serene Nanak listening to the songs of Bhai Mardana, who holds a *rubab*, a plucked string instrument, while Bhai Bala stands behind them holding a fly whisk.

At the pool men stood praying in the water, an old man swam, and children hung back on the stairs. Volunteers passed buckets of pool water to wash the white marble floor of the courtyard, a sacred duty called *sewa*, voluntary service, and cooked and served at the Langar Hall. Devotees queued for the *darshan*, the sight, of the stone, placed a hand on the mark while pouring water over it, and some kissed it.

At one Baisakhi a pilgrim in his sixties, in a saffron headscarf, put his hand on the mark and found that his hand too fitted Nanak's. "Miracle!" he said, and he laid a ten rupee note on the rock, kissed it, poured water from the pool over it and cleaned it. Devotees queued behind him, hands reached out from the windows on either side of the rock, and women placed money on top of it.

Some kept watch over the water. In 2011 Surjeet Singh, a Pathan Sikh of thirty five from Peshawar whose family came from a village in the Khyber Agency, chased boys out of the pool, "This is no place to play!", as his way of performing *sewa*. His shirt read "Never forget 1984", with the image of Sant Jarnail Singh Bhindranwale.

The procession with hymns, the *Nagar Kirtan*, had been drawn in. Until a few years before it had marched around the town of Hasan Abdal as local Muslims looked on; in 2011, because of the growing threat from Islamic extremists, it remained within the protected confines of the gurdwara. "I have always wanted to come to Pakistan. This is the land of Guru Nanak," said Avtar Kaur, fifty five, from Amritsar, but "here I have heard that the procession will not be allowed to leave the premises of this Gurdwara. That is sad."

Foreign pilgrims were usually given rooms, while Pakistanis settled wherever there was space. In the underground corridors older Indian pilgrims recalled the Khalistan days of insurgency, Khalistan being the name of a proposed separate Sikh state: Harvinder Singh, sixty five, whose shop in Lajpat Nagar in Delhi had been burnt, Jasdeep Singh, fifty four, and Virinder Kaur, fifty five, from a village in Amritsar District. Sikh boys served *doodh-soda*, milk with soda, at a stall, and volunteers guarded the crowded Langar Hall. Maninder Singh, a photographer of forty four from Amritsar, said, "I actually belong to Pakistan": his parents came from a village in Gujrat and from Shah Alamgir, and on his visit for Guru Nanak's festival the year before he had taken a brick from his mother's ancestral house.

The festival was also a market. A T-shirt reading "Never forget 1984" sold well at a stall behind the main shrine, kept by a boy from the south of the country, and a woman in a sari bargained over its price before confessing, "I don't understand this currency." On sale were CDs of *Sikh Gurdwaras in Pakistan*, a documentary sanctioned by the government, a poster of all the functioning gurdwaras of Pakistan, and Ravinder Singh's latest collection of *kirtan*. From behind an iron gate a man threw *nimko*, a savoury snack, into the crowd. Vendors sold head covers with Khalsa symbols, plates for *aarti*, the worship with lamps, and posters of Hindu deities and Sikh Gurus. Most of them were Pakistanis who travelled all over the country through the year attending Hindu and Sikh festivals. One of them, Vishal Chand, said, "I belong to the city of Multan but I live in Quetta"; almost all his goods came from India, brought by friends and relatives on pilgrimage.

## The Guru's Verse

The worship at Panja Sahib is sung, and its *kirtan* usually includes verses from the Guru Granth Sahib. Nanak's *bani*, his devotional verses of supplication, entreaty, humility and reverence, was compiled as part of the Adi Granth by Guru Arjan Dev, the fifth Guru, in 1604. The Guru composed verses in Hindi on the truth of God and on divine unity, and a singer named Mardana sang his verses and drew people into the snare of devotion. In Aurangzeb's time the worship of his followers centred on reading their master's verses, which they recited with song and melody.

His *aarti*, the hymn sung as lamps are waved in worship, begins:

*In the salver of the firmament, / The sun and the moon shine as lamps*

On the oneness of God he sang:

*My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One*

And again:

*God is One, / The Path is One*

His poem on the sacred thread, the *janeu*, runs:

*From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment*

Of washing he sang:

*As hands or feet besmirched with slime, / Water washes white*

And in another song:

*The herons in their white feathers dwell in the sacred shrines of pilgrimage*

The *Babur Bani* is a long poem that Nanak wrote at the time the Mughal Babur attacked the Punjab, destroying the cities of Eminabad and Lahore on the way, and one of its lines runs 'Coming in seventy-eight (1521 C.E)'.

The Guru's verse is sung outside the gurdwaras too. The singer Abida Parvin has said: "I sing Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah, Mian Muhammad Bakhsh, Hazrat Khawaja Ghulam Farid, Sacchal Sarmast, Baba Farid, and Guru Nanak." In Sindh, Sain Vasan Shah became famous for reciting the *banis* of Shri Guru Nanak and the *kalams*, the poems, of the Sufis.

## The Setting

Hasan Abdal was a historic town that had kept its character, with narrow, curving streets and wooden balconies, set on hillocks. A few Hindu temples survived, used as makeshift houses or in some cases as wandering grounds for stray animals. Facing the gurdwara stood the remains of a Mughal garden in a dilapidated state, a garden mentioned in the *Ain-i-Akbari*, written by Abu-Fazl in the reign of Akbar, the third Mughal emperor.

In 2011 the town was home to about fifty to sixty Sikh families, who had moved there in the few years before from the tribal areas because of the rise of insurgency there, while the rest of the town was Muslim. Most of them spoke only Pashto, and they were mostly confined within the boundaries of the gurdwara, protected by police officials, tall walls and barbed wire on all sides. Sikh families from the tribal areas had moved to Peshawar, Hasan Abdal and Nankana Sahib and started living at gurdwaras.

Wali Kandhari's own shrine stands on the mountain above the gurdwara. It is a separate Muslim shrine, and at Baisakhi, when thousands descend upon the gurdwara, several of the pilgrims travel up to it as well.

A Mughal emperor returning from Kashmir, with his retinue, his servants and his elephants, made his halt at Hasan Abdal. Two of his intimates who died on that road, Mir Fathullah Shirazi and, before him, Hakim Abu'l-Fath Gilani, were buried there. The royal standards rested at Hasan Abdal for some time, and a garden was laid out before the court left for Kabul.

## Legacy

Since 1947 Panja Sahib has lain within Pakistan, cared for by the Evacuee Trust Property Board and the Pakistan Sikh Gurdwara Prabandhak Committee, and it remains one of the principal destinations of the Sikh *jathas*, the bands of pilgrims who travel from India and the diaspora on the great anniversaries, above all the Baisakhi festival, when the shrine draws large and joyful congregations. Baisakhi at Hasan Abdal was among the few religious events kept alive by the foreign pilgrims after Partition.

There are several sites all over Pakistan where Nanak stayed or is said to have performed miracles, and the gurdwaras built there later by his devotees still stand tall. Another impression of the Guru's body is shown at the shrine of the goddess Hinglaj, where the engraved footsteps of Guru Nanak connect that temple to the Sikh and Nanak *panth*, or sect, traditions.

Devotion to the Guru reaches beyond the Sikhs. Many Muslims acquainted with his teachings are devoted to him too, and Nadeem, a Muslim painter, said that "Guru Nanak is like a Muslim Sufi Saint". A Chishti Sabiri Sufi master of the twentieth century described Guru Nanak, with Krishna, Rama, Sita and Lachman, among the spiritual luminaries outside Islam, as a believer in the one true God and a religious leader with a divine connection, *nisbat*. Baba Farid's writings are revered among the Sikhs, for he is held to have influenced the founder of their religion.

Uniting a miracle of the founder Guru with a modern tale of sacrifice, and a marked rock with living springs, Gurdwara Panja Sahib holds an honoured and enduring place among the sacred sites of the Sikh faith.
