## Overview

At Uch Sharif, in the southern Punjab, stands the shrine of Sayyid Jalaluddin Bukhari, who lived from 1308 to 1384 and is known by his honorifics *Makhdoom-i-Jahaniyan*, "the Master of the World", and *Jahangasht*, "the World Traveller". A grandson of Jalaluddin Surkh-Posh Bukhari, he was among the most learned and widely revered saints of the Suhrawardi order of Sufis, or Muslim mystics, in fourteenth century India, a tireless traveller and preacher whose authority was sought by sultans and commoners alike. His shrine is one of the principal *darbars*, or saints' shrines, of Uch Sharif and a major centre of pilgrimage in the southern Punjab.

In his time Uch was, for a while, the centre of the Suhrawardi order, chiefly thanks to him: after the death of Shaikh Rukn al-Din the order had declined at Multan, and at Uch it was renewed. He was held in the highest esteem by the rulers of the Delhi Sultanate, particularly in the Tughluq age, brokered the peace between Sultan Firuz Shah and the Samma ruler of Thatta, and left a line of descendants and disciples whose branches reached into many regions of the subcontinent. His shrine stands in one of Pakistan's richest concentrations of sacred architecture, and it is kept by his descendants.

## The Saint

Jalaluddin was born at Uch on 15 Sha'ban 707 AH, in the Hijri or Islamic calendar, which fell on 9 February 1308. He came of the Husaini sayyids, the descendants of the Prophet through his grandson Husain, of a respected family of Bukhara, and he was born into the Bukhari sayyid family founded by his grandfather, Sayyid Jalaluddin Surkh-Posh Bukhari, "Surkh-Posh" meaning the one clad in red. The grandfather was a disciple of Shaikh Baha al-Din Zakariya. He had three sons, Sayyid Ahmad Kabir, Sayyid Baha al-Din and Sayyid Muhammad, and his successor was his son Sayyid Ahmad Kabir. Accounts of the saint's father differ, but he is most often named as this Sayyid Ahmad Kabir, and Jalaluddin and his younger brother, Sayyid Sadr al-Din, known as Raju Qattal, were his sons.

Sayyid Ahmad Kabir, also called Ahmad Kabir al-Din, was himself a venerable Sufi. He attended Shaikh Jamal Khandan Ru, and his inward training was given by Shaikh Sadr al-Din Arif of Multan.

In full the saint was Sayyid Abu Abdullah Jalal al-Din Husain bin Ahmad Bukhari, *bin* meaning son of. His name was Sayyid Jalal al-Din Husain; Abu Abdullah was his *kunya*, a name of honour formed with *Abu*, "father of"; and his title was Makhdoom Jahaniyan Jahangasht, though some called him simply Makhdoom Jahaniyan. He was honoured, too, with a chain of titles of praise: the lamp of the family, the manifestation of the lights of the Lord, the dawning place of the epiphanies of the All Glorious, the unveiler of the mysteries of the Path, the guide on the ways of Reality, the master of guidance, and the leader of the *awtad*, the saints called the "pegs" of the world.

He began his schooling at Uch under the learned Qazi Baha al-Din, a *qazi* being a judge. After the *qazi*'s death he came from Uch to Multan to finish two textbooks of Islamic law, the *Hidaya* and the *Bazdawi*, under Shaikh Musa and Maulana Majd al-Din, and then he went to Medina and studied further.

Having received a formal religious education, he became his father's disciple. He took his first *bay'a*, the pledge of initiation, at his father's hand in the Suhrawardi order, the Sufi order founded by Shihab al-Din Suhrawardi, and from him received the *khirqa*, the patched cloak of the Sufi, as the mark of his *khilafat*, the authority to guide others. He then drew inward grace from his uncle, Sayyid Sadr al-Din, known as Muhammad Ghaus, and at Sadr al-Din's direction he became the disciple of Shaikh Rukn al-Din Abu al-Fath of Multan, completed his training under him and received from him, too, the *khirqa* of *khilafat*. He is counted among the famous *khalifas*, or spiritual deputies, of Shah Rukn-e Alam, together with Maulana Zahir al-Din, Sultan Hamid al-Din Hakim, Shaikh Fakhr al-Din Iraqi and Shaikh Sama al-Din Dehlvi. Of Shaikh al-Arif at Multan he said that he taught every beginner and every advanced student without any distinction.

He wore other *khirqas* as well. He claimed the *khirqa* of his sayyid descent from Hazrat Ali, the robe that had come to Ali from the Prophet, and he wore the *khirqa* of Baha al-Din Zakariya, which he had received from his father. The *khirqa* is said to have come to him from many other masters besides, among them Shaikh Qawam al-Din, the *khalifa* of Rukn al-Din Abu al-Fath, Qutb al-Din Munawwar, Imam Abdullah Yafi'i, Shaikh Ishaq Kazaruni, Shaikh Amin al-Din, Shaikh Hamid al-Din, Sayyid Ahmad Rifa'i, Shaikh Najm al-Din Kubra, Shaikh Nur al-Din and Hazrat Khizr, the immortal guide of seekers in Muslim tradition.

He made the Hajj, the pilgrimage to Mecca, and at Mecca he stayed with the Shaikh al-Islam, the chief religious dignitary, Shaikh Afif al-Din Abdullah Matri, for outward and inward benefit. He spent six years in Medina and Mecca and returned home in 752 AH. At Medina, when he led the prayer in the Prophet's mosque in the imam's absence, he stood out of reverence one row behind the place where the Prophet used to stand.

He earned the name *Jahangasht* through his travels. He journeyed across the Muslim world, performed the Hajj many times and visited the great centres of learning and sanctity of his age, drawing grace from hundreds of guiding saints, though accounts differ over how often and how far he travelled. For most of the reign of Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq he seems to have lived abroad, while his *khanqah*, the Sufi lodge where disciples gather, was administered by his brother.

After his return he went to Delhi, where Shaikh Nasir al-Din Chiragh-i Dihli also initiated him into the Chishti order, a Sufi order prominent in South Asia, and gave him a further *khirqa* of *khilafat*. He is said to have taken *bay'a* and *khilafat* in fourteen *khanwadas*, or spiritual families, and the practice of initiation into more than one order seems to have been started in India by him. He was a Suhrawardi who also had close ties to the Chishti order.

He married, and of his sons, Sayyid Mahmud Nasir al-Din also lived at Uch and attracted disciples from Multan and neighbouring areas; he had a very large family and is believed to have died in 847 AH, 1443 to 1444. Another son was named Muhammad.

Makhdoom Jahaniyan died on 10 Zil-Hijja 785 AH, the day of Eid al-Adha, the Feast of the Sacrifice, which fell on 3 February 1384; accounts of the year of his death differ. Most accounts place his grave at Uch, where his shrine became a focus of devotion.

## The Sultans of Delhi

Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq appointed him Shaikh al-Islam and made him head of the Khanqah-i Muhammadi, a Sufi lodge at Siwistan, the town of Sehwan, to which several villages were attached. He accepted, and for a time he held the office over Siwistan and its dependencies and discharged it well, but he did not remain there long: he resigned at the order of Shaikh Rukn al-Din Abu al-Fath.

Muhammad's successor, Sultan Firuz Shah Tughluq, was his devotee and obedient to him. In Firuz's reign the saint lived mainly at Uch and came several times to Delhi. One of his visits lasted from Sunday 8 Rabi II 781 AH, 24 July 1379, to Tuesday 17 Muharram 782 AH, 23 April 1380. The Sultan gave him a royal reception and went on visiting him in his *khanqah* throughout his stay, and so great was the saint's hold on him that all his recommendations were granted. From him, after his arrival from Uch, the Sultan learned to say the *chasht*, the morning prayer, and the *tahajjud*, the prayer of the night, and he derived many benefits from his company. The governors of Sind, too, sought his help.

He used that regard for others. When Sultan Firuz, advised by the *ulama*, the religious scholars, summoned Shaikh Sharaf al-Din from Bihar to account for his conduct, the order was cancelled at Makhdoom Jahaniyan's request, and it seems that his strong recommendations, which neither the Sultan nor the *ulama* could ignore, saved Shaikh Sharaf al-Din from the axe. The abolition by Sultan Firuz of about twenty eight taxes, laid largely on the towns of the empire, may be ascribed to his influence.

He is credited, too, with the peace of Thatta. Accounts of its year differ, placing it between 1362 and 1367, but when Sultan Firuz marched on Thatta with a large army, the Samma ruler, Jam Jauna bin Babinya, sought to arrange terms of peace through him. It was agreed at last that the Jam's son, Mani, should rule as a tributary of Sultan Firuz of Delhi, and the peace was generally hailed as a triumph for Makhdoom Jahaniyan.

It is told that Khan Jahan, the *wazir*, or chief minister, of Sultan Firuz, had imprisoned the son of a *muharrir*, a clerk. The saint went nineteen times to plead for him and was not received. The twentieth time Khan Jahan sent word asking whether the Sayyid had no shame. He answered, calling him dear one, that every time he came he earned merit, and that he wished to free the wronged man from Khan Jahan's hand so that Khan Jahan too might reach the rank of merit. Khan Jahan came to him bareheaded, with a rope round his neck, asked his pardon and became his *murid*, his disciple, and he released the clerk's son with a robe of honour and a horse. When he offered the saint a *nazrana*, a gift of homage, the saint gave it to the clerk's son.

At Delhi the Sultan and his nobles used to pay their respects to him. Shaikh Ahmad, though urged to become the saint's disciple, would not leave his own *pir*, his spiritual guide; and on a visit to Delhi Makhdoom Jahaniyan called on Ahmad-i Khattu and embraced him, telling him that he reminded him of his friend, Baba Ishaq.

## Teaching and Devotion

He strongly opposed the customs that had grown up around Shab-i Barat, the festival held in the evening of the middle of Sha'ban, the eighth month of the Muslim calendar, when fireworks were let off, graves were whitewashed and lighted lamps were placed on them. Muslims, he taught, should pray and give to the poor the money that would otherwise go on crackers and lamps; only in India, he added, was there such disregard of the holy law, for such innovations were not to be found in Ghazna, Khurasan or Arabia. He sternly disapproved of giving God such Hindi names as Thakur, "Lord", Dhani, "Rich", and Kartar, "Creator", and he advised the *a'imma*, the imams, not to accept any payment that came from illegal sources of revenue.

He urged dervishes, Sufis and *ulama* to visit rulers, officials and the rich in order to win help for the deprived, and he justified his own visits to Sultan Firuz on these grounds. Dervishes, however, should never allow such people to visit them at their *khanqahs*, and if it did happen, the time should be used well, in preaching on the meaning of the *Sharia*, the religious law. Royal stipends, he ruled, were *makruh*, disapproved, in the collections of *fatwas*, or legal rulings, but not invalid under the *Sharia*. He preferred to call his disciples *Akhi*, "brothers", an ideal he took both from the Akhi system of the craft guilds of Anatolia and from the Akhi and Futuwwa brotherhoods of the dervishes of Khurasan and Transoxiana. Beginners on the mystic path, he taught, should not concern themselves with trying to understand the *Wahdat al-Wujud*, the doctrine of the unity of being, for it was for experts only.

His teaching ranged over the *khirqa*, over patience, *sabr*, and the fear of God, *taqwa*, over the office of the shaikh, and over *shari'at*, *tariqat* and *haqiqat*, the law, the path and the Truth. Whenever he took a *murid*, he would say that he was only a *wakil*, an agent. He distinguished two kinds of *khirqa*: the *khirqa-yi tasawwuf*, the cloak of Sufism, is the robe of companionship, *suhbat*, while the *khirqa-yi tashabbuh*, the cloak of likeness, is the *khirqa* of blessing, *tabarruk*, for which companionship is not a condition. For being a shaikh he laid down three conditions, and without all three it was not valid: that he be learned in the sciences of *shari'at* and *haqiqat*; that the *ulama* of his age accept him and become his believing *murids*; and that he seek nothing but God Most High. His daily *wird*, the litany he recited, included the teaching that *Ya Hayy, Ya Qayyum*, "O Living One, O Sustainer", is the Greatest Name of God.

Five hundred *rak'ats*, the cycles of the ritual prayer, by day and night were his custom, and even in his last years he prayed a hundred; he could recite the Qur'an in all seven of its *qira'at*, or canonical readings; he performed much *dhikr*, the remembrance of God, aloud; and his dress was plain. Whether he listened to *sama'*, the Sufi practice of spiritual listening to music, is not settled. His humility reached to the turban: he prayed in a cap, saying that the turban is for men alone, and "I am not a man".

He is said to have written in almost every religious field, and his disciples gathered his teachings. At Delhi his disciple Abu Abdullah Ala al-Din Ali bin Sa'd bin Ashraf bin Ali al-Quraishi al-Husaini recorded his discourses in the *Khulasat al-Alfaz Jami al-Ulum*; the saint encouraged him in the work and explained points he could not understand. Another disciple, Makhdumzada Abdullah, compiled a summary of the discourses he gave on different occasions, the *Siraj al-Hidaya*. The *Khazinat al-Fawa'id al Jalalaliyya*, composed by Ahmad bin Ya'qub in 752 AH, 1351, and the *Khazinah-i Jalali* of Abu al-Fazl bin Ziya are compendiums of his teachings.

## Disciples and Descendants

His successor was his younger brother, Sayyid Sadr al-Din, who won fame under his nicknames, Raju and Qattal, "the Slayer", for his militant evangelism; his *sajjada*, the prayer carpet and seat of a saint's successor, passed to his brother and not to his son. Among his *khalifas* were Qazi Abd al-Malik, known as Shah Ajal, and his brother Raju Qattal, and the Suhrawardi line spread further through the sons and grandsons of both Makhdoom Jahaniyan and Raju Qattal.

In his time the order spread from Uch to Gujarat, the Punjab and Kashmir, and its influence reached even the Delhi of Sultan Firuz Shah. Shaikh Qiwam al-Din went to Uch to receive initiation from him in the Suhrawardi order, and a Shaikh Qawam al-Din, a famous elder of his time, is counted among his *murids* and *khalifas*. To Shaikh Rajgiri, a native of Daryabad in Awadh, he gave the title *Akhi*, and he gave him his *khirqa* when Akhi first settled at Kanauj. Kalpi became a centre of the order through the prestige of Shaikh Siraj Sukhta, a favourite imam of his; it is told that Qadir Shah, the ruler of Kalpi, sought Siraj Sukhta's protection, and that the shirt the Shaikh gave him to wear cured him. In Kashmir the Suhrawardi order was revived by Sayyid Muhammad Isfahani, a disciple of his, and Sayyid Jamal al-Din, a Sufi of his order, made a spectacular impact on the Sufis of Kashmir. Ashraf Jahangir Simnani came to Uch and drew on his spiritual grace and blessings.

Makhdoom Jahaniyan carried the prestige of the Bukhari sayyids of Uch to its height. He was one of the masters of the second period of the Suhrawardi order, and he was then based at Uch. A guide with a wide following, he strengthened the Suhrawardi presence across the Punjab and Sindh, and his descendants and disciples spread the *Jalali* Bukhari line far and wide, a lineage of sayyids who remained religiously and politically prominent for centuries. His grandson was Qutb Alam. He stands seventh among the ancestors of Mauj Darya of Lahore, with Jalal al-Din Surkh-Posh ninth, and he appears with Shaikh Rukn al-Din Abu al-Fath of Multan in the spiritual chain of Mian Wada. Sain Qutb Shah was a Husaini Bukhari sayyid of his line, and Qutub Ali Shah was his descendant in direct line. Among the male ancestors of the modern saint Zauqi Shah, traced for thirty six generations back to the Prophet Muhammad, was Makhdoom Jahaniyan; after the fifteenth century that lineage fell into obscurity, until it suddenly reemerged into history during the British Raj.

The customs and ideas of the Qalandars, dervishes who wholly abandon legal norms and social conventions, and of the Nath yogis, an order of Hindu ascetics, had a considerable impact on the beliefs of a section of his followers. Unlike other Sufis they adopted Shi'ism, and like the Madari dervishes they went naked and sat before fires to warm themselves; their hair was unmatted, but they shaved their heads, beards, moustaches and eyebrows, and spent their time wandering from place to place. When they came into the order they offered all their goods to their preceptors, and in return they were given a cap and the spiritual tree of the order, worn on the head or hung round the neck. Of these Jalali saints, those who had achieved a certain standard of perfection would, it is said, like the Haidari dervishes, swallow snakes, calling them Ali's fish, and they called scorpions Ali's prawns. *Malang* dervishes too, wandering ascetics, and others called Dudae, naked from head to foot, were counted among his *murids* in every quarter, and they performed the *dhikr* aloud.

## The Shrine

The shrine of Makhdoom Jahaniyan Jahangasht is a busy *darbar* amid Uch Sharif's famous complex of tombs, and it is kept by his descendants. It stands close to the celebrated tiled tombs of Uch, among them that of Bibi Jawindi, who is held to be his descendant, though accounts of her relation to him differ.

By the 1870s his *roza*, his tomb, at Uch was a place of pilgrimage for the people. Pilgrims come throughout the year to seek the blessing of the Master of the World. The shrine keeps the customary devotions, prayer, *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis, and *langar*, the free communal kitchen, and its annual *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death, draws crowds from across the region.

In his lifetime Mir Sayyid Ali, the Hamadani sayyid, passed through Uch on his way to Kashmir and visited the saint's *khanqah* there; when Makhdoom Jahaniyan was told of the visit, he took little notice and remarked merely that *Hamadan*, "the All Knowing", was none but the Great Knower of the Invisible World, God Most High.

## Legends

Tradition tells how he came by the title Makhdoom Jahaniyan. On a night of Eid he kept vigil at the tomb of Baha al-Din Zakariya and then presented himself at the tombs of other saints; at each he asked for an *idi*, an Eid gift, and from each the title Makhdoom Jahaniyan was conferred on him.

The stories begin in his childhood. At seven, it is told, he was given dried dates from a tray set before Shaikh Jamal al-Din Khandan, and he ate them stones and all, explaining that there was blessing in the stones too, and that to throw them away would be disrespect. It was said of him that whenever he embraced anyone, he drew into himself at that moment whatever gift that person had.

Two *khirqas*, it is said, came to him in dreams, one from Rukn al-Din Abu al-Fath and one from Nizam al-Din Auliya, and each was found on his head when he woke. At Medina, tradition holds, he greeted the Prophet's grave with peace, calling the Prophet his grandfather, and an answer came to him from the grave, returning the peace and calling him son.

One Ramadan, the month of fasting, it is told, he kept *i'tikaf*, a retreat of prayer, in the Jami Masjid, the congregational mosque, of Uch. Sumra, the governor of Uch, came into the mosque, where many people had gathered about the saint, and turned them all out. The saint asked Sumra whether he had gone mad, that he troubled the dervishes, and no sooner had he said it than Sumra went mad. His mother brought him and asked pardon, and the saint told her to bathe Sumra, clothe him, take him to the tomb of Hazrat Jamal al-Din Khandan, and then bring him back. She did so, and after Sumra had made his apology, through the saint's prayer he was entirely restored.

At Delhi, it is told, people distressed by drought asked for his prayer, and so much rain fell that, overwhelmed, they asked him to pray for it to stop; he prayed, and the rain stopped.

One story turns on a moment's distraction. His youngest son, a child of four, played near his prayer mat during the *chasht* prayer and drew his attention. Afterwards he told Sayyid Shams al-Din that it seemed hard to him that the boy would live, for in the very midst of the prayer his heart had inclined towards him; at the time of the noon prayer the boy fell into a fever, and that same night he died.

It is told, too, that a saint of Panipat reached Delhi by spiritual power in a single hour, found Makhdoom Jahaniyan in the throes of death, prayed for him and gave him some years of his own life, and returned to Panipat in a moment.

Tales of his journeys carry him further still. In them he made the rare and hazardous journey to China and Mongolia, converting the people he met on the way; he met yogis, all of whom in the end he converted to Islam; and he travelled through Ceylon, where Islam had failed to penetrate, and converted a yogi there too. All the stories of three generations of the Sufis of Uch tell of contests with Hindu yogis in feats of supernatural power, and they habitually end with the vanquished embracing Islam.

Conversions are ascribed to him. It is believed that the Sumirahs of lower Sindh, who were Isma'ilis, a branch of Shi'a Islam, adopted Sunni Islam under his influence, and a Jat chief of Sindh, afterwards named Abdullah, is said to have embraced Islam through a supernatural feat that he performed.

A story is told of his death bed. Nawahun, a Hindu who was the *darogha* of Uch, a revenue official entrusted with keeping accounts in Persian, visited him as he lay dying, and while praying for his recovery declared that the Sayyid was the seal of the saints, just as the Prophet Muhammad was the seal of the prophets. The saint held that these words amounted to a profession of faith. Nawahun was afterwards handed over to Sayyid Raju, and when he again refused to embrace Islam he was executed.

## The Setting

Uch Sharif is an ancient city some seventy miles from Multan, near Bahawalpur in the Punjab, built on a hill overlooking the confluence of the Sutlej and Chenab rivers. Its name means high or great. In April 1996 a visitor spent a day wandering through the magnificent blue and white tiled mosques and desolate tombs of this ancient centre of learning, trade and pilgrimage. Uch Gilanian, the city of saints in Bahawalpur, was where the family of the poet Bulleh Shah, who were sayyids, had settled, and where he was born. Muhammad Bakhsh, of a hereditary family of *qawwals*, the singers of *qawwali*, at Kasur, once said, "We came with Bulle Shah's family from Uch Sharif, and we have learned his poetry from our ancestors, who have always sung here". In the early 2000s the landowning sayyids of Bahawalpur and Multan supported the shrines of their ancestors.

A legend of the town's founding tells of Shaikh Safi al-Din Kaziruni, who was ordered to mount a camel, travel wherever the animal led him and remain where it finally halted. The camel halted in the middle of a desert, and there the Shaikh founded a town later called Uch. Another legend of Uch tells of a contest in flight with a yogi.

Uch was also a seat of the Gilani Qadiris, Sufis of the Qadiri order, which traces its descent to Shaikh Abd al-Qadir Jilani of Baghdad. The first missionaries of the Qadiri order settled at Uch in the late fifteenth century, and from there the order spread into the subcontinent, where it soon gained a firm footing, and was carried as far as Indonesia and Malaysia. Shaikh Muhammad al-Husseini al-Jilani, a native of Turkey who travelled to Khurasan and then Multan before settling with his family in Uch, founded the first Qadiri *khanqah* there, and his son, Abd al-Qadir Jilani Thani, who died in 1533, is the famous Qadiri saint of Uch Sharif. Four sons of Hazrat Muhammad Ghaus lie at Uch Sharif beside the tomb of Shah Muhammad Ghaus, and in the nineteenth century some of Abd al-Qadir Sani's descendants held the *sajjada* at Uch.

In the nineteenth century the sayyids of Uch were famous. Where a dispute over a man's descent arose and they accepted his lineage as sound, the matter was closed, and no one then dared say a word against it. The elders of the Faqir family lived at Uch Sharif Sayyidan and served the sayyids of Uch, and a Gilani forebear of Hazrat Pir Muhammad Shah came from Uch Sharif in the time of Akbar Shah.

Uch lay on the roads of armies. The first campaign of the conqueror Shihab al-Din took it and left a deputy there, and his second came again to Multan and Uch and went on to Gujarat; Mirza Pir Muhammad crossed the Sind and took Uch and Multan; and on one march toward Jodhpur, Uch was a dependency of Multan. In Aurangzeb's reign it was a *qasba*, a small town, and the province of Thatta ran from the borders of Multan and Uch to Thatta and Kach Makran.

On one journey to Uch Sharif, the boundary between Sindh and Punjab became a blur on the road through Bahawalpur, where the cultures of the two provinces merge: some roadside hotels were called Punjab Hotel and others Sindh Hotel, and the Siraiki, the language of the southern Punjab, spoken there was more like Sindhi, while in Multan it was closer to Punjabi. The older city stood on a mound, with the massive *havelis*, or mansions, of the Gilanis and the Makhdooms. The group of shrines of the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries was reached through the market, and on the mound were graves covered arbitrarily with the blue and white Multani tiles that had been recovered from these historical shrines. The river Sutlej, the lost river of Pakistan, used to flow from here, before it was lost after the Indus Water Treaty was signed between India and Pakistan in 1960. Guru Nanak, the founder of the Sikh faith, is said to have visited the town on his way to Sindh, after he had settled down. Of his traces there, a well lay hidden within a field, shielded by a grove of trees, abandoned, with juice tetrapaks and wrappers floating in its black water, and beside it a grassy mound was said to have been the Gurdwara of Nanak, a Sikh place of worship.

## Legacy

Makhdoom Jahaniyan Jahangasht is remembered as one of the great Suhrawardi saints of medieval India, the learned and far travelled Master of the World whose authority spanned the Sultanate and whose lineage shaped the religious landscape of the Punjab and Sindh for generations. His memory is bound to that of his grandfather Surkh-Posh and to the whole flowering of sanctity that made Uch Sharif a spiritual capital of the south, and his shrine keeps alive the memory of the age when Uch, with Multan, was a beating heart of Sufism in the subcontinent.

In the poetry of the Punjab he is one of the Five Pirs, saints credited together with special powers and with miraculous intervention for Ranjha, the lover in the romance of Hir and Ranjha by Waris Shah. In the poem, "Sayyid Jalal of Bukhara, whom men also call Makhdum Jahanian" is among the holy men whose help Ranjha seeks; Ranjha "saw by their countenances that they were holy men and besought their help". His gift to Ranjha is a dagger, which the hero grasps in a later passage:

*and he grasped the dagger given him by Makhdum Jahanian* (translated by Charles Frederick Usborne)

The poet Bulleh Shah, too, invoked the Five Pirs in his lyrics, a company in which Jalal ud Din Makhdum Jahaniyan of Uch is counted.
