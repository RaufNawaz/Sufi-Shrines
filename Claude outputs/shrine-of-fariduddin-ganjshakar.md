## Overview

At Pakpattan, on the old bank of the Sutlej in southern Punjab, stands the shrine of Hazrat Baba Fariduddin Mas‘ud Ganjshakar, known as Baba Farid, one of the founding saints of the Chishti order of Sufis, or Muslim mystics, in South Asia, and among the most beloved holy figures of the Punjab. A disciple and spiritual heir of Khwaja Qutbuddin Bakhtiar Kaki of Delhi and the master of the great Nizamuddin Auliya, he made the town then called Ajodhan into a centre of Sufi devotion so important that it came to be renamed Pakpattan, "the Holy Ferry". His tomb, perhaps the most famous shrine of its kind in the Punjab, remains one of the greatest places of pilgrimage in Pakistan, most of all when its *Bahishti Darwaza*, the Gate of Paradise, is opened at his *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death.

He was also a pioneer of Punjabi as a language of spiritual poetry, and verses attributed to him were later gathered into the Sikh scripture, the Guru Granth Sahib, a mark of how far his memory reaches across the communities of the Punjab. He has been called Pakistan's most famous Sufi saint and the patron saint of Pakistan, and since the partition of India Pakpattan has stood as the unrivalled centre of a distinctly Pakistani Sufism. The shrine is the focal point of the town, which seems to have grown up entirely around it.

## The Saint

Baba Farid was born around 1173 at Kothewal, near Multan, the village known today as Tibba Haji Deen; accounts of the year of his birth differ. He came of a family of learned émigrés of Central Asian origin, who traced their descent to the Commander of the Faithful, Hazrat Umar ibn al-Khattab, and counted themselves of the house of Farrukh Shah, king of Kabul. His forebears left Kabul for the Punjab in the middle of the twelfth century, driven by the upheavals of the age, the destruction of Ghazna by Ala al-Din Husain of Ghur in 1150 and 1151 and the invasion of the Ghuzz six years later.

Accounts of the family's coming differ, but his grandfather is said to have come from Kabul. He is remembered as Qazi Shuaib, who came to Lahore with three sons and his goods, went on from Lahore to Kasur, and was made *qazi*, or judge, of Kothewal, a town in the Multan region between Maharan and Ajodhan, by the Ghaznavid sultan of the Punjab; there he settled. Farid's father is most often named Shaikh Jamal al-Din Sulaiman, and his mother Qursum Khatun, daughter of Mulla Wajih al-Din Khujandi. His father died while he was still a small child. He had an elder brother, Azz al-Din Mahmud, and a younger, Najib al-Din Mutawakkil, "the Trusting in God", and the three brothers were held to be unique in their age; he had a sister too, Hajira, famous by the title Jamila Khatun. Najib al-Din became his brother's disciple and *khalifa*, his deputy; the woman saint Bibi Fatima Sam used to call him her own brother, and he carried the family's messages between Hansi and Ajodhan. Farid's own given name was Masud, and the name Farid al-Din, it is said, was bestowed on him by Hazrat Farid al-Din Attar.

His mother was an exceedingly pious woman who prayed through long vigils at night, and while his father was a scholar, it was she who exerted the greater influence on the future saint and first set him on the path of prayer. She is counted among the women of their families who contributed to the spiritual formation of great Sufi leaders, and by the 1870s she was remembered as the gnostic of her age, a woman whose prayers were answered and whose every word came to pass.

He was taught at home, and he had memorised the Quran by the age of twelve. As a young man he went to Multan to study, reading the *Nafi*, a famous book of *fiqh*, Islamic law, with Maulana Minhaj al-Din Tirmizi, who taught in a mosque there, and acquiring the religious sciences. There, it is told, a chance meeting changed his life. Khwaja Qutbuddin Bakhtiar Kaki, the Chishti master of Delhi, came to Multan and entered the mosque in the Sarai Halwai quarter where the young man sat with his book. After the prayer the Khwaja asked what he was reading. The *Nafi*, came the answer; and when the Khwaja asked whether he knew that his benefit would come from this *Nafi*, the young man replied that his benefit would come from kissing the Khwaja's feet, and laid his head on them.

He asked to become the Khwaja's disciple, and in time he followed him to Delhi; accounts differ over where he took his pledge of initiation, at Multan or at Delhi, and over whether his master first sent him away to complete his studies at Qandahar. He is said to have travelled widely as well, to Iraq, Syria, Siwistan, Ghazni, Bukhara and Qandahar, and to have benefited from the company of many masters, among them Shaikh Shihab al-Din Umar Suhrawardi, Shaikh Baha al-Din Zakariya and Shaikh Farid al-Din Muhammad Attar Nishapuri.

In Delhi he lived in a small cell near his master's *jama'at-khana*, the hall where the master's disciples lived and gathered, and under guidance he gave himself to the severe austerities for which he became famous. When Khwaja Mu'in al-Din visited Delhi, he was so impressed by them that he took a special interest in the young disciple, foretold his later fame, and asked Khwaja Qutbuddin to join him in prayer for his disciple's future greatness. In renunciation and austerity, in knowledge and practice, in inner unveiling and in miracles, Farid was held to have outstripped everyone of the Chishti line, and he was wholly given to spiritual taste and longing, to love, to ecstasy and to *sama*, the Sufi practice of listening to music. His fast was perpetual; his days were given to instructing the people and his nights to worship. He is said to have stood in contemplation for twenty years without ever sitting, his feet swollen and bleeding, and to have kept the *roza-e tai*, a fast unbroken by night, and the *roza-e Da'udi*, the fast of David. His reputation for vigils, fasting and penances went back to his boyhood, when the severity of his observances made people think him crazy. Disciples of the Chishti Sabiri order hold that he underwent twelve years of spiritual training before completing his journey on the path.

Among his devotions was the arduous *chilla-e-ma‘kus*, the "inverted" *chilla*, a *chilla* being a retreat of forty days in seclusion. He asked his master's leave to perform it, and the Khwaja, knowing that such exercises gave a devotee unnecessary publicity, permitted it only at last. It required a man to tie a rope around his feet and remain suspended head down in a well for forty days and nights, fasting and praying. Farid found a lonely mosque in Uch and, taking its *mu'azzin*, the caller to prayer, into his confidence, performed the *chilla* there, the first Sufi of India to do so. In this he carried on a Sufi practice of Central Asia; he never made it binding on his followers, but it was integral to his own discipline, and he saw such hard bodily ascesis as crucial for forcefully dispelling selfishness.

It is from the sweetness that his long fasts are said to have miraculously produced that he earned the title *Ganjshakar*, "the Treasure of Sugar". Many stories are told of how he came by the name, and the one most often told is of the pebbles. Overpowered by fasting for three days, it is said, he put some pebbles in his mouth, and at once they turned to sugar. Believing this to be the work of the devil, he spat them out; but at last, overcome by extreme hunger, he ate some pebbles that had become sugar so that he could go on praying, and his master approved, telling him that whatever was received from the unseen world must necessarily be good.

After receiving from his master the *khirqa*, the patched cloak of the Sufi, as the mark of his *khilafat*, his authority to guide others, he settled at Hansi, in the Hisar district, for his fame in Delhi had become an obstacle to prayer and meditation. Tradition adds that he went for the sake of a *majzub*, one spiritually intoxicated and absorbed in divine love, Mian Saranga Hansvi, who wept that now the saint sat on the seat of guidance the crowds kept him even from the sight of him. It was mainly his deep affection for Shaikh Jamal al-Din, who lived at Hansi, that kept him there for twelve years: to Baba Farid, Shaikh Jamal was his *jamal*, his beauty.

He was at Hansi when Khwaja Qutbuddin died, and he reached Delhi five days after the death. By his master's will, Qazi Hamid al-Din Nagori, with Badr al-Din Ghaznavi, handed over to him his master's relics: the prayer carpet, the turban and the wooden sandals that had come down hand to hand from the masters of Chisht, and the master's cloak and staff. Farid put on the cloak, prayed two cycles of prayer on the prayer mat, and stayed in his master's house. The relics marked him as his master's successor, and he became the head, or *qutb*, of the Chishti order; but his asceticism and his complete withdrawal from the world left no room for rivalry with Shaikh Badr al-Din, who came from Ghazna and succeeded the Khwaja in Delhi. Seeking distance from the intrigues of the capital, in a politically confused time, he left Delhi and settled in the Punjab, on the river Sutlej, at Ajodhan.

## Life at Ajodhan

Ajodhan, known today as Pakpattan, did not welcome him. When he came, after wandering from city to city, its people were harsh in their manners, worshipped the world and had no faith in dervishes, and he hid himself among them under a *karir* tree. Accounts differ over when he arrived and how many years he lived there, but it was his home for the rest of his life. He lived far from the major cities where other Chishti masters were increasingly settling, on one of the main routes from Multan to Lahore and Delhi, and in shifting the centre of the order from Delhi to Ajodhan he meant it to keep its distance from imperial authority, for he did not wish to associate with the rulers. A Jami mosque, the mosque of the Friday congregation, had already been established in the town, with a resident *qazi*, and a Muslim governor sat at nearby Dipalpur; the mosque, now part of the shrine complex, is large enough to show that a substantial Muslim population lived there at that time.

He lived near the Jami mosque in a small house of mud walls under a thatched roof, for he had strictly forbidden the use of burnt bricks, which by Chishti tradition were not simple enough for an ascetic's dwelling, and its door stayed open until midnight as a welcome to visitors. His possessions were few. A small rug served as his sitting rug by day and as his blanket by night, though it hardly covered him, so that covering his feet uncovered his head; his master's staff rested behind his head as a pillow; and his clothes were tattered. His *madrasa*, his school, was like a poorly funded public school whose students could not even afford a proper meal.

His food was wild fruit and millet bread. Fasting by day, he broke his fast in the evening with a cup of sherbet, often mixed with dried grapes, of which he never drank more than half, dividing the rest among his visitors. After prayers two pieces of bread smeared with ghee were brought to him; one he gave away, and the other he ate himself, sharing it with his favourite disciples. When he first came to Ajodhan, he and his household lived on wild fruits such as *pilu*, and there was no salt in the food; his circumstances forced him to let his disciples carry the begging bowl round, which they did for years, and such was his trust in God that whatever came in was spent. Outside the town grew a grove of *karir* trees, and under one that was especially thick he used to spend his time in meditation. Nizamuddin remembered that the night he ate *delah*, the fruit of the *karir*, was like a festival to him, a gift of grace.

He kept an open *langar*, the free communal kitchen, though his own family often went hungry. It was at Ajodhan that his children were born, and they often suffered the pangs of starvation and great hardship. He refused the land grants that were offered to him and gave away all the money and other gifts brought to his lodge, and his family is said to have faced starvation many times as a result. Like Qutbuddin before him, and Nizamuddin and Nasir al-Din after him, he never made the *hajj*, the pilgrimage to Mecca: having embraced poverty, they were not technically able to go.

He met with hostility there. The *qazi* of Ajodhan was his fierce opponent, hostile to the Sufi movement, and the saint was harassed by the *qazi*'s officers and by the rudeness of the townspeople. Once the *qazi* asked the *ulama*, the scholars of religion, of Multan for a *fatwa*, a legal ruling, against anyone who lived in a mosque, was known as a dervish, listened to music or danced; the *ulama* refused when they discovered that it would be used against Baba Farid. The *qazi*'s opposition did not succeed, and the saint went on guiding the people, but official oppression continued: a *qalandar*, a wandering dervish, made an attempt on his life, and later the saint became convinced that a Muslim magician was using witchcraft to kill him. The son of Shihab al-Din the sorcerer is said to have worked magic against him, and the saint forgave him his offence.

He kept kings and their gifts at a distance, and carefully avoided the court and its ministers. To a Sufi leaving Ajodhan for Delhi he gave a warning against the *maliks* and *amirs*, the nobles and commanders of the court: "Have nothing to do with maliks and amirs, and beware of their intimacy as dangerous; no darwesh ever kept up such intimacy, but in the end found it disastrous." He refused all government grants for his family and his disciples. When he was offered a *farman*, a decree, granting him villages, with a gift of cash for the dervishes, he returned the deed and accepted only the money, which he spent on the dervishes; accounts differ over whether the giver was Ulugh Khan or Sultan Nasir al-Din. By this total rejection of government patronage he averted a crisis that threatened the Chishti order, and convinced his disciples that the wisest course was to accept no land grant of any kind. Holiness, he said, could be obtained only by ignoring princes; yet the rule against government service applied only to his *khalifas*, and not to his ordinary disciples.

Verses he recited before Ulugh Beg reminded him of the just rule of Firidun and urged a high degree of tolerance. When an official asked him to intercede with the governor of Ajodhan, he sent the governor a message asking him to treat the man kindly, but the governor refused to heed him. Ulugh Khan, who became Sultan Ghiyath al-Din Balban, was too discreet to ask the saint to pray for his succession to the throne, but he paid close attention to the saint's words for a propitious omen, and it is said that the saint foretold his crown and throne. Asked for a letter to Sultan Ghiyath al-Din Balban on a petitioner's behalf, the saint wrote: "I entrust this man's fate to God and then to you. If you give him something, God is the giver but you will be thanked for it." A note of his to Sultan Ghiyath al-Din, written in Arabic, has been preserved too. Later Chishti tradition holds that Balban gave him a daughter in marriage, but the story is doubted, and later records are thought to exaggerate the closeness of the saint and the sultan.

His last years were hard. As a result of Sher Khan's hostility towards him, merchants and wealthy supporters seem to have stopped sending gifts to the Sufis of his *jama'at-khana*, so that he, his family and the rest of his community were reduced to near starvation. When Sher Khan turned against him, he is said to have recited:

*'Alas! You have no knowledge about me; / When you would gain knowledge about me, you would repent.'*

Yet the crowds came. He was tremendously popular, and the soldiers of Sultan Nasir al-Din's army also visited him. Once the great crowd so concerned him that he left his house; a sleeve of his shirt was hung from the roof facing the street, and the people who came to kiss it tore it to shreds. He went to the mosque, where at his instruction his disciples formed a circle around him so that the devotees could not come too close, but a *farrash*, a menial servant, broke through the circle of his protectors, fell at his feet and kissed them.

Already in his lifetime two kinds of people gathered around him: the full time residents of his lodge, initiated into the Chishti order, who lived a communal life of a strongly ascetic kind, and the masses, who came to receive amulets. One who came was Sayyid Muhammad bin Mahmud Kirmani, a merchant on the road between Kirman and Lahore, who used to call on the saint at Ajodhan; he became his disciple, at last settled in Ajodhan, and served him loyally for about eighteen years, and his grandson was Amir Khurd, author of the *Siyar al-Auliya*. Hindu yogis are said to have visited his retreat and held discussions with him, and the subjects discussed at his gatherings were of great interest to visiting Siddhas, whose beliefs were founded on Hatha Yoga.

He married and had a large family. Accounts give him between two and four wives, among them Bibi Kulsum, the mother of Shaikh Nasr Allah. Some of his children died of starvation in infancy, but five sons and three daughters survived and outlived him. The eldest son, Nasir al-Din, lived as a cultivator. The second was Shihab al-Din, and the third Badr al-Din Sulaiman, who would succeed his father at the shrine; it was mainly through Badr al-Din that the descendants of Baba Farid spread throughout India and became a significant part of the Sufi movement of the subcontinent. The fourth son, Nizam al-Din, preferred the life of a soldier, and he is said to have been killed fighting a Mongol army that had attacked Ajodhan. The fifth, Shaikh Yaqub, was a *malamati*, one of the Sufis who hide their piety and invite the blame of others, and he died in obscurity. A saying of Khwaja Yaqub describes the *muwahhid*, the one who truly confesses the oneness of God: his main concern is good action, whatever he does aims at divine grace, and water does not drown him and fire does not burn him.

His daughters were Bibi Mastura, Bibi Sharifa and Bibi Fatima. Bibi Sharifa, who was widowed very young and never remarried, was the most pious, and her father often said that if women could have become *khalifas* he would not have hesitated to make her one. Bibi Fatima is remembered as a prominent daughter through whose elder son the Faridi line of blood descent runs, and one of Baba Farid's daughters was married to his disciple Shaikh Badr al-Din Ishaq. His descendants, the Faridis, have shaped the continuation of Chishti identity, and so numerous did his line become that even in the seventeenth century Baba Farid himself was called "the Second Adam".

He died at Ajodhan in the month of Muharram, the first month of the Islamic year, in 1266; accounts of the year of his death differ. It is told that after the evening prayer in congregation he fell unconscious; on waking he asked whether he had prayed, and he said the prayers a second and then a third time, asking, "Who knows what may happen?" At the end he cried out, *Ya Hayy, Ya Qayyum*, "O Living one, O Eternal one!", and gave up his soul to God. Accounts of his age at death range from ninety to ninety five years.

His fourth son, Nizam al-Din, was then with Balban's army at Patiali, about two hundred kilometres east of Delhi, and when he reached Ajodhan the coffin was being taken for burial outside the city walls. He suggested to his brothers that it be brought inside, so that pilgrims could also receive spiritual guidance from the descendants of Baba Farid, and his advice was taken; so the grave came to lie within the town. The household was so poor at the end that there was no money in the house for a shroud, and the door of the house was broken up and its bricks set in the grave.

## Disciples and Successors

Baba Farid's most outstanding *khalifa* was Nizamuddin Auliya. Nizamuddin came to Ajodhan at the age of twenty, leaving Delhi in 655 of the Hijri calendar, 1257 or 1258, and was initiated at once and his head shaved; the saint offered him a bed in the *jama'at-khana*. He made three visits in all, the third and last in February 1265, and after it he was appointed *khalifa* in Delhi: on 29 June 1265 he received from Baba Farid his *khilafat-nama*, the written warrant of his *khilafat*. Near the end of his life Baba Farid delegated his authority to this young follower, charging him to become the embodiment of love, through whom the comforting shade of divine mercy would pour forth in the capital city of Delhi, reaching every corner of the Islamic empire in India. His parting words to him were remembered: "You will be a tree under whose shadow the people will find rest." Accounts differ over how the tokens of Baba Farid's authority, his staff, his sandals and his robe, came to Nizamuddin, but they came to him, and when Nizamuddin was laid in his own grave his body was covered with the cloak he had received from Baba Farid.

The training was severe, and the method by which Baba Farid prepared Nizamuddin to take up his place as successor in the order stayed engraved in the disciple's memory; the *pir*, the spiritual guide, Baba Farid explained, is the *mashshata*, the hairdresser, of his *murids*, his disciples. It is told that after eating the cowpeas Nizamuddin had bought by pawning his turban, Baba Farid blessed the disciple's kitchen, saying that he had asked God that seventy three maunds of salt, a maund being a traditional measure of weight, be spent in his guest house every day. Later Nizamuddin found himself unable to abolish prostration before the *pir*, because both Qutbuddin Bakhtiar Kaki and Baba Farid had permitted it, and he drew up the diplomas of his own disciples by the strict procedure Baba Farid had used.

The number of his *khalifas* is not settled, but he is most often said to have invested seven with spiritual power. By his own account they were chosen in three ways. The most outstanding, the *Rahmani khalifas*, were those whom God inspired him to choose; the second were those he chose himself for their own merits; and the third were disciples he accepted as successors on the recommendation of others. Among his disciples, Jamal al-Din of Hansi and Nizamuddin were his special friends.

His deputies carried written warrants that had to be sealed at Hansi. It was his custom that every *khalifa* sent out to a country carried a *farman* over Baba Farid's own signature, which Khwaja Jamal al-Din Hansvi sealed at Hansi before the deputy set out. If Shaikh Jamal sealed a warrant it was reckoned authentic, and if he did not, Baba Farid would not accept it either. The rule guarded against forgery, for a disciple whom he thought unworthy of a warrant had threatened to prepare a document of his own and use it to enlist disciples. Maulana Badr al-Din Ishaq, who was also the steward of his *jama'at-khana*, prepared the copies and signed them, and they were then endorsed by Shaikh Jamal al-Din, the saint's senior disciple. When Jamal al-Din received his warrant he received from Baba Farid a copy of the *Awarif al-Ma'arif*, a book whose study Baba Farid popularised, and it passed down to Jamal al-Din's grandson through Nizamuddin.

In Jamal al-Din's house, it is told, lived a *kaniz*, a maidservant, and a chaste woman, whom Baba Farid addressed as Mother of the Faithful. When the saint asked her what their Jamal was doing, she answered that since meeting Baba Sahib he had wholly abandoned his village, his means, his estate and his work as a preacher, and girded himself to hunger and labour; and Baba Farid, delighted, gave praise to God and wished him joy. To Jamal al-Din's young son, Burhan al-Din Sufi, he gave the *khirqa* of *khilafat*, the prayer mat and the staff. Shaikh Jamal al-Din lies buried at Hansi.

Accounts of Sabir are not settled, but tradition makes him the disciple whose warrant Jamal al-Din tore. Ala al-Din Ali Ahmad Sabir, it is said, came to Ajodhan with his mother and lived with his maternal uncle, Baba Farid. The tradition goes on that the saint entrusted him with distributing the *langar*, that over twelve years he never ate a morsel of it, and that when Baba Farid learned this he called Ala al-Din *sabir*, the patient, and the title stuck. It tells that Baba Farid entrusted him with the *wilayat*, the spiritual domain, of Delhi, and told him to have his warrant sealed at Hansi first; that Sabir rode into Jamal al-Din's lodge at Hansi still seated in his palanquin; and that Jamal al-Din tore the warrant up and would not seal it. At Ajodhan, the story ends, Baba Farid said that what Jamal had torn Farid could not stitch, and entrusted Sabir instead with the *wilayat* of Kaliyar, signing the deed himself and this time not sending him to Jamal al-Din. In the same tradition, Baba Farid gave Sabir his daughter Khadija Begum, known as Sharifa, in marriage. Sabir's followers came to regard him as the true inheritor of Baba Farid's saintly authority, and the only one of his followers to hold closely to the saint's rigorous concentration on the control of the body.

Through these two disciples the order divided. The centuries old division between its two great branches stems from his time, and the branches are named after his disciples Ala al-Din Ali Sabir and Nizamuddin Auliya; Sabir is the founder whose name the Chishti Sabiri order bears. Not every Chishti followed Nizamuddin, even if the majority did, for Nizamuddin was not the only follower of the passionate and ascetic Baba Farid. In the hierarchy of the Chishti order Baba Farid stands below Qutbuddin Bakhtiar Kaki and above the pair of Sabir and Nizamuddin. He is counted the third of the five human pillars on whom the Chishti order was built in India, one of the three Chishti giants, with Nizamuddin and Nasir al-Din, who complete its first cycle, and one of the first generation of the order's masters born in India.

Other disciples pass through his story. Shams al-Din Turk was told at his leave taking to settle at Panipat, because its *wilayat* had been entrusted to him; but Baba Farid declared his own grant of *khilafat* insufficient for him, saying that his attaining grace and perfection depended on another guide, and sent him to Sabir at Kaliyar. Sabir once sent Hasan the *qawwal*, the singer of Sufi devotional music, away with a few *gular* figs, which Hasan brought to Baba Farid at Ajodhan; the saint shared the figs among those present, and whoever ate of them, it is said, was rid of his grossness and gained inner light. A grandson, Shaikh Ala al-Din, received into Islam a Hindu *majzub*, Sohan, called Ajodhani Pak Pattani, and Shah Kaku Chishti of Lahore was of the saint's progeny. Generations later, Burhan al-Din Gharib asked Nizamuddin for the gift of being under the direct spiritual gaze, the *nazar*, of Baba Farid.

## Teaching and Poetry

Baba Farid's teaching was the pure Chishti gospel of love: God was to be sought through humility, through *faqr*, spiritual poverty, through patient endurance, and above all through a boundless compassion that returned kindness for cruelty. He is remembered for counselling his disciples to answer harshness with gentleness, and to give those who bring thorns the softest of replies. To him Sufism was a strenuous exercise that led to a pious life within the society in which one lived. It demanded humility, modesty, patience, fortitude and a heart cleansed of all conceit; and to deny the self meant to be careful never to give offence to another, and, where offence was given, to make sincere and copious apologies.

Five hundred of his aphorisms were collected, and they show a broad and humane teaching. One is a parable of renunciation: the alms tax, the *zakat*, of the religious law is five dirhams out of two hundred; the *zakat* of the Sufi path is to pay a hundred and ninety five and keep only five; and the *zakat* of the Truth is to pay everything and keep nothing. He encouraged learning among his disciples and held the *ulama* nobler than the common people, though he disliked their complacent indifference to others; yet the *faqirs*, the mystics who have embraced poverty, he held nobler still, standing among the scholars like the full moon among the stars. Harm, he said, lies in contrivance, and safety in submission; and fasting is the first half of the way along the Sufi path, prayers and pilgrimages the other.

When a disciple asked whether he should give up study for prayer, Baba Farid is said to have answered that he should do both until one predominated, for both were needed in the life of a dervish. Repentance, *tauba*, he taught, is of six kinds, of heart and tongue, eye, ear, hand, foot and the *nafs*, the ego. A dervish who seeks eminence in this base world is no dervish but one who brings dervishes into disrepute, an apostate from the Sufi way. Love has a hundred stations, of which the first is patience under affliction from the Friend. The *kulah*, the Sufi's cap, may not be worn until one guards the four worlds of eye, ear, tongue, and hand and foot. Four questions, he said, were put to seven hundred masters of the Sufi way, who all answered alike: the wisest is he who abandons sins, the most alert he whom nothing troubles, the richest the contented, and the neediest he who abandons contentment.

Prayers are handed down in his name as remedies: Sura Baqara, recited between the *sunnat* and *farz* prayers of dawn for three days, fulfils a purpose; Sura An'am recited seventy times fulfils a need; the words that declare there is no god but You, glorify You and confess oneself among the wrongdoers free one from grief and worry; and frequent recitation of a prayer that calls on God, in the name of the Merciful, as everlasting in might, dominion and permanence, possessor of majesty, generosity, bounty and giving, loving, lord of the glorious Throne and doer of whatever He wills, removes straitness in livelihood. He is also the source of particular formulas of *zikr*, the remembrance of God, some of them in Indian languages: "Say *wuhi hi* upwards, *hi hi* to the left side of the breast, *hin hi* toward the heart." Such phrases belong to a long tradition of adapting the mantras of Hatha Yoga to Islamic themes and Sufi practice.

He gave amulets too, the *ta'widh*, usually a scrap of paper on which the names of God or verses of the Quran were written, and huge crowds gathered daily at his lodge to receive them. He had asked his own master about amulets and been told that the fulfilment of desires belonged to no one, but that amulets contained God's name and His words and could be given to the people; and he once gave Nizamuddin a pot of ink and ordered him to write amulets for the people.

He was very fond of *sama*. *Sama*, he said, is the heart's ease, which sets in motion the people of love who swim in the sea of love. Asked about its lawfulness, a question disputed among the *ulama*, he replied, "Praise be to God!", and then: "One group has been burnt to ashes, while the other is still arguing." He is counted among the masters of India who favoured music, felt its ecstasy and danced. Once, it is told, he wished to hear *sama* when no musician was present, and had the letters of Qazi Hamid al-Din Nagori read to him instead, and at once fell into an ecstatic swoon.

Nizamuddin testified that neither Baba Farid nor Qutbuddin nor any Chishti master before them had written a book, and when Nizamuddin tried to write down what he heard from Baba Farid he found himself unable to write a word; some of Baba Farid's sayings, though, Nizamuddin recorded in a letter. Three collections of sayings, the *Fawaid al-Salikin*, said to hold his master's sayings as Baba Farid compiled them, the *Asrar al-Auliya*, said to have been compiled by Badr al-Din Ishaq, and the *Rahat al-Qulub*, said to have been compiled by Nizamuddin, are most often held to be fabrications, and two other works, the *Ganj-i Asrar* and the *Risala-i Irfani*, are only attributed to him.

He was a poet as well. He is said to have written excellent poetry in Arabic, in Persian and in Hindawi, the local tongue, and he recited the Quran perfectly, popularised the study of the *Awarif al-Ma'arif* and lectured on the *Lawa'ih* of Qazi Hamid al-Din Nagori. What sets him apart in the history of the Punjab is that he expressed his vision not only in the Persian of the learned but in the language of the common people, writing for them in words they could understand at a time when the elite used Persian and Arabic. He is honoured as one of the earliest and greatest poets of Punjabi, and is believed to be its first; he laid the basis for a literature in the Punjabi vernacular, and he first used the image of a wife abandoned by her husband for the devotee and God. He is said, too, to be the creator of the *kafi* form, in which the human soul is cast as a woman and set in the imagery of the medieval institution of marriage. There is no settled view of how much of the verse is truly his. Most of the Hindawi songs sung at the *sama* gatherings of his time are lost, though a few surviving verses have been ascribed to him and to Qazi Hamid al-Din Nagori, and a verse he composed in the Multani dialect survives, its precise meaning difficult to decipher.

His *shlokas*, or couplets, have such authority that the Sikh Gurus included a body of them in the Guru Granth Sahib. Guru Nanak is said to have gone to Pakpattan, two centuries after the saint, to gather his poetry from his descendants, receiving the poems from the scribe of Sheikh Ibrahim, a descendant of Farid, who had compiled them, and he wrote them down in his notebooks; and the fifth Guru, Arjan, put together the first copy of the Granth out of those notebooks, in 1604, so saving Baba Farid from obscurity. More than a hundred *shlokas* bear his name there, more than any other of the poets selected for it, and some of his best Punjabi poetry is preserved to this day in the Gurmukhi script in the sacred texts of the Sikhs, though there is no consensus on the authorship of the *shlokas*. Their tone is unmistakable: the ache of the soul for God, and the memory of death that makes the world small.

کاگا سب تن کھائیو، چُن چُن کھائیو ماس
دوے نیناں مت کھائیو، مینوں پِیا ملن دی آس

*O crow, eat all my flesh, pick and eat it piece by piece, but do not eat these two eyes, for I still long to see my Beloved.*

The same voice turns the seeker's eye inward, away from the faults of others:

فریدا جے تُوں عقل لطیف ہیں، کالے لِکھ نہ لیکھ
آپنڑے گریوان مہ سِر نیواں کر ویکھ

*Farid, if you are of subtle wit, write not against another’s account; bow your head into your own collar, and look there instead.*

Two of his *dohas*, or couplets, are preserved with a Persian rendering, and both attack false initiation and outward asceticism. Their sense is this:

*You give a cap as a mark of initiation, and this indicates that you are presumptuous*

*You shave your head but what you should do is to cut lust from the heart*

His voice lives on in later poets. A number of the Sikh Gurus wrote poetry using "Farida" as a vocative, a tradition that already existed in relation to Baba Farid. Bulleh Shah, steeped in the technical and intellectual traditions of Rumi and Baba Farid though he mentions neither, made two of his *kafis* exact quotations from their work, and the same confessional vein runs strongly through Baba Farid and Bulleh Shah alike. At one *ʿurs*, an evening *sama* of three hours was filled with ancient Hindi songs of Amir Khusrau and the Persian songs of Baba Farid himself, and a song called his most popular *qawwali* runs:

*I did not sleep with my love tonight / And every bit of my body aches*

Waris Shah opens his romance of Hir and Ranjha with Baba Farid, as part of a formal invocation that gives an ode to God and to Love and declares obedience to the Prophet Muhammad and his four companions, and more specifically to Pir Chishti Shakarganj, Baba Farid of Pakpattan:

*Praise also to the beloved Pir Chishti Shakarganj. When Shakarganj made his abode at Pakpattan, the Punjab was delivered of all its troubles.* (translated by Charles Frederick Usborne)

At the poem's close Waris Shah credits the saint with the work itself, writing that with the aid of Shakarganj he has conferred this benefit on the world.

The *qawwals* of the Chishti order sing his name in a Hindi song of Amir Khusrau, whose second verse runs "Sadqa Baba Ganj-e-Shakar ka / Rakh le laj hamari (Nijam Piya)":

*In the name of Ganj-e-Shakar / Protect our honour (Nijam, Beloved)* (translated by Regula Burckhardt Qureshi)

Another song runs "Ganj-e-Shakar ke rup men, Mahbub piyara sowat hai":

*In the likeness of Ganj-e-Shakar, the dear Beloved is asleep* (translated by Regula Burckhardt Qureshi)

The stanza that names him is certain to bring offerings, for it appeals to Nizamuddin in the very name of his own spiritual guide, Baba Farid; and Amir Khusrau's song, words and music alike, is part of the special heritage of the Qawwal Bachche, and is widely sung throughout the Chishti order.

A *qawwali* performed in the United Kingdom opens by invoking him, with the line "Sir devĩ te vafā na mangĩ-ehī pīr Farīd dā dāse":

*Give thy head, expect no loyalty in return, / O, this is Farid, the Saint's wisdom* (translated by Shemeem Burney Abbas)

Another line of the same performance, "Yār Farīd ābād thī vaṇ o jhok-rab phir vīrān basāe", runs:

*O lover Farid, alive were those habitations once: may God bring the wilderness to life again!* (translated by Shemeem Burney Abbas)

But for the sacred frame of its opening, when the ensemble invoked Baba Farid, the performance might have been taken for a secular love song in which a lover seeks a beloved and serenades her. Poets have made him a measure too. In Punjabi there are *barahmasas*, poems that follow the twelve months, about Fariduddin Ganj Shakar of Pakpattan; a biographer of Mir Dard wrote that the mountain of Dard's patience would have made Sheikh Farid Ganj-i Shakar "bite his fingers from amazement like sugarcane"; and the Mughal grandee Bayram Khan is said to have composed a poem of four lines on Farid's name.

## The Shrine

Baba Farid's tomb at Pakpattan grew, over the centuries, into a great shrine. With his death began the career of the vast shrine complex based on his tomb, which came in time to take in a mosque, a *langar khana*, the welfare kitchen, and a number of related buildings. Since the thirteenth century the tomb has linked the people of the region to a wider universe of Islamic piety and to the spiritual hierarchy of the Chishti order, and for centuries the shrine complex has served as a central marker in the history of the Punjab. By the 1870s his illuminated *rauza*, his tomb, at Pakpattan was a place of pilgrimage. It remains among the most visited *darbars*, saints' shrines, in Pakistan.

The shrine stands on top of a mound that overlooks the whole town, the highest point of the area. When Farid moved there with his students the mound was abandoned, probably holding within it the secrets of an ancient city; his *madrasa* stood on the mound where he is buried, and today the shrine is spread over a vast tract of land. Stairs lead up to an open courtyard, with a new mosque on one side and the shrine of Baba Farid in front, where, on one visit, a long line of men stood waiting. The tomb itself is a modest building. Behind it rises a splendid structure, several metres higher, where his progeny are buried; the graves of the women of his family are covered with *purdah*, a screening curtain, and here too women are not allowed. Facing the shrine stands an ancient *karir* tree. In the nineteenth century the tomb had two doors, and one of them was explained by a dream of Nizamuddin's in which the Prophet said that whoever entered it would be safe. People came there in lakhs upon lakhs, a lakh being a hundred thousand, from all the land of Hindustan, from the Punjab and from elsewhere, and the crowd was kept in order by beating.

The grandest building in the complex, however, is not the saint's own. Soon after the death of his grandson Ala al-Din in 1335, Sultan Muhammad commissioned two engineers to build his tomb, which proved to be one of the finest examples of Tughluq architecture in the subcontinent. An inscription on the tomb itself dates it to Safar 737, September or October 1336. It is the most imposing structure in the whole shrine complex, dwarfing by far Baba Farid's own tomb.

Around the shrine there grew a family of others. Small memorial shrines to Baba Farid began to appear, scattered across the countryside of central Punjab, and they were built not by the Tughluq sultans, who raised the main structures at Ajodhan, but by the common people themselves. Devotees came to see the Pakpattan shrine as one in a network of sacred *wilayats*, territories under the spiritual authority of one saint or another that dot all of North India, ranked in a hierarchy in which the shrine of Baba Farid was the supreme shrine of the Punjab, to which others were subordinate. The ranking was acted out by an unwritten etiquette governing attendance at the shrine's rituals by the *sajjada nishins*, the hereditary custodians, of other shrines; one *sajjada nishin* counted among its subordinates the shrines of Shaikh Jamal al-Din at Hansi and of Nizamuddin Auliya in Delhi, and those of Piran Kaler, Golra Sharif, Taunsa Sharif, Uch Sharif, Kasur and Panipat, among others. Most of Baba Farid's "daughter" shrines in the Punjab were founded by his own descendants, and so looked on Pakpattan as their spiritual superior, while one in the nearby town of Dipalpur had been given land by an earlier Diwan and saw itself as subordinate for that reason. Among them, in Pakpattan itself, is the shrine of the saint's daughter's husband, Badr al-Din Ishaq, which the Diwan visited at its own *ʿurs*.

The shrine has known violence and the hand of the state. By the 2010s a blast at its entrance had killed six people, and women were searched by policewomen. At such shrines photography is strictly prohibited, and where they once stayed open throughout the night as a shelter to all, they are now shut at midnight on the government's order. The shrine is among those that have come under attack in a wave of Islamic purism. The crowds press hard: thousands of pilgrims are packed into an extremely cramped area, so that disciples generally avoid lingering long in the shrine. Books are sold in the myriad bookstalls that flank the country's major Sufi shrines, most prominently in the narrow lanes around this tomb complex.

Its keepers have been a line of hereditary custodians, the Diwans of Pakpattan, descended from the saint's family. Baba Farid's tomb and its custodians, called *diwans*, *gaddi nishins* or *sajjada nishins*, are counted among the shrines of Pakistan comparable to Ajmer. On his death the seat passed at once to his son Badr al-Din Sulaiman, who held it until 1281. Although every Diwan since has been the eldest son of his predecessor, Baba Farid's first and second sons were passed over for Badr al-Din, the third, and he alone received the office not from his predecessor but directly from the Chishti elders in Chisht, in western Afghanistan. His accession at once set a pattern of hereditary religious leadership at the shrine. The chief successor, who at other shrines was simply the *sajjada nishin*, one who sits on the prayer carpet, was and is at Pakpattan the *diwan*, a title taken directly from the language of the royal courts of Muslim India, and perhaps alluding to his work of collecting revenue. The Diwan is a direct lineal descendant of the saint, believed by his devotees to be Baba Farid's living representative.

Around the Diwan the shrine kept a ranked society. Below him came his family, then the *khalifas* and the shrine's functionaries, then the Chishti caste, then the leaders of the clans, and at the bottom the common Jat cultivators, the peasant disciples who spoke of themselves as "we smaller people". The saint's descendants grew so numerous that they formed a separate caste of landholders in the area, the Chishti caste, marrying among themselves and holding both economic privileges and ritual standing among the local clans. A British assessment report described the Chishtis as a Muslim tribe of a partly religious character and considerable local influence, who did not work the land themselves but depended entirely on tenants for its cultivation.

The clans were bound to the family by marriage and by oath. By 1623, thirteen marriage alliances had joined the Khokhar clan to the shrine: seven of the Khokhar brides were daughters of clan chiefs, three brides went to the Diwans themselves and six to the sons of various Diwans, and the earliest was a Khokhar bride given to a son of Diwan Ahmad Shah, who held the seat from 1452 to 1474. Of five exchanges with the Bhattis, two brides were daughters of Bhatti chiefs and three grooms were sons of Diwans, the earliest alliance dating back to the late 1400s. Chishti daughters, it seems, were kept within the caste. The Khokhars, Bhattis, Dhudhis and Hans of the country around Pakpattan all took *bay'a*, the oath of initiation, with the family and became its *murids*: the clans swore allegiance not to Baba Farid himself but to his family, the Chishti caste of Pakpattan, and they could, it was claimed, place ten thousand horsemen and foot soldiers in the saint's service. Living *pirs* in the villages carried the saint's grace onwards. A cultivator of Dipalpur, a Watuzai Pathan, told the court in the 1930s that his ancestor sixteen generations back had been a disciple of Baba Farid, and that he himself was a *pir* of his tribe.

The rulers of Delhi came to the shrine of the saint who had kept them at a distance. The Khalji sultans seem to have taken no interest in it, but the whole picture changed with the Tughluqs, who ruled from 1321 to 1398. Sultan Muhammad ibn Tughluq bestowed the city of Ajodhan on the shrine, which seems to be the earliest known instance of the court assigning local revenues to its support. During the brief tenure of Diwan Mu'izz al-Din, from 1335 to 1338, the same sultan granted an endowment for the shrine's public kitchen, and he called Mu'izz al-Din himself to Delhi, placed him in government service and sent him to Gujarat as deputy governor, while the Diwan's brother was appointed Shaikh al-Islam, the chief religious dignitary, of India. Never were the shrine's affairs more firmly welded to the interests of the court; its leaders were now under Delhi's control, and the shrine became even more dependent on royal patronage. Muhammad ibn Tughluq was deeply devoted to the descendants of Baba Farid. When Ibn Battuta visited the shrine, in the fourteenth century, the saint was by then clearly identified with the physical shrine complex, and his fame had spread as far as Egypt. Sultan Firuz Tughluq repaired the tomb of Baba Farid, granted robes of honour to his descendants and confirmed them in possession of their villages and lands.

No major ruler passed by the area without showing deference to the shrine's spiritual power. In October 1398 Timur, in the midst of his plunder of northern India, visited it. In March 1571 the emperor Akbar opened the sixteenth year of his reign at Pakpattan, where he implored strength at Baba Farid's shrine, and at Fatehpur Sikri he drew on the spiritual blessing that came from his ascetic patron in the Punjab, Baba Farid. In 1629 a *farman* of the emperor Shah Jahan recorded that the sacred town of Pakpattan with all its dependencies had by old agreement been held in grant from earlier emperors for the expenses of the shrine's *langar*. It was held by Shaikh Mohammad, the *sajjada nishin* of the shrine and a descendant of the saint, and its proceeds went to his own upkeep and that of the dervishes and *khadims*, the shrine's attendants, to feeding travellers, and to the repair and adornment of the building; and the *farman* ordered the whole *muhal*, the revenue district, continued in endowment to the shrine. In return the Diwans performed several functions for the Mughal government. Above all, on certain tracts of land they received the government's share of every crop on which revenue was taken in kind, while the tax on cash crops such as cotton, indigo or tobacco was paid in cash and went straight to the revenue officials without passing through the Diwan.

As Mughal power failed, the shrine armed. The Diwans had often played important political roles in the history of medieval Punjab, with close relations with the sultans and the Mughal emperors of Delhi, and as Mughal authority declined their political roles even outweighed their religious ones. From the early 1700s, a functionary of the shrine testified, the Diwans were inclined to make conquests, maintain armies and build forts, and they stopped giving spiritual instruction themselves, employing *maulvis*, *imams*, *khatibs* and *mubalighs*, scholars, prayer leaders, preachers and missionaries, for the purpose. In 1757 Diwan Abd as-Subhan, who held the seat from 1752 to 1766, gathered an army of his Jat disciples, attacked the raja of Bikaner, and so for the first time extended the shrine's territorial holdings east of the Sutlej. Around 1776 the Diwan of the day, supported mainly by his Wattu disciples, beat off a Sikh attack on Pakpattan in which Hira Singh was killed, and pursued the retreating Sikhs with four thousand horsemen, killing a great number of them. In 1810 the shrine's extensive holdings were seized by Ranjit Singh, and the Diwans, their brief period of political independence at an end, went back to their former place as political intermediaries; Ranjit Singh allowed the Diwan and his retainers only 1,000 rupees a year for their upkeep, from the town duties of Pakpattan, with a fourth share of four small villages, terms much worse than under the Mughals. Among the keepers remembered by name is Hazrat Diwan Muhammad Subhan, who was *sajjada nishin* at the time of Maulvi Fakhr-ud-Din's yearly visits.

Under British rule the succession came before the courts, for the British at times interfered directly in the running of shrines such as Pakpattan, going so far as to settle disputes over succession in colonial courts. In December 1934 the religious head of the shrine, Sa'id Muhammad, died, leaving his eleven year old son, Qutb al-Din, as his chosen successor. Only four months later a group of devotees at the shrine, themselves descendants of Baba Farid, filed a plaint in the District Court disputing the boy's claim and putting forward a rival claimant, Ghulam Rasul. The case ran from 6 April 1935, when the plaint was filed, to 2 December 1938, when the judge decided it for the defendant, Qutb al-Din: 159 depositions were filed for the plaintiff and 172 for the defendant, covering more than 836 typed pages, and the two sides filed nearly three hundred documents besides. The case went on appeal to the Lahore High Court in 1939.

At issue was who had the right to choose a new Diwan: the Chishti clan, as the plaintiff claimed, or the previous Diwan, as the defendant held. The boy's father had placed a turban on his head during the Bahishti *mela*, the fair of the Gate of Paradise, in 1933, and on the fortieth day after his father's death the *sajjada nishin* of the shrine at Ajmer placed the turban on his head again; the rival claimant was turbaned instead by representatives of the whole clan, but since he was not given access to the shrine, the part of his *dastar bandi*, his turbaning, that needed the shrine had to be left out. A point of law was whether the late Diwan had received a dream in which Baba Farid revealed that his son Qutb al-Din should succeed him, and the judge, Guru Datta, ruled that the defendant had sufficiently proved that Diwan Sa'id Muhammad had received the revelation and that the nomination was made on its strength. Neither side argued that the disciples should have any say in the matter. "The murids were never consulted," one villager said, "they only said Amin to the biradri's election", the *biradri* being the brotherhood of the clan; another said, "I am a follower of the Gaddi Nashin whosoever may be occupying it." In the same decade a debate raged in Chishti circles over whether the British could compel a minor who had become Diwan at Pakpattan to be educated at a British college in Lahore, and the *sajjada nishins* of all the other major Chishti shrines of North India protested that the *sajjada nishin* should be a dervish, with no need of the education given to the sons of rich families.

In Pakistan the state has a hand in the festival: the government's control of the *ʿurs* of Baba Farid may give the observance an institutional and ideological face that many mystics would resist or resent. In 2001 the shrine's chief custodian was Diwan Maudud Mas'ud Chishti, and a member of his family was Badr Moeenuddin, the Diwan's maternal uncle. That year the Diwan is reported to have argued with the Awqaf Department, the department of religious endowments, insisting that the shrine be paid an annuity of 1,500,000 rupees rather than the 150,000 rupees it had been promised. To one disciple, the Awqaf takeover broke a tradition of custodianship six hundred and fifty years old. The present guardians of the shrine are of the family of Baba Farid, and they are among the richest and most powerful families of the region; his progeny have received gifts and awards from different rulers. By Guru Nanak's time, it is told, the power of the descendants had grown until they were great feudal lords and political players of the region, and Sheikh Ibrahim, the twelfth successor of Baba Farid, who held the position of Diwan, found it frustrating, and abhorred the institutionalisation of spirituality practised in his family's name.

## Devotion and Festivals

The shrine's most famous feature is the *Bahishti Darwaza*, the Gate of Paradise, a door plated with silver, the southern door of the tomb, which is opened only once a year, at the saint's *ʿurs* in the Islamic month of Muharram. Pilgrims believe that to pass through it is to be cleansed of sin, and for the days it stands open a vast multitude presses through it, in one of the largest gatherings of the Punjabi year. *Qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis, the free kitchen, and the recitation of Farid's verse fill the shrine, above all through the *ʿurs*.

The *ʿurs* falls on the fifth of Muharram, the day of his death. It is a festival of several days celebrating his "marriage" with God, and at it cowrie shells, once used as the smallest units of money, are thrown over the crowd, in imitation of the moment when, it is told, Baba Farid went into ecstasy on realising that Nizamuddin Auliya had just attained a status rare even among the great ones, that of becoming the beloved of God Himself. For the Chishti Sabiri order it is a retreat of a week. The festival is old. Amir Khusrau described in detail the *ʿurs* of 1315: the pilgrimage of pious people to Ajodhan, the recitation of the saint's wonderful deeds, and the entertainments performed by an ensemble of dervishes. By 1623 all the shrine's major rituals were traced to the generation after the saint's death: the *dastar bandi*, the turbaning of a new Diwan; the regular ordering of ecstatic singing at the shrine; the public kitchen, from which the Diwan directed the giving out of food and sweets on formal occasions; and the opening of the southern door of the tomb at the *ʿurs*, which let the masses of common devotees pass by the sanctum, ritually entering heaven.

The Gate remains closed throughout the year until the evening of the anniversary, when it is opened by the Diwan, the saint's direct lineal descendant and the shrine's official representative. For devotees, to pass through it is a direct encounter, bringing salvation, with Baba Farid's living grace, his *baraka*, and a ritual reenactment of the saint's eternal union with God. The Diwan personally unlocked and opened the Gate and passed through it, and was followed by throngs of disciples, estimated by the 1880s at fifty to sixty thousand; all through the night, in states of intense religious ecstasy, the devotees filed through the Gate of Heaven and past Baba Farid's tomb, decked with flowers, at once commemorating and ritually reenacting the ascension of their patron saint to heaven. Because only the Diwan opened the Gate, devotees held that it was only through him that they gained access to Baba Farid, and only through the saint that they gained access to heaven.

In 1938 a witness gave the order in which they passed. The Diwan opened the Gate and entered first, and his companions, about 100 to 125 of them, followed; then the holders of passes, some 400 to 500; then the dervishes of the *hujra*, the resident mystics, numbering 1,000 to 1,200; then the plaintiff in the succession case, Ghulam Rasul, and his party; then the *sajjada nishins* of other institutions, and so on. The great masses of common devotees were let in only after all the dignitaries had passed.

In the early 1830s pilgrims, both Hindus and Muslims, came to the shrine, and all who passed through the doorway were held to be saved from the fires of perdition. The doorway was about two feet wide and could not be passed without stopping, and the chamber itself could not hold thirty people crowded together. A superlative heaven was believed to await those who were first to enter the tomb on the day, and the rush for precedence could be better imagined than described. As the pilgrims came out, having rubbed their foreheads on the foot of the saint's grave, the air rang with their shouts of "Farid! Farid!" The pilgrims' cry has also been heard as "Ya Farid, Haq Farid!"

Turbans and sugar flow outward from the Diwan. In the 1930s a villager testified that at the *ʿurs* only the *sajjada nishin* gave out the *pechas*, the turbans, and that he must give them out personally from the hundreds he had with him; in 2001 the Diwan mounted a large stage in the centre of the courtyard to hand out strips of cloth soaked in saffron, known as *pechas*, to the assembled masses. The giving out of sugar stood for the outward flow of Baba Farid's bounty and blessing, binding Diwan and disciples to their spiritual ancestor, whose title Ganj-i Shakar, the treasury of sugar, was tied to the legends of his severe austerities. The *dastar* of succession is a special saffron turban reserved for the Diwan alone, which was touched to the saint's tomb before it was placed on the candidate's head. When the Diwan went to the *ʿurs* of the shrine of Badr al-Din Ishaq he was carried in a palanquin, and he gave the descendant there a hundred rupees in cash, a horse and a silver saddle.

The *langar* has fed pilgrims since the first successor. Set in the shrine's courtyard just opposite Baba Farid's tomb, it was a vast operation, daily feeding the many pilgrims who came to pay their respects and devotions; in the 1930s a former kitchen worker reckoned that 240 to 320 pounds of wheat, three or four *mans*, a *man* being a traditional measure of weight, were used there every day, with lentils, *dal*, in proportion. On one visit the *langar* hall stood behind the *qawwals*, offering free food to all.

Offerings and vows bind devotee and saint. Devotees offered the shrine a gift called *futuh*, usually of sweets, which even in Baba Farid's own day swelled to enormous proportions, and his successors and their assistants, like those of other shrines in the Punjab, still write *ta'widh* for the masses. Disciples regularly made religious vows, "contracts", with Baba Farid as their mediator with God, in hope of favours such as the fertility of women, good crops or relief from illness. "We give nazranas to Dewan Sahib out of love, respect and regard for the Pir and to invoke his blessings for the good of the murid," a cultivator said in the 1930s, the *nazrana* being a gift of homage. Some disciples set aside their *nazrana* for the shrine's public kitchen and others for the Diwan, and some gave it to the Diwan when he visited them in their villages. Today there is always a rush outside the shrine, where several shops sell religious goods, and devotees buy a *chadar*, the sheet of cloth laid over the grave, with flowers and incense sticks to present to the caretakers of the shrine, for it is thought inauspicious to visit the grave of a saint without a gift. Not every devotee approved of every practice: one old cultivator declared that to prostrate oneself before the tomb of Baba Farid was not permissible under Muslim law.

*Qawwali* sounds there at every hour of the day, for at the shrine of Baba Farid *qawwals* perform at all times. On one visit a group sat in the shade of a building across from the *karir* tree, one man on the harmonium and one on the *tabla*, the hand drums, while the rest clapped, singing, aptly, the verses of Baba Farid; a young man began whirling on his knees in a trance, another devotee threw notes of ten rupees into the air, and women and men sat together. Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan was himself a devotee of the saint and performed at his shrine. A *dandiya* group, dancers who perform with sticks, has played there, as at other famous Muslim shrines.

The Chishti Sabiri order keeps its own *sama* in a room beside the shrine, where its shaykh sits directly opposite the performers, and throughout the 1970s one of its shaykhs, Shahidullah Faridi, pointedly refused to allow either the Sabri Brothers or Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan to perform at the annual *ʿurs* in Pakpattan. Of the *ʿurs* of 1990 Wahid Bakhsh wrote that on the night of the *ʿurs* the *qawwals* sang special Persian and Hindi ghazals for the people of the order, knowing their taste, but that on the arrival of the Diwan the *qawwali* reverted to mob entertainment, and the disciples fixed their attention on the saint himself, who made up for every deficiency. Orthodox critics argue that Sufis like Baba Farid allowed *qawwali* because they wanted to win Hindus over to Islam, and that today there is no need for it.

The grave chamber is closed to women. They are not allowed inside the chamber where the grave lies, and from a verandah beside the shrine they can catch a glimpse of it; at Pakpattan, a woman disciple from Peshawar said, women cannot even enter the inner sanctum of the tomb, and the most they can do is touch and kiss the threshold. In 2001 the men of the Chishti Sabiri order sat in rows before the main door of the tomb while the women of the order looked down from the rooftop balcony above.

The pilgrims come from near and far. On the eve of the *ʿurs* in 2001 tens of thousands of devotees flooded the provincial town of Pakpattan. For its devotees, Pakpattan, within easy reach, only several days' travel in the Punjab, was a place where God's grace was made concrete and accessible, where Heaven and Earth met. After the partition of India, when its Pakistani disciples could no longer easily travel to Ajmer, the Chishti Sabiri order transferred the *ʿurs* of Khwaja Mu'in al-Din to Baba Farid's shrine, and at Pakpattan the order keeps separate living compounds for men and women beside the saint's tomb. Its disciples offer their prayers together inside the *dargah*, the shrine, and afterwards sit in silent contemplation facing the tomb, but they do not perform *zikr* there: "At Pakpattan we do not do anything extra because Baba Sahab's influence is so great and so powerful." A woman disciple described a typical day at the *ʿurs*, from the morning prayer, at the women's compound or at the shrine, to *muraqaba*, meditation, at the shrine, and the preparation for the *qawwali*; Wahid Bakhsh recorded a full week of *mujahada*, spiritual exercises, at the shrine, three strenuous hours each morning from eight to eleven and three each evening from five to eight. At the *ʿurs* of 20 July 1977, Shahidullah Faridi answered the charge of idolatry: "we come here because these relics have an association with a saintly person like Baba Sahib. We come here because they are signs of Allah."

A meditation circuit of the nineteenth century made Farid al-Din in Pakpattan its third station, after Qutbuddin in Delhi and Mu'in al-Din in Ajmer, and Hasan Nizami made a journey to Pakpattan, partly on foot, in emulation of the journey a saint once made to find his master Farid. Malaysian disciples who could not travel gathered in Kuala Lumpur on the evening of the *ʿurs*, listening to recordings of *sama* on a small cassette player.

Hindus and Sikhs have come too. Once Baba Farid found a place in the Guru Granth Sahib, his person and his shrine became holy for the followers of Guru Nanak as well. Yet for years Sikhs have petitioned the Pakistani government for visas to attend his *ʿurs*, in vain, and in the absence of other religious communities Pakpattan is no longer a place of conflict or cooperation between them: the shrine is now marked as a space for Muslims alone. His day is kept far from Pakpattan. Among the anniversaries kept at the shrine of Nizamuddin Auliya in Delhi is that of its saint's own spiritual guide, Baba Farid. In Lahore the silk worker Shaikh Faiz Bakhsh kept seventeen *ʿurs* a year, among them Khwaja Farid al-Din's, and at each he had food cooked in plenty and given out to the poor and needy. At Nankana Sahib the *qawwals* begin with a devotional song expressing love for the Muslim saint Baba Fareed, and the Sikh community, which feels a strong affinity for him, rejoices.

The crowds at the Gate have brought tragedy. Late on the night of 1 April 2001, the fifth day of Muharram and the eve of the saint's death anniversary, more than one hundred men were crushed to death in a stampede at the tomb. They had climbed the narrow stairwell at the extreme north end of the shrine, a passageway usually reserved for the Diwan and his personal guests, when an impatient crowd of several thousand onlookers broke through a weak police cordon and came down on them from above, and a police contingent posted at the entrance closed and locked the doors, sealing off the only means of escape. Among the dead was a fourteen year old boy from a group of Chishti Sabiri disciples, the son of a prominent devotee and the great grandson of the Chishti Sabiri master Shaykh Muhammad Zauqi Shah, who lived from 1877 to 1951. Blame was disputed between the authorities, who pointed to a delay in opening the Gate while the Diwan quarrelled with the Awqaf Department over his annual payment, and the Diwan's family, who blamed poor crowd control and security.

The disciples came to see the dead as martyrs. Believing that nothing happens by accident close to a saint's tomb, they reasoned that the loss of life must be made sacred and understood as *shahadat*, martyrdom, and they now commemorate the "marriage" of both the saint and the young boy with God. Several disciples told of dreams in the days before the *ʿurs*: visions of the order's shaykhs, the death of family members and, for one woman, an attack by a black goat. The scale of the tragedy seems to have had no precedent in Pakpattan's seven hundred years, but with such crowds accidents were not uncommon: in 1911 it was recalled that some thirty years before, several lives had been lost in the crush at the Door of Paradise, and that before British rule, it was said, this happened every year. The tragedy of 2001 quickly faded from the attention of Pakistan's media.

## Legends

Tradition tells of a sign at his birth. There was doubt about the moon that begins the fast of Ramadan, and a holy man staying in the town said that a child would be born that night in the house of the *qazi*, Farid's father, and that if the child drank milk the fast should not be kept; that night Farid was born, he did not drink, and the people fasted. His mother's prayer, too, is said to have worked a wonder: a thief who entered the house glanced at the praying woman and was instantly blinded, and when he begged her to restore his sight she did so; the next day he came back with his family and became a Muslim.

A story is told of a single pomegranate seed. It is said that Shaikh Jalal al-Din Tabrizi met him at Kothewal, where the townsfolk had come to think the young man deranged and spoke so to their visitor. Jalal al-Din broke open a pomegranate and offered it to him; Farid, who was fasting, refused it, but after his guest had gone he found a seed left on the ground, kept it in his turban, and broke his fast with it in the evening. At once his heart was illuminated, and he lamented that he had not eaten the whole fruit; but in Delhi his master consoled him, saying that in each pomegranate only one seed was endowed with spiritual blessing, and that he had had it.

Heaven, the stories say, marked him early. Gharib Nawaz prayed that God would accept their Farid and raise him to the rank of the most perfect dervish, and a voice came from the unseen: Farid was accepted, and he would be the unique one of the age. Gharib Nawaz told Qutbuddin that he had brought a great royal falcon into his net, whose nest would be the Sidrat al-Muntaha, the lote tree of the furthest boundary.

At Ajodhan the land itself is said to have borne witness. The saint had bought a plot of land from a man, paying its price, to add to his *khanqah*, his lodge, but months later a second claimant brought a suit at Dipalpur before the royal governor. The saint was summoned, in person or by proxy, and sent an attendant; witnesses were demanded to prove that the land had belonged to the seller, and the saint, wearied, said that this "broken neck" should ask the land himself, and that if the land did not bear witness they would send witnesses. The governor came in person, on horseback, and thousands of onlookers gathered. When the claimant asked the land, it made no sound; when the attendant asked on the saint's behalf, a voice came from the land saying that it had belonged to the man who sold it to Farid and was now Khwaja Farid's property. The governor punished the claimant severely, and as he rode away after the judgement his horse stumbled; he fell, his neck broke at that moment, and he died.

Some stories turn on his anger. It is told that a dervish asked three times for the comb on his prayer mat, and at last said that if the saint gave him the comb it would bring him blessing; the saint told him to go and not meddle with his state, for he had cast him and his blessing into running water, and outside Ajodhan the dervish went to bathe in the river, could not come out, and drowned. Another story tells of a *qalandar* who was set on striking a disciple: the saint asked him to strike the wall instead, and when the *qalandar* did so, the wall fell down. The saint is said to have begged five travellers not to cross the desert; ignoring his advice, they set out along the desert road, and soon word came that four had died of heat exhaustion and that the fifth, who at last reached a well, had drunk so much water that he died too. Six dervishes, it is told, asked him for provisions and were given the dates that lay before him; displeased, they threw them away, and saw that the dates had turned to pure gold.

He is said to have known what was hidden. When Arif was entrusted with a hundred *tankas*, coins, for the saint and kept fifty, the saint smiled and said that Arif had already divided the sum as a brother would, rather than handing over the whole of it. Nizamuddin told of him at prayer, prostrate under his garment, describing a visitor he could not see, a Turk of medium height with a slightly yellowish complexion, and telling him to go away before he was disgraced, whereupon the man took to his heels and disappeared. His brother Najib al-Din, it is told, made nineteen visits to him at Ajodhan, and on each occasion the saint prayed that there might be a further visit; on the nineteenth he did not pray so, and a few months before the saint's own death Najib al-Din died. A musician about to travel was told, the story goes, that the limit of the saint's spiritual influence lay at a certain water tank, and that beyond it began the domain of Shaikh Baha al-Din, whose prayers he should seek; the musician did so and completed his journey safely. And it is told that once at Multan, when he sat with Khwaja Baha al-Din, one asked the other how far his striving had brought him, and the answer came that if the word were spoken the chairs would fly; the chairs rose, and a hand was laid on them with the words that this talk had been by way of conversation, not of instruction, and that they should stay where they were.

A hard story is told of his poverty. One of his wives, it is said, came to him with the words that a son of theirs was at the point of death from starvation, and the saint, bowing his head, replied, "What can Mas'ud do?", and told her that if it was God's will and the boy died, she should tie a rope around his feet, throw him out and come back. The story is grim but probably exaggerated, for several of his children lived to manhood and were a credit to their ascetic father, whom they followed in many ways.

His mother is said to have vanished on the road. When he settled at Ajodhan he asked his brother Najib al-Din to bring her to Ajodhan from Hansi or Delhi, where she was then living. On the way, in a waterless wilderness, she felt thirst, and when water was brought two hours later she was no longer under the tree; the whole forest was searched, and no trace of her was found. Two years later some bones resembling human bones were found, which were thought to be hers, as if she had fallen prey to a lion or some other beast, and they were put in a bag tied shut; but when the bag was opened it was empty, without a single bone, to the astonishment of the two brothers, and it was understood that this too was one of the divine mysteries. Her disappearance is dated to 643 of the Hijri calendar, and so, it is said, she has no grave. The saint had prayers said for her forgiveness, and much food cooked and given out to the dervishes and the poor.

His relics are said to have worked wonders. Nizamuddin related that he used a hair from Baba Farid's beard as an amulet: whenever a sick person came to him for an amulet, he gave him the hair, and after keeping it a few days the sick man was cured. Once, when a friend's child was ill, the hair could not be found, and the child died; the moral of the story is that the hair could not be found because the child was destined to die. Baba Farid was felt to carry an inner spiritual heat and force that could either propel others or singe them, depending on the sincerity of their approach to him. Both the Diwan and the shrine itself are perceived to carry his *baraka*, so that both act as intermediaries for an intermediary, continuing vehicles of the saint's power to mediate.

He is said to go on speaking after death. He appeared to Nizamuddin in a dream and reproached him: "You have remarked that even if there is nothing in the house, one is expected to show perfect courtesy to whomever comes. How then has it happened that someone has gone from your house with a heavy heart?" Muhammad Ghaws, a master of the Shattari order, dreamt that he found himself in one of the highest celestial realms, where he came face to face with Baba Farid, who explained that he shared that station with Bayazid, Junayd and Zu'n-Nun. In the memory of the Chishti Sabiri order, Shahidullah Faridi had a vivid dream in which the patron saint himself confirmed his standing as *khalifa*: Baba Sahib was standing at the door of his *mazar*, his tomb, while people shouted to him to make them his disciples, and he ordered Shahidullah to make them *murids*. Wahid Bakhsh, passing the tomb, thought that Baba Sahib had not left many books to read, and at that moment, he said, a voice came to him, the voice of Baba Sahib: "You are my books!" Disciples told that Baba Farid had met his future wife in a prescient dream. In one story a child who opened a closed door found a saintly person there who said, "No, no. Let her come. She is my daughter," and was told afterwards that the man was Baba Sahib. A Malaysian pilgrim of 1979 was told that in Pakpattan nobody's requests are rejected, and in the shrine received illuminations and fell, aware of being unconscious and yet still able to hear the people talking all around. And Shahidullah Faridi, at the shrine at the time of the *ʿurs* as the people came out of the Gate of Heaven, is remembered saying: "These are the real universities, these places. Real knowledge is given to you through the attention of the saint, through inspiration."

In the romance of Hir and Ranjha by Waris Shah, Baba Farid, as Shakarganj of Pakpattan, is one of the Five Pirs who meet the hero, Ranjha. The Five Pirs are believed together to possess special powers, and their miraculous interventions on Ranjha's behalf are a notable feature of the poem: "First came Khwaja Khizr, lord of all the waters, then Shakarganj, the holy saint of Pakpattan." Shakarganj gives Ranjha a kerchief, and at the crisis of the story, at midnight, Ranjha remembers the Five Pirs, kisses the handkerchief of Shakarganj and takes the ring of Lal Shahbaz.

## The Setting

Pakpattan was a river town long before the saint. The shrine stands on the right bank of the Sutlej, the most southeasterly river of the Punjab, roughly halfway between Ferozepore and Bahawalpur, in a small pilgrimage town some 120 miles southwest of Lahore, a journey of about four hours by road. The town is the ancient city of Ajodhan, and as the principal ferry point on the Sutlej it served from ancient times as a major crossroads of the trade between the Delhi region and Multan. In Aurangzeb's reign it was known as Patan, also called Ajodhan, in the district of Dipalpur, east of Multan. Ajodhan was once a small and insignificant town, beside which, on top of an abandoned mound, Baba Farid used to teach his students while the Sutlej wound through it. One had to go into the desert to reach his tomb, as to reach that of Hamid al-Din at Nagaur in Rajasthan; in 1352 Shaykh Zayn al-Din Shirazi, the main successor to Burhan al-Din Gharib at Khuldabad, followed a pilgrimage itinerary that joined Pakpattan to Ajmer and Delhi. Near Pakpattan, too, the cave retreat of Sabir is preserved, and there is a shrine of phallic offerings, referred to as Aban Shah.

Places beyond Pakpattan are linked to him as well. At Tibba Haji Deen, the old Kothewal where he was born, the house of his birth is preserved, and in its open courtyard are seven graves which an old caretaker said were those of Baba Farid's father, grandfather, uncles and siblings; the family of the caretaker Ghulam Shabbir has served this shrine for seven generations. Nearby is another shrine, dedicated to the childhood of Baba Farid, a well within a house, covered by an iron cage on which devotees tie saffron coloured threads. Because of his presence, it is believed, the well and its water became sacred, and drinking water from it, a shrine official said, will cure all one's problems. Pilgrims walk barefoot on the dusty roads of the village out of respect for its holiness. The shrine at Tibba Haji Deen is older than the shrine of Baba Farid at Pakpattan, but less important, for fewer followers come to it.

Chistian, a small city, is said to have been founded by his family and named after his school of thought. Local tradition holds that in a jungle outside an old fort there was a spot where Baba Farid had spent some time in meditation, and that after the saint settled at Pakpattan he asked his grandson, Tajuddin Chisti, to people the place. Tibba Nanaksar, about five kilometres from the shrine of Baba Farid, has a *gurdwara*, a Sikh place of worship, and the shrine of a descendant of the saint, Baba Fateh Ullah Shah Noori Chisti; pressure even from the Diwan of Baba Farid's shrine is said to have led the government to renovate the *gurdwara*.

In Lahore, it is told, he came for ascetic practice and sat on the mound that came to be called Farid Astana, refusing to sit where his *pir* had sat and saying that he would keep his *chilla* at the side of his feet; he is also counted among the elders who came to the tomb of Data Ganj Bakhsh and kept *chillas* there. Outside the Delhi Gate of Lahore lies the tomb of Shah Kaku Chishti, one of his progeny. One *chilla* of Baba Farid adjoins the shrine of Khwaja Mu'in al-Din in Ajmer, and another associated with him is found outside the tomb of Qutbuddin in Delhi. Gisu Daraz once ordered a disciple to perform forty days of continuous meditation and penance in the Delhi cell of Baba Farid, a cell later incorporated into the tomb of Qutbuddin. The Chilla of Baba Farid near the shrine of Nizamuddin Auliya in Delhi is a place of *qawwali*, and its Chilla Mahfil is perhaps the most exclusive or intimate *qawwali* gathering at Nizamuddin's shrine. At Khuldabad a staff is kept which is called the staff of Baba Farid.

Further afield, the city of Faridkot, then known as Mokhalpur, was named after him by its ruler, Raja Mokhal, and the Baba Sheikh Farid Agman Purb Mela is held there each year for three days, from 21 to 23 September, to commemorate his arrival in the city. At Uch a banner at Jalaludin's shrine shows him in a simple white dress with a green shawl on his shoulders.

## Legacy

For the people of the Punjab, Baba Farid is not a remote historical figure but a living presence: "Farid" is a household name, his sayings are proverbs, and his verses are sung by *qawwals* and folk singers alike. Through his disciple Nizamuddin Auliya and the generations that followed, the Chishti order he helped to root in India became the most widespread of all the Sufi paths of the subcontinent; through his Punjabi poetry he became a father of the language's literature and a shared inheritance of Muslims and Sikhs; and through his shrine at Pakpattan he gave the Punjab one of its enduring centres of pilgrimage. For many centuries he has remained an immensely popular "invisible friend" to millions of villagers in the southwestern Punjab, and his shrine is a striking example of how Islam, the religion above all others "of the Book", was mediated among common villagers, most of whom could not read.

His tomb, with those of Mu'in al-Din Chishti at Ajmer and Nizamuddin Auliya at Delhi, became one of the first Muslim holy places within India, so that the Muslims of South Asia were no longer compelled to look only to the Middle East for spiritual inspiration, and Pakpattan was one of the three great cults, beside Ajmer and Delhi, on which the wealth and power of the order came to centre. For its devotees the shrine was Baba Farid: disciples spoke of the saint and his shrine in the same terms, calling themselves sometimes disciples of Baba Farid and sometimes disciples of the *gaddi*, the seat, that is, the shrine, which, in their eyes, has sustained the saint's *baraka* all along through its line of Diwans. "As the Sajjada Nashin in office is a representative of Baba Farid, the same sanctity and reverence is shown to the Gaddi Nashin as is shown to Baba Farid," a landholder told the court in the 1930s.

Many clans claim him as the one who made them Muslim. Before the court in the 1930s, witnesses named the Wattu, the Siyal, the Dogar, the Kathia, the Bhatti and the Tiwana. "Our Tiwana tribe was converted by Baba Farid," said a cultivator of Lyallpur; another said, "Baba Farid converted us Bhattis into Islam"; and a Wattu villager, "Khewa, one of my ancestors, was converted into Islam by Baba Farid, and since that time we have been following the gaddi in dispute." The great majority of the Jat and Rajput groups that became Muslim in the medieval period claim to have been converted either by Baba Farid or by his contemporary Baha al-Haqq Zakariya, though the claim is doubted. His cult travelled with Punjabis far from home: in Bengal the Mughal officer Mirza Nathan was a faithful disciple of Farid al-Din Shakarganj, probably because his ancestors had come from the Punjab, where the saint's cult was especially prominent.

He belongs to more than one faith. The Sikhs respect him so highly that they have made his Punjabi verses part of their holy scripture, and because he influenced the founder of their religion he is a revered Sufi among the Sikhs as well as among his Muslim devotees; as a poet of the Granth he is given a special status in the Sikh religion and revered like the Gurus. Guru Nanak, like Farid, chose Punjabi as his medium of expression, and he was impressed that Baba Farid had lived a life of simplicity and shunned violence. A picture of Baba Farid accompanies the picture of Guru Nanak at the shrine of Guru Nanak at Nankana Sahib. The Janamsakhis, the traditional lives of Guru Nanak, tell of a meeting between Nanak and Farid, a meeting explained by the belief of the Sufis and of the Nath yogis that great saints did not die but remained accessible to important mystics of later ages through spiritual conversation. Baba Farid is counted with Waris Shah and Bulleh Shah among those who spread the message of peace and brought several religious communities together instead of shedding blood.

His line reaches far. Shaikh Salim Chishti of Fatehpur Sikri belongs to the Faridi line, and Hazrat Inayat Khan was of the lineage of Farid al-Din and Nizamuddin. In the oath of ascension that Khidr teaches Muhammad Ghaws, Baba Farid is the only saint of South Asia, invoked by the Intimacy of Farid al-Din Ajhodani. The Chishti Sabiri order of today places his tomb at the centre of the sacred geography of Sufism in Pakistan, and its disciples contemplate him daily; one of its shaykhs, Shahidullah Faridi, took the name Faridi to mark his special relationship with the saint of Pakpattan. Sufi shrines such as Pakpattan remain vital centres of popular piety, nodal points of local identity and culture.

His life yields the three marks of the pious Chishti: to shun every stable income, to engage in meditation, often aided by music, and to train worthy disciples. He was an extraordinary ascetic, known for his controlled ecstasy, and yet, unlike his master Qutbuddin, a loyal father and husband; it has been said that "no saint excelled him in his devotions and penances." Compassion, poverty, and the sweetness that gave him his name remain the marks of his memory.
