## Overview

Gurdwara Sri Mal Ji Sahib stands in Nankana Sahib, near the railway station and close to Gurdwara Kiara Sahib, and commemorates one of the miracles told of Guru Nanak Dev Ji's childhood. Nankana Sahib is the old Talwandi, the town in which Guru Nanak, the first Guru of the Sikhs, was born, and the gurdwara is one of the several there that mark events from his childhood. Pilgrims come to it on the great anniversaries.

Its name comes from the *mal*, a shady tree also called *van* or *jal*. The gurdwara remembers a *sakhi*, a story from the Guru's life, in which the young Nanak once lay asleep beneath such a tree while a great cobra shaded his face from the sun. A second gurdwara of the same name stands at Kanganpur in Kasur District; it is a different site.

## The Legend of the Cobra

The story belongs to Nanak's boyhood at Talwandi. Tradition tells that the young Nanak, tending his father's cattle, lay asleep beneath the *mal* tree in the heat of the day. The local landlord, Rai Bular, riding through his fields with his attendants, is said to have come upon the sleeping child and seen that a great cobra had spread its hood over Nanak's face to shade him from the sun. As the men approached, the story goes on, the cobra withdrew quietly. Accounts of how the wonder unfolded differ.

This wonder, told together with the miracle of the untouched crop at Kiara Sahib nearby, is said to have convinced Rai Bular of the boy's spiritual greatness, so that he became a devoted follower of Guru Nanak and later granted him half of his estate.

## The Gurdwara

A gurdwara was first built on the spot by Diwan Kaura Mall, and it was later renovated in the time of Maharaja Ranjit Singh, sovereign of the Punjab. It is larger and more ornate than Kiara Sahib. A central dome crowns it, with domed kiosks at the corners and porches along the hall, and its interior is faced with old ceramic tiles, each some four inches square and each depicting a cobra in memory of the miracle.

The gurdwara stands near the railway station, about five hundred metres from Gurdwara Janam Asthan, the shrine of the Guru's birthplace. Kiara Sahib is close by, and the two form the eastern cluster of the Nankana Sahib shrines, furthest from the Janam Asthan.

As sovereign of the Punjab, Ranjit Singh spent large sums on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras, and he attached vast tracts of land to them so that the revenues from the lands could support their running. After the British took over the Punjab, and amid sectarian divisions among the Sikhs, a large number of important Sikh gurdwaras came to be administered by Hindu *mahants*, heads of religious houses.

Accounts of how the town's gurdwaras fared after Partition differ, but they are most often said to have fallen into a bad state, with no Sikh left in Nankana Sahib. Giani Pratap, who came with a pilgrimage party from India in the 1960s, decided to stay and look after them, and it was the first time since Partition that anyone had taken the initiative to care for the gurdwaras of Nankana Sahib. Most of the town's gurdwaras were kept locked all year round and opened only at the time of the festival, with security guards employed at the entrance to all of them.

## The Guru

Accounts of his birth differ in their details, but Guru Nanak is generally held to have been born at Talwandi in 1469. The *janamsakhis* are the cycles of tales told of the life of the first Guru of the Sikhs, the earliest known of which dates from 1658, nearly 120 years after his death. In the janamsakhi telling, his father was Kalyan Chand Das Bedi, or Mehta Kalu as he was affectionately called, accountant to the Bhatti family under Rai Bular, and his mother was Mata Tripta. The Bhatti family, in that telling, was that of Rai Bhoe and his son Rai Bular; the head of the Bedi family, Shiv Ram, was accountant to Rai Bhoe, and his son Kalyan Chand Das Bedi succeeded him in that office. Nanak belonged to the Bedi caste.

Stories cluster around his birth. Tradition tells that Mata Tripta had no child for a long time after her marriage, and that she would regularly visit an ancient Hindu temple on top of a mound not far from Rai Bhoi di Talwandi to pray for a child; accounts of how his birth came about differ. The village astrologer, Pandit Hardyal, is said to have foretold greatness for the child. Even in childhood, it is said, signs of inner vision and of wonders beyond the ordinary appeared in him. At school, the janamsakhis tell, the boy showed a brilliance never before seen in any child in Talwandi.

At thirteen, in the janamsakhi telling, he refused the sacred thread, the *janeu*, in his father's courtyard, saying that "it is our actions and our actions alone" that give a man his position among men. At sixteen, the janamsakhis go on, he herded cattle, and he became well acquainted with a dervish, Sayyad Hussain, and gained an intimate knowledge of the Sufi line of thought; accounts of the dervish's name differ. Talwandi lay in the path of numerous mendicants, holy men and *sadhus*, the Hindu ascetics, who camped in a grove of trees on the outskirts of the village. It is believed that in the years between the episode of the *Sacha Sauda*, the "true bargain", and his move to Sultanpur, a period of six to seven years, the young Nanak began to frequent the old mound of Talwandi, and that he spent a lot of time there in conversation with the yogis, *sadhus* and dervishes who passed through the town.

From Talwandi, in the janamsakhi telling, Nanak moved to Sultanpur, around 60 kilometres away, as keeper of Nawab Daulat Khan's *modikhana*, or store, bathing in the Bein rivulet and holding evening gatherings at which his hymns were heard. It is widely believed that his religious life began at Sultanpur. The janamsakhis tell that he vanished into the stream for a week and was found on its bank saying: "There is only one God. There is no Hindu, no Mussalman." That saying was questioned at the court of Sultanpur, at Malik Bhago's court in Eminabad, and by friends and family at Rai Bhoi di Talwandi itself.

He married Sulakhini, daughter of Mul Chand and Chando Rani of the town of Batala, on 24 September 1487, though accounts of the marriage differ. They had two sons, Sri Chand, born on 8 September 1494, and Lakhmi Das, born on 12 February 1497.

Guru Nanak spent a major portion of his life in what is today Pakistan: he was born there, first preached travelling through the region, and eventually settled in a small village now on the Pakistani side of the border. He set out on his journeys from Talwandi with Mardana, a singer, and came back to it between them. The journeys, the *udasis*, took him far: east all the way to Bengal, south to the island of Sri Lanka, north to Kashmir, Nepal and Tibet, and west to Mecca, mostly on foot, over about twenty four years, before he returned to settle at Kartarpur. Accounts of how many journeys he made differ, but they are most often counted as four. About 1520 he returned from eastern India to find the Punjab plunged into a number of significant political crises. He preferred to stay on the outskirts of villages, and whenever he came to an area he would visit its religious shrines, Muslim, Hindu and of any other denomination. Crowds upon crowds came from every land to become his disciples, and Mardana sang his verses and drew people into the snare of devotion.

In the janamsakhi telling, Nanak and Mardana came home to Talwandi in 1506, after the first journey; his mother, Mata Tripta, felt great joy, and Mehta Kalu was a little in awe of his son. On a later return, after Pak Pattan, the janamsakhis tell, he found that Talwandi was no longer an obscure little village but had become a place of pilgrimage, and he sat by Rai Bular, who was sick and failing fast. Kartarpur in its turn became a town of pilgrims like Talwandi and Sultanpur. On his road home from Chuhrkhana, it is told, Nanak crossed a vast jungle that lay then between Nankana and Chuhrkhana.

He passed over his sons when he chose his successor. It was said that Lakhmi Das was a worthy son but that spiritual fortune was not his lot, and on his deathbed the Guru appointed not a son but his most loyal student as his spiritual successor: a man of the Khatri caste named Lehna, to whom he gave the title of Guru Angad. He asked Angad Dev to move to Khadur, away from Kartarpur, to avoid the ire of his sons. His eldest son, Sri Chand, ignored by his father when it was time to appoint the next Guru, is believed to have founded a sect of his own, the Udasis, who follow his tradition. Guru Nanak died on 22 September 1539, though accounts of the date differ.

He lived as a householder, with a wife and sons, and he taught that all men and women were equal, irrespective of caste, creed, wealth, social standing and gender, and that a good life rested on honest labour, compassion, selfless service and prayer. Born into a Hindu household and educated in a Muslim milieu, he was neither a practising Muslim nor a Hindu. He offended both Hindus and Muslims by saying that there was no Hindu or Muslim, and his advice to Bhai Bala and Bhai Mardana was that "if one is a Muslim then one should be a good Muslim and if one is a Hindu then one should be a good Hindu". He associated with people of the lower castes, calling them people of God, and criticised high caste landlords and government officials as exploiters. He was a *fakir*, a poor wandering mystic, or a *jogi*, an ascetic, who spent his entire lifetime in a few clothes.

Nanak himself is held to have abhorred the association of miracles with religious personalities, yet the stories of his life are full of them. Legend tells that at Mecca the Kaaba miraculously turned towards his feet. It is told, too, that a trader who told Nanak that his mules carried "Just some mud" found his sugar and *gur*, raw cane sugar, turned to mud until he repented.

## Devotion and Festivals

The great anniversaries bring pilgrims to Gurdwara Mal Ji Sahib. Guru Nanak's birthday is kept at Nankana Sahib every November, in the festival called Guru Nanak Jayanti or Gurpurab. Official estimates put the pilgrims who come to Nankana Sahib for it at ten to fifteen thousand every year; in 2011, by media reports, only three thousand came. That year the festival began on 8 November and ended on the 10th. Police stood at every entrance to the city, no car was let in, and devotees walked barefoot.

Trains brought pilgrims from Delhi, Gujarat and Amritsar across the border at Wagah, where representatives of the ETPB and the PSGPC received them and a *langar*, the free communal kitchen, was arranged; the tradition of the *langar* is believed to have begun with Guru Nanak. The pilgrims stayed three days at Nankana. There were three days of *Akhand Path*, the unbroken reading of the Sikh scripture, and the *Nagar Kirtan*, the procession singing hymns through the town, followed the closing *bhog*, the rite that ends the reading, on the last day; then the pilgrims went on to other cities. Richer pilgrims from Britain, America and Canada came by chartered plane, and travel companies promised them "all" the gurdwaras in Pakistan. For those few days the whole city looked like a little India. Pilgrim tourists came to Nankana Sahib and to Hassan Abdal, and Jeeta Singh of Manora held a small festival of his own every November, when the festival of Guru Nanak was celebrated at Nankana Sahib.

In Aurangzeb's reign it was said that most of the disciples and devotees of Baba Nanak, as the Guru was also called, were men of spiritual states, whose word was accepted, given to austerities, and whose prayers were answered. The sum of their worship was the reading of their master's verses, which they sang to melody. Many had put away worldly ties and lived at one with their friends and without war with their enemies, and their faith in their leader was seldom found among others. Their master's name was always on their tongues; they counted service to those who came and went, the traveller and the guest, as the greatest worship; and whoever arrived at midnight speaking Baba Nanak's name they treated as a brother and a friend and served, even a stranger, a thief, a highwayman or an evildoer.

In the same reign the Udasis, devotees of Baba Nanak, were counted as a fourth class among the dervishes of Hind, India; they worshipped in their master's way, and their worship, too, was the singing of his verses. Near Sambhal lay Nanak Mata, a place where Baba Nanak's disciples and devotees thronged to pray.

## The Guru's Verse

Guru Nanak's sermon was his *bani*, devotional verse of supplication, entreaty, humility and reverence. He composed verses in the Hindi tongue on the truth of God, setting out the unity of God in clear words and shining metaphors, and poets such as Kabir and Nanak used the *doha*, a couplet whose two verses rhyme, to write their mystical poetry. Nanak brought into his verse several words that were not part of the Punjabi of his time.

Among his poems is one on the sacred thread, the *janeu* that the boy refused at Talwandi:

*From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment*

His *aarti*, a hymn of worship with lamps, begins:

*In the salver of the firmament, / The sun and the moon shine as lamps*

He sings of the oneness of God:

*My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One*

*God is One, / The Path is One*

Another verse speaks of washing:

*As hands or feet besmirched with slime, / Water washes white*

In one modern retelling of his life, Nanak sings to innkeepers who meant to rob him and Mardana:

*The herons in their white feathers dwell in the sacred shrines of pilgrimage*

In the *Babur Bani* Nanak lays the blame of Babur's wrath at God's feet, and one line dates the invaders' coming:

*Coming in seventy-eight (1521 C.E)*

His verse is gathered in the Guru Granth Sahib, the Sikh scripture, which holds the spiritual poetry of the Sikh Gurus, Guru Nanak among them, and of other spiritual teachers such as Bhagat Kabir, Baba Farid and Bhai Mardana. The poems of the later Gurus are recorded not in their own names but in that of Guru Nanak. His *bani* was compiled as part of the Adi Granth by Guru Arjan Dev, the fifth Guru, in 1604.

The verse is sung beyond the gurdwaras too. The singer Abida Parvin counted Guru Nanak among the poets she sang, with Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah, Mian Muhammad Bakhsh, Hazrat Khawaja Ghulam Farid, Sacchal Sarmast and Baba Farid, and in Sindh Sain Vasan Shah became famous for reciting the *banis* of Shri Guru Nanak and the *kalams*, the poems, of the Sufis.

## Nankana Sahib

Nankana Sahib, the old Talwandi, lies about forty miles southwest of Lahore, in what is now Pakistan, and 180 kilometres from Kartarpur by way of Lahore. Originally called Rai Bhoi di Talwandi, the town was renamed Nankana Sahib in honour of Guru Nanak, who was born there. Because he spent his early years here, there are several gurdwaras in the city, marking events from his childhood, and pilgrims visit all of them; accounts of their number differ, from six to seven. Nankana is held to be one of the holiest cities in the world for the Sikhs, for the founder of their religion was born here.

Tradition tells that Talwandi was destroyed thirteen times and thirteen times, like the fabled phoenix, rose again from its ashes, for it lay between the Ravi and the Chenab, in the path of the armies that had set their eyes on Delhi. Its last rebirth, the tradition goes on, took place under the supervision of Rai Bhoe, a Bhatti Rajput who had converted to Islam.

An old mound rests behind Gurdwara Janam Asthan, away from the hub of the new settlement. It is held to be an archaeological mound: the settlement there was destroyed, after which Rai Bhoi di Talwandi was built within a boundary wall, and Nanak was born in the new town. The climb up the mound was steep, with no proper path, to a top at least twenty metres from the ground, covered with shrubs of *karir*, a thorny desert bush, with shards of broken pottery underfoot.

The city had a population of over a million. One main road ran through it, the buildings were old and the road had potholes, and most of the buildings were from the late British or the early Pakistan era. From the roof of a building in the city the small white domes of the gurdwaras could be seen among the minarets of the mosques, with the railway station and the remains of a few Hindu temples, cone like structures rising from houses abandoned at the time of Partition. During the Musharraf era, from 2001 to 2008, newly built roads and other government facilities came to the city, and in 2005 Nankana Sahib was separated from Sheikhupura and made a district of its own, with the city as its headquarters. Sikhs abroad asked for an international hotel and for visa free "holy city" status, with no progress.

Guru Nanak was celebrated all over Nankana Sahib. There were a Guru Nanak High School, a Guru Nanak grocery store and a Guru Nanak cloth shop, all run by Sikhs, while the town's Muslims generally refrained from honouring Guru Nanak in this way. Most of the Muslims of Nankana Sahib came from across the border after Partition. Some shopkeepers kept separate utensils for Muslims and for everyone else, and one restaurant owner refused Sikh customers, complaining: "We become prisoners within our own city." Not all traders felt so, since the pilgrims brought business.

## The Sikhs of Nankana Sahib

Giani Pratap housed the family of Kartar, a Sikh of the town, for a short time inside one of the gurdwaras in his care, and made Kartar's father a *sewaadar*, a worker, there. Most of the community were not natives of the city but recent migrants from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, where there is a considerable Sikh population. Families arrived after the wars of 1965 and 1971 and after the demolition of the Babri Mosque in 1992, and the community grew; accounts of its size differ, but it was most often put at about two hundred households, with Jatha Singh as its leader for several years.

Most of the newcomers were housed within the gurdwaras, in rooms generally reserved for pilgrims, and the influx gave a confidence boost to the Sikhs, who until then had been dominated by the Muslim majority. Many Sikh families practised *hikmat*, the traditional medicine, and young men moved to Lahore, Faisalabad and Gujranwala for trade. Guru Nanak High School was the only school in the country where the Granth Sahib was taught to Sikh children. The caretakers of Gurdwara Rori Sahib at Eminabad, Pathan Sikhs, moved there from Nankana Sahib. Several people at Nankana Sahib felt that the PSGPC was not representative of the Sikh community in Pakistan.

The town's festivals grew with the community. Lohri and Guru Gobind Singh's birthday had only begun to be kept a couple of years before, made possible by the migration from the tribal areas; earlier there had been only a prayer at one of the town's gurdwaras. On Guru Gobind Singh's birthday in January 2011 the community held a cricket tournament on the ground of Guru Nanak Government College, with teams called Khalsa XI, Nankana XI and Singh XI. For the Sikh New Year, on 14 March 2011, *qawwals*, singers of Sufi devotional music, were invited from Faisalabad to sing first to Baba Farid; it was the first time since Partition that Muslim *qawwals* had been invited to perform at Nankana Sahib.

The festival has also seen violence. In November 1985, on Guru Nanak's birthday, a year after Operation Blue Star, thousands of Sikh pilgrims had come to Nankana from all over the world. At that time the SGPC in Amritsar still managed the Pakistani gurdwaras through the Indian High Commission. During a speech calling for a separate Sikh state, the gate of one of the town's gurdwaras was closed, and Counsellor B. Jain and First Secretary K. K. Khanna were attacked and barely escaped alive. The religious festivals at Nankana and in other cities of Pakistan went back to being religious festivals.

## Legacy

With Kiara Sahib, Gurdwara Mal Ji Sahib stands among the shrines that turn the town of Guru Nanak's birth into a map of his early life. Across Pakistan there are several sites where Nanak stayed or is said to have performed miracles, and gurdwaras built there later by devotees still stand.

In Aurangzeb's reign Baba Nanak was called the leader of the caravan on the roads of truth and the bearer of the torch of the mystic way. His seat passed on through his successors: Guru Har Rai was called the holder of Baba Nanak's seat, his *sajjada nashin*, one who sits on the prayer carpet. In the same reign the place of Baba Nanak lay twelve *kos*, an old measure of distance, from Batala, on the bank of the Ravi, and his descendants lived there.

Muslims have claimed him too. Many Muslims acquainted with his teachings were devoted to him and visited his shrine; Nadeem, a Muslim painter, said that Guru Nanak is like a Muslim Sufi saint, and a Muslim storyteller at Gurdwara Sachkand called him "a true devotee of God. He was a rightly guided soul." Baba Farid, it is said, became an inspiration for Guru Nanak when Nanak visited his shrine on his travels. Nanak is held to have been a spiritual follower of Baba Farid, and to have travelled to Pak Pattan to collect the saint's poetry from his descendants. Farid's verses are revered among the Sikhs, for he is held to have influenced the founder of their religion, and at one of the shrines of Guru Nanak at Nankana Sahib a picture of Baba Farid accompanied the picture of the Guru. A twentieth century shaykh of the Chishti Sabiri Sufi order counted Guru Nanak, with Krishna, Rama, Sita and Lachman, among the spiritual luminaries outside Islam who believed in the one true God and had a "Divine connection", *nisbat*.

His followers are many, in the Punjab and beyond. Nanak Panthi Hindus in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Punjab and Sindh keep a picture of Guru Nanak in their temples beside the pictures of Hindu deities. Many Lohanas in Sindh follow the teachings of Nanak, and the Nanakpanth among the Hindus of Sindh may have been brought from the Punjab by the Aroras in the eighteenth century. At the shrine of the goddess Hinglaj, the engraved footsteps of Guru Nanak connect that temple to the Sikh and Nanak *panth*, or sect, traditions.

The gurdwara keeps a story of Nanak the son: a child herding in his father's fields at Talwandi, asleep in the heat of the day and shaded by a cobra.
